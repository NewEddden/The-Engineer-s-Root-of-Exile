Chapter 139 - In Public

He answered the letters on the fourteenth, all of them, on the new paper, in his own hand, not Hale's.

Six of them, and a seventh that had come since. He did not answer them the same way. Ferra had told him that a man who answered seven houses with one letter copied seven times would have seven enemies by the spring, and a man who answered each of them in its own words would have only five, and she had been right about smaller things. So he wrote each one. To the old poor house in the north-west that wanted Grayrock's stone, he wrote that Grayrock's stone would be sold, in time, through the House of Ducatis, at a fair price, to anyone, and that the house would be welcome to buy it. To the rich new house in the capital that wanted an old name for its grandchildren, he wrote that his own name was older than was good for anybody and that he would not wish it on their daughter. To the house that owed money to a house that owed money to the House of Ducatis, he wrote nothing about money at all, and Saban, reading it over his shoulder, said that was the most cutting thing he had ever seen written on paper.

And in every one of them, somewhere, in its own words, the same thing. That the holder of Kaldo was grateful, and honoured, and would not marry their daughter. Because he had already asked somebody else.

He did not say who in the letters. He did not have to. By the time the riders had carried them out of the South Gate, the whole of the high street knew.



It cost him. He knew exactly what.

He sat at the table on the Monday and Saban priced it for him, the way Saban priced everything, without being asked, because Saban said a man should know what he had paid for a thing before he decided whether he had been robbed.

The north-west house had a cousin on the king's council who would have voted with Kaldo on the stone. He would not now. The rich new house in the capital lent money to half the merchants on the coast road, and every one of those merchants would hear by the spring that the prince of Kaldo had turned down the house's daughter, and the coast road's price for Kaldo's soap and spirit would go up a little, because it could. The earl's second son had a brother who commanded the crown's garrison at the Isle of Shards. And Sallow.

"Sallow," said Saban, and stopped.

Nobody at the table said anything.

"The first queen's brother," said Saban. "The first queen. Your brother's mother." He turned his hat once on his knee. "She sent you her niece. Her own niece, in her own colours, with her own steward riding at the carriage window. It was the best match in the kingdom for a disgraced third son in a plague barony, and every house at court knew it, because she made sure they did." He put the hat down. "And you'll send the girl home."

"Yes."

"For the Chancellor." Saban did not look at Ferra. "Who has no title. And no house. And no name in any book but Keller's."

"Yes."

Saban looked at him for a while across the table. Then he picked his hat up again.

"I'd have done the same," he said. "I'd have done it worse. I'd have told the steward why." He put the hat on his knee. "She'll take it as an insult to her house. That's what she'll take it as. Not a man choosing a woman. An insult to Sallow, from Amara's son, in front of the court." He looked at the table. "She'll remember it."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Saban. "I'm saying it so it's in the book." He looked at Ferra. "It's in the book?"

"It's in the book," said Ferra, and did not look up.



He told Isolde himself, in person, on the fifteenth, before her steward could hear it from anybody else.

He did it in the counting room, because that was where she was. She had been there most mornings since she came, in her grey wool, at the end of Ferra's table, with Ilsa Renner's Grayrock ledger and a pen, learning to check a column, and Ottoline had stopped arguing with her about carrying after the first week and started explaining it, which was the highest compliment Ottoline paid anybody. When Emeric came in she looked up from the ledger, and saw his face, and put her pen down.

"Ah," she said.

He asked her to walk with him. She got up, and took her cloak off the peg, and came, without looking at Ferra, and Ferra did not look up from her own book, and Ottoline at the end of the table looked from one to the other of them over her spectacles and then very deliberately at the ceiling.

They walked on the wall. Along the north stretch, in the cold, in the thin February sun, with the river below and the shed under the north wall and the black hull in her cradle inside it, and Somebody Came lying at the foot of the slip with her funnel cold.

He told her. He did not make a speech. He had learned in January what his speeches did. He told her that he would not marry her, and that it was not because of anything she was or had done, and that he had asked somebody else, and that she had not said yes yet, and might not, and that he would not marry anybody else whatever she said.

Isolde walked beside him with her hands in her cloak and listened to all of it.

"The Chancellor," she said, when he had finished.

"Yes."

"I knew." She did not sound surprised. "I knew the first night. At dinner. When I asked her about the books and she looked up from her plate." She walked a few steps. "I thought you didn't know. At first. I thought, he doesn't know, and she does, and she's going to sit at that table every night and watch him marry me and say nothing." She looked at the river. "That's what would have happened at home. That's what my aunt would have arranged. That's what I was sent to do, if you want to know. To be the woman at your table that the Chancellor watched."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Isolde. She smiled her quick smile, and it went away. "Everybody here knows everything. It's very strange. At home nobody knows anything, and everybody pretends to know everything. Here it's the other way round." She was quiet. "She was kind to me."

"Ferra."

"She didn't have to be. She knew exactly what I was. She knew it the moment I got down out of the carriage, the same way I knew what she was." Isolde stopped walking. She stood at the parapet and looked down at the river. "And she sat me down at her table and showed me how to rule a page. And let me check a column. And told my aunt's steward I was busy." She looked at her own hands on the stone. "Nobody's ever told my aunt's steward I was busy."

Emeric did not say anything.

"I don't want to marry you," said Isolde. "You should know that. It isn't anything you are. You'd be a perfectly good husband, I expect, if you were somebody else's. I don't want to marry anybody my aunt sends me to." She looked at the river. "There's a knight in my father's household. In the north. He has no money and no lands and he's been in love with me since I was fifteen and he's never once said so, because he knows exactly what I'm for." She was quiet. "I think, if I go home unmarried, from the prince of Kaldo, in disgrace, the way my aunt will say it, my father may decide I'm not worth sending anywhere else. And then there's the knight." She turned from the parapet and looked at him. "So you see. You've done me a kindness. You won't know it. My aunt won't. But you have."

"I'm glad."

"So am I," said Isolde Sallow. She put her hand out, as a man would have, and he took it, and she shook his hand once, firmly, the way Ferra had taught her to shake hands at the counting room table because Ferra said a woman who was going to check a man's columns should shake his hand first so he knew she meant it. "Tell the Chancellor," she said, "that I'll miss her table. And that if she ever needs eyes in my aunt's house, she'll have them." She let go. "She'll know what I mean."

She went back along the wall toward the keep stair, in her cloak, not quickly. At the head of the stair she stopped and looked back.

"Your speech," she said. "The one you didn't make. Did she stop you?"

Emeric looked at her.

"I thought so," said Isolde, and went down the stair.

She left on the twentieth, in the carriage with the green and silver arms, behind the four matched greys, with her eight riders and her aunt's steward on his tall bay at the carriage window with a face like a closed door. The high street came out to watch her go, as it had come out to watch her come. At the South Gate the carriage stopped, and the window came down, and she put her head out and looked back up the high street at the keep, at its windows, once, counting; and then at the counting room's window on the ground floor, where a red head was bent over a book and did not look up.

Isolde raised her hand to it anyway. Then the window went up, and the carriage went out under the arch, and was gone down the south road.



Saban's man in the capital wrote on the twenty-third, by Saban's carrier, in nine days.

The first queen had received her niece home. She had received her in her own rooms, in private, and nobody knew what had been said. But the next day, at the king's dinner, in front of the whole of the high table, the first queen had said, to nobody in particular, that she had heard Amara's son had refused a daughter of Sallow for a smith's girl with ink on her hands, and that she supposed it was what came of sending boys to plague towns, and that she wished him joy of it. And the high table had laughed, because it was expected to. And the king had not.

That was all Saban's man knew. The king had not laughed. He had gone on eating. He had said nothing at all. And after a while the high table had stopped laughing, one after another, and finished its dinner in silence.



Ferra answered in public on the twenty-sixth of February, at a sitting of the table she and Ward had required, in front of all six, and Hale, and the minutes.

She had not answered in private. She had not said anything at all about it since the night of the ink. She had sat across the table from him every Monday and done the business of the four baronies, and held out her hand for figures without looking up, and when he went to her room at night she let him in and they worked, and sometimes she let him stay late, and sometimes, with the shutters closed, she put her hand flat on his chest and stopped whatever he was saying, and did not need ink to do it; but she did not say yes, and she did not say no, and he did not ask again, because she had told him he would have to wait for the rest.

At the end of the business on the twenty-sixth, when Hale had read the last item and put down the sheet, Ferra did not close her book.

"There's another matter," she said. "For the minutes."

Everybody at the table looked at her. Hale picked his pen back up.

"The holder has asked me to marry him," said Ferra.

She said it to the table. Not to him. In the voice she used for a total. Bram, at her side, went very still. Imani, at the end by the door, did not move at all. Armand, at the fire end, folded his hands on his blanket.

"He asked me in January," said Ferra. "He's refused seven houses since, in writing, and the first queen's niece in person, and the minutes have the cost. Saban priced it." She did not look at Saban. "I said I hadn't said no. I'm saying the rest now, here, because it has to be said here, at this table, in front of all of you, and in the book. Not in my room. Not on a stair."

She put her hand flat on the book.

"Yes," said Ferra. "To him." She did not look at Emeric. "And not yet to a wedding."

The table was silent.

"Clause nine," said Ferra. "The Chancellor's stay. It's the only thing at this table that isn't his. The day I marry him, every man in the four baronies who wants to say he's wrong and can't will look at the stay and see his wife. I won't have that. Not until I know how a Chancellor marries the man her stay binds, and can show the four baronies that she has. I don't know yet. I'll work it out." She lifted her hand off the book. "Until then I'll hold the stay, and I'll hold him, and I won't hold a wedding. Write it down."

Hale wrote it down. His pen was the only sound in the hall.

Then Bram put both his hands flat on the table and pushed himself up out of his chair and stood there, a big grey man in a clean shirt at a table he had never once in two years stood up at, and looked at his daughter, and opened his mouth, and shut it, and opened it again.

"Your mother," he said. And did not say anything else. And sat down again, very heavily, and put his face in his hands.

Ferra did not look at him. She looked at the book. Her face had gone white, the way it went, with the freckles standing out like something spilled. And under the table, where nobody at the table could see it but the man sitting across from her, her foot found his, and stayed there.

Ward said: "Good."

Armand said: "About time."

Saban said that it was the worst bargain he had ever seen struck at any table and he'd have made it himself in a heartbeat.

Imani, at the end by the door, looked at Ferra down the length of the table for a long breath. Then she looked at Emeric. Then she said, quite clearly, in her own voice, dry and level: "Yes. Good." And meant it. And looked down at her papers.



That night he went up to the line room late, as he always did, past the foot of the east tower's stair.

The light was on under Vantic's door, at the top of the stair, as it always was at that hour. He had stopped at the foot of the stair every night since the spring, for about as long as it took to read a line, and gone on. She had told him on the river that she heard him. That she had never once asked what line he read.

He stopped.

He stood at the foot of the stair and looked up at the light under the door. And found that he did not know what line he was reading, tonight, and that he had never known, and that it had always been something like the shape of a person on the other side of a door, quiet, reading too, at the same hour, in a room that smelled of damp and not of mice.

He did not go up. He had never gone up.

From behind the door, very faintly, he heard a pen. Writing. A short line. And then stopping. And then nothing at all, for as long as it would take a woman to sit very still at a desk with her pen lifted, listening to a man at the foot of her stair.

He went on up to the line room.

He did not stop at the foot of the east tower's stair again after that. He thought she would know why. He thought, going up, that she would be the one person in Kaldo who would understand it exactly, and would write nothing about it to anyone, ever, and he was right about both.



At the dawn drill on the twenty-seventh, in the yard, in the frost, with the garrison doing Rylan's sets along the wall and Ward watching, Emeric stood at one end of the yard with his hands at his sides and looked at the sand sack at the other end, hanging from its post, forty feet away.

He did not lift his hand.

He saw the fire. He saw it where he wanted it: on the sack, at the height of a man's chest, a thumb of flame, no bigger. And he let it go, out of himself, without his hand, without pointing, the way he had let it go in the hold of the boat in December with his wrists tied behind his back.

A thumb of flame stood on the sand sack, forty feet away, at the height of a man's chest. It burned for a breath. Then it went out, and left a black mark the size of a coin on the canvas.

The garrison at the wall had not seen. They were counting sets. Ward had.

He did it again. And again. Ten times, before the drill ended. And every time, he had to see the sack. He had to be looking at it. He tried it once with his eyes shut, seeing the sack in his head instead, the way Rylan said a knight saw the jump; and the flame went somewhere, he felt it go, and nothing happened on the sack at all, and a little later Maren came out of the kitchen door at the other side of the yard and wanted to know who had singed her drying cloth.

Line of sight. Still. He could put fire anywhere he could see. Not anywhere he could not.

Ward, at the guardroom door, wrote something on Rylan's board, in a square after Emeric's name, that Emeric could not read from where he stood. When he went to look at it afterward, it said: ten. And under it, smaller: one to Maren.
