Chapter 139 - You Didn't Walk

He went to the ward the next night, because Ferra had said before anything else, and because if he waited another night he would not go at all.

It was late. The cooper was gone home and the boy with the arm was gone home, and there was only one bed full, at the far end by the stove, an old man from the dock quarter with a cough, asleep, and one of Imani's women on a stool beside him with her knitting. The lamp was lit on the desk by the side door. Imani was at it, with the register.

She looked up when he came in. She saw his face, and put her pen down, and did not say anything.

"Can I talk to you," he said. "Not here. Somewhere the register can't hear."

Imani looked at him a moment longer. Then she got up, and said something quietly to the woman with the knitting, and took the lamp off the desk, and went out through the side door and across the lane, and he followed her.

It was her own house. Her own door, the one she had put her hand flat on in the first summer, and behind it the room he had stood in once, the night she put her hand on her own door frame and told him to go home: a small room with a low ceiling and a fire, and two chairs by the fire, and a shelf above it with a razor on it folded in leather and a net needle worn bright at the eye. She put the lamp on the shelf beside them. She did not sit down. She stood by the fire with her arms folded and looked at him.

"Highness," she said.

"Don't."

She waited.

"You asked me what I was," Emeric said. "Twice. In the laundry yard, the spring before the war. And at your desk, a year ago, with a lamp stone between us." He did not look away from her. "I didn't answer either time. I'm going to answer some of it now. Not all. I don't know how to say all of it yet. But some."

Imani did not move.

He took the lamp stone out of the lamp. He did not reach for it. He looked at it, sitting in its brass cup on the shelf beside the razor, glowing; and he let the mana go out of him, the way he had let it go behind the shutters every night for five years, told what a weight was and told to take it off.

The lamp stone rose out of the lamp.

It came up out of its cup, slowly, glowing, and hung in the air above the shelf at the height of her face, and the light of it went round the little room as it rose, over the low ceiling and the two chairs and the razor and the net needle, and threw her shadow up the wall behind her, very large.

Imani looked at it.

She did not step back. She did not make a sound. She stood by her own fire with her arms folded and looked at a lamp stone hanging in the air of her house with nothing holding it, and he watched her face, and saw it do nothing at all, for a long breath; and then he saw something go across it that was not surprise.

"You didn't walk," he said. "The night after the war. You were asleep against the wall by Tess's bed, with your head on your knees. And there was a bed beside you with nobody in it. And I lifted you into it. Like that." He let the stone come down, slowly, back into its cup. "And put my coat over you. And went home. And when you brought the coat back and asked me how you'd got into that bed, I said you walked."

The lamp stone settled in its cup. The light steadied.

"I lied to you," Emeric said. "In front of Ferra. And you knew it was a lie, and you put it away with the rest and closed the lid, and I watched you do it. And you brought it out again at your desk a year ago, I saw you, when I didn't answer you. You remembered it then." He was quiet. "I've wanted to say it every day since. That you didn't walk."

Imani unfolded her arms.

She looked at the lamp in its cup. At him. At the bed, he thought, in her head, the one by Tess's, and the wall, and how far it was from the wall to the bed, which she had known exactly, the morning she woke under his coat.

"Gravity," she said.

"Yes."

"Not fire."

"Fire too. Fire's the one I show."

"And the dog that was always sick. And the knot. And the room." She did not make them questions. "And the bead, in a night, that Ivo can't grind. And your ankle on the keep stair." She looked at him. "And your hands, in December, hot as a stove after you'd been in a river."

"Some of it. Not all. There's more I can't say yet. I don't know how."

"No," said Imani. "I don't suppose you do." She was very still. "Is that what you came to say?"

"No."

He told her the rest.

He told her the way he had told Ferra. Badly, in the order it came, without a speech, standing in her little room by her father's chair with his hands at his sides. That there were two. That he had stood in Ferra's doorway with her name in his mouth and Imani's had come up behind it and he could not say either. That he had not chosen. That he had tried, every night, and it came out two. That where he was raised a man who said that was a man who could not make up his mind, and maybe he was. That he could not bear to hurt either of them, and the only way he could see not to was not to choose, and he knew how selfish that was. That he had been afraid to tell them because he thought he would lose them both. That he had let her think, for a week, that he had chosen, because he had not had the courage to come to her desk and say he had not.

"I told Ferra first," he said. "Last night."

Imani looked at him.

"Of course you did," she said. It was not bitter. It was exact. "She'd have found it in a column anyway." She looked at the fire. "Does she know you're here?"

"She sent me. She said she wouldn't carry it. That it was yours, and you should have it from me, before she'd hear another word."

Imani was quiet for some time.

"Did she," she said.

She sat down. Not in her chair. In the other one, the one by the fire with the net needle above it on the shelf, that had been her father's, that he had looked at the first time he came to her door and not asked about. She sat in it with her hands in her lap and looked at the fire, and he stood, because she had not asked him to sit.

"I sent a child to do my drawings," Imani said. "For a week. Because I saw ink in her hair and your hand going into hers at the table, and I did the sum, and I thought, well, there it is, he's chosen, and it's the right choice, she's the one who knows him, and I'm a net-mender's daughter with a ward, and it was always going to be her." She did not look at him. "I called you Highness. I sat at that table and was sensible and said the right things about standpipes. I thought I was being kind. To her. To you. I thought I was being dignified." She turned her head and looked at him then. "And you let me."

"Yes."

"You let me do all of it. You stood in the door of that shed with my girl telling you about tadpoles and let me think I'd lost, when I hadn't even been asked." Her voice did not rise. It went lower. "That's what I mind. Not that there are two. I don't know what I think about that yet. I mind that you let me be sensible about a thing that wasn't true."

He did not say anything. There was nothing to say that would not have been about himself.

Imani looked at him a while longer, in the firelight, from her father's chair.

"Go home, Emeric," she said.

It was the first summer. Her hand flat on the door frame. Not unkind. Not anything. A healer telling a man there was nothing more to be done tonight and he should go and sleep.

He went. He went out through her door into the lane, past the ward's lit side door, where the woman knitting by the stove did not look up, and up the hill, and did not know, all the way up, whether he had just lost her or been told to wait, and understood by the top of the high street that she did not know either.



The Sallow steward wanted an answer.

He had wanted one for three weeks. He had been very patient about it, in the way that a tall grey man with very good gloves is patient on behalf of a queen, which is to say that he asked at dinner every second night, in a voice pitched so the whole high table could hear, whether the holder had yet had leisure to consider his mistress's niece's situation. And the six letters from the other houses sat on Ferra's trestle in a pile, unanswered, and two more had come, and their riders were eating at the inn at the barony's expense and asking the innkeeper how long a prince took to read a letter.

Isolde did not ask. She went to the counting room every morning in her grey wool and checked Ilsa Renner's columns and argued with Ottoline about carrying, and at dinner she sat on his right as the steward made very sure she would, and asked Ward about the garrison, and asked Maren about the pastry, and did not once ask Emeric anything.

On the twentieth she found him on the wall, on the north stretch, in the afternoon, where he had gone because there was nobody on it.

She came along the wall walk in her cloak with her hands in it and stopped beside him at the parapet and looked at the shed under the north wall, and Somebody Came at the foot of the slip with her funnel cold.

"Two of them," said Isolde.

He looked at her.

"I've watched you look at the Chancellor at every dinner for three weeks," said Isolde. "And at the healer, at the end of the table, when she comes, which isn't often. And I've watched them both not look at you." She looked at the river. "And I've watched you stand in this exact place every afternoon for four days with a face like my uncle's dogs when they've been left out in the rain." She was quiet. "In my aunt's house, a man of your rank with two women he couldn't choose between would have both of them and tell neither. Everybody would know but the two women. That's how it's done." She turned her head. "You look like a man who told both."

"Yes."

"Ah," said Isolde. She looked at the river again. "Then you're braver than my uncle. And stupider." She smiled her quick smile, and it went away. "I'll tell my aunt's steward you're ill. It'll buy you a week. He's frightened of illness. He thinks the south is full of it."

"Why?"

Isolde did not answer at once.

"Because she was kind to me," she said. "The Chancellor. And because the healer looked at my steward's cough at dinner on the second night, across the table, and told him what it was and what to do about it, and he was furious, and he's been doing it ever since, and it's better." She took her hands out of her cloak and put them on the parapet. "And because I don't want to marry you, if you'd like to know. And I'd rather go home having been refused for something real than for nothing." She looked at him sideways. "So decide something real."

She went back along the wall.



On the night of the twenty-second, Ferra went down the hill.

He felt her go. He had kept his sense open all day every day since the spring, thin, round him, without thinking; and in the last week he had found it opening further than he meant, at night, at his window, reaching out over the town the way a man's hand reaches out in the dark for something on a table, without his telling it to. Looking for two notes. Finding them. One in the keep, two floors below him, small and hard and close. One down the hill at the top of the lower ward, wide and warm, in the house across the lane from the ward.

At a little after the second bell after dark the small hard one moved.

It went along the passage under his feet and down the keep stair, and out through the keep yard, and under the arch, and down the high street. Steadily. At a walk. He knew the walk. He had walked beside it on the ice by the cooper's and on the road above Wayford and up the high street from the school with a roll under her arm. It went down through the inner ward and the upper town, past the tower with its blue roof, past the square, into the lower ward, along the lane to where the wide warm one was.

And stopped there.

Beside it.

He stood at his window in the dark with the shutters open and the cold coming in and felt the two of them, down the hill, in a little room across the lane from the ward with a low ceiling and two chairs by a fire. Two notes. Side by side. Not moving.

He could not hear anything. He could not see anything. He did not know what they were saying, or whether they were saying anything, or whether one of them was angry, or both. He knew only where they were, and that they were together, and that he was not.

He did not go.

He wanted to. He wanted to so much that his hands hurt on the sill. He stood at the window and thought of going down the high street in the dark and standing in the lane outside Imani's door, the way he had stood in Ferra's passage, and it was the worst idea he had ever had, and he did not go, and he did not shut the sense either. He could not. He stood at the window and held it open and felt them, because it was the only part of whatever was happening that he was allowed.

The door opened behind him. He did not turn round. He knew the note: a wall.

Ward came in and shut the door and did not light anything. He came and stood at the other side of the window, with his back to the wall, and looked out at the dark town, at nothing.

"You are listening," said Ward, after a while.

"Yes."

"Stop."

"I can't."

Ward did not say anything else. He stood at the other side of the window in the dark, as he had stood behind Emeric in every room of his life, and did not leave.

The notes down the hill did not move much. Once the wide warm one got up and went across the little room to where the fire would be, and stood there, and came back. Once, near the end of the second hour, the two of them were very close together, closer than two people sit in two chairs, so close he could hardly tell one note from the other; and he did not know whether one of them was holding the other, or which, or why, and he stood at the window with his hands on the sill and did not breathe.

A little before the fourth bell they moved.

Both of them. Together. Out of the little room, out into the lane. And up. Up through the lower ward, past the square, past the tower, up the high street, under the arch, into the keep yard. Side by side. At a walk. The small hard one and the wide warm one, together, slowly, up the whole of the town in the dark, as if they were two women walking home from a long evening who had a great deal still to say and no hurry about saying it.

Into the keep. Up the stair. Along the passage two floors below him. Into Ferra's room.

The door shut. He felt it shut.

Not his. Hers.

He stood at the window. Beside him, in the dark, Ward let out a breath that was not quite a laugh.

"Go to bed, Highness," said Ward. "They will tell you in the morning, or they will not. Either way you will want to have slept."

He did not sleep. He lay on his bed in his clothes and felt the two notes two floors below him, in Ferra's room, close together, until the window went grey; and some time before it did, he could not have said when, he felt them both go still in the way a person goes still when they have fallen asleep in a chair.



They came to his office at eight, before the counting room opened.

Both of them. Ferra first, with the barony's book under her arm, out of habit, he thought, because she had nothing to write in it. Imani behind her, with her sleeves not yet pinned for the ward, and her hair tied up hard. They had not slept much either. He could see it. Ferra's eyes were red at the rims. Imani looked the way she looked at the end of a long night with a fever, tired all through and quite clear.

Ferra shut the door. Imani went to the window and leaned on the sill with her arms folded. Ferra stood in the middle of the room with the book against her chest.

He stood up from his table. He did not know what to do with his hands.

"Sit down," said Imani. "You look terrible."

He sat down.

"We've talked," said Ferra.

"All night," said Imani. "She kept a list. Even of that."

"I kept a list because there were a great many things to say and I didn't want to forget any of them," said Ferra, without looking round. "Somebody had to." She opened the book. There was a page in it, a fresh one, in her small upright hand, ruled. He could not read it upside down from where he sat. She did not turn it round. "There are terms."

"There are always terms," said Imani, to the window.

"There are always terms," Ferra agreed. She looked at the page. "The first is that nobody is first." She did not look up. "Neither of us is the prince's wife. Not in the way the court means it. Not the woman at the high table in green wool while somebody else does her work. I keep the books, all four baronies of them, and the stay, which is still mine and still says what it says, and that doesn't change for you or for her or for anybody. And she keeps the ward." She looked at Imani. "Say it."

"I keep the ward," said Imani. "And the hospital when it's built. And the water and the drains and the burials and the food and every child who comes through my door with the flux. And I don't give up one bed of it to sit at your table and be looked at." She was looking at him now. "That was my reason. You should know that. Not that you didn't choose. That if you'd chosen me I'd have had to stop being what I am to be what you are. And I wouldn't. I'd have said no to you and meant it and hated it." She was quiet. "Two is different. Two means nobody has to be the prince's wife. Two means there's one of us at the table with the ledger and one of us at the ward with the knife, and neither of us is anybody's second." She looked at Ferra. "She saw it before I did. Around midnight. It annoyed me very much."

"The second," said Ferra, before he could say anything, "is that there is nothing either of us knows about you that the other doesn't."

He looked at her.

"She told me about the lamp stone," said Ferra. "In her house. And I told her about the beams in my room." Her mouth moved very slightly at the corner and stopped. "We've compared columns. Every line. Everything you've told either of us, and everything you've shown either of us, and everything either of us has ever written down about you and not asked." She put her finger on the page. "From now on, what you tell one of us, you tell both. What you show one of us, you show both. Whatever the rest of it is, the part you said you can't say yet, the thing that makes Imani's dog sick and your hands hot after a river, when you're ready to say it, you say it to both of us, in the same room, at the same time." She looked at him. "No more secrets that one of us has and the other doesn't. That's the term I won't move on."

"Nor will I," said Imani.

"Yes," said Emeric.

"The third," said Ferra, "is not yet."

"Not yet to a wedding," said Imani. "Hers or mine. She's got the stay to think about, and I've got a ward that's been the whole of my life since the plague, and I'm not handing half of it to anybody in a week." She looked at the shelf on his wall, at nothing. "I've never been a wife. I'll be one when I'm ready, and not when the court is, or the first queen is, or you are."

"Not yet," said Ferra. "That's all. Not never." She looked at the page. "And the fourth."

She stopped. She looked at Imani.

"The fourth," said Imani, "is that you don't get to make this easy on yourself. There'll be days you're miserable. There'll be days we are. There'll be a day she's angry with you and I'm not and you'll want to come to the ward and be comforted, and I'll send you back up the hill to her, because that's the arrangement." She unfolded her arms. "We'll tell you when. You'll listen."

"Yes."

"You keep saying yes," said Imani. "Say something else."

He looked at the two of them. Ferra in the middle of his office with the book open against her chest and her eyes red at the rims, standing very straight. Imani at the window with her arms unfolded now and her hands at her sides, tired all through, quite clear.

"Why," he said.

It came out of him rough.

"Why would you. Either of you. I came to you both and said I couldn't choose and wanted not to have to, and it's the most selfish thing I've ever said to anyone, and you went and sat in a room all night and came back with terms." He did not know what his face was doing. "Why?"

Ferra looked at the page in her book for a long breath.

"Because I didn't want her sad," she said.

"And I didn't want her sad," said Imani.

They did not look at each other. They did not need to.

"We both sat in that room," said Ferra, "and both of us had the other's line in our heads all night. Every moment with you. Hers and mine. The river and the ward. The pen and the bead." She closed the book. "I'm not going to stand in the counting room for the next forty years knowing I won because she was sensible. And she's not going to stand in the ward knowing she lost because I got there first. Neither of us would have. Neither of us could have stood it." She held the book against her chest. "So."

"So," said Imani.

Imani came away from the window.

She came across the office to where he sat at his table, and stood in front of him, close, the way she had stood at the corner of her desk in the ward a year ago with a lamp stone shining up between them. And he stood up, because he could not stay sitting, and her face was a hand's breadth from his.

"I said I'd ask again," said Imani. "What you are."

"Yes."

"I'm not going to." She looked at him. "I know enough. I know you lied to me about a bed and came and told me so, a year late, with a lamp stone hanging in my house. I know you came to my door and stood by my father's chair and told me the most stupid selfish true thing a man has ever told me, and didn't dress it up. That's enough to be going on with." She looked at his mouth, and he saw her see him see it, exactly as she had a year ago. "The rest you'll tell us both."

She did not stop this time.

She kissed him. Not long. Not like the ink. Quietly, and certainly, the way she did everything she had decided to do, as if she had been standing at the corner of a desk for a year with the question unasked and had finally, this morning, set it down and picked up something else.

Behind her, Ferra had turned to the window.

"I'll look at the river," said Ferra, to the window. "It's very interesting this morning. There's a heron."

Imani took her mouth away. She did not step back. She stood a hand's breadth from him with her eyes on his, and something at the corner of her mouth that was not quite a smile, and did not quite stop.

He did not know what to say. He looked from one of them to the other, Imani in front of him and Ferra at the window with her back to them and her book against her chest and her ears gone pink under the black-ended hair, and he could not find a single word in either of his lives.

"I don't understand," he said. "How. Two of you. How do you just,"

"I counted," said Ferra, from the window, without turning round. "Two's even."

Imani looked at him, a hand's breadth away, quite seriously, the way she looked at a wound she was about to explain to a man who did not want to hear it.

"Even in the body," she said. "Two lungs. Two kidneys. Two eyes, two hands, two of everything that matters enough to keep a spare. Every organ that comes with a copy comes with exactly one, and stops." She put one finger flat on his chest, over the left side, the way she had in the gate passage in July, and held it there. "Nothing in a body comes in threes. Don't go looking."
