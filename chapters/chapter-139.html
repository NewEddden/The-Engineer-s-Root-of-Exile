Chapter 139 - The Bins

Ferra came north at the start of the summer by road, in daylight, with the weekly rider and two of Ward's men and a box of books, and told the keeper at every relay post on the way that she was going to Wayford to see the barony's accounts there, which was true, and which she did, on her first morning, at the counting house with Pennock, for three hours, before she went anywhere near the ink house.

Pennock's books were in order. She had known they would be. She told him so, and he said thank you, and neither of them said anything else for the rest of the three hours, and at the end of it she thought that she had not spent so pleasant a morning in a year.

Then she went up the junction road.

The room had been limed white and it was the cleanest room she had ever been given. It had two printers down its long wall on trestles and a table under the window and a stove, and a shelf at the far end with a row of bottles on it that nobody touched, and a box with its card still tied round it that nobody had opened. It had Wenna and Dace in it, both standing up when she came in. And it had, laid out along the edge of the table in a row, three small stacks of printed sheets and, beyond them, two clean places with nothing on them.

She put her box down and looked at the stacks.

"Bins," she said.

Dace had made them. He had had them made, rather, by a cooper in the town who thought they were for rags: five open boxes of new deal, a little wider than the standard sheet and deeper than a hand, with a slot in the front of each for a card. Ferra wrote the cards herself. PLACIT. STRADE. HOLLIN. DRAY. VENNOR. She put them in their slots, and set the five boxes in a row along the back of the table under the window where the light fell across them, and put each stack into its box, and looked at the two that were empty, Strade's and Vennor's, for a little while.

"He writes at the manor," Wenna said. "Lord Vennor."

"I know. It's in the book. And she doesn't write in it at all." Ferra sat down. "Show me how it comes."

They showed her. They had been doing it for a month and they were proud of it, and she let them show her all of it, though she had read their weekly sheets and knew most of it already. The printer moved, when it moved, and a page came off it with its head at the top: the name of the knot that had heard it, which was a house, and the number of the knock, which was a boiling, and the root's count, which Wenna turned into a day and an hour on a slate by the stove with a table of figures Ferra had sent up in the spring. The page went into the bin of the house. Somebody read it the same day. What was nothing stayed in the bin. What was something went into a folder, and the folder went south once a week in the rider's sealed bag, five days to the keep, to her.

It was a good method. It had two faults in it, and she found them both before the stove had burned down.

The first was that the bins went by the knot and the knock, and neither the knot nor the knock knew who was holding the pen.

The second was the rider.

She wrote the rules that afternoon.

She wrote them at the table under the window on one side of one sheet, in her own hand and not Hobb's, and pinned them to the wall above the bins where nobody could sit at the table without seeing them.

Every page was read the same day by one of them, and the day it was read was written on its back with the reader's mark.

Every page was compared with the book of hands before it was filed. There was not yet a book of hands. She made one that evening: a quire of the standard sheet stitched down one side, a leaf to a house, and on each leaf the first page that house had ever sent them, cut out and pasted, with the name of the writer underneath where the writer was known. Hollin's leaf had two, the housekeeper's round laborious hand from the laundry list and Hollin's own square one. Dray's had one. Placit's, which had begun to come in the week before, when the warden of the Staple had decided that he could not be the last man in Wayford writing grey, had one. Strade's leaf had nothing on it. Vennor's had nothing on it.

A page whose hand was not in the book did not go up until it had been matched, or until it had been marked in red at its head as hand not known, so that whoever read it next knew what they were reading.

Anything that bore on no crime and no danger to Kaldo stayed in its bin. On the first day of every month the bins were emptied of it into the stove, by the two of them together, and one line entered in the room's book and copied into the bag for her box: the day, the number of sheets, bearing on no crime, destroyed, and both their marks. Not what the pages were. How many.

What went up, went up in the sealed bag. She entered the bag's days in her own book in a column: written, read, sealed, south, opened. Five days between the third and the fifth. She looked at the five for a while, and then she wrote a line under the column, in the place where the book said risks, where she had written the river and the voyage and the two halves of the relay key being in one town at once: the bag is five days behind the pen. Anything that must be done inside five days will not be done from Kaldo. She dated it and signed it. It did not make the five days any shorter. It was only that a risk she had written down was a risk somebody had decided to take, and a risk she had not written down was an accident.

The pages that were nothing were the hardest part, and she had known they would be.

Dray owed money. He owed it to a horse dealer at Milford, and to a tailor, and to his own steward, which was the one that made Wenna's mouth go small when she read it, and he wrote to all three of them in the same week in the same young untidy uphill hand, promising each of them the same quarter's rents. It was not a crime. It was a young man in a large house, and the south was full of them. Wenna put it in the bin.

Hollin's child had a fever. It was a girl of six, and it went on for eleven days, and Hollin wrote to the physician at Milford four times in eleven days, and the last letter was a single line, pressed so hard at the ends of the words that the head of the printer had laid its dots in pools. After that there was a letter to the physician thanking him, and after that nothing more about her. Dace read those. He read them in order, in one sitting, and filed them, and went out into the yard for a while.

And Placit wrote to a woman at Milford who was not his wife.

He wrote to her twice a week. He wrote well, better than he spoke, and more simply, and some of what he wrote was about his house and the Staple and the weather and some of it was not, and Wenna read the first of them and came to the end of it and turned it face down on the table and sat with her hand flat on it.

"What do I do with these?" she said.

Ferra looked at the rules on the wall, which she had written two days before, and which said what to do with them.

"Bin," she said. "First of the month."

"We read them."

"We have to read them. We can't know they're nothing till we've read them." Ferra took the page from under Wenna's hand, without looking at it, and put it in Placit's bin face down, and squared the stack. "Then they're nothing. Then they go in the stove, and we write down how many, and we don't write down what."

Wenna looked at the bin. She was seventeen. She had come top in every sum in Orrin's school for three years running and top in every sum Ferra had set her for two years since, and she had never in her life been asked to read a letter from a man to a woman that was not meant for her, and she had just read one, and she would read a great many more before the summer was out, because that was the work.

"Do you mind it?" she said.

Ferra thought about how to answer that, and found that she did not want to, and went back to the Hollin bin.

She did not write anything about it. There was nowhere in any of her books where it would have gone.

The page about the man came on the ninth day.

It came in the afternoon on the left-hand printer, under HOLLIN. 3. and the count, and it was four lines long:

To Crisp. The man from Tollet is to be moved Thursday after dark and not across the square. Take the back lane. Say nothing to the lad.

Dace read it first, because it was his hour. He read it and put it on the table without filing it, and went to find somebody, and the somebody he found was the prince, who was in the shop in front talking to Wendel about gum.

Ferra was at the counting house with Pennock. She was not there. She came back an hour later to the ink house and found the page on the table and Dace sitting beside it, and the prince gone, and Saban's man already gone after him, and she read it standing up with her coat still on.

"Where's he sent him?"

"To watch Hollin's house," Dace said. "Thursday. After dark. To see where the man goes."

Ferra turned the page over. There was nothing written on the back of it. She turned it back again and looked at the hand.

It was a good hand. It was clerkly and small and quick, and it sloped a little backward, and it was not Hollin's, which was square and pressed at the ends of its words, and it was not the housekeeper's, which was round and laborious and held its pen too hard. She knew that at once. She had pasted both of those into the book of hands with her own fingers five days before.

She did not say so. She looked at Dace, and Dace looked at the page, and then at the book of hands, which was on the shelf, where Dace had been going to take it down to do the comparing when he had finished the three pages ahead of this one in his pile, because he did the pages in the order they came, which was the right way to do them, and he was slower than Wenna and never wrong.

"I'd not got to it," he said.

"No."

"I'd have seen it."

"You would," said Ferra. It was true. It was also no use to anybody now.

Thursday came and it was nothing.

Saban's man sat in the lane behind Hollin's stable from dark until the second bell with his back to a wall and a bottle he did not drink from, and at the first bell a cart came down the lane, a covered cart with one man driving it and a lad walking at the horse's head, and went along the lane, and past the man, and out of the end of it toward the south road, not across the square. The man could not see what was in the cart. He could not have followed it without leaving the lane, and he did not leave the lane, because he had been told to watch Hollin's house, and Hollin was in Hollin's house all evening at his own table with eleven people round it, and never came out.

The man driving the cart looked at him as it went by. Just looked, the way a man looks at another man sitting in the dark in a lane where nobody sits.

Saban told them the rest of it on the Saturday. Hollin's steward was a man called Adlow, clerkly and small and quick, who had kept Hollin's books for nine years, and on the Friday morning Adlow had asked Hollin's groom whether anybody had been seen round the lane in the night, and on the Friday afternoon he had asked the same thing of the girl who swept the yard, and since the Friday he had not, so far as anybody in Hollin's kitchen knew, written a single thing at the desk in the little room where he did the books, which was the room under the knot. He did his letters now at a table in the back of the Staple, where he went every day in any case, and where the knot on the ram could not hear a man at the back of the building through the width of the hall.

Adlow's hand went into the book of hands on the Saturday night, on Hollin's leaf, under Hollin's and the housekeeper's, with his name under it.

Ferra entered the rest in the fund's book, in the column for risks, under the five days. Page from Hollin's batch in the steward's hand; hand not compared before a man was sent. One of Saban's men seen. The steward no longer writes where we can hear him. She dated it. She looked at it for some time. Then, because it was true and the book was the place where true things went, she wrote under it: A hole in the net, made by the net.

The prince read it over her shoulder that evening, at the inn table, with Ward and Saban and Hobb round them and the inn's supper going cold. He did not say anything for a moment.

"I read it and I went," he said. "I didn't ask whose hand."

"No."

"I'll ask whose hand."

"You'll not be asked to," Ferra said. "It'll say. It'll be written at the top in red." She turned the book round so that he could see the rule on the page before, which said so. "The rule's not for you. You'll forget. The rule's so you don't have to remember."

Saban laughed, once, into his cup. The prince did not. He looked at the rule for a while longer, and then at her, and then he picked up his spoon.

The page from the Staple came four days after that, and it was the first page in Strade's hand that the net had given them.

It was not in Strade's ink.

It came in under STAPLE. 2. and the count, which said that the knot on the ram had heard it and that the ink was out of Placit's boiling, and Wenna, whose hour it was, took it off the bed and did not file it. She took the book of hands down off the shelf first, the way the rule said, and found that the hand was not on Placit's leaf, or anybody's, and marked the page in red at its head, hand not known, and brought it to Ferra.

Ferra knew whose it was before she had it the right way up.

She had never seen Dame Strade's hand. But she had read every page of Sethe's four boxes over nine days in the spring with the two of them at the other end of the table, and among the letters in those boxes had been a small number from the five, and among the letters from the five had been perhaps a dozen in a hand that wrote shorter than any of the others, small and upright, every letter stood separately on the line like a row of pickets, as though whoever wrote it did not trust one letter to lean on the next.

"Strade," she said.

"It's Placit's ink."

"Placit is warden. He'll have sent two of his bottles across to the Staple's desk as a gift to the house. It's the sort of thing a warden does." Ferra laid the page down flat on the table. "And she'll have used what was on the desk. She'll not have thought twice about it."

"She doesn't trust him," Wenna said. "His Highness. Saban said she didn't. Hobb said."

"She doesn't trust him. She's no reason to distrust his ink." Ferra looked at the page. "Nobody would. Nobody in the world would think to."

It was a tally. That was all it was, on its face, and on its face there was nothing wrong with it that Ferra could have put her finger on, and she read it three times looking for the thing and did not find it. It had a heading, Second quarter, to the day, and under the heading five short lines in the small upright picket hand, each with a figure at its end, and a sixth line, drawn under the others and ruled, with a total.

Intake. Held against rent. Yield. Kept. Losses.

That was all. No units against any of them, and no goods, and no names. Whatever the Staple took in and held and got out of what it held, it knew what it was, and it did not need to write it down to know. Ferra had kept books like that. Every clerk who kept a book for one master kept one like it in the end, a book that only the two of them could read, because the two of them already knew what it was about.

She did not know what it was about.

She saw the shape of it. She could see it the way she could see the shape of a year's grain from a page of tallies without knowing which field was which: something came in, and was held, and gave something, and some of what it gave was kept back, and some of it was lost, and the losses were small against the rest and did not change much from line to line. It was a business. It was the Staple's business, in the Staple's own upper room, in the hand of the woman who kept its books. That much was plain. What the business was, was not written anywhere on the page.

Held against rent, she thought. That was a phrase she had seen before. She could not think where.

She put the page in Strade's bin, and then took it out again, and put it in the folder for the bag.

She went home at high summer, when the rules had held for three weeks without anybody but herself having to look at them to follow them.

She went the way she had come, by road in daylight with the rider and Ward's two men, and told the keepers at the relay posts that the Wayford accounts were in order, which they were. The prince stayed. He was learning the south; he sat at the Staple's table on weighing days and went to see Dray's horses and to Placit's house for supper, and in the evenings, at the inn, he read the weekly folder before it was sealed and did not act on anything in it without asking whose hand. She had watched him not do it twice. She thought that he would forget the third time, and that the rule would be there when he did, and that was what a rule was for.

Vantic was at the long-room table on her first evening home. She said good evening. She passed Ferra the bread. She did not ask where Ferra had been for a month, or why the Wayford accounts had needed the Chancellor to go and look at them in person when Pennock's books had never once in two years needed anybody to look at them twice, and Ferra knew from the fact that she did not ask that she knew the answer to the first question and had been wondering about the second for four weeks. They talked about the price of lamp oil instead. Vantic had a figure for it that was a Copper lower than Ferra's. Ferra went and looked it up after supper, and Vantic's was right.

The mill was in a temper.

She went down to it on her third morning because the converter had begun to make steel while she was away and she had kept the top tally for the converter since the autumn, thirty-eight Gold Rand and something over, and she wanted to see what thirty-eight Gold Rand looked like when it came off the rolls.

It looked like three bars of good bright steel split down their length like green wood.

They lay on the floor of the rolling shed by the mill race, where they had been thrown, each as long as a man and as thick as his wrist, and each open along its middle in a long ragged seam that had torn on the rolls with the bar still red, so that the two halves curled away from each other at the ends like the two halves of a split log. Bram was standing over them. Jorrin was standing beside him, not close. The boy who had been on the tongs was standing a long way off by the door, where Bram had put him, with a face on him like a boy who has been told three times in a morning that it was his fault.

Bram had said it was the heat. Then he had said it was the rolls. Then he had said it was the boy. The rolls had been checked and the heat had been checked and the boy was twelve and had done what he was told, and Bram knew it, and was standing over the bars with his hands hanging and his face set, a man who had built the thing with his own hands off drawings on the backs of rail tallies and had run the first blows himself and had had them come out of the pot as good steel as anybody in the south had ever made, and then had watched it tear itself in half on his own rolls three times running, and did not know why.

Emeric was in Wayford. Emeric was five days up the road, sitting at the Staple's table on a weighing day, being taught how a bale of rags was graded.

Ferra did not say anything. There was nothing she could have said that would have been any use.

Colm came in while they were standing there.

He had not come for the bars. He had come down from the glasshouse to borrow a crucible, because one of his had cracked and the foundry had spares, and he stood in the door of the rolling shed with his leather apron on and his hands black to the wrist from his own work and looked at the three bars on the floor, and at Bram, and did not go for the crucible.

He went and crouched by the nearest bar instead and looked at the seam. He put his thumb in it. He rubbed his thumb and finger together and smelled them.

"That's the same as my glass," he said.

Bram looked at him.

"When it was green," Colm said. "Before I got it clear. There was something in the sand that shouldn't have been there, and you couldn't see it, and it took the colour. The grit took it out. Off the Grayrock carts, the black grit." He stood up. "There's something in your iron that shouldn't be there. I'd try the grit."

"It's not glass," Bram said.

"No," Colm agreed. "I'd still try it."

Bram looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the three bars, and at Jorrin, and at the boy by the door, and Ferra saw him decide it: that nothing else had worked, and that there was no shame in trying a thing because a glassblower had said so, and a good deal of shame in not trying it and the glassblower being right.

He sent the boy to the glasshouse for a sack.

They charged the pot again after the noon bell with a double handful of the black grit thrown in on the iron, and blew it, and poured, and when the ingot had set enough to handle, Bram and Jorrin and the boy put it through the rolls, red, the whole length of it, and Ferra stood by the race with her book open and watched it come out of the far side of the rolls long and flat and whole.

It did not split. They put it through again, and it did not split. Bram picked up the end of it in his tongs when it had gone from red to black and turned it over and looked along it, the whole length, the way he looked along a blade, and set it down, and turned to Colm, who had been sitting on an upturned bucket by the door the whole afternoon eating an apple.

"Well," said Bram.

"Your crucible's cracked as well," said Colm. "The spare. I looked."

Ferra wrote it in the forge's book, on the converter's page, under the day's date. Three bars spoiled on the rolls, split hot. The pot charged again with black grit from the Grayrock spoil, a double handful, and the bar rolled whole. And then a line she had never before in all the years of all her books had any cause to write, because in all those years every answer to every problem in the works of Kaldo had come from one place, one man, a notebook nobody else could read:

Found by C.

She blotted it. She looked at it. She thought that Emeric would read it in the weekly bag in five days' time and she found that she wanted, very much and for no reason she could have given, to see his face when he did.

The bag from Wayford came in at the end of that week with the rider, sealed, and she opened it in the afternoon at her own table in the room at the end of the passage with the window that faced south, and read it through.

It was a thin week. Hollin on rags. Dray on horses. Placit to Milford, once, which Wenna had not sent south and had entered only as a number. Nothing from Vennor. And at the bottom of the folder, in Wenna's red at its head, hand known: Strade, a second page from the knot on the ram, under STAPLE. 2., which was the same tally as the first, carried forward, a fortnight on.

The same five short lines in the small upright hand, each letter stood on its own like a picket, as though it did not trust the next one. The same heading. The same ruled line at the foot. The figures had moved, a little, the way the figures in any running book move from one fortnight to the next.

Intake, one more. Held against rent, the same. Yield, a little down. Kept, the same.

Losses.

The figure against losses had been one, the fortnight before. It was two now. It was a small figure against the others. It was not the kind of figure that anybody who kept books would have looked at twice, and Ferra looked at it twice anyway, because that was what she did, and then a third time, trying to see what it was that the Staple had lost two of in a fortnight, and what it was that it kept count of so carefully in the upper room of its hall that it would not write the name of it down.

She did not know. She put the page in the folder with the first one, and put the folder in the box, and turned the key.
