Chapter 90 - Tallow

The harvest came in the third week of August, and it was the biggest anybody on the west side could remember, and the west side said so in the way the west side said everything, which was by complaining about it.

There was too much of it. The barony farm's wheat stood so thick in the third year of the rotation that the reapers had to stop every few yards to straighten their backs, and the sheaves went up in the stooks so close together that a man could not walk between them without knocking one down, and the carts went in and out of the farm gate from first light to last, and still there was more. Farris walked the stubble with his hands behind his back looking like a man who has been given a present he does not know where to put. Hollis came over from his own strip, which had done nearly as well, and stood at the edge of the barony's field and said that it was not natural for a field to do this, and that no good would come of it, and then asked Farris what he had put on the clover ground in the spring and wrote it down on the back of his hand.

Then it all had to be threshed, and the chaff got out of it, and that was where Linnet Shaw came in.

Winnowing, in Kaldo, was done on the threshing floor on a windy day. A man tossed the threshed grain up out of a wide flat basket, and the wind took the chaff off it as it fell, and the grain came down clean, or cleaner, and he did it again, all day, until his arms gave out or the wind dropped. On a still day it was not done at all. The third week of August was still. It had been still for a week, and the threshed grain lay on the barony's floor in grey heaps with the chaff in it, and Farris looked at the sky every hour as though the sky owed him money.

Linnet came out on the Tuesday because Armand had sent her, with her shawl over her head against the sun and her sleeves rolled up, and looked at the heaps, and at the sky, and at Farris.

"Toss it," she said.

Farris looked at her, and then at Emeric, who had come out with her because he wanted to see it, and then, because he could not think what else to do, he tossed a basket.

The grain went up. And a wind came across the threshing floor from nowhere at all, out of the dead still air, low and steady, as steady as water out of a pipe, and took the chaff off the grain as it fell and carried it away across the floor and over the hedge in a long pale stream like smoke, and the grain came down into the basket clean.

Farris stood with the basket in his hands and looked at it.

"Again," said Linnet.

They did the barony's whole floor by sunset. Two men tossing and a third bringing the heaps up, and Linnet standing at the windward end of the floor with one hand held out flat in front of her and a look of mild boredom, as if she were holding a door for someone who was taking too long, and the chaff going over the hedge all afternoon in its long pale river until there was a drift of it in the next field as high as a man's knee. The whole of the west side came to watch. They stood along the hedge in a row with their hats in their hands, and nobody said anything for most of an hour, which for the west side was a kind of shouting.

"It isn't natural," Hollis said at last, to nobody in particular.

Linnet did not look round.

"Neither's wheat," she said. "Somebody had to make that too."

Hollis thought about that for the rest of the afternoon. At the end of it he asked whether she would do his floor, and what she charged, and she told him, and he said it was robbery, and she said yes, and he paid it.



The three men from the forest came on the Thursday, while the barony's grain was still going into the granary by the cartload.

The billhook man had walked out along the north road to meet them, because Hale had had a letter back, by way of a priest in their village who had read Hale's letter out to them and written down their answer, and the answer had said a day. He brought them in at the South Gate with the air of a man showing visitors round his own house. The bowman first, with his arm moving properly now. The swordsman, who had left the sword at home and looked lighter for it. And the big one, the one Emeric had put in the ditch, who came through the gate last and looked round the gate yard and at the walls and the lamps and the people, and then saw Emeric by the well and stopped dead.

There was an awkward moment in the gate yard while everybody remembered the ditch.

"I was hungry," the big man said, as if that settled something.

"I know," said Emeric. "So does Ward."

Hale wrote them down. Farris took the big one before Hale had finished writing, because Farris had seen him pick up a sack of the barony's wheat with one hand to get it out of his way, and Farris had four days of grain still to get under a roof and a granary man who was sixty. By the end of the afternoon the big man was carrying two sacks at a time up the granary steps, one on each shoulder, and the granary man was following him up and down the steps telling him he would do himself an injury, and the big man was telling him he wouldn't, and was right.



Paxton came himself, in the last week of August, with four men and no banner.

He came for his casks. Twenty of them, promised the spring before last at this year's price and half paid, and he had written in the spring to ask after them in a way that meant he expected them on the day, and this was the day. He came also, it turned out, to sell something. Milford was cattle country, and cattle meant tallow, and the soap works had eaten every pound of tallow Kaldo's own butchers and boats could render since the spring, and Abloh had been rationing it by the bucket and saying things about it in the soap yard that he did not say anywhere else. Paxton had heard. Paxton heard most things about tallow.

And Dunnel came with him, because Dunnel was coming anyway on the western round, and because Dunnel went wherever there were people.

They did the business at Ferra's trestle in the back room of the exchange, with Vantic at the other end of it, because anything the barony sold against the security was the House's business too. Paxton was a broad man in a plain brown coat, the man Emeric had seen from the wall in May of last year, sitting his horse at the edge of the lamplight with a steel cap on and watching the smoke. He did not wear the cap now. He had a weathered square face that did not look as though it had been asked to smile very often, and had mostly declined.

He asked Ferra four questions.

What a hundredweight of rendered tallow was worth to Kaldo delivered at the South Gate, and not at Milford's gate, because the road between was his cost and not Kaldo's. Whether the House's factor saw the contract, and would have to put her name to it, because if she did he wanted to know before he signed and not after. Which months Kaldo's river came up over the low road on the Milford side, in Kaldo's experience and not in his drovers' opinion. And who would sign for Kaldo.

He did not ask Emeric anything. He did not look at Emeric while he asked them, or while Ferra answered them, or while he listened to the answers with his hands flat on his knees. Ferra answered all four without looking anything up, and he nodded once at the end of each answer, the way a man nods when a weight on a scale comes out where he expected.

Then he took the contract Ferra had drawn and read it, and changed two lines in it with Ferra's pen, and pushed it back across the trestle. The two lines moved the tallow carts off the road in the two months the river came up, and on in the two months after. Ferra read the changes. They were in Kaldo's favour. They cost Milford a month of storage. She looked at him.

"My drovers," Paxton said. "Not my tallow. I've lost two men on that road in a wet March. I'm not losing a third for a barony's soap."

Vantic read it after Ferra, and initialed it.

"One thing more," Paxton said. "My beasts. When the carts come, the drovers will want a night's fodder for the oxen in your yard. It's a few Gold a year. I'd have it in the contract."

"No," said Vantic.

Everyone looked at her. She did not look up from the page.

"The barony's fodder is pledged stock," she said. "It is the House's security. It can be sold at the posted price. It cannot be given."

Paxton looked at her for some time. Then he nodded, the same nod he had given Ferra's answers, and said he would send his own fodder on the carts, and Ward, standing by the door as Ward always stood by a door, looked at Vantic for a good deal longer than the moment was worth.

Dunnel had sat through the whole of it on the bench by the door, and had been silent for very nearly an hour, which Emeric suspected was the longest he had ever been silent in his life. When the contract was signed he got up as though he had been let out of school.

"My sister," he said to the room. "I told you she wouldn't believe it. The barony's post. Well. She believes it now. She's told her whole street. She's told the priest." He beamed at Emeric. "She wants to know what you eat."

"Bread," said Emeric.

Dunnel looked at him with enormous delight, as if he had been given something to take home, and Emeric understood that by the end of the week the whole of his sister's street, and the priest, would know that the prince of Kaldo ate bread.



Paxton found him in the gate yard afterward, while the casks were being loaded, and stood beside him by the well with his hands behind his back and watched the loading, and did not say anything for long enough that Emeric began to wonder whether he meant to.

"I warned you," Paxton said.

"You did."

"I marched because Renner asked, and I've no wall. A man with no wall does what the man with the army asks, or he hasn't got a man with no wall any more, he's got a field with a house on fire in it." He watched a cask go up onto the cart. "I stood back because you'd answered. Stay at the back and nobody comes looking. I stayed at the back. Nobody came looking." He paused. "I've been called a coward in three halls since. Wayford's. My own. One I'd never been in before. I don't mind it much. It was cheap, for what it bought."

Emeric did not say anything. It did not seem to be the kind of speech that wanted answering.

"I'm not fond of you," Paxton said. "I want that understood. I don't know you, and what I know I'm not sure about, and you killed a man I'd sat at table with for twenty years, and I'm not going to write you letters." He looked at Emeric for the first time. "But I'm in. For what that's worth. Which is a barony with no wall and a lot of cows."

"It's worth a good deal."

"It's worth what it's worth." He turned back to the carts. "And there's a thing you should know, because it's useful. Sethe thinks Milford is still his."

Emeric waited.

"He wrote to me in the spring. The way he's written every spring for fifteen years, about the weather and the wool and what a shame about Renner. I answered him the way I've answered him for fifteen years. The weather, the wool, what a shame." Paxton's square face did not change at all. "Men like Sethe don't think other men can read. He thinks I'm his because I've never once given him a reason to think I'm not. It's the only thing I own that he hasn't priced." He looked at Emeric again, straight, the way he had looked at Vantic. "I'd thank you not to spend it for me."

"I won't," said Emeric.

Paxton nodded, the scale nod, and went to see to his casks.

Saban came across the yard a little later, from wherever Saban had been, which was usually somewhere he could hear things, and stood where Paxton had stood.

"The halls are talking," he said.

"About Paxton?"

"About Grayrock." Saban took his hat off and turned it in his hands, which meant he was pricing something. "Wayford's hall. Milford's. That third one past Wayford, whose name you won't remember. They've heard the widow signed with you. Inside a year of you burning her husband in an ore yard." He put the hat back on. "They don't know what's in the contract. They don't care. A hall doesn't read contracts. A hall reads who is standing next to whom." He looked at the carts. "And this summer, everyone's watching who stands next to you."



The harvest-home was on the Saturday, in the market square, because there was nowhere else in Kaldo big enough to hold everybody who had carried a sheaf or a sack, and Ferra had said that if the barony was going to feed them all it was going to feed them in one place where she could count the loaves.

It was trestles from end to end of the square, borrowed from every house that had one and some that had only doors, and benches, and lanterns on poles, and the wall lamps above all of it as the light went, because the river had not dropped yet and all eight were lit. Maren's kitchen had been cooking for two days. There was bread from every oven in the lower ward, and the barony's own mutton, and the beans and the onions and whatever else Maren had decided people deserved, and the spirit, a cup each and a second cup if Ferra did not see.

It was loud from the first cup.

Osric told the story of the forest, to a table that had heard it from Ward and wanted it from somebody who would tell it properly. In Osric's version there were nine bandits, and the third was a giant, and the giant was sitting four places down the same bench eating mutton with both hands, and when Osric reached the part about the ditch the giant put his mutton down and said, quite reasonably, that he had been hungry, and that there had been four of them, and that he had not been a giant until about a year ago, when he started eating again. The table laughed until Osric threw a crust at him and missed.

Ambrose had found Hollis, or Hollis had found Ambrose, and they had been arguing since the first cup about whether lightning could curdle milk. Ambrose said it could and was going to find out. Hollis said it couldn't and would not have it found out on his cows. Tess sat between them in her chair with her chin on her hand and told them both, at intervals, that they had not done the experiment yet and did not know, and they ignored her and went on, and she wrote down what each of them said with a look of great patience.

Bram ate. He ate steadily and in silence through most of the evening, the way he did everything that mattered, and then at the end of it he stood up on his bench, which nobody had ever seen him do, and said in a loud flat voice that the mill had a roof and the river had a mill and the town had its water, and Harl had been right about the dust, and sat down again. Nobody knew what to do. Then the man who kept Harl's stones now stood up at the far table and raised his cup and said Harl, and the whole square said it back, and drank, and Bram did not look up from his plate.

Dorren sang. It turned out he could. Linnet said he had always been able to, and that it was why Armand had kept him on three winters when he had no talent for anything else, and Dorren said that was a lie and sang another one.

Sela had brought the children, the whole school, and fed them at the end of the square at a long table of their own, and stood over them with her arms folded, and toward the end of the evening Arthur was found under the table with three other boys practising Rylan's leg hold on each other, and was taken out by the collar.

Imani came late, from the ward, and sat at the end of a bench with her sleeves still rolled up and ate a bowl of Maren's beans as if she had not eaten since morning, which she had not, and Ambrose came and showed her his hands, which were healing, and she told him so, and he went away delighted. Ferra went up and down the trestles all evening with her book, counting the loaves against what the ovens had sent, and found she was two loaves short, and went and found the two loaves in the possession of the big man from the forest, who gave them back without being asked. Vantic sat at the end of a table with Saban and talked about the price of tallow on the coast for most of an hour, and did not drink her second cup, and did not leave until the end.

Dunnel talked to everybody. He talked to the bandits and the farmers and the garrison and Sela's children and Ambrose and Linnet and a dog. He told Paxton's drovers, who had known him for twenty years, everything about Kaldo that they had already seen with their own eyes that afternoon. He told Emeric, twice, that his sister was going to want to know everything, and that he was going to have to make most of it up, because she would not believe the true parts.

The lanterns burned down. The square emptied slowly, the way squares do, from the edges inward. Paxton went to bed in the inn without saying goodnight to anybody. Somebody carried Tess's chair down the steps at the corner of the square and Tess said who had told him he could, and he put it back.

In the morning Dunnel went west with Paxton's drovers and Paxton's casks, at first light, with his boy driving the cart and the bale in the back of it and his red scarf wound twice round his neck against the dew. At the turn of the road below the South Gate he stood up on the cart, which a man should not do on a moving cart, and turned round, and waved the end of his scarf at the gate for as long as he could see it.

The gate crew waved back. They did not know why. Nobody ever did, with Dunnel. They just found that they had.
