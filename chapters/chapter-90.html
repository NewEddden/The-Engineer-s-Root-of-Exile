Chapter 90 - The Unaudited Number

Ferra came up to the long room after the lamps were lit, with her ledger under her arm, and she put it down on the table shut.

He noticed that before he noticed anything else. In ten years he had seen her come into a room with a book open, with a book half open and a finger in it, with three books stacked and a sheet on top of them, and once, crossing the yard in a downpour, with a book held shut against her chest. He had never seen her set one down and leave it closed. She sat across from him and laid her hands on the cover, and did not open it, and did not say anything for long enough that he put down the pen.

Then she took two sheets out from under the ledger and laid them side by side between them, square to the edge.

He knew both. One was the clerk's fair copy of the shaft log from Grayrock, in a small upright hand he had watched write its first line three weeks ago. The other was a page from her own relay book, in her hand, taken down from the marks on the night of the dives.

"You wrote Found. Came out," she said.

"I did."

"Ward counted to sixty-one and hauled you out from under the line with your eyes open and nothing behind them. That's in the log. It's in Hask's mark and the clerk's hand. It isn't in yours."

"No."

"I asked you for what you'd done and what you'd found." She did not raise her voice. She never did. "Not what you meant to do. You meant to come out. What you did was get hauled."

He looked at the two sheets. There was nothing to say to it that was not an excuse, and he had spent the ride down the river finding out that all the excuses were true and none of them were reasons. He had not wanted the posts to carry it, and the posts could not read. He had not wanted to frighten anybody, and she did not frighten. He had come out, and that was a fact, and it was the one fact on the page that had not cost anything.

"You're right," he said. "I left it out. I'll write it in."

"I've written it in."

She had. There was a line under his, in her hand, dated the night before.

She did not pick up the sheets. She left them where they were and looked at him across them, and he understood, a little late, that the log had been the first thing and not the thing.

"I sat up with those last night," Ferra said. "Doing the arithmetic on what I'd have entered if he hadn't hauled. I didn't like the entry. I didn't like finding out I'd never costed it." She put her hand flat on the shut ledger. "So I'm going to ask you something I've put off for some years, and I want it the way I'd want a quarter. All of it. Not the part that's easy to read."

He waited.

"There are three women at your table you've never once given a reason about," she said. "The factor. The healer. Me. I want the accounts."

For a moment the room was very quiet. Somewhere below, on the stair, Ward shifted his weight and was still again.

It was, he thought, exactly what she would ask, in exactly those words, and he would have known it was her from the far side of a wall. She did not want to be told. She wanted to be shown. She had never in ten years accepted a figure she could not trace to where it came from, and she was not going to start with this one.

"It's an odd way to go at a thing like this," he said. "Columns."

"It's the only way I go at anything."

"I know." He put both hands on the table. "That's why I'm going to do it. You'd hear it if I lied, and it would be worse than anything I could say that was true."

Something happened in the set of her shoulders then, very small. He noticed it and did not know what it was for. He supposed she had been braced for him to refuse.

"In your order," he said.

"In my order."

"The factor." He had the account ready. He found, looking for it, that he had had it ready for some time and had never once taken it out. "I don't feel nothing. I'll say that first, because you'd know if I pretended. She's the only person in four baronies who can tell me no without standing on something I gave her, and I like that more than I should. I like talking to her. I like that she answers exactly the question I asked and not the one next to it." He kept his eyes on the sheets. "And it can't be anything, and here's why. She holds my paper. She has a window into every book I own, and she has it because I gave it to her, and I can't take it back. And since the capital I've known she isn't a clerk of that house. She's one of its principals. She's going somewhere in it, and a long way."

Ferra did not react to that at all. He had told the six on the evening he came back from the capital, and hers had been the smallest reaction in the room then, and it was smaller now.

"Any version of it," he said, "ends her, or ends the arrangement, or ends Kaldo standing on its own feet. Most likely two of the three. The house would never let one of its principals marry the man whose books she keeps. They'd pull her home inside the season and put somebody in that chair who reads our books for them and nobody else, and we'd lose the only brake I've got that isn't mine."

"You have it the wrong way round."

He stopped.

Ferra got up. She went to the shelf behind her chair, the long one where the barony's registers lived, and took down a book he knew by its spine: the dispatch book, every letter that had left the keep by date and carrier and destination, which she had kept since long before anybody asked her to and which had once found a road that was eating the Crown's post. She opened it on the table beside the two sheets and turned it to face him, and put her finger on a column.

"Hers," she said. "Every week. The same day of the week, for six years. When the foundry was lit and when it was cold. The winter the road went quiet and the week the covenant came off and the weeks when nothing happened at all. Look at the dates."

He looked. The column ran down the page in her even hand, one line a week, and it did not miss.

"A factor writes when the borrower does something," Ferra said. "Pays late, pays early, builds a thing, burns a thing. That's what a factor's letter is for. She writes when the week ends." She took her finger off the page. "Somebody's reading her. Every week, on a day they chose, and Kaldo is the book they read her by. The pressure runs from them onto her. They wouldn't pull her for marrying the borrower. They'd read it, and mark it against her."

He sat with that.

It was correct. He could see that it was correct the moment she said it, the way a drawing is correct when somebody moves one line and the rest of it suddenly stands up. He had spent six years reading the woman at the end of his table as an instrument of a house, pointed at him. He had never once asked what the house was pointed at. Ferra had asked, or had not needed to, and she had not got it from anything but a column she kept for another reason, which he could read for himself on the page in front of him.

"Then it ends her," he said. "Not the arrangement. Her. That's the one that holds."

"Yes."

"Thank you."

She closed the dispatch book and put it back on the shelf, and sat down again, and put her hands on the shut ledger as before, and waited for the rest.

"The healer," he said.

This one was shorter, and he had known the answer longer.

"She wouldn't want it." He had watched Imani read his signature on a hospital date as if it were a dosage, and turn a spade into the ground at the thaw without a word, and put her chair at the table in the one place she could see his face. "It'd put her inside my house. Every door I keep shut would be a door she lived behind, and she'd have to keep them shut too, and she'd hate every one of them. She's the only one of the three of you who could walk away from all of this tomorrow and be perfectly well. She knows it. I know it." He turned his hand over on the wood. "She'd say no, and she'd be right to."

Ferra considered that for some time, with her eyes on the shut ledger, and did not correct it. Whatever she thought of it, she had nothing in a book to set against it, and she did not put things on the table that she could not source.

"And the third," she said.

"It isn't close."

He said it flatly, because it was a figure and he had been asked for figures.

"And I can show my work, because it's ten years long, and you kept most of it." He did not look away from her. "The objection you wrote against my judgment early on, about Cobb, and dated, and filed, and never once collected on, though you were right. The page you sent Wayford when a figure would have done, so their steward had to hear every line of it read aloud in his own hall. The money you told me the truth about before the town could start spending it. Jorrin's father's slate, that you read me from memory rather than hand me the sheet. The clause for Ilsa's settlement that you fought me for, and won, and were right. The stay in the ninth clause that I gave you and you've never used, not once, not even on the boat. The line under mine, tonight." He stopped. "There's more. You'd want it itemised. I can do that too."

She did not want it itemised. He could see that. She had taken a pen from her coat while he was speaking, the one he had made, and had turned it end over end in her fingers twice, and put it down beside the sheets without opening the ledger.

"There's a cost," she said.

"I know."

"You don't. Not all of it." She said it as a correction, plainly. "If I say yes to anything, every number I've ever signed gets read backward. Every rate. The stay. The census pages. The ore contract. Every ruling I've made in ten years that somebody didn't like, and there are a great many, becomes a thing the baron's woman signed. I built this chair without a licence in a barony that had no reason to let me sit in it. The only thing that holds it up is that nobody can say I sat in it for any reason but the work." She laid one finger on the shut ledger. "That's the cost. It's mine. I'm not asking you to solve it. I'm telling you it's on the books."

"It's on the books," he said.

He had known that one was there. He had not known she had costed it to the coin, and he found he was not surprised.

"And there's mine," he said. "At court. A third son and a smith's daughter who kept a barony's books without a licence. Corbin would have that in every gallery in the capital inside a month, told warmly, in front of people, the way he tells everything. It would be the best gift I've given him since I left." He did not soften it. "It'd cost me there. It would cost me more there than anything I've done in ten years."

"I'd worked that out," said Ferra.

"I thought you might have."

"I wanted to hear whether you had."

There was nothing after that for a while. The lamp hissed. Down on the stair Ward had not moved in some time, and Emeric knew with perfect certainty that Ward had heard none of it, because Ward would have made sure of that before he sat down.

He looked at the woman across the table, with her hands on a closed book and the pen he had made lying beside two sheets of paper that did not agree, and he thought that he understood what had happened in the last hour. She had wanted to know where she stood. She had asked the way she asked everything, with columns, because a question like that was too large to be asked any other way by a person like her. And he had answered it, completely, with his work shown, and the answer was that it was not close, and now she knew.

It seemed to him a very good hour's work. It had been hard, and he had not lied once, and at the end of it she had what she had asked for.

Ferra opened the ledger.

It was the first time she had touched it since she sat down, and she did not open it to any page he expected. She turned to the back, past the conditions and the objections and the entries that lived there for years, to the relay book's pages bound in at the end, and to the night of the dives. His line was there, in her copy: Gallery seventy-one paces. One fork, right, at forty. Found. Came out. And under it the line she had written the night before, which he had seen: Hauled at sixty-one. Came round above the line. Said: I did it wrong. Entered from the shaft log, clerk's copy.

She took the pen, and under both of those she wrote one more line. He could not read it upside down. It was short.

Then she turned the book round and put it in front of him, and sat back, and waited.

He knew that. He had seen her do it perhaps a dozen times in ten years, and never for anything small: the night the first quarter came in above the line, the evening the census came to a number that was true. She did not say well done. She did not say anything. She put the sheet in front of him with the good figure on it and sat back and let him find it himself, and that was the whole of her celebration, and he had learned to look forward to it more than he had ever told her.

He read the page.

His line. Her correction to his line. And under it, in her hand, dated tonight: Account given in full. No part omitted. F.

He read it twice, looking for the figure that had come out well. It was a log entry. It recorded that he had answered a question. It said nothing about the answer at all.

He looked up. She was watching him, with her hands folded on the table and her face doing nothing it had not been given permission to do, and for a moment he thought she was going to say something. She did not.

He did not find it. He looked for some time, and he did not find it, and after a while she took the book back and closed it, and put the two sheets inside it, and stood.

"The log's right now," she said. "Both copies."

"Both copies," he agreed.

She put the pen back inside her coat and went to the door. She stopped there, with her hand on the frame, and for the length of a breath he thought she was going to turn round. Then she said goodnight in the voice she said it in every night, and went down the stair past Ward, who stood aside for her, and he heard her go along the gallery to her own room and the door close.

Nothing had been decided. Nothing had been signed. He sat on in the long room with the lamp, going back over the hour line by line the way he would have gone back over a drawing that worked, looking for the place where it had come out well. He found a great many good lines, and was satisfied with every one of them, and he did not find the one she had written down, though he had read it twice.
