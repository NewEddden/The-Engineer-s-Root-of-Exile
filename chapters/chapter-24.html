Chapter 24 - The Foundry Rises

The anvil went onto the cart at first light and took six men to do it, and the whole street came out to watch, which Emeric had not expected and did not enjoy.

It was not a ceremony. Nobody cheered. They came out because a smithy had stood on that corner since before the plague and a good part of the town could not remember a morning without hearing it, and now four men were carrying the anvil out of the door and a fifth was taking the shutters off to get the bellows through, and the people standing in the street were doing arithmetic about hinges.

"Twenty-six days," Emeric said, to nobody in particular.

"You've said that eleven times," Ferra said. "It has not become smaller."

"I want it said in front of them rather than in a room. Twenty-six days with no smith in this town. Say what that is."

Ferra had it, because he had asked her for it a fortnight ago and had then wished he had not.

"No horseshoes. Nothing shod, nothing reset, and it is the middle of the carting season, so we will lose horses to feet." She read it flat. "No cart bands. Two carts will come apart and one of them will come apart under a load. No hinges, no pot mending, no knife grinding, no chain, no nails, and nothing at all for a woman who has broken the only good pot in her house and now has twenty-six days of not having it. Anything urgent goes up the Wayford road at four days there and back and a Wayford price at the far end, which is the part I mind."

"And the alternative."

"The alternative is you build a foundry on the eastern arm in the spring instead of the summer and it is not lit before the frost, and then it is not lit before the following summer, because there is no such thing as building half a furnace over a winter." She closed the book. "I am not arguing with you. You asked me to price it and I priced it, and I would like it on the record that a priced thing is still a thing that hurts. You have got into a habit of saying the number and then behaving as though saying the number settled it."

Emeric thought about that for the rest of the morning and concluded she was right, which was becoming a pattern he had views about.

The foundry stood on the eastern arm where the river came down out of the hills and split above the town, and it stood there because the eastern arm ran fast. The western arm was slower and wider and better for barges and would not turn a wheel worth the name, and the two facts together had cost him three weeks in the spring and a survey he had done himself with a plank and a rope, because he had not believed the millers and the millers had been right.

The shell had been up since the spring. Stone to the eaves, slate on, and a parapet along the river side, which had gone on because Ward had walked the site once in April and said one sentence about what a building with the barony's only steel in it looked like from a boat.

Inside, it was three furnaces, and three furnaces was an argument Emeric had been having with the barony's own arithmetic since March.

"Eighteen hundred people," Ferra said, standing in the middle of it with her boots in brick dust. "You have built for four thousand."

"I've built for four thousand."

"With money we did not have, which is a thing I say to you so often that I have begun to hear it in my own voice at night."

"Then say the second half. What does the third furnace cost to have built and standing cold."

Ferra did not need the book for that one. "Stone, mortar, and about nine days of masons who were here anyway."

"And what does it cost to add it in three years, to a building that's running, with two furnaces lit either side of the hole in the wall."

She was quiet.

"Say it," Emeric said. "You're better at this than I am and you already know the shape."

"It costs you the building," she said. "You stop production to enlarge production."

"You stop production for a season to enlarge production, at a point where you've got contracts and men on wages and a debt, and you can't stop, so you don't, so the third furnace never gets built and in ten years a man reads a ledger and concludes Kaldo was a barony that made a certain amount of iron because that was all it could ever make." He put his hand on the cold brick of the third one. "Nine days of masons who were here anyway. It's the cheapest thing I've bought all year and it's the only thing I've bought that's for people who aren't born yet."

"I withdraw the objection and reserve it," Ferra said, "for the next thing you do that sounds like this one and isn't."

The furnace that got lit first was the one that could be worked by hand.

All three could, in the end, which was the whole design and had been the design since a night in the long room when he had explained a blast furnace to Bram and gone somewhere he had not meant to go. A furnace is a stack, and a stack wants air, and air is a bellows, and a bellows is a thing a man can pull. The water wheel pulled it better and pulled it constantly and pulled it without stopping to eat, and if the wheel froze or the axle went or the whole river turned to sand, then eight men on a treadle worked the number one furnace at something under half, badly, hot, and Kaldo still had iron.

Emeric had put four fire-stones in the flue of the middle furnace to hold the heat between charges, which was the magic part, and underneath it he had built a stack that would have been recognised by a mason in any century of the world, which was the part he actually trusted.

"You've done that everywhere," Bram said, on the day they lit it. "Every single thing. The ring, the shed, the drum. There's always a stupid one underneath."

"There's always a stupid one underneath."

"Because you don't trust the stones."

"Because I don't know the stones." Emeric watched the first flue-heat go up. "You know what a bellows is. You know why it works, from the inside, and if it stops you can tell me what's wrong with it. Nobody can do that with a stone. I can make one do a thing four hundred times and I can't tell you what it is, and that's not humility, it's a survey I haven't done. Until somebody does it I'll assume everything I don't understand can stop on a Tuesday."

The wheel had been in since the spring and had never once been under load.

They coupled it on the ninth day, in front of about forty people who had no business being there, and the blast came up, and the sound the furnace made changed from a sound into a note, and Bram put his hand flat on the stone of the stack and left it there for a long time and did not say anything at all.

It was, Emeric thought, the single most satisfying thing he had done in either life, and he allowed himself about half a day of it.

The hoist killed a heat on the eleventh day.

The wheel ran a shaft, and the shaft ran the bellows, and the shaft also ran the hoist that lifted charges of ore and coal up to the throat of the stack, because there was one wheel and one shaft and everything in the building came off it. Emeric had drawn it that way in March and had thought about it for a long time, and had understood perfectly well that the two loads shared a drive, and had reasoned that the hoist ran for a few moments at a time and the bellows ran constantly and the wheel had power for both.

The wheel had power for both. What it did not have was power for both at the same instant, under a full charge, at the moment the hoist took up the slack in the chain and the whole load's weight came onto the shaft at once.

The blast dropped. Not much and not for long, about four seconds, and four seconds of thin air in a furnace at heat is a furnace at less than heat, and they did it three times in an hour because charging is a thing you do repeatedly, and by the third time the middle furnace was cold enough in the wrong place that the tap came out grey and wrong and useless and they lost the whole heat and half a day of coal.

Emeric stood in front of the wheel for some time with his arms folded.

"A clutch," he said. "Off the hoist drum. Something that lets the hoist take the shaft when it wants it and gives it back, so the bellows never sees the load at all."

"Aye," Bram said.

"Say the rest of it."

"It's a dog and a lever and a spring, and it's on the drive shaft of the only wheel you've got, and it's a moving part in a place where a moving part failing stops the whole building." Bram wiped his hands on his apron. "I can make you one. I'll want a fortnight to make one I'd stand behind and I'll want the second wheel in first so there's something turning while I'm cutting into that shaft, and there isn't a second wheel, because the frame's out in the yard waiting on an axle that wants casting."

Emeric looked out through the doors at the frame in the yard, which had been sitting there since May, timbered and pinned and finished and useless, waiting for an axle that could only be cast in a foundry that was not lit until nine days ago and was now lit and had a fortnight of other people's work in front of it.

"So the wheel I need to fit the clutch is waiting on an axle from the furnace the clutch is for," he said.

"That's about the size of it."

"That's very funny."

"It's not, particularly."

The answer was a rule, and it took him the better part of an afternoon to accept a rule as an answer.

Charge between blasts. Not across them. The hoist lifts while the bellows is between strokes and never while a charge is being blown, and the man on the hoist and the man on the blast work off the same call, and the call belongs to one named man on each shift and nobody else says it.

He wrote it out, and had it read at both shift changes, and had it painted on the wall of the charging floor in letters a foot high, and it cost Kaldo nothing whatsoever and it worked from the first hour.

He was in a bad mood about it for two days.

"It's a good fix," Ward said, on the second evening, on the parapet, in the way he had of arriving where he was needed without appearing to have travelled.

"It's a procedure. It lives in men's heads. It fails the first time a new man comes on at night and nobody's told him, and then it fails properly, in the dark, on a full charge."

"So it's a drill."

"It's a drill."

"Then it's a thing I know about and you don't, so listen." Ward leaned on the parapet. "You've been building me things all year that work whether anybody's paying attention or not, and I've been grateful, and it's made you soft about the other kind. Half of what keeps forty men alive on a wall is a procedure that lives in men's heads. It doesn't fail because it's in heads. It fails because it was written once by a clever man and never drilled and never checked and nobody was ever made to say it back." He straightened up. "Drill it. Every shift change, out loud, both men, for a month. Then it's in the building."

Emeric had it drilled at every shift change for a month, and then for another month, and the phrase "between blasts, not across them" was still being said in that building by men who had never met him a long time afterward, which he had not designed and did not expect and would only find out about years afterward.

The collar came off bench two on a Thursday and would not go onto an axle off bench one.

It was the smallest possible thing. An axle seat, a collar, a bore that wanted to be twelve inches around the shoulder, and two benches full of men who had each been cutting them all week and getting them right.

Both benches were right. That was the part that took the longest to establish and the part that mattered.

Bench one worked off a rule that had come out of Bram's own shop, a length of iron with notches in it that Bram's master had made, and every collar off bench one was true to it. Bench two worked off a rule that had come from a Wayford wheelwright with one of the new hands, honest work, notched properly, and every collar off bench two was true to that. The two rules disagreed by a little under a sixteenth of an inch across twelve, and nobody in the building had ever had a reason to hold them up against each other, because a smith who makes both halves of a thing does not need to.

A foundry with two benches making the same part is not a smith.

They had sixty-odd collars, about half of which fitted about half of the axles, and nobody had done anything wrong, and Emeric stood on the charging floor with two rules in his hands and understood that he was holding the whole problem.

"How many things has this happened to already," he said.

Nobody answered, which was the answer.

"Right. Stop. Everything on both benches, stop, now, and don't cut anything until I come back."

What he came back with was a stick.

It was a bar of iron, a foot long, cut in the middle furnace, filed by Bram over a day and a half, with a single line scored at each end and nothing else on it. It went on the wall of the charging floor on two pegs and it was not permitted to leave the wall for any reason, and if any man needed a foot he took a blank to the wall and cut a copy against the bar and stamped the copy and took the copy to his bench, and every copy in the building came back to the wall on the first day of the month to be checked against the bar and any copy that had drifted was cut up in front of everybody.

Bram thought it was madness for most of a morning.

"I've had that rule off my master thirty years," he said. "There's nothing wrong with it."

"There's nothing wrong with it. There's nothing wrong with the Wayford one either. That's the whole trouble." Emeric put the bar on the pegs. "It doesn't matter which foot. It matters that it's one foot. If I'd found a rule off the crown's own arsenal that was truer than both of yours by a hair I'd still have taken yours, because yours is on this wall and the arsenal's is five days up a road."

"And if the bar's wrong?"

"Then every collar in Kaldo is wrong in exactly the same direction by exactly the same amount and they all still fit each other, which is the only property I want." He stood back and looked at it. "A part that fits is a part that fits the next one. It's not a part that's correct. I spent four days trying to work out which of your two rules was true and it was four days I'll not get back, because the question doesn't have an answer and doesn't need one."

Bram looked at the bar on the wall for a while.

"That's a stupid one underneath," he said eventually.

"It's the stupidest one I've built. It's a stick on a wall and it'll outlast the wheel."

They made twenty-four harnesses in five weeks, and Emeric knew the number was wrong while they were making them.

The steel was Kaldo's own, carbon steel out of the middle furnace, off a recipe that was written down in three places in the capital and that the capital could not reliably make at any quantity because reliability in steel is not a recipe, it is a furnace you can hold at a temperature and a man who can tell you what he's looking at. Kaldo had both, now, in a building on the eastern arm, and Emeric was careful never to say that out loud in front of anyone from anywhere else.

Twenty went to the line: plain, stamped, interchangeable, every plate cut to the foot on the wall, so that a shoulder off one suit went onto another and a man whose pauldron split at noon was not out of the fight.

Four went to Ward, Rylan, Osric and Declan, and those four carried the crystals out of their own capital-issue armour, which came off the racks and went into the furnace as feedstock afterward, and which Emeric watched go in.

"You're melting the king's steel," Ward said.

"I'm melting a suit of court armour that's been beaten twice by men from a barony with forty guards, and I'm putting the stone that was in it into something better, and the difference between those two sentences is the only reason anybody should care what I've done here." Emeric watched the plates go grey and then orange. "You'll say the second one if anybody asks you in the capital."

"I'll say the first one and let them work it out."

Bram fought him for four days over the breakers and lost and was right the whole time.

The Enhanced housings were sealed, which meant the crystal sat inside a shell that fed the harness and nothing else, and Emeric wanted a breaker in the coupling: a catch that watched the mana in the air around the housing and, if it dropped past a floor, threw the coupling open by itself and cut the crystal off from the suit.

"You're asking me," Bram said, "to put a thing that can come apart into the middle of a thing whose whole job is not coming apart. On a man. In a fight."

"Yes."

"Then say why, and don't say it cleverly."

"Because of Rylan's harness," Emeric said. "Because a housing that's coupled and live has a road out of it, and if the air round it goes thin the suit doesn't stop drawing, it draws harder, and it wrings the stone dry in about the time it takes to notice. And a stone that's been wrung doesn't come back when the air comes back. It's finished, and there are four of these in the world and I cannot make another." He put the drawing down. "You're telling me I'm adding a failure point. I am. I'm adding a small failure that happens sometimes instead of a total failure that happens once."

Bram built them. He built four of them, better than the drawing, with a spring he cut three times before he was satisfied.

"I still say it's wrong," he said, when he handed them over.

"I know."

"I'll say so again if one of them opens on a man at the wrong moment."

"I'd think less of you if you didn't."

Then he built the other things, and nobody asked him to, and that was the part he thought about afterward.

Sixty crossbows, because a farmer can be taught a crossbow in an afternoon and a bow in four years. A hammer for Ward with a gravity working in the head, which Ward held for a while and then put down and did not discuss. Three stones that did nothing at all except make an area where nothing else worked, which had taken him nine days and two failures and which he tested on empty ground at the far end of the eastern arm with nobody watching but Ferra, who wrote down the distances. And, last, a thing with a barrel and a compressed-air crystal that put a lead ball through an inch of oak at a hundred and forty paces and could not be made twice, because the crystal it needed was the last one in the pouch.

The count in Ferra's column went four, three, two, one, and then the column stopped being a column and became a line with a nought on it.

Imani said the thing about it, in the long room, in front of Ward, on the evening the last one went in.

"Nobody has declared anything," she said. "Nobody has ridden up that road and said a word to you. There's been a bad harvest of ore and a baron who's stopped answering letters, and you've spent a season making sixty crossbows and a rifle that shoots a man at a hundred and forty paces, and I'd like to know whether you understand what you look like."

"I understand exactly what I look like."

"Then say it, because Ward won't, because he thinks you're right."

"I look like a man arming a barony in secret," Emeric said. "And if I'm wrong about all of it, then in two years there's a stack of crossbows in a dry room in a barony that never needed them, and Ferra will have that in her column for the rest of her life, and I'll deserve it." He looked at her. "And I'll do it anyway, because the other way round is a season where I was right and I find out on a Tuesday and it takes five weeks to make a harness."

"That's not an answer to what I asked."

"No. It's the only one I've got."

Imani did not look satisfied. She did not leave either, and she was still in the long room an hour later going through her own lists about beds and linen and water, which Emeric understood to be the closest thing to an answer he was going to get.

He practised in an empty room, most nights, for about half an hour, and did the same three things.

Water pulled thin across a flame until it was a sheet a hand wide and no thicker than a nail. A blade of moving air, held flat, held still. A slab of stone the size of a door raised a hand's breadth off the floor and dropped.

None of it would have impressed anyone. He was entirely clear about that and had been since he was nine. Corbin could put a wall down. Corbin had done it once, at a review, casually, in front of two hundred people, and had then apologised to the mason for the inconvenience in a voice that carried. Emeric had been born with a mage's blood and about a tenth of a mage's output, which was public knowledge, had been public knowledge his whole life, and had been one of the smaller reasons the court had decided what he was.

What he had, instead, was the shape of a spell while it was still forming, and a way of getting his hand onto that shape early, which was the one thing a thousand hours in a delve had actually given him.

So the water was thin because thin was what the flame needed and no more. The air blade was a hand long because a hand was what would reach. The slab went up a hand's breadth because a hand's breadth was enough for a thing that weighed what it weighed, and going higher was throwing away a spell he might want twice.

Bram came in on him once and watched the slab go up and come down.

"That's not much," Bram said.

"No."

"You've been doing it an hour."

"Two hundred times. Same height. Same hand." Emeric let it down again. "There's nothing in this room a court mage would look at twice. What I'm after is that when I want it, it costs exactly what it costs and not a scrap more, because I haven't got a scrap more."

Bram thought about that, in his way, which took a while and looked like nothing.

"I've watched you not waste iron for a year," he said. "It's the same thing."

"It's exactly the same thing."

"Aye. Well." Bram went to the door. "That's what I'd be frightened of, if I were the sort of man who had to be frightened of you. Not the size of it."

Ferra gave him the number on a Sunday, in the long room, with the door shut.

"Nought," she said. "I want to be precise, because you will otherwise round it. There is enough in the box to pay the guard at the end of this month and to buy coal for eleven days. There is nothing behind that. Not a reserve, not a bad-week fund, not a shilling I have hidden from you, which I want stated because I have hidden shillings from you twice this year and both times you found them and both times you were pleasant about it."

"And the venture."

"The venture pays out of the foundry's takings and the foundry has taken nothing, because everything the foundry has made this season is either in an armoury or standing in a yard. Nineteen households have grain in a page and no coin, and they will not have coin until the foundry sells a thing to somebody who is not us." She closed the book. "You have three furnaces, twenty-four harnesses, sixty crossbows, a rifle, a wheel, a stick on a wall, and two thousand acres of standing grain that is worth every penny of everything you have spent and cannot be spent until it is cut."

"How long."

"Three weeks to the cut. Four to have it in."

Saban came up the eastern arm two days later, at evening, on a cart, alone, which was not how Saban travelled.

He walked the whole floor before he said anything, all three furnaces, the charging floor, the bar on the wall, and he stopped in front of the bar for longer than he stopped in front of anything else, which Emeric noticed and did not comment on.

"The seam's thin," Saban said.

"You've said that twice this year."

"I've said it twice and I've delivered twice, and I'm saying it a third time and I'll not be delivering." He sat down on a stack of pig iron without being invited. "It's not thin. Grayrock's grading everything low and pricing everything high and my last two loads came out of a stockpile at a waypoint rather than off the hill, and I'd not have known that except I've a man who counts carts. There's ore coming down that river. It isn't coming to me."

"Where's it going."

"That's the question I'd want answered, and I've written to Renner's steward three times, and I've had a very polite nothing back three times, and Renner's steward is a man who answers letters." Saban looked round the floor. "I'll tell you what I'd say if I were being paid to advise you rather than sell to you. You've built a thing that eats ore and you've got one road to ore and it comes down a river out of one barony, and I told you that in the spring and you told me you knew, and knowing it isn't the same as having done something about it."

"I know."

"Aye. You keep saying that as well."

Ward came in behind him about a quarter of an hour later, still in his riding coat, and did not sit down, and Saban stopped talking the moment he saw his face.

"There's talk on the Wayford road," Ward said. "Just talk. I want that said first because you'll build a wall out of anything." He put his gloves on the table. "Grayrock's calling in men off the hill for a muster and nobody's saying what for. Wayford's inn is full of Wayford's own household men, which it isn't in a normal August. And there's a Milford carter told one of Rylan's cousins there's ground being cleared on the plain for horses."

"Milford," Emeric said. "Paxton has a contract with me."

"Paxton has a contract with you and a border with Grayrock and about a fifth of what Grayrock has." Ward looked at him levelly. "It's talk. Rylan's gone back up to make it not talk, and he'll be four days."

"And if it isn't talk."

"Then it isn't weeks." Ward picked the gloves back up. "It's days."
