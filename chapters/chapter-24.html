Chapter 24 - The Exchange

The carpenter had spent three years mending fish boxes for the few boats still working out of the dock quarter. Before the war he had put roofs on half the houses in the forge district, and nobody had asked him about a roof since. Ferra's count turned him up on its ninth day. Rylan took him a note the morning after the walk to the exchange, and by the time Emeric went over to look, the day after the muster, the man had the broken bay stripped back to the old trusses and an opinion about the timber.

The timber was oak out of the baron's woods, which were Emeric's woods now. Cutting in them was still fined. He waived the fine for one roof, and Hale entered it in the barony's book as a waiver and not a precedent, in exactly those words, and turned the book round so that Emeric could see it said so.

"Green," the carpenter said, when the first load came down on a barony cart. He ran his thumb along a split end. "It'll move on you. First winter it'll creak like a boat."

"Will it hold?"

"It'll hold. You'll hear it holding."

Bram came down the next day to look at the old trusses, because he did not trust anybody else's eye on a joint. He stood in the middle of the long floor, where a trading crowd had once stood, and looked up until the carpenter stopped work to see what he was looking at. Then he walked the length of the room with his head back and came down it again.

"Nobody in Kaldo could cut that now."

"Why not?"

"Whoever could didn't teach anybody." Bram was still looking up at the tie beam, at the adze marks running the length of it, every stroke the same as the last. "Or he did, and they're in the rows."

He went back up the hill without another word. He had promised the church step a morning a fortnight, in front of the whole town, and he plainly did not mean to be reminded of it.

Ferra priced the bay that evening: lime from Emeric's own kiln, the carpenter's days, nails out of Bram's forge. It came to a little under a month of the shop's takings. She said so, and then she told him what else that month had been going to pay for, one thing at a time, so that he could hear each of them being put off.



Imani came while the carpenter had the new rafters up and was swearing at them.

She stood under them with her bag on her shoulder and looked up, as Bram had. Emeric had the strong impression that she was looking for something to fault, and was annoyed not to have found it yet.

"Who's running it?"

He had been asking himself that for a week, and had got no further than knowing it could not be him. A prince at the front of that room, and half the lower ward would keep its children home to make a point.

"Sela," Imani said. "I asked her last night."

He looked at her.

"She's got terms." Imani took a folded sheet out of her bag. It was in her own hand, and she read it to him in Sela's order, flat, leaving nothing out. "The room's hers. The places, the food, the order of the morning. The hours are the ones the square set, and she'll chalk them on the wall so nobody can say they weren't told. One of the north-well women has the counter mornings and Sela takes it back at noon. You teach on the mornings she names. You come in when they're sitting and you go when she tells you. In that room nobody speaks to you about anything but the lesson, and that includes her. Letters on the benches by the first morning, something they can copy from, a sheet between two."

"Is that all?"

"No. She wants it written down that she's doing it for Arthur." Imani looked at him then. "Not for you. I'm to say it in those words."

The carpenter had stopped swearing. Somewhere overhead a rafter ticked as it settled.

"Can she read?"

"She's one of your ninety-three." Imani said it as though he ought to have known, which he probably ought to have. "The priest before Doran used to take a few of the parish children and teach them to read the book. The burials and the baptisms. She was one of them."

"And the boys?"

"At her kitchen table. All three." There was a pause in it just long enough to count them. "Arthur reads a little. Not as well as he thinks he does."

"Figures?"

"She's kept a house on nothing since she married. And she's had the shop's counter since July. Nobody's got a copper past her yet."

He wrote it there and then, on the back of Ferra's price for the roof, in the words Sela had chosen: that Sela ran the room at the exchange for her son Arthur, and not for him. He signed it and gave it to Imani to carry back, and she read it over before she put it in her bag, the way she would have checked a dose.



Letters on the benches, a sheet between two. Sixty children, if sixty came, and Ferra thought they might.

He tried it that night with a pen, Kaldo ink and Kaldo paper, and stopped at the third letter. The paper was grey and soft and drank sideways. Every stroke went down fine and then furred at its edges while he watched, and by the time he lifted the pen the first letter had turned into a small brown bruise. In July, at Bram's table, Ferra had told him it was the paper. It was still the paper.

The real fix was to size it so it stopped drinking, and that meant a vat of glue and a drying loft and a month he did not have before the first morning.

So he opened Armand's notebook and turned the pages until he found the paragraph he half remembered.

It sat between two tables of crystal densities, and most of it was contempt. Children with the faculty, Armand had written, would move crumbs and spilled milk about long before they could make a glyph, by wrapping the stuff in raw mana and shoving it. It was a fault of the nursery. It wasted more than any spell they would ever cast. A nurse with the gift felt it at her back like a stove. The tower trained it out of them in their first year, and Armand had underlined the word waste twice.

He read it again. Then he got up and closed the shutters, out of habit, and put a single drop of ink in the lid of the pot.

He did not make a ring. There was nothing to make one of. He let the mana out of himself the way he would have let it into a glyph, and instead of drawing it into a shape he put it round the drop, as a hand closes round water, and pushed the mana and not the ink.

It went out of him like heat out of an open door.

The drop went with it. It slid across the lid on nothing at all, held inside a skin of his own attention, and when he brought it down onto the paper and drew it along, it laid a line.

The paper wanted it. He could feel every fiber at the edge of the stroke pulling, the same pull that had turned the pen line to fur, and he held the ink where he had put it and did not let the paper have any of it until it had dried where it lay. A stroke took a slow breath to dry. Most letters wanted three strokes.

He looked up once, a hand above the paper, where a ring would have been if there had been a ring. There was nothing there. He had shut the shutters for nothing, and left them shut.

Halfway down the page he tried a spoonful, to see. It came out of the wrap the moment he moved it and ran across the desk and into his cuff.

Drops, then. Nothing bigger.

The page took him most of an hour. When it was done it was the cleanest thing that had ever been written on Kaldo paper, every letter standing on the grey as sharp as a cut, and he could not have made a second if the barony had depended on it. The pool he drew on was lower than a whole night in the room would have left it. A headache had arrived behind his eyes and settled in to stay.

He did the sum with it sitting there. An hour a page. Thirty pages for the benches, more when the roll grew, and nobody in Kaldo who could make one but him, and only for as long as he sat at the desk making it. A pen cost a feather. He had seen this shape before, from a carriage window, over the inner rings of the capital: a man in every hull, pouring himself into a stone so that it would stay up. It was a job.

And there was the line about the nurse. Anybody in the keep with the faculty would have felt that hour through the wall like a stove. As far as he knew, nobody in the keep had it. He did not much like the words as far as he knew.

The trick went in with the coin and the candle, in the room, where there was no paper to spoil and nobody to feel anything. The page went down the hill in the morning.



Bram's apprentice was at the bellows when he came in.

He had seen her face on his third day in Kaldo, coming in with a sack of charcoal and following his eyes to the tuyere, and he had seen it most weeks since at the back of the forge, by the wall, where she could see everything. He had never once asked her name.

He asked now.

"Tess."

She was looking at the page, not at him, and she went on looking at it after he had stopped talking. Then she went to the back of the forge and came out with a plank of pear wood that Bram, it turned out, had been keeping for a handle he had never got round to making.

"She's at your exchange three mornings in seven," Bram said, "and now she's cutting its letters on the evenings between. When does she work?"

Nobody answered him, and he did not seem to expect anybody to.

Tess cut the first block in an evening and pressed it by hand onto a scrap. It came out backwards, every letter facing the wrong way, as clean as a reflection in a basin. She did not say anything. She took the block home with her, and two days later she came back with all of them, cut the other way round.

The inn had a cheese press in its yard that nobody had used since the plague. They inked the blocks, laid the sheets and turned the screw, and by noon there were forty sheets drying on a line across the inn yard. The ink still crept into the paper, but a letter as thick as a finger could lose a hair at its edges and stay a letter.



The exchange opened in the last week of September, late in the morning, because the square had said late.

Emeric did not go in. He stood at the mouth of the lane across from the door, the way he had stood at the back of the doorways all summer with a bucket in each hand while Imani did the talking, and watched them come.

Sela was on the step. The bread was beside her in two baskets, and every child took a piece going in. She said each name as the child came up to her. The lower ward's she knew already. The rest she asked once, at the door, and she did not need to be told twice. She did not miss one.

A boy came up the step from the bridge end, coughing. Sela put a piece of bread in his hand, turned him round by the shoulder and pointed him up the street toward Imani's house, and he went.

The letters were Sela's, every morning, off the sheets on the benches, while the trades took their turns around her.

Abloh had the first morning, because his mornings had been running the longest, in the soap yard, since June. He came up from the yard with a crock of lye under one arm and an egg in his coat pocket, and the first thing Kaldo's school taught anybody was how not to burn their hands.

Ferra counted sixty-two from Sela's list that evening. The floor would take twice as many. It was half full, and it was the best number Emeric had heard since April.



The rotation started that same week, and went as the muster had: grudging, and on time.

Ferra took figures on her first morning and gave Emeric the room's numbers that night at her table, in the same voice she used for the price of lime. Fourteen of them could count past a hundred. Nine could not get past their fingers, and three of those had told her they could. She had the three down separately.

Bram took his first morning at the forge, because he would not carry a fire to the exchange and could not see why anyone would ask him to. He spent the first half of it moving children back from the hearth and the second half moving the same children back again. At the end of it he told Emeric that it was a morning he would not see again, in the tone a man uses to report a tax.

Sela named Emeric's first morning for the Friday.

He came in when the children were sitting, as agreed. She was standing against the wall by the counter, and she did not look at him.

"Measure," she said.

So he taught them measure, or tried to, with a bench and a length of cord and their own hands, and somewhere in the middle of it Tess put her hand up. She was sitting at the back, by the wall, among children half her age, as though the back by the wall was simply where she sat.

"Why isn't a thumb in here the same as a thumb at the forge?"

"Show me."

She went to the small table at the front, where Sela kept the chalk, and walked her thumb along its edge, the scarred one, joint over joint, counting under her breath. Thirty-one. Then she looked at Sela.

Sela came off the wall. She stood very straight beside the table, because it was the lesson, and laid her thumb along the edge as Tess had, and Tess counted it for her. Twenty-eight.

"At the forge, Bram's is the thumb," Tess said. "Whatever his comes to."

The room waited. Sixty-odd children who had been told a prince knew things were watching a prince not know one.

"I don't know yet," Emeric said. "Write it down."

She wrote the two numbers in the margin of the alphabet sheet she shared, in a careful forge hand, and looked at Sela. Sela said nothing. Tess tore the margin off, folded it small and put it inside her shirt.

"That'll do," Sela said, a little before the hour, and he went.



Ward's yard was the strip of beaten ground between the exchange and the narrow gate, where carts had once stood waiting to unload. He would not teach anywhere he could not see Emeric, so on his first morning Emeric came too, and Ward found a use for him.

About twenty children came out to the yard. Ward unrolled the practice blades on the ground in front of them, in the same oiled cloth, handed Emeric the narrow one without a word, as he had on the road, and put him on the ground.

He did it slowly the first time, so they could see the feet. He did it fast the second time, so they could see why the feet mattered. The third time Emeric got his blade up in time and went down anyway, and twenty children who had been raised to look once at a prince's eyes and then at their own boots laughed out loud. Then they looked at each other, frightened at having done it.

Emeric laughed as well, flat on his back in the dust, and that was the end of anybody being frightened.

The rest of the morning was falling. How to go down without putting a hand out to stop it. How to roll the shoulder under. How to be up again before the man who put you down had his feet back. Arthur went down like a sack of meal the first time and like water by the fifth, and after that he was up before anybody else in the line, every time, looking round for the next one.

Near the end a boy asked Ward whether he could be a knight.

"Yes," Ward said.

A girl two along from him waited until the boy had finished being pleased, and asked the same thing, with her chin already up for the laugh.

"Yes," Ward said, exactly as before.

The boys laughed anyway. Ward waited until they had finished.

"One of the strongest knights I have ever seen is a woman," he said. "Queen Rowena of Emberfell. I have been on the ground in front of her." He looked along the line. "Who wants to laugh at her?"

Nobody did.

Emeric was still holding the narrow blade. He looked down at the grip, wrapped for a hand smaller than Ward's and worn dark where somebody's fingers had closed on it for years before his own had. In the capital nobody had ever said that about a queen above a murmur. Ward had just said it in a dusty yard in Kaldo, to twenty children, at the volume he used for feet.

He had also said yes twice, the same way, and left out the same thing both times. A windowless stone room with a drain in the floor. A tower mage forcing the mana in by waves, and nine in every ten carried out afterward. Ward had been through that room. In fifteen years Emeric had never once heard him mention it, and he did not mention it now.

Sela had stood in the exchange doorway the whole morning with her back to the yard. Arthur had been sent for letters. When the children went in he went past her with dust on both shoulders, and she brushed it off him without looking at it, and said nothing, and he went to his bench.

Ward rolled the blades back into their cloth. Emeric held out the narrow one and did not let go of it at once.

"This was hers."

"Both," Ward said. "Both were hers."

On the road, the first morning, his hands had known that grip before he did. Ward had stood looking at them and said nothing.

He let go of the blade. He did not ask the next question. Ward would have answered it in one word, and he did not want it from Ward.

After that Ward went to the yard alone on his mornings, and put Rylan at Emeric's shoulder with a list of the streets he could use.



At the end of that first week Imani came past the exchange as the children were coming out with the last of the bread.

Emeric was across the lane. He had got into the habit. She stopped beside him and looked at the building: the new end of the roof, paler than the rest, the high windows, the hours chalked on the wall inside the door where Sela had put them.

"When's your morning?" he said.

"When I've somewhere to take it."

"You said fortnightly."

"I said I'd choose." She watched a child go past with a heel of bread in each hand. Then she turned her head and looked at him. "You gave the soap a room before you gave the sick one."

He had an answer. Every version of it he could find was a promise.

He did not give her one.

She waited long enough to be sure of that. Then she nodded, as though it were the answer she had come for, hitched her bag up her shoulder, and went on up the lane.
