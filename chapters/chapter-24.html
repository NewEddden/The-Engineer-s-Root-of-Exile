Chapter 24 - The Trove

The Works froze out in the second week of the month and stayed frozen for six.

Quarry shut. Kiln shut. The woodcut kept going because the woodcut always keeps going, and the class ran three nights, and everything else in the barony stopped and waited for a thaw that was not coming until it came.

Which left Emeric with more empty hours than he had had since the road, and a man who has spent eight months holding a schedule in his head does not discover leisure when the schedule stops. He discovers that he does not know what to do with his hands.

He went looking for something to put in them and found the trunk.

It had been under the window in the second room since the day he arrived, where he had dumped it while a castle full of strangers watched him unload a carriage, and he had opened it exactly once, in April, for four minutes.

He sorted it properly on a Tuesday, which turned out to be the single most useful thing he did all winter, because until that afternoon he had been calling all of it by the same word.

Flat stones. The bulk of it. Plain, uncut, no affinity at all, greyish and dull and about the size of a thumb joint. Teaching substrate, the sort a tutor puts in front of a seven year old, and pure capacity with nothing directable in it past a simple release of charge. They carried no crest, no cut mark, and no record of ever having been in anyone's hands. His father had chosen them because they could be sold in any town in the kingdom by a man who did not want to explain himself, and that had been the whole of the thinking, and it had been good thinking.

Water-tuned pieces. A handful, properly cut, the kind of thing that has a price.

Fire-tuned. Fewer.

One air-tuned, wrapped separately, which told him something about what it had cost.

And underneath all of it, in a cloth that had been folded by somebody who cared about it, the etching stylus.

Ornate past the point of usefulness. A silver band at the grip that a child's hand would have swallowed. Given to him on his fifth birthday by both his parents at once, which made it the only object he owned that had come from the two of them together, and which was the reason he had not remembered it was in there.

Two untouched gifts from two parents, fifteen years in the dark, sitting in the same box in a cold room four hundred miles from either of them.

He put the stylus on the table and did not pick it up again for eleven days.

The drop happened on the Thursday and he did not decide to do it.

He was sitting with a flat stone in his left hand and his mother's grey crystal in his right, warm the way it was always warm now, and he let himself want things, which he had not done in a while and had not done idly in years.

Water that lifted itself out of a lake and went where it was told. Heat that held a number instead of a temper. Light in a town that burned fish fat for it, in rooms where women sewed by a wick and went blind at forty because that was what happened to women who sewed.

He went under between one want and the next.

The stone was in there with him.

That was not what he had expected, and the first thing he felt was disappointment, because the flat stone was almost nothing. It was a volume with a level in it. A jar, and how full the jar was, and that was the entire contents of a teaching stone. You could put charge in it and you could let charge out of it and there was nowhere else for either of those to go.

The water-tuned piece was not like that at all.

It had structure, and the structure had handles on it. How fast it would pull. How much it could hold before it had to let go of something. The floor beneath which it would not answer at all. And then three that belonged only to it and to nothing else in the trunk: how much water, how hard, and whether it would move water that was already there or do the more expensive thing and work at something harder.

None of them were labeled. There was nothing in the world to label them with.

And not one of them had moved since the day it was cut, by a man in a workshop Emeric would never see, some decades ago, who had set every handle where he thought a customer would want it and then sold the thing and gone home.

Emeric came up out of it and sat very still for a while.

Then he noticed where his hands were.

The crystal was in his right hand. It had been in his right hand the whole time. The water stone was on the table, three inches clear of his knuckles, and he had been turning its handles over like a man reading the back of a watch.

He moved it four feet and went back in and it was still there.

He put it on the far side of the room, behind him, on the floor, under a chair, and it was still there, exactly as present, not fainter and not further.

The next morning he had Ward carry it across the castle courtyard and stand with it at the far wall in the cold, which took some explaining and got the particular flat look Ward reserved for things he intended to do anyway.

It held.

Emeric wrote that one down before he wrote anything else, and then sat with it for an hour without writing anything after it.

Contact was for learning a stone. Reading it, opening it, finding out what was in there. Addressing it afterward was not. And the entire practice of magic in this kingdom, every guild rule and every tower procedure and every mage standing in a field with his hands on a thing, was built on the assumption that a man has to be where the work is.

Nobody had checked. There had never been a reason to check, because nobody could open a stone in the first place.

He spent the rest of that week finding out what he could not do, which took less time than he wanted it to.

He could not make the water stone produce heat. Not badly, not weakly, not at cost. There was nothing in it that pointed toward heat, and turning every handle it had in every direction produced water, done differently.

He could not make a stone give more than it took. He spent two evenings on that one, out of a kind of embarrassment, because he had known the answer before he started and had wanted to be wrong in an interesting way.

Output and duration competed for the same draw. Always. A stone pushed to run hard ran briefly, and a stone set to run all day ran gently, and the product of the two was fixed by what the cutter had put in there.

So he was not gaining power. He said that out loud, alone, in a cold room, and enjoyed the shape of it more than the situation warranted. He was gaining control of settings that every other man in the world left exactly where the cutter had put them.

The number came out of that week and it was the one mistake he made that winter that he never caught.

He wanted a working figure for how much a given stone held, because you cannot design anything against a quantity you cannot name. He derived it off the water pieces, checked it against three others, got agreement inside what he considered tolerance, and then took it into an awake delve to confirm it.

It confirmed.

He wrote a caution to himself that same evening, in his own hand, in the notebook, and the caution was correct and specific and he understood it completely. The delving space renders from his own understanding. It shows him what he knows. Therefore it cannot disagree with him about anything he already believes, and a confirmation obtained in there is not evidence, it is an echo, and he was to stop treating it as a bench.

Then he wrote the number down as settled and built every margin in the barony off it for the next three years.

It was wrong by about a sixth, in the direction that made his margins thinner rather than fatter. Every device he designed after that Thursday had less reserve in it than the man who designed it believed.

He did not open the fire piece or the air piece that week, and the not opening was deliberate.

He read them, which was unavoidable. The fire stone had an ignition threshold and a temperature ceiling and he could see three ways for it to kill somebody before he had finished looking at it.

The air stone had a flow parameter. It ran from still air, through what the maker's own structure called a directed wind, and on past that to a setting at the top of the range that the structure named a high-pressure blast.

Emeric sat looking at the top of that range for a while.

Then he closed it, and wrote one line in the notebook, and turned the page.

He did not come back to it for two years.

The first test was outdoors, in the cold, with a basin, and Ward standing off at a distance with his arms folded because Ferra's rule applied and Emeric had developed a genuine respect for procedure, largely by watching what happened to people who did not have one.

He did not touch the fire stones.

He took a water piece, read it, and moved exactly one thing, which was the floor.

He lifted it. He set the stone so that it would not answer at all below a certain input, and then he fed it, slowly, and watched it sit dead.

And dead.

And dead.

And then start.

It was the least impressive thing that had ever happened in that courtyard. A small amount of water arrived in a basin about four seconds later than it would otherwise have arrived. Ward's expression did not move at all.

Emeric grinned like an idiot for most of the afternoon.

The branch came next and the branch was where he hit the wall.

A stone would accept a condition. He proved that in two days. It would hold a thing and only do it if something else was true, and the something else could be tested and the test cost almost nothing.

But the only thing a stone could check was itself.

Its own fill. Its own draw. What was happening inside it and nothing at all about what was happening anywhere else in the world.

So the first real working he wrote was a regulator rather than a sensor. Run at this rate until you fall below this level, then stop and hold what is left.

A float valve made out of a rock, and completely blind. It would run a dry field and a flooded one identically until it emptied itself.

He wanted a stone that knew when a field was dry. There was no such thing. There was never going to be such a thing. He spent a full day being unreasonable about that in a room by himself.

He got past it on the third morning and he got past it from the wrong direction, because he was annoyed rather than because he was clever.

A stone cannot feel the world.

But a stone working against the world has to work harder or easier depending on what the world is doing to it. And how hard it is working is the one number it can always see.

So you never build a sensor. You put a second crystal where the conditions are, let it strain, and read the strain.

He tested it in the ugliest way available to him. A water stone bedded against a cart wheel propped on a frame in the yard, and Emeric leaning on the wheel with both hands like a man pushing a barrow uphill, in the snow, in front of a guard who had stopped asking.

The draw climbed when he leaned. It fell when he let go. It climbed further when Ward leaned.

He sat back on the frozen ground of his own courtyard with his gloves wet through and understood that he had just built every instrument this barony was ever going to have.

The network followed from that inside an hour and then he made himself stop.

One crystal that thinks, addressing many that do not. The thinking ones had to be capable of holding structure, which meant his mother's stone and the handful his brother had given him as a joke at a farewell banquet, and that was the entire supply in the kingdom as far as he knew, which was not very far.

The endpoints could be anything.

That was the part he had not expected and it took him the rest of the evening to believe. A stone takes its affinity from what it sits in. A stone that has spent a century in running water is a water stone. Small, weak, badly formed, worth almost nothing to anybody, and both branches of the river around this island were full of them, and had been for as long as there had been a river.

A boy with a basket was a supply chain.

The count was not what made him careful. He wrote that down too, because he wanted to be honest with himself about which fear he actually had.

He was, as far as he could establish, the only person in the recorded history of this kingdom who had found a use for a stone the Circle filed as defective. Every one of them he put somewhere a visitor could see it was a stone a visitor could ask about, and every question of that kind was a thread to pull, and somebody was eventually going to pull one.

He wrote one hundred blank stones, one that decides, underlined it once, and did not build it.

The stylus was the humiliation.

He had a gifted child's foundation and fifteen years of nothing on top of it, and the specific failure was not what he had braced for. He could design an inscription perfectly well. He could see the whole of it, cleanly, better than he could have seen it at seven.

He could not make his hand do it.

The line wandered. Not badly. Not in a way anyone would have noticed on a brooch. It wandered in the way that matters when the line is the instruction, and he knew exactly what was wrong with every cut while he was making it and could not stop making it.

And then he learned what it cost.

You need mana to etch mana. The flat stone in his off hand was the ink, and it spent its charge the way a pen spends ink, and when he ruined a cut he had not only ruined a piece of substrate. He had spent a stone to ruin it.

His father's going-away present was his entire carving budget.

There was a second thing about the ink and it was worse. A flat stone that ran dry in the middle of a cut did not fail safe. It left half an instruction sitting in a piece of crystal, and half an instruction is not nothing. It is the dangerous kind of wrong rather than the harmless kind, and he found that out at a distance of about six feet with a basin between him and it.

The other method worked and it worked well and he could not teach it to a living soul.

Inside the delve, with resonance established and the medium held in his attention, the structure rearranged itself under the depth of his comprehension. No tool. No ink stone. No hand to betray him.

It spent him instead.

And the cost scaled with depth, which he learned entirely by feel and never managed to put a number on in his life. A shallow working was nothing much, a long evening and a headache. A deep one left him useless for a day and unable to be civil for two.

It behaved like effort rather than like a tax. Two stones that came out looking identical could cost him wildly different amounts, depending on how far down he had had to go to write them.

He reached the architectural consequence before he reached the physiological one, which he noticed afterward and thought was probably diagnostic of something. Put the thinking in one stone. Keep every other working in the system as shallow as a working can possibly be.

It was the only method that worked well for him. It was not transferable to anybody. Which meant that every working in this barony, now and for as long as he could see, had to come out of one pair of hands, in direct and total violation of the doctrine he had been preaching to Bram since the autumn.

He wrote that down too. He was accumulating rather a lot of things he had written down and done nothing about.

The last thing he worked out that winter was the one he should have worked out on the road.

He had told himself at a campfire, with a dead fire in front of him and a burned man asleep on the other side of it, that a mage for a mother and a knight for a father had not been enough to stop a cup of tea, and that he was going to start again in the morning.

He never had. There had been a town. Then a baron. Then a boy on the mill road. The mornings had all gone somewhere else.

And he had been carrying the answer in his pocket the entire time.

Time ran wrong in there and it ran long. An hour inside was a minute or so outside, badly, and inconsistently, and he never got it to behave the same way twice. Which meant repetitions. Hundreds of them, in an afternoon nobody noticed him having.

And the drills cost him the way delving cost him, in attention and fatigue, rather than by spending real mana out into the world. So he could run the same motion until it came clean, without lighting anything on fire, without an audience, and without a tutor, which was fortunate, because there was not one within four hundred miles.

It did not make the pool bigger. He established that himself, carefully, over about ten days, because it was the first thing he had hoped for and he did not want to find out he was wrong about it in a field somewhere with people watching. Capacity is blood. His blood was what it was.

What it built was precision and economy, which is the whole gap between a man who can do a thing and a man who can do it without waste.

And the flaw was the same flaw as the number, and he wrote it down in the same terms and it protected him just as little. The space renders from his own understanding. It would let him practise a wrong habit ten thousand times and feel entirely correct the whole way through, and the only thing that catches that is measurement, and he had none.

The routine started that winter and nobody ever saw it. Not Ward, not Ferra, not Bram.

He went into a room, sat down, and came out four minutes later having spent three hours on the same motion, and if anyone noticed that the prince was fond of sitting quietly they put it down to the kind of man he was.

By the end of the six weeks he could read a stone, open it, set it, address it across a courtyard with his hands empty, and make one of them report on the state of the world by watching how hard another one had to work.

Nobody alive knew that any of it was possible.
