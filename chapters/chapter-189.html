Chapter 189 - The Band

They went up off the slip at first light on midwinter morning, in the snow.

Linus had said snow until nine and clearing from the west after it, and Emeric had chosen the snow. A ship that went up out of a covered shed in the grey of a midwinter morning, in snow coming down thick enough to hide the far bank, was a shape nobody on the river wall would swear to afterward. Nobody was on the river wall but the watch, and the watch had been told to look at the town.

Tess was on the slip in her chair with a blanket over her knees and snow on the blanket. Linus stood beside her with his collar up and his barometer reading written on a card, which he gave to Fletcher at the foot of the ladder as if it were a ticket. Bram stood behind them both with his hands in his apron and did not say anything until Emeric had his foot on the first tread; and then he said, "Mind the coat box going up. The brooch is pinned in it, and the pin's good, but it's not a ladder pin," and went back up the hill to the forge before the doors were open, because he had seen the ship go up before and had work.

The river doors came open on their chains. The snow blew in across the slip. Fletcher, in the top cockpit, called down the speaking tube for the matrix, and Orrin, at the stone-keeper's station, put his hand on the first lever and moved it a notch, and the next, and the next; and the K Discovery came up off her blocks with the small groan she always gave, wood taking weight in a new direction, and hung on the slip a man's height up with the snow going past her ports; and then Fletcher gave her a breath of the main stone and she slid out through the doors over the river and up, and the shed was gone underneath in the white before Emeric had finished turning his head to look for it.

Twelve souls. Fletcher in the top cockpit and Cade at the bow helm below him, whistling his one tune with the snow streaming off the glass in front of his face. Orrin and Vesk at the station amidships with their hands on the levers and the dials in front of them. Tobe at the dials too, with his pencil and the log, calling the groups through the tube every quarter of an hour in the steady voice he had found on the runs north. Ward in the saloon with his back to the wall and his arms folded. Saban at the long table with Rowena's harbor dues spread out in front of him in Ferra's copy, reading them for the fourth time. Maren in the galley, wedging her pans. Imani in the sick bay with her door open, sitting on the bunk with her box on her knees. Vantic at the saloon rail with a cup of Maren's tea in both hands. And Ferra beside her, with the book.

They came out of the top of the snow at a little under a mile.

It was very sudden. One breath the ports were white and blind and running with melt, and the next there was sun, low and gold and enormous, laid flat across a floor of cloud that went away in every direction as far as anybody could see, white and lumpy and lit pink on its eastern faces, like a field after a heavy fall with nobody yet walked on it. The sky above it was a blue Emeric had not seen since Earth. Every face in the saloon turned to the ports at once.

Vantic laughed.

Out loud, with the cup in her hands. Not a polite laugh. A short, astonished, delighted bark of a laugh, the laugh of a woman who has been told about a thing all her life and has just found out it was all true; and then she put her hand over her mouth, and took it away again, and did not apologize. Saban looked up from the harbor dues. Ferra looked at her sideways, and something in Ferra's face came very close to a smile and did not quite arrive.

Emeric's sense was open, as it always was. He had not thought about it. And under Vantic's laugh, close, in the note that he had known for years now as small and dry and tightly kept, he heard her heart. It was going like a bird's. Fast and light and hard against her ribs, a heart that was frightened and glad at once and could not choose, under a laugh that was only glad.

It was not his to hear.

He shut his sense. All of it, the way a man shuts a door he has opened on the wrong room, and sat in the saloon with only his eyes and his ears like anybody else, and looked at the cloud.



At two miles Emeric sent everybody inside.

The deck had been shut since the slip. Now the hatches to the open stair were dogged, and the seal pump ran, and the needle on the pressure gauge by the station came up off its pin and sat where Tess had painted a green band for it; and the breath candles came out of the sealed locker and went into their clips at every seat, unlit, with the masks hung beside them on their hooks. Cade went down the passage checking each one and touching it, the way Tess would have, and whistled.

"A man can't see weather through a door," said Fletcher, in the top cockpit, to nobody.

"You can see it through the glass," said Emeric.

"Glass isn't the same. Glass you look through. A door you open." Fletcher looked at the dial. "I like to put my head out."

"Your head would freeze."

"I know that," said Fletcher. "I'm only saying."



The cloud broke up short of Skerrow Head, as Linus had said it would. The coast came out from under it in pieces, brown and grey and white with snow along its tops, and the sea beside it dark and wrinkled and very far down.

Linus had given them two marks. Skerrow Head, with its light, which every coaster on the run knew; and the Chalk, the long white cliff west of it that the fishermen called the Wall. Thirty-one miles between them, by Linus's chart, which he had walked the half of himself with a chain and trusted the other half because the crown's old coast survey agreed with his. Fletcher held her at three miles by the new dial, straight and level along the coast, and Emeric sat in the second seat in the top cockpit with Ottoline's chronometer open on his knee in its rings.

"Skerrow light," said Fletcher. "Under the bow. Now."

Emeric pressed the stop on the chronometer.

They went along the coast. Fletcher did not talk. The dial in front of him read three miles and a little and did not move, and the airspeed gauge on its tube at the bow said what it said, and the sea went by underneath very slowly, the way the ground always seems to go by from high up, as if nothing were happening at all.

"The Chalk," said Fletcher. "End of it. Now."

Emeric pressed the stop. He looked at the dial. He did the sum, and did it again, and wrote it on the card Linus had given Fletcher, under the barometer reading, in pencil.

"Nine minutes and eighteen seconds," he said. "Two hundred miles in the hour. Over the ground."

"She does two hundred on the river," said Fletcher.

"She does two hundred low. Up here the air's thinner. She should be doing two hundred and fifty-six." Emeric looked at the card. "The table says so."

"Then the table's wrong."

"Or the air's moving." He put the pencil away. "Toward us. Fifty miles in the hour, near enough. More, higher up." He looked out of the glass at the empty blue above them, where nothing showed and nothing moved. "There's a wind up here, out of the west, and we're flying into it."

Fletcher looked at the sky too, the way he looked at weather over the river mouth before he would take a boat out.

"I don't see any wind," he said.

"No. You can't. There's nothing up here for it to blow."

Fletcher thought about that for a while.

"I don't like a wind I can't see," he said at last; and put her nose up, and opened the main stone a notch, and climbed.



The probe hung outside the bow on its cord, below the cutwater, where Cade at the bow helm could see it through his lower pane and the top cockpit could see it by leaning: a grey lamp stone from the charging room's bucket, spent, in a little cage of wire. At the slip it had been grey. At a mile it had been grey. At two miles, if a man looked hard and wanted to see it, it had a faint whiteness in it, like the inside of a shell. At three miles there was no doubt. It was glowing, very softly, with no charge in it that anybody had put there.

Orrin talked the whole way.

He had told Emeric in the charging room in November that he would, and apologized for it in advance, and he did. He talked about the probe, and the cold, and the dials, and the night in the war when he had stood on a hill with his hands on a bursting stone and thought he would die, and how this was not like that at all, and how it was a little like that; and the hair on his arms, which was standing up; and the taste in his mouth, which was like a copper coin; and whether Vesk could feel it too; and Vesk, beside him at the levers, said yes, and nothing else, all the way up from two miles to three, which was the most Emeric had ever heard him say in an hour.

Emeric could feel it.

He had opened his sense again at two miles, because he had to; and the mana outside the hull had been thickening since the snow, as Armand's master had said it would, slowly, the way the air grows warmer walking toward a fire. A little more with every hundred feet. On the skin of his face first, then his hands, then the band on his left wrist under the cuff, where the grey bead sat against his pulse; a pressure like warm water rising round a man standing in a bath. Not unpleasant. Not yet anything.

Linus's dial went past twenty thousand.

Linus had painted the face to twenty thousand feet and no further, because he would not paint a figure he had not proved; and past twenty thousand the brass was bare. On the bare brass, very small, in pencil, he had put a single tick and written beside it in his exact hand, 5 mi, and a question mark. The question mark was the most Linus thing Emeric had ever seen. It was his turn mark. He had written it on paper in October and pinned the paper to the top cockpit's chart shelf: turn at the pencil tick, whatever is there, whatever is not.

The needle went out onto the bare brass.

And at a little past four miles by Fletcher's reckoning, the slow rise turned sharp.

He felt it before he saw it. Everybody aboard who had any mana in them felt it before they saw it. It was like rising up through the bottom of something into the body of it: as if they had been climbing for an hour through the thin shallows of a lake toward its surface, and had come instead, without warning, to its floor, and gone up through the floor, and were in the water. The pressure on his skin went from a warm bath to a hand. Then to a weight. The band on his wrist went warm against the bone. Orrin stopped talking in the middle of a word.

The probe blazed.

It went from a glow to a lamp to a white so hard that it made a star of itself under the bow, too bright to look at through the lower pane, and threw a shadow of the cutwater up across the cloud far below. Cade swore, mildly, and put his hand up, and slid the shutter across the pane in front of his face; and the bow cockpit went dim, with the probe's light coming through the edges of the shutter in white lines.

"Dials," said Emeric, into the tube.

"Holding," said Tobe's voice. "All groups holding." A pause. "Every one of them. Steady as the slip."

Holding. Every stone in her shut in its glass case behind its stopper, keeping exactly what it had brought up from Kaldo, as Tess had built them to; and outside the glass, a hand's width away from every one of them, more mana than any of them had ever been near. He went down the sealed stair from the top cockpit, fast, into the dome, and along the passage, and through the saloon past Ward and the long table and the women at the rail, to the station; where Orrin and Vesk stood at the levers with the dials in front of them, every needle where it had been at the slip, a little down from the climb.

"Open three," said Emeric.

Orrin looked at him.

The neck pulls hung along the top of the station in a row, brass handles on cords, one to each group of the matrix and a red one at the end, alone, for the main stone in the propeller housing. Every one of them had been tried on the slip, and nobody had ever pulled one in the air, because there had never been anything in the air worth opening a neck for.

Orrin put his hand on the third handle and pulled it.

Somewhere along the starboard side of the ship, in its glass-lined case under the deck line, a stopper came out of a neck. Nobody heard it. And the needle of group three on its dial went up. Not a hair in a minute. It went up the way the needle on the pressure gauge had come up off its pin at two miles: smoothly, steadily, all the way across the dial, until it touched the stop at the top and lay against it.

Full.

Vesk looked at the dial for a long time.

"I didn't know there was full," he said.

He had carried stones since he was a boy, and charged them, and spent them, and carried them home empty, all his life; every stone he had ever held had been on its way down from somewhere toward nothing. He looked at the needle against its stop as if it were a person he had heard about and never met.

Emeric could feel the people now.

He had not meant to. It was too loud not to. The Band was in the saloon and the passage and the cabins and the galley, coming through the oak of the hull and the felt of the cabins and every riveted seam, because the seal held air and nothing else; and everybody aboard who could hold any mana at all was filling. Ward's note behind him, a knight's note, deep and slow and dense, going up like a tide in a harbor. Orrin's and Vesk's, at his elbow, quick, filling and spilling and filling. And Vantic's, at the rail, which he had known for years as small and dry and kept, opening; going up and up, past anything a factor of a banking house should have been able to hold, past Orrin, past Vesk, with no sign at all on the outside of her, and her cup steady in her hand.

She turned her head and looked at him down the length of the saloon. She knew what he had felt. She did not do anything about it.

He looked at the red handle.

"Hold onto something," he said, to the saloon, in a voice he had meant to be loud and was not loud enough; and pulled it.



The main stone drank the Band.

He had known it would. He had known since Armand's master's lamp that a stone with its neck open in thick mana would fill. He had not known, until the instant he pulled the handle, the thing the dials had just told him about every stone in the ship: that a stone working with its neck open in mana that thick did not only fill. It pushed. It pushed harder, the way a man shoves harder with his belly full, and the main stone in the propeller housing had been pushing her at full, into the wind, at four miles; and it found, all at once, without anybody asking it, more full than it had ever been.

The K Discovery jumped forward like a horse that has been hit.

Everybody standing went aft. Maren's pans went across the galley in one long brass crash. Saban's papers went off the long table in a slide. Orrin, at the levers, sat down in the passage behind the station in the middle of a sentence he had just started again and finished it sitting down. Ward, against his wall, did not move at all.

And Ferra and Vantic went backward from the saloon rail together, Vantic's cup going up out of her hands in a brown arc, toward the open well of the after stair: twelve feet of iron treads going straight down into the stores in the dark, with nothing between the women and the treads but a rail at the height of their knees.

He was four strides away.

The room did it. He did not ask it to. The delve's long moment, which had been coming to him since the summer whenever he needed it badly enough, opened under him like a floor and the saloon slowed: the cup turning over in the air, the tea coming out of it in a brown ribbon that hung, Ferra's hair lifting off her shoulders, Vantic's hand going out behind her for a rail that was not there. Everything slow, and him not slow at all.

He went. Knight's speed in his legs, poured down into them in the half breath before the first stride, and his own weight off him so the surge could not throw him: light in the air of each stride, heavy at the plant, the way Ward had beaten into him all autumn in the yard. One. Two. Three. On the fourth he had his left arm round Ferra's waist and his right hand round Vantic's wrist at the lip of the well, and he went heavy, all of him, through his heels into the deck, and the deck took it and creaked, and the three of them stopped at the edge of the treads, leaning out over them, and did not go over.

The room let him go. The cup hit the treads below and broke.

Fletcher's voice came down the tube from the top cockpit, saying something short and nautical about the main stone, and the surge eased as he took it back by notches; and the ship steadied, and ran on, smooth and fast and quiet, as if nothing had happened.

Ferra was against his chest. She had not moved since he had stopped her. He could feel her breathing, hard and quick, through his shirt, and the back of her head under his chin, and her hand, which had found his forearm across her waist and was holding on to it.

She stayed there a breath longer than the ship needed.

"Necks," she said, into his collar. "You might have put that on the list."

"It wasn't on the list."

"I noticed." She let go of his forearm and stood up on her own feet, and put her hair back with both hands, and looked down the well at the broken cup on the treads, and then at him; and her face was very white and perfectly composed. "I'll put it on now."

He let go of Vantic's wrist.

Vantic straightened. She looked at her own wrist, where his hand had been, and then down the well, and then at him, in the even way she had of looking at a column of figures that had come out a little wrong and that she would mention later.

"That was quick," she said.

That was all she said. She did not say it again, then or afterward. She had felt it go into his legs. She had been closer to it than anyone. She went to the galley door and asked Maren, who was on her knees among the pans saying a great many things, whether there was another cup.

Imani was in the door of the sick bay. She had been there the whole time, holding the frame with one hand against the surge, with her box on the floor at her feet where it had slid; and she had watched all of it. She was looking at Ferra, still white, putting the pins back in her hair at the rail. And then at him.

He could not read her face. He had not been able to read Imani's face for years, when she did not want it read. But he saw her look at Ferra again, and he saw something go across her face, quickly, like the shadow of a bird across a yard: a surprise, he thought. Not at the surge. Not at him. At herself.

She picked up her box and went back into the sick bay and sat down on the bunk with it on her knees, and did not say anything.

Orrin, sitting on the passage floor behind the station, looked up at Emeric, and at the well, and at the place by the station where Emeric had been standing four strides away, and opened his mouth.

"He was standing beside them," said Ward, from his wall.

Orrin shut his mouth. He thought about it. "Was he?"

"Beside them," said Ward. "At the rail. I saw." He unfolded his arms. "Get up off the floor, man. You'll want to see the dials."

Orrin got up off the floor. Vesk, who had been beside Ward's wall and had seen it from the other side, said nothing, and went on saying nothing, and looked at Emeric once, and back at his levers.

Emeric heard Ward lie for him. He stood at the station with the red handle in his hand and heard it, and did not look at Ward, and Ward did not look at him.



Orrin opened the rest of the necks at his word, one pull at a time, down the row; and one by one, all the way along the station, every needle in the ship went up across its dial and lay against its stop.

Fletcher opened the main stone again, carefully this time, a notch at a time, and called the speed down the tube as the airspeed gauge climbed, and the main stone's dial did not move. Full, at full push. Emeric stood at the station and watched it and waited for it to go down, the way a man watches a candle he has lit at both ends, and it did not go down. The stone was spending what it had, flat out, into a wind out of the west that he could not see, at four miles; and the Band was filling it again as fast as it spent.

"Say that again," said Saban, from the floor, where he was picking up the harbor dues.

"She's flat out," said Emeric. "And the stone's full. And it's staying full."

"For how long?"

"For as long as we're up here." He did the sum aloud, because he wanted to hear it said in the air and not only in his head. "Every stone in her that's open fills as she spends it. The main stone. The channels. The matrix. She doesn't burn charge up here. She doesn't burn anything." He looked at the dial. "A ship that never runs dry."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Then why come down?" said Saban.

"I don't know yet." Emeric looked at the shuttered pane at the bow, with the probe's light in white lines round its edges. "That's what we're going to find out."

He went back up to the top cockpit. Fletcher had her at a little under twenty-two thousand on the bare brass, and Emeric told him to climb to the pencil tick, slowly, and Fletcher did, at a notch a minute, with the cloud floor going away below until it was only a white haze lying on the sea. The probe, under the bow, blazed, and blazed brighter as they climbed; and then, a little way under the tick, it did not.

It stopped. It did not go dimmer. It only stopped getting brighter, the way a pot stops getting hotter when it boils. He felt it on his skin at the same moment: the weight that had come on at four miles, steady all the way up, did not grow any more. Tobe's voice came up the tube to say that every group was holding full and none was climbing any more.

"Top of it," said Emeric. "This is the top."

"Of what?" said Fletcher.

"Of whatever this is." He looked at the needle on the bare brass, a hair under Linus's pencil tick. "Above here there's nothing to fill her. She'd go on on what she carries. Thinner air and no more mana." He looked up through the glass at the sky, which was nearly black at the top now, blue going to night overhead in the middle of the day. "Turn."

"There's nothing up there," said Fletcher.

"No. That's why we're turning." He put his finger on the paper pinned to the chart shelf, his own October hand: turn at the pencil tick, whatever is there, whatever is not. "I said I would."

Fletcher looked at the paper. Then he put her nose down, gently, and brought her down into the body of the Band again a thousand feet, and ran her west.

The westerly took what it took. It was stronger up here than at three miles, much stronger, and they made, by the next run Fletcher timed off a headland, a little under two hundred and fifty over the ground, when the air they were moving through was going past the bow at three hundred and more. But she was not paying for any of it. Emeric sat in the second seat and looked out of the glass at the Band, which looked like nothing. Empty blue sky over a white haze, very bright, and the sun, and nothing in any of it to show that anything was there at all.

He thought of a road on Earth. Six lanes, at four in the morning, lit and empty, the lamps going away in a line to the horizon, waiting for the day's traffic as if it had a right to it. Somebody had built it for traffic that did not exist yet when they poured it.

"This is where they'll run," he said.

Fletcher looked at him.

"Ships like her. In a few years. Ten, twenty." He did not look away from the glass. "Up here. In a lane, four miles up, that fills whatever goes along it for nothing. No coal. No stops. A ship could go from Kaldo to the capital up here and land with more charge than she left with." He heard himself. He went on anyway, because it was in his mouth and true. "You'd want a mark at every port to tell a ship where to come down. A light. A signal. Lanes, east and west, at different heights, so they don't meet. Somebody to keep the book of who's up here and where they're going."

Ward had come up the sealed stair behind them. He stood in the hatch of the top cockpit with one hand on the frame.

"Who else," said Ward, "do you mean to build ships for?"

Emeric did not answer. Fletcher, at the stick, looked straight ahead out of the glass, with an expression that said very clearly he had heard the question and would like to hear the answer and would not ask for it himself.

"Later," said Emeric.

"That's not a no," said Ward, and went back down the stair.



Vesk felt it first.

It was a little over three quarters of an hour since the floor. He came up the stair and stood in the top cockpit's hatch where Ward had stood, and said, in his quiet way, that there was something wrong in his head. Not pain. A pressure, behind the eyes, like the beginning of a fever, that had not been there at the foot. Emeric looked at him; and then, because he knew what came next, went down the stair to the saloon.

Maren's nose was bleeding.

She was at the galley door with a cloth to it and her other hand on the frame, telling Imani she was perfectly well and it was the dry; and Imani, beside her, with the box open on the long table and her sleeves pinned, had already unrolled a length of linen packing out of its waxed paper and was telling her to tip her head forward, not back, because back put it down her throat. Saban was at the table with his hand flat over his eyes. Ferra, at the rail, was very pale and very upright, writing in the book, and her pen was not quite steady.

"Headache," said Ferra, without looking up, before he could ask. "Since the turn. And my stomach." She wrote. "I'm putting the time in."

It came on them one after another, over the next quarter of an hour, in an order Emeric watched and did not say aloud. Maren first, and Saban, and Ferra. Then Fletcher, in the top cockpit, swearing at the blood on his glove. Then Imani herself, who packed her own nose with one hand without stopping packing Saban's with the other. Then Orrin, and then Vesk, who had felt it first and bled after. Cade and Tobe later than Orrin, much later than Maren, and lightly, a few drops each on the handkerchiefs Imani handed them, Tobe looking at his in surprise as if it belonged to someone else. Ward, last of everybody, a single line from one nostril, which he wiped with his thumb and looked at and did not mention.

Not Vantic. Not him.

He noted that, and the order of the rest of it, and put it away.

He knew what the textbooks on Earth said. Thin air: headache, sickness, bleeding from the nose, the lips going blue as the blood ran short of air, and then the judgement going, and then everything. He went to the station and took a breath candle out of its clip and struck the match in its top and set it burning in its tin on the station's shelf, under Orrin's nose, where the air it gave off would go into Orrin first; and he looked at the pressure gauge on the wall.

The needle sat in Tess's green band. It had not moved since two miles. The pump had held the seal all the way up; the air in the ship was the air of two miles, thinner than the river's and thick enough, and the candle burning on its shelf was making more of it for nobody who needed it.

He looked at Orrin's lips. They were pink. He looked at Maren's, and Saban's, and Fletcher's when Fletcher came down the stair with his glove to his face. Pink. Every one of them. He took Orrin's wrist and counted. Fast, from the climb and the fright and the talking, and full, and strong.

Not the air.

The candle burned down in its tin and changed nothing. Nobody's lips were blue, and every second nose in the ship was bleeding, and the probe under the bow was too bright to look at, and he stood at the station and felt the Band on his own skin like a weight, and understood.

It was the mana. Too much of it, too thick, going into people who had never been made to hold it, through the oak and the felt and every seam in her; not killing them, and not good for them either, the way a man made to drink a great deal of water fast is not drowning and is not well. Armand's master had come down from his last climb with a headache and a sore stomach and his nose bleeding, full, and the tower had said it was the thin air and the cold. It was not the thin air. It had never been the thin air.

Armand's master had been right about the lamp. He had been right about all of it.

"Take her down," said Emeric into the tube. "Just under the floor. A few hundred feet. Slowly."

Fletcher took her down. Slowly, the way he did everything with her, a notch a minute, with the needle coming back off the bare brass toward Linus's twenty thousand; and at a little under four miles the weight came off Emeric's skin as suddenly as it had come on, as if he had stepped up out of a bath onto a cold floor. The probe under the bow dimmed, from a star to a lamp to a glow, and Cade slid the shutter back off his pane and looked at it and whistled.

The open groups lay against their stops. They had come down full, and they stayed full, because she was flying on them only lightly now, and a full stone takes a long while to spend.

Within a quarter of an hour of the floor, Maren's nose had stopped. Then Saban's. Then everybody's. Imani went down the saloon with a basin and took the packing out of each of them in turn, and looked at it, and dropped it in the basin, and said "Better" to each of them in a tone that dared them to say otherwise. Ferra's pen went steady again. Orrin started talking.

They ran on, just under the floor of the Band, on full stones, west.



She came down on the sea a little after noon, thirty miles out from the inlet mouth, in the lee of a long low island with nobody on it, which Fletcher's chart marked with a cross and the single word Goats.

Three hours from Kaldo, near enough. Fletcher put her on the water as he always did, as if he were setting down a full cup, and tucked her legs up, and she sat on her hull in the lee of the island in a long slow swell with the sun on the water and no other sail anywhere on the sea.

Emeric had meant to run straight up the inlet. It was eighty miles. An hour and a half on her foils. They would have been on Rowena's quay by the middle of the afternoon.

Imani said no.

She said it at the long table, with the basin of used packing at her elbow and her own nose still faintly rusty at the edge where she had missed it, and she said it in the voice she used on the low town in the plague years to people who wanted to go to a funeral with a fever. Half the ship had a headache. Two of them had been sick in the heads. Saban could not see straight. Maren had bled through two packs. She did not know what the Band had done to anybody's blood, or their heads, or anything else, because nobody did, because nobody had ever come down from it but a few mages a long time ago who had been laughed at; and she was not going to walk twelve people onto a queen's quay with blood on their collars to find out in front of a foreign court. They would lie here till morning. They would eat. They would sleep. She would look at everybody again at dawn.

"She doesn't know we're coming," said Emeric.

"Then she won't mind if we come tomorrow," said Imani.

Ferra, at the end of the table, wrote something in the book, and underlined it.

"What was that?" said Emeric.

"The time," said Ferra. "And who decided." She blotted it. "So that when we're asked, there's an answer."

They lay there until morning.



It got cold at dusk. Not the cold of four miles, which had stayed outside the hull where it belonged; the ordinary wet cold of a winter sea, coming up through the planking as soon as the sun went, which the heater stones fought and did not quite win, because the heater stones had been fighting four miles of it since the snow and were tired.

The deck was open again, low down. Maren made a stew that had been three days in the making and Vesk ate two bowls of it without speaking, which Maren took as the highest praise a man could give. Orrin went to bed in the third cabin and was snoring through the felt within ten minutes, as Ward had said he would, and Ward, in the first, the one he shared with Emeric next to the women's, lay on his berth with his boots on and his arms crossed and looked at the ceiling. Saban was asleep at the long table with his head on the harbor dues. Fletcher and Cade sat in the bow cockpit with the chart and the chronometer, working out the noon sight they had not been able to take, and arguing.

Ferra went up on deck after supper to look at the island.

She was up there a long time. Emeric saw her through the iris port, at the rail, small, in her coat, with the wind off the sea pulling her hair out of its pins strand by strand and her hands bare on the rail because she had taken her gloves off to write something and not put them back on. Then the wind took her hair down altogether, and she stood in it with her hair blowing across her face and did not do anything about it for a while; and then she came down.

He had a heater stone in his hands, from the spares locker, and a reason, when he knocked on the women's cabin door half an hour later; and the reason was true, because the heater in that cabin was the forward one, nearest the bow, the coldest place in the ship, and he could feel through the door how little was left in its stone.

Imani opened the door.

The cabin was two berths, one over the other on the inboard wall, and a strip of floor, and a shelf, and the heater in its iron box at the foot, ticking faintly as it cooled. One electric bulb in the ceiling in a wire cage. Ferra was sitting on the edge of the lower berth with a cup of Maren's tea between her hands, still in her coat, her hair down her back in a red tangle from the wind, and Imani had been standing behind her, with one knee on the berth, putting it up.

She went back to it as soon as she had let him in. She did not say anything about it. She took up the comb from the berth and drew it through Ferra's hair from the crown to the ends, once, firmly, and divided it in three with her fingers, and began to plait it, quickly, the way she bound a wrist, with her mouth full of pins; and Ferra sat and let her and held the cup and looked at the heater.

"My fingers won't," said Ferra. "I've been trying for half an hour."

"She came in and sat down and tried to put a pin in with her hands like two fish," said Imani, round the pins. "I watched her drop it four times." She took a pin out of her mouth and put it in, flat against the plait, and took out another. "Nobody should do anything with their hands at that temperature. You can't feel what you're doing. You'll put a pin through your own scalp."

"I wouldn't have," said Ferra.

"You'd have tried." Imani put in the last pin and turned the end of the plait up and under and pinned that too, and sat back on her heel, and looked at it. "There."

Ferra put her hand up and touched it.

"Thank you," she said. She sounded surprised. So did Imani, a little, when she said that it was nothing.

Emeric knelt at the heater and opened the box.

The stone in it was a fire stone the size of a hen's egg, and he did not need the spare in his hand; he had known that through the door. It was not dead. It was tired. Most of what had been in it had gone into keeping two women warm at the cold end of a ship for seven hours, at four miles and then on a winter sea, and there was very little left. He took it out of its seat in his bare hand and held it.

Fire was his. It always had been, and it cost him nothing, or near enough nothing that he had stopped noticing. He closed his hand round the stone and let it go into it, out of his palm, slowly, the warmth that was always there in him under everything; and the stone took it the way a dry field takes rain, and grew warm in his hand, and then hot, and then too hot to hold, and he put it back in its seat and shut the iron door.

The heater ticked. Then it stopped ticking and began, very faintly, to hum.

"Oh," said Ferra.

The cabin warmed. It was a small cabin. In a few minutes he could feel it on his face, and Ferra put the cup down on the shelf and took her coat off, and Imani, still kneeling on the berth, untied the strip of cloth from her own hair, which had been up since Kaldo, and let it down and shook it out, and put her fingers through it at the roots and sighed.

He was still on the floor, with his back to the iron box. He had meant to go.

"You're warm," said Imani. She was looking at him. "You were warm on the climb too. I watched the frost come on the ports in the saloon and not on the one by you." She put her head on one side. "Is that the fire? Or the other?"

"Both, I think."

"You're a stove," said Ferra.

"Then sit by the stove," said Imani, "and stop being cold at it from the other side of the cabin."

She meant Ferra. She said it to Ferra. But Ferra was looking at him, and then she got up off the berth, and came the two feet across the floor, and sat down on it beside him with her back against the iron box too, in the warm, and put her cold hands inside his coat, flat, against his shirt, as if he were a stove; and he jumped, and she laughed, the short breath through her nose; and then she did not take them away.

Imani watched them from the berth.

He looked up at her. He did not know what his face was doing. He knew what he would see on hers, if it was there, and he looked for it, and it was not there. She was sitting on the edge of the berth with her hair loose round her shoulders and her hands in her lap, looking at Ferra's hands inside his coat, and her face was quiet and interested and a little astonished, as if she were looking at a result.

"I thought I'd mind that," she said.

Ferra went still against him.

"On the climb," said Imani. "When he caught you. I thought, standing there with the box at my feet, I'll mind this. I waited for it." She looked at her own hands. "I didn't. It didn't come. I stood in the door and looked at you against him and waited for it to come, and what came instead was that I was glad you weren't at the bottom of the stair." She looked up. "And that you'd have hated anybody else to see your face."

"I didn't have a face," said Ferra.

"You had a very particular face," said Imani. "I'm a healer. I see them for a living." Something happened at the corner of her mouth. "I've been thinking about it all afternoon. It surprised me more than the sky did."

Nobody said anything.

The heater hummed at Emeric's back. The ship moved on the swell, a long slow lift and fall, and somewhere through the felt Orrin snored.

Then Imani got down off the berth onto the floor, on his other side, in the warm, and put her back to the iron box, and took his left wrist in her hand the way she always did, as if it were hers to take, with two fingers on the inside of it below the band; and he felt her count. He felt her lips move. She had done it on the north tower's leads in August, after he had let her fall a man's height through the dark and caught her, and it had been fast then too.

She lost count.

He watched her lose it. He saw her get to some number and stop, and frown, and start again, and stop; and then she gave it up, and let go of his wrist, and put her hand flat on his chest beside Ferra's, inside his coat; and leaned across, and kissed him.

It was not short, this time.

And when she drew back, a breath, not far, Ferra's hand came up from inside his coat and took his jaw and turned his face to her, and kissed him too, the way she had in the linen press, as if she had finished a sum; and then neither of them was first, and neither of them was waiting. He did not know afterward whose hands were where. His were in Ferra's hair, and the plait Imani had made five minutes before was coming down under them, pin by pin, and Ferra did not care, and then they were in Imani's, which was thick and soft and smelled of the vinegar of her ward and something warmer under it. Somebody's mouth was on his neck. Somebody's fingers had found the buttons of his shirt and were dealing with them, impatiently, one at a time, and somebody else's had got there from underneath and were spread on his ribs. He kissed Ferra and then Imani and then Ferra and did not know which, by the end, except by the taste: tea, and salt from the wind on deck; and something bitter and clean that was Imani's.

They were on the berth. He did not remember getting there. A berth meant for one, in a cabin meant for two, with the upper berth a foot above their heads, which Imani hit with her elbow and swore at in a word he had never heard her use; and Ferra laughed against his collarbone, helplessly, and then stopped laughing, because his hand had gone under the hem of her shirt at the back to find the skin there, warm now, and she arched into it and caught her breath.

Imani's laces had come loose. He had done that, or she had, or Ferra had, reaching across. The neck of her dress had gone wide over one shoulder, brown and smooth in the light of the one bulb, and he put his mouth there because it was there, and felt her go still and then not still, her fingers tight in his hair. Ferra's shirt was most of the way up her back and she was dragging at his to match it, frowning at it as if it were a column that would not add. Neither of them looked at the other except once: Ferra's eyes, very close, going past his shoulder to Imani's face, and Imani's to hers; and a look went between them, quick and startled, like two people who have reached for the same rail on a stair at the same moment and found each other's hand, and do not let go of the rail.

He felt it come up in him. The heat that was always there; and the other thing under it, the rising one, that had lifted Ferra off a floor in the linen press without being asked. He felt the glyph begin to come, faint, at the edge of his sight, violet; and the berth begin to be lighter under the three of them; and he understood, all at once and very clearly, where this was going if nobody stopped it, and how far, and that it would be soon.

And his sense, which he had never shut since the foot of the Band, counted for him.

Ward. A wall away. Awake, on his berth, his boots on, his note as slow and steady as a clock and as close as the next room, because it was the next room. Vantic across the passage, alone in the fourth cabin, not asleep either, small and dry and full to the brim with the Band still. Orrin through the far felt. Saban at the long table. Fletcher and Cade in the bow, arguing about the noon sight, five yards and two partitions away. Every note in the ship near enough to touch. Every one of them near enough to hear.

He let the glyph go out.

It went. The berth was ordinary under them. He took his hand out from under Ferra's shirt, slowly, and put it flat on the blanket, and lay there with his heart going and the two of them over him, and breathed.

"Stop," he said.

They stopped. Both of them, at once, the way two people stop when they are listening for the same sound.

"Ward's on the other side of that," he said, and tipped his head at the wall to the first cabin. "He's awake. Vantic's across the passage. She's awake too. Fletcher and Cade are in the bow doing sums out loud." He looked at the upper berth a foot over his face. "I can feel every one of them. Which means every one of them is near enough to hear."

"The cabins are felt-lined," said Ferra. Her voice was not steady. "I signed for the felt. Forty yards of it. Abloh's."

"Felt isn't a wall."

Ferra looked at him. Her hair was everywhere. Her mouth was red. She looked at him the way she looked at a figure she had been about to argue with and had just found was correct.

"No," she said, after a while. "It isn't."

Imani had sat back on her heels at the foot of the berth with her dress off one shoulder and her hair down round her face, breathing; and she did not pull the dress up. She looked at him with her eyes narrowed a little, as if he were a patient who had said something she had expected.

"That's one reason," she said. "You've got another. You've got the face."

"Yes."

"Say it, then."

He lay on the berth and looked at the two of them, one on each side of him in the light of the one caged bulb, in a cabin meant for two, on a working ship on a winter sea with Orrin snoring through the felt and Saban asleep on the harbor dues; and said it, badly, because it was true and he had not had time to put it in order.

"It's the first time," he said. "For all three of us. Like this." He looked at Ferra. "It shouldn't be on a berth with a bunk over it and Orrin snoring and Ward lying awake a wall away counting. It should be somewhere better than that." He looked at Imani. "It should be something. I don't want it to be the night we lay up off a goat island because half the ship had nosebleeds."

Ferra was quiet.

"What, then?" she said.

"I don't know yet."

"You do," said Imani. "You've got the face for that too."

He did not say anything.

Ferra looked at him for a long time. Then she sat up on the berth, and pulled her shirt down at the back, and put her hair over one shoulder and looked at the ruin of Imani's plait in it, and took the last two pins out of it and held them in her palm.

"You're planning something," she said.

"Yes."

"And you won't say what."

"No."

"Good," said Ferra. She stood up. She was not quite steady on her feet, and the cabin was very small, and she put one hand on the upper berth to keep her balance and did not look at either of them; and she got her book off the shelf, and her pen, and sat down again on the end of the berth by Imani's feet, and opened the book to a clean page at the back, past the manifest, past everything, and wrote at the top of it, in her square careful capitals, a heading.

He could read it upside down. WHAT HE IS PLANNING.

She ruled a line under it. Then she wrote, under the line, a figure one, and a full stop, and sat with the pen over the page and looked at it.

"Nothing yet," she said, to the page. "I'll get it." She looked up at him. "Go to bed. Your own. And put your shirt right on the way, or Ward will have something to say about it in the morning and he'll say it to Fletcher."

He went. At the door he looked back. Imani had pulled her dress up onto her shoulder and was sitting at the head of the berth with her knees drawn up, and Ferra at the foot with the book on her knees and the pen in her hand; and Imani leaned over, without a word, and looked at the page, and pointed at something on it with one finger; and Ferra, without looking up, slapped the finger away, and Imani laughed, out loud, the dry short laugh through her nose; and the heater hummed.

He shut the door, and opened the next one. Ward was on the lower berth of the first cabin in the dark with his boots on and his arms crossed, exactly where his sense had put him, looking at the ceiling. He did not turn his head. He did not say anything at all, very loudly, while Emeric climbed into the upper berth, or after.



They ran up the inlet at first light, on her foils.

Imani looked at everybody at dawn as she had said she would, by the light of the saloon's bulbs, eyes and lips and noses and the insides of wrists, and passed them all, and passed herself. Fletcher put the legs down and the foils bit, and the K Discovery came up out of the water onto her three thin struts and went up the inlet at forty-five knots with the spray going off her in two white sheets and the sun coming up behind her over the sea.

The inlet was a long one, narrow and deep, with brown hills on both sides going up into terraces, and the terraces green even in midwinter, with vines on them in long bare rows. The air coming in at the deck hatch was warm. Not warm; not Kaldo's cold. It smelled of salt and of something under the salt that Emeric did not know for a while and then did, from Earth, from a school trip to a spa town: rotten eggs, faint, the breath of a mountain with fire in it.

At the head of the inlet, at the end of eighty miles, there was a mountain.

It stood up at the end of the water against the morning, grey and enormous and broken at the top, with a long white plume going up from its shoulder and leaning east on the wind. Under it, on terraces that climbed from the water's edge halfway up its knees, there was a city: white walls and red roofs, row on row, stairs between them, vines everywhere on the walls, a dome on a crag over the harbor with the sun on it; and off the cliff beside the dome, falling a hundred feet into the sea in a white sheet, a waterfall that steamed.

Ferra was beside him at the iris port with the book under her arm. She had been on deck since dawn. Her hair was up very hard.

"Four," she said, to the port.

He looked at her.

"Guesses." She did not look at him. "I have four. I don't like any of them." She looked at the mountain. "I'll have it by the time we go home."
