Chapter 189 - The Board That Needs Nine Hands

Vantic came for the spring quarter day in the last week of the frost, when the roads were iron in the morning and soup by noon, and the House's carriage came over the Left Bridge with mud to its axles and her clerk walking beside it to spare the horses.

She sat at the loan table in the counting room across from Ferra, as she always did, and opened her small book and Ferra's big one side by side, and read. Saban sat at the end with the carts' tallies. Emeric came down at mid-morning for the part that needed him and took the chair at the head of the table, and Ward took the chair by the door, and neither of them looked at the other.

It was the second quarter day he had sat through knowing what he knew, and he had found that the knowing was not the hard part. The hard part was that nothing about the morning was different. She read at the same pace. She asked about the frost's damage to the tower's course and what the lime would cost to lay it again and the sulphur on the last Marsh Hen run, all of which she had read about in the book before Ferra could tell her, and she asked it in the same dry, easy voice, a little amused, that she had used on the price of wool for seven years. He had expected, without ever quite saying so to himself, that once he knew he would see it. He did not see anything. That was, he supposed, what seven years of a letter every week bought a person.

She came to the clause about the tower's draw a little before noon.

She did not turn a page for it. She reached into the back of her own book and took out a letter, folded twice, with the House's seal broken on it, and unfolded it on the table in front of her so that it lay flat under her hand. Emeric could not read it upside down at that distance and did not try. He could see its shape. A page, most of a page, in a large formal hand that went from margin to margin like a clerk's. And under that, set a little apart, as though somebody had taken the pen from the clerk at the end, four or five lines in a smaller hand, close and upright.

"The House agrees," Vantic said. "The draw against the tower moves a fortnight past the thaw. The House's man will walk it then." She put her finger on the letter. "It asks no fee for the change and sets no condition on it. You may have Ferra initial the margin, and I'll initial it under her, and that's the whole of it."

Ferra had her pen up already. She held it over the margin for a moment without writing.

"No fee," she said.

"None."

"The House has charged a fee for every line it ever changed in an instrument of ours. Every one of them's in the book."

"They are," said Vantic. "I drew most of them."

She did not say anything else. She did not say why the House, which had never in seven years given Kaldo a Copper it had not been obliged to give, should give it a fortnight for nothing, and she did not look at the smaller hand at the foot of the letter while she did not say it. Ferra waited long enough for her to say it if she was going to, which was not long, and then wrote her initial in the margin and turned the book round, and Vantic wrote hers under it, small and neat, and turned it back.

"Thank you," Emeric said.

"Thank the House," said Vantic, pleasantly, and folded the letter along its folds and put it back into her book, and went on to the paper works.

He did not look at Ward. After a while, when the talk had gone on to the paper works' second vat and the price of rag, he got up and went back upstairs to the long room, because there was nothing else at the table that needed him, and because he found he did not want to sit across from her any longer with a thing in his head she did not know was there. At the top of the stair he stopped, with his hand on the newel post, and let himself think it once. Somebody at the far end of the Thursday tide had read the clerk's page, and taken the pen, and written the yes himself.

He did not think the name. He went in to the day book.

In the afternoon he rode out to the flat with Ferra and Ward, because the board was ready to be looked at and he had promised Ferra she would look at it before he asked her for anything.

The north gate was hung. The oak had gone into its arch in the last dry week before the quarter, two leaves of it, iron-strapped, each as broad as a barn door and a good deal heavier, and the gate company had swung them shut and open a dozen times for the pleasure of it and then left them open, because there was nothing yet to shut them against. The other three gates stood with their arches empty and the oak for them stacked under canvas in the gate towers' lee. Carts went in and out under the north arch all afternoon, and every carter looked up at the new leaves going under them, and most of them touched their caps to nobody in particular.

The board was in the gate tower's first-floor room, where the gate's watch would sit when there was a watch to sit there. Nevin had built it over the winter at a bench under the window, the way he had built the island's board years ago, and Emeric had told him the same thing he had told him then, that it did not have to be pretty, it had to be in the right place, and Nevin had apologised for the houses anyway.

There were not many houses. That was the first thing anybody would notice. It was a slab of the ridge quarry's dark slate the size of a kitchen table, in a case of oak with a lid that closed down over it, and on the slab was the circle: the wall, raised a finger's height, grey, with its sixteen towers standing out of it in little square knuckles and its four gates cut through it at the quarters. Inside the circle the palace sat on its rise within its ring of blue-grey lead that stood for water, and the second ring and the third, and the aqueduct coming in at the north. The first quarter had its two streets of blocks, and the market with its tower. The rest of the slab was bare, with the streets scribed into it as lines and nothing standing on them, because nothing stood on them in the world yet. Nevin had asked whether he should cut the houses that would be there, and Emeric had said no. A map that showed what was not there was lying.

And along the top of the raised wall, all the way round, seated flush into the grey, there were slivers of pale stone no thicker than a hair, set close, so many of them that from the door they looked like a line of frost along the top of the ring.

Ferra walked round it once without saying anything.

It was a thing she did. Emeric had seen her do it round a new ledger, and a new instrument, and the forge the first night she saw it: go all the way round a thing before she let herself have an opinion of any part. The table stood out from the wall far enough for her to walk behind it. She went round it slowly, with her hands behind her back and her book under her arm, and stopped once at the south gate, and once at the first quarter, and came back to where she had started, and looked at the line of slivers.

"How many?" she said.

"Ninety-six. Six to every span between two towers." He put his finger on the nearest stretch of the model wall, between the north gate's tower and the next. "The island's nodes only have to see. These have to count, and pace a man, and tell one man from three walking close. That wants them nearer together. Nevin's men have cut the traces in the first quarter's arc, north gate to east gate, twenty-four of them. The rest wait."

"Wait on what?"

"On what runs them."

He took the case's lid by its edge and turned it back further, so she could see the back of the slab, where on the island's board the deciding stone sat on a shelf nobody had reason to look at. There was a shelf here too, cut into the case behind the backing, long and narrow. It had nine cups cut into it in a row, each the size of the first joint of a man's thumb. All nine were empty.

Ferra looked at the nine cups for a while.

"The island's board has one," she said.

"The island's board has twelve stretches." He let the lid down again. "I built it with twelve because that was how the island's wall broke up, where the corners and the stairs and the gates fell. I didn't know when I built it that twelve was close to all one stone will hold. I found it out this winter." He sat down on the edge of the watch's bench under the window, because this part was going to take longer than standing. "It isn't the stone. The stone would hold a great deal more, if holding were all it had to do. It's the working. Every stretch it keeps, it has to keep in order with every other stretch, so that when a man walks from one to the next it knows he's the same man and not a second one come out of the wall. Twelve stretches is sixty-six of those. Twenty-four is two hundred and seventy-six. Ninety-six is..." He stopped, because he had worked it in the delve and did not need to work it again aloud. "It isn't twice as hard when you double it. It's four times as hard. I built the whole circle's working in the delve and ran it, where a mistake costs nothing, and past a dozen stretches it smears. A man walks along the south arc and the light comes up a stretch behind him, and then two, and then it lights the tower on the far side of the gate because it's lost which of its two gates he went through."

"So you break it up."

"Into eight. Twelve stretches to a stone, two spans of wall each. Eight stones, and each one keeps its own arc and nothing else." He looked at the model wall where the arcs would meet, at every second tower. "And then the joins. Where one arc hands a man to the next, the two stones have to agree about him. With two stones there's one join and I can write it into both of them by hand. With eight there are eight joins all the way round, and they all have to agree with each other, and they won't unless something sits over them and settles it. That's the ninth. It doesn't watch any wall at all. It listens to the other eight and keeps them honest." He was quiet for a moment. "And every one of the eight spends some of itself telling the ninth what it saw. That's the price of doing it in pieces. It's a slower board than the island's, for all its size."

Ferra did not write any of that down. She stood by the table with her hand flat on the oak of the case, not on the slate, and looked at the frost-line of slivers going round the ring.

"Nine," she said.

"Nine."

"There are eleven in Kaldo that anybody knows of." She did not open the book. She did not need to; she had written the list more times than he had. "Yours. The six on the table's wrists. The island's board. The pump house on the irrigation line. The bead, which holds the Wayford names. And the chipped one, in the strongbox, bound to nothing." She took her hand off the case. "Ten of them are spoken for. The bead you've said you won't wipe. That leaves one, and it's chipped, and it was your mother's."

"I know."

"So it's a board that wants nine hands on it," Ferra said, "and we have one to spare, and we won't spare it."

Ward had stayed by the door. He was looking at the model, at the north gate and the arc of wall going east from it round the first quarter, where the twenty-four traces had been cut. The first quarter was where the people were. Near two thousand of them, in two streets and a market, behind a wall that said nothing whatever to anybody about who was walking on it at night.

"The walking watch," Ward said.

"Doubled on the first quarter's arc from tonight," said Emeric. "Until there's a board."

Ward nodded. It was what he would have done, and he had wanted to hear it said. He went out and down the stair to see about it.

"Where do they come from?" Ferra said.

"I don't know." He looked at the empty shelf, although the lid was down over it now. "Somewhere somebody digs them. Corbin didn't carve eight of them himself. Somebody found them in the ground, and cut them, or found them cut. There's a vein of them somewhere, or there was." He stood up. "Armand will know."

Armand did not know. Armand knew the opposite, which was worse.

He said it in his study that evening, in the chair by the fire with his stick across his knees and a rug over them that he had stopped pretending was anybody else's idea. He had grown thinner again over the winter. His hands on the stick were all knuckle. But his voice was the voice he had always had for a question he found interesting, dry and quick, and he had come a little awake at the question the way a fire does when you open the door.

"There's no mine," he said. "There never was."

"There has to be somewhere they came from."

"There has to be somewhere they came from," Armand agreed. "That's not the same thing as a mine. Nobody has ever found one in rock. Not in a vein, not in a seam, not in a pocket in a cave, not in any working in any kingdom whose records I have read, and I have read a great many kingdoms' records, most of them out of boredom." He turned the stick a quarter turn on the hearth. "They turn up. That's the only word anybody has ever had for it. One in a dead lord's cabinet when his heirs sell it up. One in a pawnshop in a port. One that a ploughman brought to his priest because it was carved and he thought it might be holy, and the priest sent it to the tower because it wasn't. I have seen perhaps thirty in my life, including the ones on your table. I've never once seen one come out of the ground in front of me, and I've never met a man who had."

"The Circle keeps them?"

"The Circle keeps three, in a case in the library, with a card under them that says what they are, which is nothing." Armand's mouth moved. "The Circle doesn't want them. Nobody does. The Empire wanted them once, a very long time ago. There's an account in the Ostrel archive, which I read at thirty and have not seen since. The Empire's scholars took a dozen of them and poured into them, one at a time, a mage at each, harder and harder, to make them wake. They cracked. Every one of them. Split along the carving like a nut." He looked at the fire. "They wrote them down as dead, and dead they've been, in every book since. Worthless, carved by somebody for some purpose nobody knows, holding nothing and doing nothing. That's the whole of what the world knows about them." He glanced at Emeric. "Except what you know, and I know, and the rest of that table knows, which is that the world is wrong."

Ferra, on the settle by the door with her book on her knees, wrote something small. Emeric did not ask what.

"Then where does anybody buy one?" he said.

"Ask Saban," said Armand. "It's a shop question. I only know the books."

Saban was in the long room, and he did know. He knew it the way he knew the price of everything that had ever changed hands in a port, which was without seeming to have learned it.

"Curio men," he said. "Dealers in old things. Every port has two or three and the capital has a street of them. They'll have one, some of them. Two, sometimes. In a tray with the old seals and the broken clasps and the carved bone, and they'll sell it you as a paperweight, or a luck stone, or a thing a queen once owned, according to what they think of your coat." He considered. "Three Silver, in a port. Six in the capital, if they've heard it called royal. More if you look like you want it. Less if you buy it with a box of other rubbish and don't look at it."

"Never more than two?"

"Not loose." Saban turned his cup. "Once in a dealer's life, a whole set turns up. In a case, out of a house that's been sold up to the walls. They're eight, when they come whole. Always eight. Eight in a case, like a set of good spoons. A dealer who gets one of those asks a Gold for it and waits a year for a lord with a sense of humour." He looked at Emeric over the cup and did not say the next thing, because he knew Emeric had already thought it.

Emeric had. A case with eight stones in it, set down on a banquet table with both hands in front of two hundred people. He had never wondered where his brother bought them. He wondered now, and could not see that it mattered, and put it away.

"You could have bought them for years," Ferra said, when Saban had gone to bed.

It was not a reproach. It was a line in a ledger that did not close, and she wanted it to.

"I could have bought one," Emeric said. "Once. Maybe twice." He was at the window, where the lamps of the island's market street went down toward the water. "Corbin gave me eight of them at a banquet to say I was worth nothing. The whole court laughed. The whole court knows what they are, because that was the joke. Worthless stones for a worthless prince." He did not turn round. "If that prince, seven years into his exile, starts buying worthless stones, and goes on buying them, and pays for them, somebody in the capital is going to hear that a curio man in a port sold one to Kaldo, and then another curio man, and then another. And somebody will ask why the drunk wants more of his brother's joke. And the only answer that makes any sense of it is that they aren't worthless." He turned round then. "A price is a rumour, Saban says. The moment I pay for one, I've told the whole trade what I think it's worth. The moment I pay for ten, I've told the capital."

"So you don't."

"I buy three." He came and sat down across from her. "One at a time. Never asked for by name. Never more than one from any dealer, and never by anybody who'll be seen buying the next. Each one bought with a box of other things, cheap, by somebody who looks as though he doesn't know what he's got. Out of the fund, not the book."

"Three isn't nine."

"No. Two of them go on the first quarter's arc. Two arcs. One join."

"And the third?"

He did not answer that. He had an answer, of a kind, and it was not one he could have put into a sentence she would have written down, so he did not try. Ferra looked at him for a moment longer, and then opened her book to the fund's page, and wrote a line under the last one. Three curios. Saban's hands. Per the box.

They came in over five weeks, one at a time, by three roads.

The first came from Wayford in the second week, in a crate of odds and ends that a Wayford carter Saban used for small errands had bought at the sale of a paper-maker's widow's house on the square, because the widow's son wanted the house cleared and the carter had a cart: a brass inkstand with a cracked lid, two dozen seals on a string, a box of quills gone yellow, a pewter candlestick, and at the bottom of the crate, rolled in a stocking, a grey stone the size of the first joint of a man's thumb, carved over every face. The whole crate had cost four Silver. The carter had written it on his bill as sundries, and had not been told what he was buying, and had not asked.

The second came from the capital in the fourth week, by the Crown's post, in a letter-packet from one of Saban's men there, a grain factor's clerk who bought old things on his half day for the pleasure of it and was known for it in the street of curio men. He had bought it from a man with a stall under the old exchange, out of a tray of luck stones, with a cracked seal ring and a bone whistle, for six Silver the three, which was more than they were worth and less than the man had asked. The clerk had not been told what he was buying either. He had been told to buy something grey and carved if he saw it cheap, and something else with it, and he had done both.

He bound the two of them the night the second came, in the gate tower, alone.

It was slow, as it always was. He sat on the watch's bench with the case open and the first stone in his closed hand and went into the room, and held it there until it answered, and then held it longer, until it was his in the way a dog is somebody's. Then he wrote its arc into it: twelve stretches from the north gate's tower east to the second tower along, each stretch where Nevin's sliver said it was, and the join at the second tower written in by hand, so that it knew what the other stone would say there and would believe it. Then the second stone, twelve stretches on from there to the east gate, and the same join written in from its side. It was past the third bell when he seated them in the first two cups on the shelf, one beside the other, with seven empty cups running away from them along the shelf into the dark of the case.

Ward came up the stair at the fourth bell with a lantern, because he had said he would, and Emeric went down and out onto the wall walk at the north gate with a second lantern and walked east.

He walked it slowly, at a watchman's pace, past the first tower and the second and on toward the east gate, with the first quarter's roofs below him on his right, dark, and a few windows still lit, and the market tower standing up out of them with its tank against the stars. The wall walk was broad and new and his boots were loud on it. At the east gate he turned and came back, faster, and at the second tower he stopped, and waited the count of ten, and went on.

When he came back up into the watch room Ward was standing over the board with the lantern held low, and he did not look up.

"Walked all the way," Ward said. "Out at a watchman's pace. You came back faster than you went. At the tower, where the two stones meet, you stood still for about ten, and there was one of you the whole time, not two." He moved the lantern a little, along the lit arc, and then past it, round the dark three-quarters of the ring, where seventy-two hair-thin slivers lay in the slate and showed nothing at all. "That part's good."

"That part's good," said Emeric.

They stood and looked at the board together for a while: a quarter of a circle of wall lit faintly along its top where a watchman could put his finger and say there, and the rest of it grey, and the palace on its rise in the middle of its lead ring, and nine cups on a shelf behind the backing, two of them full.

The third came up the lower water on the Marsh Hen at the end of the fifth week, the day the thaw began, in Saban's own coat pocket. He had bought it himself on Emberfell quay, from a Solreth coaster's mate who had a tray of southern trinkets he was trying to be rid of before his ship sailed, along with a string of amber beads he said were for a niece and a knife with a horn handle that he said he had wanted anyway. The stone had cost him one Gold and two Silver, because the mate had seen him look at it. Saban was annoyed about that. He said so twice, laying it on the long-room table among the day's papers, and said that he had been buying things on quays for thirty years and had never once let a man see him look.

"It was the only one on the quay," he said. "It was that or wait a year."

"It's the right price," said Emeric.

"It's a Gold too much."

"It's the right price for this one."

Saban grunted, and let it go, and began gathering his papers. At the door, with his hand on the frame, he stopped, the way he stopped when he had kept the thing he had actually come to say until the end.

"The oil," he said. "Rowena's merchants took the order and bespoke it from Duvrenne's factors at the quay before I'd finished my wine. Light and heavy both. The factors will fill it after their spring fair, and the first casks come round on somebody's boat to Emberfell by midsummer, if the factors don't find a better buyer in between, which they might. Nobody asked what it was for." He paused. "I told you they wouldn't."

"You did."

"And one other thing." Saban did not come back into the room. He stayed in the door with his coat on, half turned, as though it were an afterthought, which meant it was not. "My people on the south road. The inns between here and the coast. There's a man been asking after Kaldo. Not after the works, or the city, or the ships on the lower water, which is what men ask after now. After the prince." He looked at the table, not at Emeric. "Whether he's well. How he looks. Whether he keeps his health. He paid in silver that isn't ours, with a ship on one face, and he paid more than the questions were worth, and he called your mother Amara. Not the queen. Not the late queen. Amara, the way you'd say the name of somebody you'd sat at a table with."

Ward, by the fire, had turned his head.

"Corbin's," Emeric said. "He's had a man watching this road since the court. He'll have old palace people. Anybody who served in my mother's rooms would call her by her name."

"Corbin's men don't ask whether you're well," said Saban. "They ask what you're building. They pay in Rands, because they're paid in Rands, and they pay what a question's worth to the Copper, because the man who pays them checks." He shrugged, one shoulder, in the door. "I don't know whose he is. I only know he isn't Corbin's. I've watched Corbin's men in those inns for two years and they don't talk like that."

"Where is he now?"

"Gone on. West, they think, or south to the coast. He was only asking." Saban put his hat on. "I'll hear if he comes back."

He went out. Emeric sat with the third stone on the table in front of him among the papers, grey, whole, carved over every face, and thought about a man who asked whether he was well, and could not make it fit anybody he knew. He thought about it for longer than it deserved. Then he thought that he had a wall with a quarter of it lit and three quarters dark, and that the man was gone west, and put it away in the place where he kept things that did not yet mean anything.

He took the third stone to Ferra in the morning, in the counting room, before the day's clerks came up.

She unlocked the strongbox without being asked and folded back the green silk, and there was the chipped one, where it had lain since the treasury box, with the bead beside it and Keller's copies and Armand's readings under them all. He put the new one down beside the chipped one on the silk. They were the same stone, as near as two stones could be. The same size, the same grey, the same lattice over every face. One had a chip out of a corner, small and old and worn smooth. The other did not.

"Not in the board," Ferra said.

"Not yet."

"The board's short seven."

"I know."

She waited. He did not say anything else. She had a way of waiting that had more room in it than most people's asking, and he had learned to stand in it without filling it, and she had learned to let him. After a moment she took up her pen.

Then she put it down again, and went to the shelf by the window, and took down the little brass balance in its case that she had borrowed from Imani's dispensary a long time ago and never given back, the one that weighed in grains. She set it on the table and zeroed it, and put the chipped stone in the pan, and wrote the weight. Then the new one, and wrote that. She did not explain why. She weighed everything that went into the box that could be weighed, and always had, and he had never once asked her why either.

In the strongbox, she wrote, in the box's own book, in the hand she used for things that had happened. The Queen Amara's second stone, the chipped one, bound to nothing, as received; so many grains. With it, from this day, one curio of the same kind, bought on Emberfell quay by S. for one Gold and two Silver, whole, bound to nothing, as received; so many grains. Two others of the kind bound in the fourth week to the first quarter's arc of the north board. Seven cups empty.

She read it back. It was all true. She folded the silk over the two stones, the chipped one and the whole one, side by side in the ridges where a dead queen's combs had lain, and shut the lid, and turned the key, and put the key back on its cord.

"Seven," she said.

"Seven," said Emeric.

Neither of them said where seven more might come from. There was nowhere that either of them knew of, and they both knew that the other knew it, and the clerks were on the stair.
