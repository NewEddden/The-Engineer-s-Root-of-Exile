Chapter 226 - Cupped Hands

For eleven days nothing happened, and he went and looked at it every one of them.

He went in the mornings, before the long room, down the gallery from his rooms to the pass-box, and stood at its outer door and looked through the two panes of glass into the round room. Imani's beds round the edge, green and thick in the light that came down through the dome. The willow slips, which had put out leaves. The three grey shrubs in their corner, which had put out more. And in the middle, the round bed of dark earth, raked level, with the mark in its middle where his hand had pressed it, fading a little every day as the earth settled, and nothing else.

He put his sense into it every morning. He could do that from the gallery; the glass held the medium, but it did not stop him feeling what the medium was doing on the other side of it. And the stone was there, every morning, a finger deep, chip up, the same. Grey. Still. A little heavier, perhaps; he could not tell by the sense, and Ferra's scales were on the other side of the glass. Lying in a room full of the band, doing what it had done in the silk in the box for years, which was nothing.

On the twelfth morning it was not the same.

He felt it before he saw it. The stone in the earth had changed its place in the medium. It was not lying in the band the way a stone lies in water any more. The band was going into it. Not much. The way the medium went into a flask cell's neck in the veins of the ship, a steady thread of it, drawn in. The stone was drinking.

He stood at the pass-box door with his hand flat on the glass and did not move for a long time.

On the fifteenth morning there was a crack in the earth.

It was very small. A line across the raked surface of the bed, just off the middle, where the earth had lifted a hair's breadth and split, the way the crust of a loaf splits in the oven. Under it, when he put his sense down, the stone was not a stone. It had opened along its seams. The fine grey net across its old broken face had been a seam all along, it seemed, or one of several, and the stone had come apart along all of them at once, not broken but parted, the way a pod parts, and something pale was coming up out of the middle of it through the earth.

He sent Hobb for Ferra and Armand and Bram and Imani, and did not leave the door until they came.

It came up on the seventeenth day.

A stem. Pale, almost white, as thick as a straw and bent over on itself at the top like a shepherd's crook, pushing the earth up ahead of it. And on the top of the crook, riding up out of the earth on it, the two halves of the stone.

They were grey. They were the stone, still; he could see the chip at the corner of one of them, worn smooth. But they were halves now, hollow, each one a thin grey shell like the halves of a walnut, and they rode up on the stem the way the two halves of a bean ride up out of the ground on a bean's stem, and when the stem straightened, on the nineteenth morning, they opened.

They opened like hands. Two grey hands, cupped, held up side by side on the top of a pale stem the height of a man's finger, open to the light that came down through the dome. And lying in them, in each of them, like a thing held out to be given, a leaf.

"Hollow," said Ferra.

She was at the pass-box door beside him, at dawn, with her book under her arm. Armand was behind them on the gallery with his instruments. Bram had not gone home at all.

"Hollow Crystals," Ferra said. "Everybody calls them that and nobody knows why. They're solid stone, all the way through. Everybody knows that." She looked at the two grey halves held up on the stem. "Somebody knew. Once. Somebody saw one do that."

Nobody answered her. She wrote it down: the date, and the hour, and the stone opened, two halves, a stem, two leaves. And then, after a moment, because she wrote everything, like cupped hands.

It grew for six days. He watched it grow.

The two first leaves were small and round and a green so dark it was nearly black. Between them, on the eighth day of the stem, a bud came up, and opened, and it was not a round leaf but a long narrow one, pointed, with a fine pale vein down its middle, and another after it, and another, set round the stem in a spiral, each one a little above the last. By the sixth day the seedling was the height of a man's hand, with its two grey half-shells still held out below its first leaves like a pair of cupped palms, and seven long leaves above them going up in their spiral, and it was the most beautiful thing Emeric had ever seen.

He did not say so. He stood at the door every morning and every evening, and looked, and did the arithmetic.

It had woken at two thirds of the band. It had grown at the band, since Ferra's red line: every day the gauge dropped a little as the seedling drank and the dome leaked what little it leaked through the pass-box's seals and the pitch on its seams, and every day Bram put a cartridge in the collar and brought it back up to the line. It had grown a hand in six days.

At the band.

And it had woken in the first place, all those summers, a few grains' worth in an iron case, because the band was the thickest air there was. Thickness woke it. Thickness fed it. That was the whole of what the book said and the whole of what the stone had done. The drinking had begun at two thirds and the stem had come at the band. Everything he could see said that more meant faster.

He knew Armand would say no. He knew what Armand had said at the gauge, with the red lead still wet on the brass: not a grain over it, not because you think more might be better. He knew what that meant. It meant that Armand had read the band at the band and found a number, and that the seedling was growing at that number, and that everything past that number was a guess. He knew all that.

He also knew what a guess looked like when it was the right one. He had made a great many guesses in his life, in two lives, and some of them had been right, and the ones that had been right had looked exactly like this: a thing he could see in front of him that said more, and a careful man at his shoulder who said wait.

On the seventh evening, after the long room, when Armand had gone back across the bridge to the tower and Bram had gone home to his bed at last, he went down the gallery to the collar with a cartridge in his hand, and put it in the collar, and turned it down to the zero, and felt the spike take it and the band come out through Bram's cup into the dome in its soft round breath.

Then he put in another.

The gauge went past the red line in the first breath of the second cartridge and kept going.

He watched it go. A third over the line. Half over. He stood at the gauge with his hand on the drum's stone and his sense in the dome, and felt the dome fill past anything he had ever felt on the ground, past the band, thicker than the band, thick the way the air in the Drop House's bottle was thick with a fresh flask just poured, and he put his sense on the seedling in the middle of it and felt it drink. It drank. It drank faster. He felt the thread of the band going into it through its stem and its leaves, a hard steady pull, like a man drinking who has been thirsty.

He went up to bed. He slept well, for the first time in a week.

In the morning the leaves had curled.

Not all of them. The top three, the newest, the ones highest on the stem. They had curled back on themselves along their length, the way a leaf curls in a drought, their edges drawn under, their undersides showing pale. And along the curled edges, all the way round, there was a colour that had not been there the night before. A thin line of it, like the rim of a coin. Bronze.

The lower leaves were still flat and dark. The two grey half-shells were still held out below them like hands. But the top of the seedling, where it had been growing a leaf a day, had stopped. He could see it had stopped. He did not need the sense. There was a bud at the tip of the stem that had been opening yesterday evening and was not opening now; it sat closed and small and dull at the top of the curled leaves, and he put his sense on it, and it was not drinking. The thread had gone. The whole seedling lay in the thick air of the dome like a stone again, and took nothing from it.

He stood at the pass-box door and looked at it for a long time.

Then he went and got Armand.

Armand did not say anything. That was the worst of it.

He came across the bridge from the tower in the carrier's cart with his instruments on his knees and walked up the palace stair and along the gallery to the pass-box and looked through its doors at the seedling for a long moment, at the curled top leaves and the bronze on their edges, and then he looked at the gauge, where the stone behind its window stood half again above Ferra's red line, and then at the empty cartridge sleeves on the rack by the collar, two of them, and then at Emeric.

He did not say I told you. He did not say anything at all. He set his instruments down on the gallery floor, and took off his spectacles, and folded them, and put them in his coat, and asked for a stand.

"Three heights," he said. "Not one. At the floor, at the height of the plant, and at the crown. As high as you can get a stand into that dome." He looked up at the glass. "Through the box. I'm not going in and neither are you."

Bram built the stands in the morning, three rods on one base, the tallest of them twenty feet long in three pieces that screwed together, and put them through the pass-box piece by piece and set them up inside with his arms in Imani's rubber sleeves, sweating, swearing softly at the sleeves, while Imani stood beside him and told him what to do with his elbows. The shortest rod held its reading stone a hand above the floor, by the edge of the middle bed. The next held one at the height of the seedling's top. The tallest held one up under the crown of the dome, among the struts, where the light came down strongest.

Armand read them through the glass. He had never had to read a stone through glass before and he said so, and did it anyway, with his hand flat on the pass-box's inner pane and his eyes shut. It took him a long time. He wrote three figures in his book, and looked at them, and read the three stones again, and wrote three more, and they were the same.

"It stands in layers," he said.

Emeric waited.

"Like water in a pond on a still day. Warm at the top, cold at the bottom." Armand turned his book so Emeric could see the figures, though Emeric could not read the tower's notation any better than Imani could. "It isn't even. It's thinnest at the floor and it gets thicker as it goes up, and at the crown, up in the glass, it's thick. Much thicker. The gauge is at a man's shoulder, halfway, and it reads the middle." He put his finger on the top figure. "Last night, when you filled it, the crown went to twice the band. Near enough. At the plant's height it was half again. At the floor it was barely over the line."

"The top leaves," said Emeric.

"The top leaves were in half again the band. The bottom ones were at the band." Armand closed the book. "The top leaves curled and the bottom ones didn't. And the bud at the top stopped." He looked through the glass at the seedling. "It wanted the band. Not more. It woke in the band and it grows in the band, and past the band it burns. At the edges. Like a leaf in too much sun." He was quiet for a moment. "I don't know why. I'm not going to guess. I'm going to tell you what the stones say, which is that the plant grew at my line and stopped over it."

Emeric did not say anything for a while.

"The layers," he said at last. "That's your control."

"That's my control." Armand almost smiled. "I've wanted one since the first seed stone. There's never been anywhere in the world to get one. And now I have one in a glass bowl in the middle of your house. The floor's one reading and the crown's another, and the plant's between them, and if the plant does well at one height and badly at another, I know which height it wants, and I didn't have to guess." He put his spectacles back on. "It's the best thing that's happened to my book in forty years. I'd be obliged if you didn't kill the plant before I've finished with it."

They drew the dome down that afternoon, into the stones, until the gauge stood on the red line and not a grain over, and Armand read the three heights again and wrote them down, and they were in their layers still, thin at the floor and thick at the crown, but the plant's height stood at the band.

The rule went into the book that evening, in Ferra's hand, at the long room. It was Armand's rule, but he did not write rules; he wrote readings. Ferra wrote the rule.

The dome is held at the Secretary of the Arcane's line, the band's density at night, measured at the height of the plant. Not above it for any reason. The line moves up as the plant grows, and the Secretary of the Arcane moves it.

What is over the line comes out into empty stones, on the gallery, by the vent. Never into the open air. Never into the palace. The stones it goes into are entered in the book, and filled, and used, and nothing is wasted and nothing is let go.

And under it, in smaller letters, which she did not usually do with a rule: Filled over the line, E., the seventh evening, alone. Top leaves burned. Drawn down.

Emeric read it over her shoulder. He did not ask her to take the last line out. It was true, and it was a line in the book, and it was the kind of line he had spent years teaching a barony to write down about itself: what was done, and by whom, and what came of it. He found that it cost him something to read it in her hand, and he found that he would not have wanted it any other way.

Imani, at the far end of the table, looked at him.

"You told me you'd tell me," she said, "every time it got out."

"It didn't get out. It went into the stones."

"It went somewhere it wasn't meant to go," Imani said. "That's the same thing, to me." She held his eye until he nodded. "Next time, the same hour."

"The same hour."

The seedling did not die.

The three top leaves stayed curled, with their bronze edges, and did not uncurl; he had not expected them to. But on the fourth day at the line the bud at the top of the stem, that had sat closed and dull above them, began to drink again. He felt it at dawn from the gallery, the thread coming back, thin, steady. And on the sixth day it opened, and it was a long narrow dark leaf with a fine pale vein down its middle, flat, perfect, set a little above the burned three in its spiral, and after it another.

He learned to read it. Everybody did. It was a gauge anybody could read, which the brass strip by the pass-box was not. If the leaves stood flat and dark and the newest one was opening, the dome was right. If the top leaves began to curl at their edges, it was over the line, and somebody drew it down. If the leaves drooped, all of them, from the bottom up, it was under, and somebody put in a cartridge. Imani's people learned it in a week. Bram's boy who carried the cartridges learned it in a day.

And at night there was the other thing.

He saw it first on the tenth night at the line. He had gone down to the gallery late, after the house was asleep, to look, as he did most nights now, and the screens were drawn: the tarred canvas on its rail round the inside of the drum's head, hanging down round the whole of the dome inside the glass, so that from the city, from the wall, from anywhere, the dome at night was only a dark shape on the top of the palace against the stars. And inside the screens, with the lamps on the gallery out and the round room dark, he stood at the pass-box door and looked through the glass at the seedling, and it was glowing.

Not much. Not the leaves. The veins. The fine pale vein down the middle of every leaf, and the smaller ones that ran out from it to the edges, were faintly alight in the dark: a thin cold light, blue-white, like frost in moonlight, tracing every vein in every leaf of the plant in lines so fine he could only see them with his eyes wide in the blackness and the rest of the room gone. The stem too, faintly, all the way down to the two grey hands. It was the band, going up through it. He could feel it in the sense at the same moment he saw it with his eyes: the thread coming in through the stem and running out along the veins, and where it ran, it lit.

It was steady. It did not flicker. It glowed all night, the whole plant, in the dark behind the canvas, as faint and fine as a drawing of itself in light.

He stood and looked at it until his feet were cold. Then he went and woke Ferra, because it was the kind of thing that went in the book, and because he wanted, very much, for somebody else to have seen it.

She came down the gallery in her nightgown with a shawl round her and a lamp stone in her hand, and he made her put the lamp stone out, and they stood at the pass-box door in the dark side by side until her eyes came round to it.

"Oh," said Ferra.

She did not write anything down. Not then. She stood with her hand on the glass of the outer door, very still, and looked at the plant glowing in the dark in the middle of Imani's garden, with its seven good leaves and its three burned ones and its two grey hands held up below them, lit from inside along every vein.

After a while she said, "That's the gauge. At night. If it's glowing, it's drinking. If it's not, it's not."

"Yes."

"And if anybody sees it, it's the end of everything."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long time.

"Then I'll want the screens in the book too," she said. "Every night. Who drew them, and when." And then, because she was Ferra, and it was the middle of the night, and she had just seen a thing nobody in the world had seen for a very long time: "It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen."

He did not say anything. He had thought the same thing on the sixth day, and not said it. He put his hand on the glass beside hers, not touching it, and they stood there in the dark and watched his mother's tree drink the band until the sky above the canvas began to go grey.
