Chapter 226 - A Mile Up

Ferra was in the counting room with the lamps lit and ten days of the barony's figures stacked on her table in the order the clerks had put them, which was the wrong order. She did not look up. She had known his step on the stair for nine years.

He put Bryda's letter down on top of the stack.

She read it through once, quickly, as she read everything the first time, to find out what it was; and then again, slowly, with her finger under the figures, to find out whether it was true. At the line the pen had gone through she stopped, and her mouth moved at one corner.

"She's learned," she said. "To tell me first. Two years ago she'd have written to you, and you'd have walked about with it for a day being pleased, and told Bram, and Bram would have told the foundry, and I'd have heard it from a carter." She read the line again. "The crown will want this hill back when it hears."

"Will it?"

"It'll want to. It can't. It licensed that hill in March, by the weight, in Keller's own words; every ton that comes up out of that hole pays the crown its share before it's off the hill." She was already ruling a clean sheet. "So we put a scale at the pithead and a crown's man beside the scale, and we write every ton in front of him, and send him home with a copy every quarter day." She wrote BRYDA at the head of the first column. "A hill that's paid its licence in front of the crown's own eyes is the hardest hill in the realm to take."

He sat on the corner of her table and watched her rule the rest of it; and after a while, without looking up, she put her free hand on his knee, and left it there, and went on writing with the other.



The first table of the duchy sat the next morning at eight, in the long room, at the same table where the barony had sat since Ferra brought it a rent roll and a funeral dress, because nobody had suggested anywhere else and Ferra would not have gone if they had.

There was nothing to mark it. Maren had sent up bread and the second-best butter. The only thing on the table that had not been there in March was a square of card in front of Ferra's place, with a single rose drawn on it in ink inside a ring; and before anybody had sat down she pushed it across to Emeric with one finger.

"For the seal cutter," she said. "Unless you'd rather it said something else."

It was the drawing from the cabin roof, cleaned and inked. It had no crown on it and no letters and nothing else at all.

"Send it," he said.

"And the flag. Gold on black, on the hull, by Friday." She looked down the table at Fletcher, who had been asked to the table for the first time in his life and was sitting at the end of it with his hat on his knee and the face of a man who expected to be asked to build something he would not like. "If the sail loft can spare a needle."

"Ask Abloh," said Fletcher.

"I did. He said ask you."

Fletcher looked at the ceiling. "Friday," he said.

So that was the device of the duchy of Kaldo, and it took about as long as a man takes to butter bread. Emeric found he was glad of it.

"Ward," said Ferra.



Ward did not sit at the table. He never had. He stood at the end of it nearest the door, with his hands behind his back, and when Ferra said his name he looked down the length of it at all of them, one at a time, as he looked down a line before a drill.

"The man with the knife," he said. "He was on the coach road in the north, two Novembers ago. By the back wheel, with the lamp oil burning. The Chancellor wrote him down. Scar through the left eyebrow. A mouth that had been laughing." He did not look at Ferra. "He was in the palace's brown on the night. He had been in it, the captain of the king's guard says, nine days. He came in on a steward's note. The steward does not remember writing the note." He was quiet. "He was paid to kill a duke at the king's table, in the king's hall, before two hundred people, and he walked in on paper. That is not a man. That is the people who hired the man on the coach road. They reached the coach road, and they reached the hall. They will reach here if they want to."

Nobody said anything.

"Saban," said Ward.

"The hire," said Saban. He had his hat on the table in front of him upside down, as he always had it, and he turned it once. "I've a name for the steward's clerk who wrote the note the steward doesn't remember, and an address for the clerk's sister, and a man who drinks with the clerk's sister's husband. It'll take the summer. It'll cost." He looked at Ferra. "It'll cost more than the summer."

"How much more?"

"I'll tell you when I know whether it's worth it." Saban put his hat back the right way up. "If it's the people I think it is, it's worth whatever it costs, and I'd rather not say who I think it is at a table with the windows open."

Ward looked at the windows. Then at Emeric.

"Steel in every coat," said Ward. "Bram has it in hand. Nobody comes to the keep on a note I have not read. That is all."



Bram read the ore figures.

He did it standing, with his spectacles down his nose and Bryda's letter held out at the length of his arm because he would not admit his arms were not long enough any more, in a voice like a man reading out the weights of iron at a market: the face found on the twenty-first of May; the level pumped dry in a fortnight; the samples assayed three times; Fennick's shots by the ounce, at which his mouth twitched; the guess at the bottom, underlined.

And under the table, somewhere about the second assay, a foot found Emeric's.

It came in from his left, where Ferra sat, slowly, in its grey stocking, out of its shoe, and settled against the side of his boot, and stayed there. It did not do anything else. It was simply there, warm through the leather, while her father read out the copper; and Ferra sat beside him with her pencil moving down her own copy of the figures, checking her father against Bryda, her face perfectly composed, and did not look up once.

She had done it at this table four years ago, in the middle of a sentence of his about the coke ovens, and he had lost the sentence. He did not lose this one. He did not say anything at all, which was easier.

"A thousand tons," said Bram, and put the letter down. "She's guessing."

"She says she's guessing," said Ferra, writing.

"She'll have guessed low." Bram took his spectacles off. "Bryda never guessed high in her life. Her husband did, and look at him." He looked at Emeric. "That's copper for every wire Tess can draw for ten years. And the bath. And sheet for the roofs if you want it." He sat down. "And a fight with the crown, if Ferra's wrong."

"I'm not wrong," said Ferra.

The foot did not move.



Then it was his turn, and he stood up, because what he had to say was the kind of thing a man ought to stand up for.

"Most of you know what we found on the way to Emberfell," he said. "Some of you were there." He looked at Imani, who looked back at him with her chin on her hand. "Four miles up there's a layer of the sky thick with mana. A stone opened up there fills while you watch it. Nobody's ever stayed in it, because nobody could get there and stay; the old climbs touched it and came down bleeding." He put his hands flat on the table. "We can get there. And every stone in Kaldo that goes flat, every fire stone in the works and every lamp stone in the lanes and every stone in every band, can be filled up there for nothing, if somebody carries it up and brings it down."

He let that sit.

"Orrin and Vesk have been filling the works' fire stones by hand since the second year. Two mages' whole working lives, spent putting back what the works burns. Up there it's an hour." He looked at Ferra. "Kaldo can have more mana than it can use. More than anybody on the continent. If we can carry the Band down."

"Can we?" said Ferra.

"Not in the ship. She's too valuable and she's needed and she can't carry racks." He turned to Fletcher's end. "Master Fletcher."

Fletcher took his hat off his knee and put it on the table, which was how Emeric knew he had been listening.

"I want an ore lighter," said Emeric. "Iron. Forty feet. Flat deck. One hatch. The ship's channels down her sides, a sealed house aft, and three legs."

Fletcher looked at him.

"An ore lighter," he said.

"Yes."

"With legs."

"For beaching."

"With what in her?"

"Ore," said Emeric.

Fletcher looked at him a while longer with no expression whatever, a man who had built a ship with legs and been told it was a market boat. Then he looked at Bram, and Bram looked back at him over his spectacles and did not help.

"Flat deck, forty feet, one hatch, a house with a seal on it, channels, legs," said Fletcher. "Iron." He picked his hat up again. "I'll want the slip after hull two's out of it. Eight weeks from the keel, if Bram's plate comes when he says."

"It'll come," said Bram.

"It never comes when you say."

"It'll come when I say this time."

Fletcher put his hat on his knee and said nothing else, and Emeric understood that he had just been given an iron hull by a man who would spend the next eight weeks working out what it was for and would never once ask.



Linus had his map rolled under his arm, standing against the wall by the window because he had come in late and would not take a chair from anybody. When Ferra looked at him he unrolled it down the middle of the table over the bread, with Maren's butter under one corner and Bram's spectacles under another.

It was the whole town, inside the wall and out to the moat, at a scale Emeric had never seen it drawn: every house and yard and well and drain, in Linus's fine hard copperplate, every lane named in the capitals that were going up on the corners in white enamel, and the levels in red at every corner to a tenth of a foot. He had surveyed it all spring while Emeric was at court. Emeric looked at the lower ward, which he had counted from the wall every year, and saw it from above for the first time, with the drains drawn in under it like veins; and saw, all at once, where the water stood when it rained, and why the west lanes smelled in August.

He went up to his room and came back with a book he had written himself in his first year, from memory, in the small hand he kept for things nobody else would read: a round town with a garden at its heart and avenues going out from it like spokes, and a ring of rail round its edge, and farms beyond it that nobody might build on. A court stenographer in another life had drawn it, a man called Howard, and spent the rest of his life getting small pieces of it built. Emeric opened it at the drawing and laid it on Linus's map, over the keep.

Linus looked at it for a long breath with his head on one side. Then he took a pencil out of his coat and put its point on the canal ring round the inner ward, and drew slowly round it, once.

"You've the ring already," he said. "And the farms. And one avenue." He looked up. "You want four."

"I want four."

"And wards between them, round gardens. Six." Linus did not ask how Emeric knew what a town round a garden looked like; he had stopped asking things of that kind long ago. "The lower ward stays; the terraces are the best housing in the town." He tapped the west lanes, low against the wall. "This goes first."

"Nobody moves out until the new house is standing," said Ferra.

"No," said Linus. "Nobody."

They talked about it for an hour, the best hour of the morning: where the avenues would cross the canal ring; the market hall and the records hall, which stayed, because Ferra said so; a service tunnel under each avenue, which Bram said was a fortune in brick and Emeric said was cheaper than digging up an avenue every time a pipe split. And somewhere in that, Linus, writing in red in his own margin, said the thing that became the rule.

"Nothing goes in the ground that the plan will have to dig up again."

Ferra wrote it down. "It goes on the records hall wall," she said. "Not this one. A clean one, with the avenues and the wards and the bridges and nothing about the tunnels or the pipes or what goes in which year. The town's map. Anybody can look at it. Nobody gets the rest." It was her rule from the winter, from Wayford: a man who shows the whole of a thing before it works is asking to be argued out of it. He had signed under it. He nodded.

"The town's map," said Linus, and rolled it up, butter and all.

On the counting room's tape that afternoon, among the noon figures and the price of oats at Wayford, was a line from the north gate he had not known was coming: the main open at the arches since the eleventh of May, the pressure holding at the low lanes, the old cistern on standby. Linus and Tess had opened the valve while he was in the capital and told nobody, because there had been nobody in particular to tell. The town had drunk from it for three weeks without noticing. That was the point of a main. Ferra ticked it and went on to the oats.



The others went at noon. Fletcher went to look at the slip and Bram went after him to argue about plate; Linus went with his map and Saban with his hat; Ward went to read the notes at the gate. Armand, who had sat at the end of the table all morning with his stick across his knees and his eyes half shut and said nothing, went last, slowly, and stopped at the door with his hand on the frame, and looked back at them, and seemed about to say something; and did not, and went.

So it was the three of them.

Ferra slid a sheet out from under her book. It had a list down the left side in her small square hand, each word with a line after it: water, works, roads, the school, the hospital, the garrison, the books, the trade, the post, the lanes. Against some she had written a name in pencil, and rubbed it out, and written it again.

"It's a duchy now," she said. "A duchy has offices. People will want to know whose door to knock on. These want somebody at the top of each with a seal and a book and the right to say no to you."

"Ministries," said Emeric, without thinking, and heard it come out of his mouth in a word that had nothing to do with this room.

"What's a ministry?" said Imani.

"It's a church word. Here. Somewhere else it means..." He stopped. "A secretary. A cabinet. A council of..."

"A cabinet is a cupboard," said Ferra. "I'm not sitting in one." She looked at the list. "Every word you've got for it comes from somewhere it was already working. We haven't got it working." She drew a single neat line under the ten words and wrote nothing under it. "Don't name it until it works. Names stick. Call it a thing now and it fails, and everybody remembers the name."

"The Polytechnic's got a name," said Imani.

"The Polytechnic works." Ferra had written that one too, this month: the school's apprentice trades, dyers and glassmen and founders and Tess's line men, made into one thing with its own workshops and a works floor and a room for testing, because they had been running in pieces since Commencement and the pieces had stopped being able to find each other. "That's why it's got a name."

Imani looked at the list. "Mine says hospital."

"Yours is the only one that's working already. Which is why it's first, and why it's yours." Ferra folded the sheet into her book, and looked at Emeric, and her mouth was not quite straight. "You've been looking at the window since ten."

"Have I?"

"At the river. At the shed." She did not look at Imani. Imani did not look at her. "You've got something."

"He's got something," said Imani to the ceiling.

He stood up and picked up his coat. At the door he stopped, as Armand had, and looked back at the two of them at the long table in the noon light, both looking at him with exactly the same expression, which was patience running out.

"Bring a coat," he said. "Tonight. A warm one."

And went out, and heard, as the door shut, Ferra open her book again.



Ward cleared the shed at nine.

Emeric had not asked him to. He had told Ward at two that he meant to take the ship out that night with nobody aboard but himself and the two of them, and Ward had said, "Out where," and Emeric had said, "Over the sea. Up. Not far," and Ward had nodded once and gone away; and at nine the yard was empty, the night watch had been moved to the far gate, and the great doors stood open on the lake.

Ward was inside, in the sealed hull at the stone-keeper's station, with a lamp, reading the dials. He went down them one at a time with his finger, the matrix and the reserve and the main and the channels, as he had watched Fletcher do a hundred times and had never been asked to do himself; and then turned the lamp down, and came along the deck to the gangway.

He stopped in front of Emeric.

He did not say anything. He looked at him, and past him, at the river path, where two lamps were coming down from the keep, close together; and then back.

Then he went down the gangway and across the yard and out at the little door in the gate, and shut it behind him, and Emeric heard the bar go down on the other side.



She went out across the lake under the slip lamp's last light, with the steady humming in its box under the bow cockpit and the trim keys clicking by themselves, little dry clicks like a clock in another room, and her lift levers at the third notch; and at the lake's far end, where the reeds stood up black against the water, he took her up.

He flew her from the top cockpit, alone, with the two of them on the step of the sealed stair below him in their coats. Nothing aboard her decided anything; the steady kept her level and the levers held her height and every stone did exactly what he had written into it with his own hand, a stone at a time, over a year. But he had written every one of them, and he could feel them. His sense was out round the whole of her, thin, as it always was, and every stone came back to him small and bright and drawing, each exactly where he had put it, taking the charge it should and not a hair more. He did not need to look at a dial.

They went out over the marsh and the river mouth with the lamps out and the moon not up, low, and then over the sea, and the sea was black and wrinkled under them with no light on it anywhere; and a mile out he put the main to rest and the levers up a notch, and another, and she went up.

The cloud was at about three thousand feet. They went into it and it closed round the glass of the cockpit grey and wet and featureless and stayed there, so that there was nothing to see at all and the only thing that said they were climbing was the dial and the small pressure in the ears, and Imani on the stair below him said, "Oh," and swallowed. Then the grey thinned, and was silver, and was gone.

They came up out of it into the stars.

The cloud lay below them in every direction to the end of the world, flat and white and faintly shining, as if the sea had frozen into a field of snow; and over it the sky was black and clear and so full of stars that the dark between them looked thin. There was no sound. The main was at rest and the matrix only held, which made no sound, and there was no wind, because she was going with it.

He took her up to about a mile. Well under the Band. He set the levers at the notch that held her there, and the steady took her and she stopped climbing, and hung, and the trim keys clicked once, twice, and were quiet.

Then he came down the stair.



He had cleared the saloon himself that afternoon, while Fletcher and Bram were at the slip arguing about plate: the long table unbolted and carried forward in two pieces, the chairs stacked in the galley. Under the after water tank, low in the hull, he had put a fire stone, set low, in an iron pocket, so that the whole floor was warm to the hand, as a hearthstone is warm in the morning; and on the warm boards he had laid every mattress and quilt from the four cabins, edge to edge, wall to wall. And he had opened the irises, four each side, round brass irises with a small thick pane behind each; and the saloon was full of starlight, cold and white, lying across the quilts in eight pale circles. There was a lamp on its hook. He did not light it.

The two of them stood in the saloon door in their coats and looked at it.

Ferra said nothing at all for a long breath. Then she took her book out of her coat pocket, where he had known it would be, and opened it at a page near the back, and held it up to the starlight from the nearest port so that she could read it. It was a column. He had seen the top of it, once, on the first voyage, upside down: WHAT HE IS PLANNING. There were four lines under the heading. Three were struck through; one had a question mark after it.

She took her pencil out, and under the four, in the starlight, she wrote a fifth.

"The ship," she said. "She said it at Emberfell. I wouldn't write it down. I said it was too obvious." She ruled a line under it, and closed the column, and closed the book. "It was too obvious. I'll never forgive either of you."

"I said it on the bed at Emberfell," said Imani, "after midnight, half asleep. It counts."

"It counts," said Ferra, and put the book down on the floor by the door, very carefully, square to the boards, and took off her coat.



There was supper, in the cold store off the galley, where Maren had put it that afternoon with no questions asked and a paper on the cloth in her round hand that said FOR THREE: cold chicken, and bread, and a crock of the pickled onions Imani liked and Ferra did not, and a bottle of the Emberfell wine Rowena had sent. They ate it on the quilts in the starlight with their coats over their knees, because the floor was warm and the air was not; and Ferra ate half the bread and none of the onions and drank a third of a cup of the wine and did not pick the cup up again.

"I've a list," she said.

Imani put down her chicken.

"I wrote it at Emberfell," said Ferra. "After the bath house. When we'd agreed." She was not looking at either of them; she was looking at the cup. "Things I wanted to ask. Things I didn't know. I've been adding to it since." She took a folded paper out of the bodice of her dress, where it had evidently been all evening, warm. "It's long."

"How long?" said Emeric.

"Two sides." She unfolded it. Her hands were not quite steady, and she saw that they were not, and held the paper tighter, and it did not help. "I thought if I had it written down I'd know what to do. I always know what to do if it's written down." She looked at the first line. "I don't know what to do."

Imani reached across the quilt and took the paper out of her hand, gently, and folded it up again without looking at it, and put it in Ferra's lap.

"Nobody knows what to do," she said. "I didn't. He didn't. You find out." She put her hand over Ferra's on the folded list, and left it there. "That's the whole of what I know about it that's any use to you. You find out. And you say. Whatever it is. You say it."

Ferra looked down at Imani's hand on hers, and then up at Imani; and something passed between them that Emeric was not part of and was not meant to be, and was glad of: the look of two people who had sat up one night at Emberfell with the lamp out and decided a thing together, and had kept it.

"You say it," said Ferra.

"Out loud."

"I'm good at saying things out loud."

"You're the best in the barony," said Imani. "Duchy." She let go of Ferra's hand. "Start with your boots."

Ferra laughed. It came out of her short and surprised, like a cough, and then again, longer; and she put her face in her hands and laughed into them, and Imani started laughing too, at her; and Emeric sat on the quilts and watched the two of them laugh in the starlight a mile over the sea and thought that he had never in his life been so frightened and so happy at once.

Then Ferra took her hands away from her face. Her eyes were wet from laughing. She looked at him.

"Come here," she said.



Ferra had double-knotted her boots, because she always did, and the knots had pulled tight in the cold; and in the end Emeric knelt on the quilts and did them for her, with his head bent, and her hand came down on the back of his neck where the lace had rubbed it raw a week ago, and rested there, very lightly.

"It's better," he said.

"She used the jar," said Ferra. "Every night. I watched."

"I used the jar," said Imani, behind him. She was undoing her own buttons, quickly, as she did everything with buttons. "He forgot again on Saturday."

He got the second boot off and stayed where he was, kneeling, with her stockinged foot in his hand, the foot that had been against his boot under the table that morning; and looked up at her; and her face in the starlight was quite white, and quite set, and her mouth was trembling at one corner.

"Say," he said.

"I'm frightened," said Ferra. "I'm frightened and I want to and I don't know which is louder." She let out a breath. "There. That's said."

"Which is louder now?"

She thought about it. She actually thought about it, with her head on one side, as she thought about a figure.

"Want," she said. "By a little."

"Then we'll go by a little," he said, and set her foot down on the warm quilt, and kept his hand round it, and felt her whole leg jump anyway.

Her dress buttoned up the back. She could not reach the buttons and never could; Imani did them down for her, from behind, quick and plain, the way she would have undone a patient's gown, while Ferra stood in the starlight with her arms crossed tight over her front and her eyes on Emeric; and when the last button went, Imani put her two hands on Ferra's bare shoulders for a breath, from behind, the way a second puts her hands on a fighter's shoulders before the bell, and said something in her ear that Emeric did not hear, and let go, and stepped back.

Ferra uncrossed her arms.

The dress went. She stepped out of it, and out of the shift under it, and stood on the quilts in the cold white light from the port with nothing on at all and her chin up, as she had stood in the cabin of the lift hull in the black and the roses, and let him look; and he looked; and the colour came up into her face and down her throat and over the freckles on her shoulders, which went on, he saw, a great deal further than he had known, and she did not look away from him once.

"Well," she said. Her voice shook. "That's done. That was on the list."

"What was?"

"Whether I'd be able to." She swallowed. "I am."

He got up off his knees and took her face in his hands and kissed her, and she put her arms round his neck and held on, hard, with her whole cold length against him through his shirt; and he felt her shaking, all through, not with cold; and then he felt Imani at his back, her hands at his shirt, pulling it up and over his head from behind so that he was blind in it, and Ferra laughed into his mouth, and the shirt went somewhere into the dark of the saloon and was not seen again until morning.

It was warm on the quilts. They lay down in a heap, the three of them, in the pale circles of starlight, and for a long while there was nothing to it but that: skin and the warm floor and the cold air over them, and hands, and mouths, and nobody in any hurry. Imani was on his left and Ferra on his right and he was between them, and that was how it went, mostly, and it was not complicated at all, which surprised him; it was only kind, and slow, and very new to all three of them, and every so often somebody laughed. Ferra's hands were cold and she apologised for them and he said he did not mind and she put one flat on his chest to prove he did, and he did, and she laughed at him. Imani found a place under his ribs that made him flinch, and used it without mercy, and he caught her wrist, and she let him, and kissed him, and turned her head and looked across him at Ferra, who was watching them with her lips parted and her eyes very wide.

"It's all right," said Imani to her. "Look as much as you like. He's used to it."

"I'm not used to it," said Emeric.

"You're getting used to it," said Imani, and slid her hand down his side, and he stopped being able to argue with her about anything.

Ferra watched. Then, after a while, slowly, she did what Imani had done: put her own hand where Imani's was, beside it, and watched his face while she did, and saw what it did, and her eyes went wider still.

"Oh," she said. "That's not on the list either."

"Put it on," said Imani.

"I'll put it at the top."



He lifted them when Ferra was ready.

She told him she was. She said it clearly, the way she said a figure, holding his face in both hands so that he had to look at her: "Now. I'm ready. Now." And he raised his hand from the quilt with the glyph coming up pale on the back of it, and lifted the three of them off the floor, one at a time.

Imani first, because she knew it. She came up off the quilt hair first and laughed and caught his forearm. Then Ferra, who had been lifted before, to the beams of her own room and into the top of an apple tree, but always right way up, with a floor under her or a branch to hold; and who came up now off the warm floor into the cold air of the saloon and kept coming, and did not come down, and turned, slowly, end over end, with her hair coming loose round her and her eyes enormous, in a pale ring of starlight from the port; and opened her mouth, and laughed.

She laughed until she was breathless with it, turning in the air with her arms out and nothing under her at all, a mile over the sea; and he took his own weight last, and came up after her, and the price of it settled under his breastbone, two holds and his own and a draught of air to turn them, a steady draw like the last two miles of a long climb, and he found he did not care at all.

"Your list," said Imani.

It had been under her knee on the quilt when the hold took her, and it had come up with her, and slipped away from her in the air, and was going up past them now toward the saloon's ceiling, turning slowly, two sides of small square writing, unfolding as it went, until it caught on the lamp's hook and hung there.

Ferra watched it go. She did not reach for it.

"Leave it," she said. "I know what's on it."



He had told himself that he would be careful and slow, and he was, because he could not have been anything else; but it was Ferra who set the pace, and she set it as she set the pace of everything, by deciding, and then by saying.

She said a great deal. She said "There," and "Not yet," and "Again," and once, sharply, "No, that one," and once, in a voice that was not her voice at all, nothing he could make out. Imani was beside her the whole time, on the other side of him, in the air, close, with one arm across his chest and her other hand somewhere he could not keep track of; and when Ferra's hand came out blindly across him, looking for something to hold, Imani's was there, and took it, and held on.

When it came to it, Ferra stopped.

She stopped all at once, with her breath held and her fingers locked through Imani's across his chest, and her face changed; and he stopped with her, at once, everything, and eased the hold until she was only floating beside him, free, with nothing keeping her there but her own hand in his.

"That hurt," said Ferra.

She said it with enormous indignation, the voice she kept for a clerk who had carried a figure wrong.

"I know," he said.

"You don't know. You can't know." She was breathing quickly. "Nobody wrote that down. I read everything. Nobody wrote down that it would..."

"I wrote it down," said Imani, close, at her ear. "In January. You read it. You initialled it."

Ferra turned her head and looked at her.

"You said stings," she said.

"It stings."

"It does not sting. It hurts. Those are different words. I'll not have the wrong word in the book."

And Imani laughed out loud, in the air, with her forehead against Ferra's shoulder and her hand still locked in Ferra's, and after a breath Ferra laughed too, unwillingly, crossly, with tears standing in her eyes that did not fall but came off her lashes in the starlight and drifted, small and bright, past Emeric's face; and while she was laughing the thing that hurt went out of her face, a little, and then more, and she breathed out, long, all of it, and looked at him.

"Wait," she said.

He waited. He would have waited all night. She held Imani's hand and breathed, slowly, in the air, and he held as still as a man can hold who is holding three people off a floor; and the glyph on the back of his hand lit the three of them, and the list turned against the ceiling over their heads.

"Stings," said Ferra, at last, as if conceding a point in a long argument. "It stings now. I'll allow stings." She moved, a very little, and her breath caught, and this time it was not because it hurt. "Oh," she said. "All right. I'll allow that too."

After that she did not say anything that was a word for a good while.



The hold wobbled when she kissed him. It always had. It wobbled now, and he lost the height of them by a foot all at once, and the three of them came down out of the air onto the warm quilts in a heap with a thump that rattled the irises in their rims; and Ferra, underneath, said "Ow," and then laughed so hard she could not breathe, and Imani lay across both of them with her face in the quilt and shook, and Emeric lay on his back between them looking up at the list, which had stayed exactly where it was, on the lamp's hook, turning gently, and did not come down.

"Again," said Ferra, when she could speak.

So he took them up again.

It was not quick, any of it, and it was not graceful, and none of the three of them knew what they were doing, and all three found out. Imani found out what she wanted and said so, with the plainness she used for a wound. Ferra found out that she did not need the list, and said so, and then said a good deal else. He found out that a man can be wanted from two sides at once and that it is not a thing to be managed but a thing to be grateful for, over and over, slowly, in the air under the stars with the warm floor somewhere below, until he could not tell which way the floor was and did not care. Imani had two fingers at his wrist once, out of habit, and he felt her start to count; and then he felt her take them away, on purpose, and put her whole hand flat on his chest instead.

"Not tonight," she said into his shoulder. "I'm not counting tonight."

When it came to Ferra at last, he felt it before she did. He felt it in her note, which he had known for nine years across a counting room table as quick and exact and bright, and which went, all at once, out of order: every figure in it at once, everywhere, like a ledger thrown in the air. She said his name, with Imani's hand gripped in hers hard enough to hurt. Then she said nothing at all, and the glyph went white across the whole saloon, and the stars went out behind their own reflections in the eight round panes, and the list against the ceiling lit up in it, every word, two sides of small square writing; and nobody read it.



Afterwards they lay on the quilts in the dark with the irises open and the starlight gone grey.

Nobody had slept. Nobody had wanted to. Ferra lay with her head on his left shoulder and Imani with hers on his right, and the quilts pulled up over the three of them against the cold, and their two hands met on his chest in the middle and stayed there, Ferra's and Imani's, the fingers laced, quite casually, as if they had always done it.

"Is that it?" said Ferra.

"Is what it?"

"The bigger occasion." She did not lift her head. "I said mine would be. In January. In front of Ottoline."

"Was it?"

Ferra considered it. He felt her consider it, against his shoulder.

"It was much bigger," she said. "I'd underestimated it by a good deal." She was quiet. "I'm never wrong by that much."

Imani laughed into his other shoulder, without opening her eyes.

The dawn came up under the cloud: a grey at first, in the east, and then a long line of pale gold along the cloud's rim, and then the whole field of it lit pink from one horizon to the other, as if somebody had turned up a lamp behind a sheet. The stars went out one at a time. The light came in at the ports and lay across the quilts in eight pink circles that moved, very slowly, as the ship turned on the wind.

The list came down at last when the ship turned on the wind and the lamp swung on its hook. It came down slowly through the pink light, turning, and landed on Ferra's bare shoulder, and stayed there.

She picked it up without looking at it, and folded it in half, and in half again, and held it in her fist against his chest with Imani's fingers laced over hers.

"I'm keeping that," she said.



They had drifted east all night on the wind, a long way. He brought her home in the grey before sunrise, low and fast along the coast with the sea under her, and then low over the marsh with the reeds going by under her keel and the herons getting up out of them in long grey strings, and set her down on the lake at the shed's end as quietly as he had ever set anything down, and walked her in on the channels.

The doors were open. Somebody had opened them. The yard was empty, and the little door in the gate was barred, and there was nobody anywhere at all.

They put the saloon back as it was, which took the three of them an hour. Imani found his shirt in the galley, over the cold store's door. Ferra polished the long table with her own sleeve, because there was a ring on it from a cup, and then went on polishing it after the ring was gone, all the way down, because she had started.

Fletcher came in at seven to look at the slip, and came aboard, because he always came aboard, and went through her from bow to stern as he did every morning, with his hands behind his back, looking at nothing in particular; and stopped in the saloon door.

He looked at the long table for a long breath. It shone. It shone the whole length of it, as it had not shone since the day it was fitted, with the morning coming in at the ports and lying on it like water.

"Who's been polishing the saloon?" said Fletcher, to nobody in particular.

Nobody was there to answer him. He stood a while longer. Then he went forward to look at the bow cockpit, and did not come back to it, and never asked again.



Emeric meant to tell Bram that day.

He had decided it on the walk up from the shed, and he said so at the top of the keep stair, outside the counting room door, before Ferra could go in.

Ferra stopped with her hand on the door.

"You'd tell him," she said.

"He's my friend."

"He's my father." She turned round. "Different column."

"He knows we're courting. He stood up at the table and said your mother and sat down. He's not a fool, Ferra. He'll know."

"He knows we're courting," said Ferra. "He doesn't know the rest. And he's not going to." She looked at him with her chin up and her face perfectly composed and pink, a woman who had not slept at all and did not look it. "Not from you. Not from me. Not from anybody."

"Why?"

She was quiet. He watched her consider whether to tell him the reason, and decide on a different one; he saw her do it, and let her.

"Because it's fun," said Ferra.

He looked at her.

"It's the first thing in my life I've ever kept off the books," she said. "I've written down every figure I've ever had, and every one I was told and every one I worked out for myself, and every time I was wrong, in the column, in ink. This one I'm not writing down." Something came into her face that he had seen there perhaps twice. "And my father taught me to count in his forge when I was four, and I have never once in my life been able to keep a thing from him that he wanted to know. I'd like to see if I can." Her mouth moved. "I think I can."

It was not all of it. He could see that it was not. There was something under it that she had decided he did not need yet, and he thought he could guess at the shape of it, a question her father would ask once, kindly, that she had no answer to, about a clause in her own book; and he let her keep it, because she wanted to.

"Rules," said Ferra.

"Rules."

"No notes. Not to me, not to her, not under any door, not in any book." She counted them off on her fingers, exactly, as she counted rents. "Nothing in writing anywhere. And you come down after the second bell. Never before. My father's in the keep till the first bell most nights, with the drawings, and he goes home by the river stair, and he looks up when he goes." She folded her hand. "After the second bell he's in his bed and the stair's dark and the night watch is on the far gate."

"You've worked this out."

"I worked it out at Emberfell," said Ferra, and opened the counting room door. "On the stair. With a lamp."

She went in. He heard her say good morning to the clerk.



Imani was at breakfast in the kitchen, at the end of Maren's long table, with a cup of tea and a plate of eggs and her bag on the bench beside her, because she was due at the hospital at eight; and she looked up when he came in, and looked at his face, and went back to her eggs.

"She's said no," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Because you've the face of a man who's been told no by Ferra and liked it." Imani ate a piece of egg. "And because she told me on the path this morning that you would, and that she would, and what she'd say. Second bell?"

"Second bell."

"She told me that too." Imani put her fork down and looked at him across the table, and her face was quite serious, and quite kind, and quite immovable. "I'll tell you what I told her. I'll not lie to anybody's father. If Bram asks me a straight question I'll give him a straight answer." She picked her fork up again. "He won't ask me. Bram never asks anybody anything he doesn't want to know the answer to. But if he does, I'll not be asked to lie."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's ridiculous. She's twenty-nine and you're a duke and the whole thing's ridiculous." Imani finished her eggs. "But she's enjoying it. She's never enjoyed anything she wasn't working at in her life." She stood up and picked up her bag. "So I'll not spoil it."

She went past him to the door, and stopped there, and came back the two steps, and put two fingers on the side of his neck, under the jaw, where she had taken them away in the night.

"Sixty-four," she said. "Good. Go and sleep for two hours, you look like a man who flew a ship all night." Her mouth moved. "Second bell," she said, and went.
