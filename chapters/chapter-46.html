Chapter 46 - The Other Ways

Vantic came back from her house on the nineteenth of February with a flat wooden case she had not taken away with her, and put it on the long table before she took her coat off, which was not like her.

"Two things and then I would like a fire," she said. "The first is that my house has read your quarter and is content, and I am obliged to tell you that the word content is doing a great deal of work and is the warmest word my house owns."

"And the second."

"The second is that I sat in a room in the capital for four days in the first week of February and heard this barony's name six times, and every one of them was in a sentence about price." She got the coat off. "Not about the war. Not about the hill. Price."

Saban came up the western road on the seventh of April with the whole of it, and he had been six weeks on the coast, and he laid it out in order because that was the arrangement.

Eleven quotations. Grain, iron stock, and the surplus of the foundry's ordinary work, which was the three things Kaldo had to sell. He read them out and Ferra wrote them down and the room got quieter as he went, because by about the fifth of them everybody in it could hear the shape.

"Nine under," Saban said, when he had finished. "Call it nine in the hundred, against what the same houses were quoting a Milford man in the same fortnight for the same stuff off the same water. The lowest is eleven under and the highest is six, and there is not one of the eleven that is out on its own far enough that you could stand up in a room and call it anything."

"Say what it is."

"It is a price," Saban said. "That is the whole of what I can tell you and I want to be careful here, because you will want it to be a plot and I have watched you want things before." He put the sheets down. "There is no letter. There is no man. Nobody has to have met anybody. One large house on that coast decides what a Kaldo price is, and the second house hears the number from a clerk and does not undercut it because why would he, and the third house hears it from the second, and by the fourth nobody remembers where it started and every man of them will tell you honestly that it is simply what Kaldo goods fetch. That is not a conspiracy. It is a fashion. It is much harder to break than a conspiracy and there is nobody to be angry at."

"Somebody started it."

"Aye. And you will not find him, and if you found him he would be a factor in a counting house who set a number in October because a man he plays cards with mentioned that the Kaldo hill had had a war on it and might be desperate, and he would be genuinely bewildered to hear that he had done anything at all." Saban shrugged. "And he would not be lying."

Emeric was quiet for a moment, and then asked the question he had learned to ask before the other one.

"What does it cost."

Ferra had it by the afternoon and she did not soften it.

"Nine in the hundred off everything we sell is not nine in the hundred off the barony," she said. "It is nine off the top of the only part that is free. Seed does not care about the price. The reserve does not care. Debt service does not care and Milford's grain does not care. All of it comes off the surplus, which is the last line and the thin one, and nine off the whole take is somewhere between a third and two fifths off that line, and I will say a third because I would rather be pleasantly wrong."

"Say it as years."

"Seven becomes nine and a half." She said it evenly. "Nine and a half if nothing else happens, and something else always happens, and I have never in eleven years written a number in this book that got smaller on its own."

"And if we sell into it?"

"Then we have agreed with them about what Kaldo is worth, in writing, eleven times, and next spring the number is not nine, it is twelve, because a price that has been accepted once is not a guess any more, it is a record." Ferra ruled the line. "And if we do not sell into it we are sitting on grain and iron in a barony that has to pay in October."

"So it works either way."

"It works either way. That is what makes it good."

He went at it for eleven days and got nothing worth having.

He tried the obvious thing first, which was to find another coast, and Saban had already done that in the sixth week and produced Velmoor, which was further, and thinner, and would take about a fifth of what Kaldo had to sell, and would take it at a price that was better than nine under and worse than standing, and would take four months to arrange.

"Take it," Emeric said.

"I've taken it. It is worth having and it does not solve it and I will not let you leave this room thinking it does."

He tried the second thing, which was to be worth more rather than cheaper, because the one honest advantage Kaldo had was that its iron was better than anything on that coast and everybody who had bought a bar of it knew so. That ran into a wall in about a day and a half, and the wall was that a barony which cannot commence a new undertaking cannot open a second furnace, cannot build the drawing shop that would let it sell finished work instead of stock, and cannot put up so much as a shed to do it in.

He got as far as writing the first paragraph of a letter to Vantic before he stopped and read it back and recognised the shape of it, which was a man asking a question he had already been answered.

He put the letter in the fire, and then took it out again, half caught, and put it in the deferred pages instead with the date on it, because a thing you have burned is a thing you can pretend you never wrote.

Ward said the version that hurt on the eighteenth, on the wall, in the rain, which was where he said most things.

"You keep calling it after the war," he said.

"It is after the war."

"It's after the fighting. That's not the same and you know it's not the same, you've a page in your book about the difference." Ward had his back to the parapet and was watching the mill road the way he had watched it for two years. "Two hundred and fifty men came off a hill in August because somebody worked out what Kaldo cost to take. Then they went home and told him. Whatever else that man is, he's not a fool, and a man who's not a fool does not try the same door twice."

"So he tried the windows."

"He tried what there was. Price. Carriage. Credit on a hill with no salt. Whether a house in the capital answers your letters." Ward wiped the rain off his face with the back of his wrist. "It's the same war, and it costs him nothing, and there's nobody standing in a field for you to shoot at, and that's the bit I'd been dreading since about October and I've not said so because I'd nothing useful to put after it."

"Say the useful part now."

"There isn't one. That's why I've not said it." He pushed off the parapet. "I can hold a wall. I've held one. I can't hold a price."

The last thing happened at the end of the month and was not about the coast at all, and Emeric did not understand for another season why it was the only useful thing that came out of April.

He had been going back through the year's correspondence with Ferra to see what could be leaned on, and he had asked her for the letters out to the capital houses, and she had brought the file, and he had gone through it and asked the question with no weight on it whatever.

"How many of these got answered?"

Ferra stopped with her hand on the next bundle.

"I do not know," she said.

"Guess."

"I will not guess and that is not modesty, it is that I have no basis to guess from and I have just this second understood that." She sat down. "I have every letter that came in, filed by the day it came, and I have a copy of every letter that went out, filed by the day it went. I have never once put those two files next to each other, because there has never been a reason to, because a letter that is not answered is not an event. It is a silence. Silences do not get entered."

"Is that ordinary?"

"It is universal. There is not a house in this kingdom that keeps a book of the letters it did not receive." She was already reaching for paper. "I am going to start keeping one."

"What'll you put in it?"

"Every letter out, the day it went, by whose hand it went, and whether an answer came and on what day, and where there is no answer I will leave the line open and it will sit there open being ugly." Ferra ruled the first column and looked at it. "It will be worth nothing for a year. I want that said now, by me, so that you do not come to me in August and ask me what it has told us. It will tell us nothing until it has enough in it to have a shape, and if it never has a shape then I have wasted an hour a week and we will have learned that the roads are the roads."

"Start it in the ordinary book."

"No." She said it without looking up. "It goes in a book of its own, and the book stays in the strongbox with the venture pages, and I will tell you why, because you will ask. If it is in the ordinary book then anybody with a reason to be in the ordinary book has seen it, and if it does have a shape one day, the one thing I would want is that nobody knew I had been looking."

Nine in the hundred, eleven houses, and Saban says there is no man and I have spent eleven days looking for him anyway. It is a fashion, not a plot, and a fashion cannot be arrested, argued with, bought off, or shot off a horse at two hundred paces, and I have caught myself three times this month reaching for the one of those I am actually good at.

Seven years is nine and a half. Ferra says a number in that book has never once got smaller on its own, and she said it in the same voice she used in November to tell me the line had run the other way, and I do not think she noticed she used the same voice, and I noticed and did not say.

Sell into it and we have agreed with them, in writing, eleven times, about what Kaldo is worth. Refuse and we are sitting on grain in a barony that pays in October. It works either way. Whoever set that number in a counting house in October did not design that and does not know he did it, and it is still better designed than anything I have built this year.

Ward says he can hold a wall and cannot hold a price, and he has been carrying that since October and did not say it because he had nothing to put after it, and I want it written down that he apologised, more or less, for bringing me a true thing without a remedy attached. Two years and he still thinks the true thing is only worth having if it comes with the other half.

I wrote the first paragraph of a letter to Vantic asking for the drawing shop and stopped at the paragraph and burned it and then took it out of the fire. It is in the deferred pages, dated, half burned, and it can stay there looking like that.

Ferra has started a book of silences. Every letter out, by whose hand, and whether an answer came, and where it did not the line stays open. It will be worth nothing for a year and she told me that first, unprompted, before I could be pleased about it. Nobody in this kingdom keeps a record of the letters they did not get. There has never been a reason to.
