Chapter 46 - The Still

The trouble with the surplus was that it was grain.

It lay on the granary floor, turned morning and evening, sweet and dry, and there was nowhere in the world to send it. Wayford grew its own. Milford grew more than its own. The coast bought grain, but the only way to the coast was down the silted river in the salt boatman's skiff, a few sacks at a time, and his fares would have eaten most of a cartload before it got there, and what was left at the other end would not have bought a pair of boots.

"Wheat keeps a year in a good granary," Ferra said. "Two, if the rats don't learn the stones. After that it's pig feed." She had the granary's page open. "It's worth what it saves us buying in a bad year. Which is a great deal. Next year."

"And this year?"

"This year it's worth what somebody will pay for it within a day's cart." She turned the page round. "Which is nothing, because everybody within a day's cart has the same harvest."

He had been thinking about it since the reeve's tallies came down the hill, and he had been thinking about it in the wrong direction, he decided, sitting at her table. He had been trying to make the grain lighter to carry. The answer was to make it smaller.

"A barrel of spirit," he said, "is a cartload of grain, with the water taken out and most of the rest of it left behind. It doesn't spoil. It doesn't feed rats. A boatman can take a barrel of it down to the coast and bring back something worth more than his fare." He paused. "And the ward needs it."

Ferra looked up.

"For what?"

"For hands. For knives. For the edges of a wound." He had wanted it since the first morning he stood at the side door of the ward and watched Imani wash her hands in soap and boiled water and then go straight to a cut with them, and known what was still on them. "Strong enough, it kills what washing leaves. You can't boil a hand. And you can carry it in a bottle to a farm."

Ferra wrote something in the margin of the granary page. He could have read it upside down. He did not try.



The still wanted copper, and so did everything else.

Emeric had drawn the dynamo in August, on four sheets, at night, in his own room with the shutters closed: the thing that would take the turning of Harl's wheel and make current out of it, more current than twelve jars or a hundred jars could make, steadily, all night, for as long as the river ran. It was the reason for everything he had done since January, if he was honest, and he had been honest about it with nobody but the four sheets. The four sheets wanted copper. They wanted it in quantities that made Ferra, when he finally showed them to her, put her pen down.

And now the still wanted it too.

They had it out in the forge, on a wet afternoon in the first week of October, with the rain coming in under the door and Tess on the bellows: Emeric and Bram and Ferra, who had come down from the kitchen with the copper page because the copper page was what the argument was about.

There was not much on the copper page. The Grayrock bars from last winter, less what had gone into the jars, less what had gone into the wire, less the fifth they had skimmed off as slag. A little over four bars, Ferra said, and the next lot from Grayrock not yet asked for, and the next lot from Grayrock going to be a fifth over at least, if it came.

"A still wants copper where the spirit touches it," Emeric said. "The head, where the steam comes off. And the worm, where it cools."

"Why copper?" said Bram. "Iron holds water. Iron holds steam. I've an iron pot that'd take a calf."

"Iron gives the spirit a taste. And the steam carries a stink off the wash that copper takes out of it and iron doesn't." He could have said more about why, and decided not to, because Bram did not need why. "The pot can be iron. Your pot. Copper only where it must be."

"And how much is where it must be?"

Emeric told him. Bram looked at Ferra. Ferra looked at the page.

"That's one bar," she said, "and a bit of another. Out of four." She did not look at Emeric. "And your four sheets want forty."

"The sheets can wait."

"You've been waiting on that since January," Bram said, which was true, and which Emeric had not known Bram knew.

"Then they can wait till Grayrock answers," Emeric said. "The still pays for itself by spring. The sheets pay for nothing till the whole of them's built." He looked at Ferra. "That's a sum. You do it."

She did it, on the edge of the copper page, while the rain came in under the door. It came out his way, and she said so, and wrote the still's bar and a bit across the bottom of the page in a line of its own, and beside it drew an empty ring for the forty bars the four sheets wanted.



Bram made the pot out of the calf pot, cut down and riveted, with a lid like a shallow bowl turned over and a hole in the top of it the size of a fist.

The head and the worm were copper, and copper was the slow part.

The head was a beaten dome, the shape of a helmet, with a pipe coming off the top of it and bending down. Bram raised it out of a sheet of copper beaten flat from half a bar, on a stake in the forge, over three days, tapping round and round in rings from the middle outward while the sheet rose under the hammer into a bowl and then a dome, and Tess watched every blow and did not ask a single question until the third day, when she asked why he never hit the same place twice running, and Bram told her, and she went quiet the way she did.

The worm was a pipe, a long one, coiled down inside a tub of cold water so that the steam from the head went round and round in the cold and came out at the bottom as liquid. A pipe that long had to be made out of strip. Bram cut the strip, and rolled it round an iron rod as thick as a thumb, length by length, so that its edges met along the top in a seam. Then the seam had to be closed, along every inch of every length, so that it would hold steam, and that was the part nobody in Kaldo could do.

Brazing wanted a small, hot, steady flame that could be held on one place along a seam until the brass melted into it, and then moved along exactly as fast as the brass would follow, and no faster. A forge fire was too big. A candle was too small. A blowpipe, a jeweler's pipe for blowing a lamp flame into a point, would do for a ring. On copper this thick the heat ran off into the metal faster than a man's breath could put it in.

So Emeric did it.

He did it at the bench in the forge, in daylight, with the door open and the lane looking in, because there was no reason not to: it was fire, and the whole town had heard about the dragon he made out of a laundry hearth. He drew a thread of flame off the forge, no thicker than a straw, and narrowed it to a point, and laid it on the seam where Bram had packed it with brass filings and flux, and held it there until the brass ran, bright, like a bead of water along a crack, and then moved it on. A hand's length. Then another. The glyph sat in the air above his fingers the whole time, small and steady, and the children who had come to the door to watch counted out loud how many hand's lengths he did before he had to stop, and argued about it.

It was not hard. It was only long, and it never stopped, and by the end of the first afternoon there was a pain behind his eyes that he knew well by now, and his hand had begun to shake a little when he lifted it off the seam, which he did not let anybody see.

It took four afternoons. The worm, when it was done, was as long as the forge was wide, and Bram filled it with dry sand, stopped both ends, coiled it round a log in the yard, and then pulled the log out and shook the sand out of it, and set the coil in a cut-down barrel, and filled the barrel from the well, and poured a bucket of boiling water in at the top of the worm, and stood at the bottom with a cup.

The water came out the bottom into the cup, warm. Not a drop came out anywhere else.

Bram looked at the cup, and at the worm, and at the seams, every inch of them closed in a fine bright line of brass.

"Hm," he said.

The nights were easier, those weeks. He was still drawing copper wire in the dyehouse after dark, with the sacking over the shutters and Tess on her stool, a little of the copper that was left, for the day the four sheets had their forty bars: a length here and there, through the small plate, by the same pull as in July. It no longer brought his nose to bleeding. Three lengths had been a night's work in July. In October he could draw five and walk home.

Tess wrote it down. She wrote everything down. She had stopped showing him the page.



The casks came from the cooperage.

It had been shut since the cooper and both his sons died, and its hoops had gone into the plows a year ago, and nobody had thought it would open again, least of all the man who opened it, who had been the cooper's journeyman for six years before the plague and a carter for four since. He came to the keep at the start of October, having heard that the prince was going to want barrels, and asked whether the barony would let him have the shop, and Hale looked up the lease, and the barony would.

He had no iron for hoops. Bram had none to spare. So he went back to what his master's master had used: hazel, split and shaved and bent green round the staves and nailed, rows of them close together up the barrel's sides, pale and springy, the way casks had been bound in Kaldo before anybody there could afford iron. He brought the first one up the lane on his shoulder to show Bram, and Bram turned the barrel round with his foot, looking at the wooden hoops, and then said, "It'll hold," and the journeyman went red to the ears.



The first run came off the still on the third Saturday in October.

The inn had brewed it for them, the mash and the ferment, in its own brewhouse with its own woman, who had brewed the inn's ale for twenty years and did not want to be told anything and was not told anything. The wash came up the lane in her barrels, sour and yeasty, and went into Bram's pot, and the pot went on the fire in the forge yard, and the head went on the pot, and the worm stood in its barrel of cold water at the end of the head's pipe, and everybody stood and looked at the bottom of the worm.

It took most of the morning. Then a thin clear thread began to run out of the end of the worm into the jar Emeric had set under it, and the yard smelled suddenly and powerfully of something that was not quite bread and not quite anything else.

The first of it he threw away, a cupful, because the first of any run was poison, and said so, and made Bram and Tess and the inn's woman all watch him pour it into the ditch, so that nobody would come back for it in the night. The rest he kept. That was weak, the first time through, and he ran it again in the afternoon, the weak spirit through the pot a second time, and what came out of the worm at dusk was alcohol strong enough that a drop of it on the back of the hand went cold and vanished in the time it took to look at it.

He took the first jar of it down to the ward himself, after dark.



Imani was still there. She was always still there. She was at the desk by the side door with the register open and a lamp beside it, her hair still tied up from the day, and two of the beds were full, and one of her women was asleep in a chair by the CATCHING room with her head on her chest.

She came to the step, and looked at the jar, and said yes before he had asked.

He showed her how to use it as an antiseptic. A little in a dish. Her hands in it, the whole of both hands, between the fingers, under the nails, and then shaken dry in the air, not wiped. The edges of a wound, before and after, not into it. A blade, a needle, the inside of a bowl. She listened to all of it with her head on one side and then did it herself, there at the desk, with her own hands, slowly, feeling it go cold on her skin and vanish, and looking at her hands afterward as though she expected them to be a different color.

She did his next, without asking. She tipped a little into his palm and worked it over his knuckles and between his fingers, a good deal faster than she had done her own, and let go. It found a split by his thumbnail.

"It stings in the cracks," she said.

"It's meant to."

"Does it work?"

"It works better than anything else I can give you. Not as well as I'd like."

She nodded. It was the only honest answer, and she had plainly been waiting to see whether he would give it. She put the jar on the shelf beside the soap, where the most important things went, and stood looking at it there.

Then she took two of the little horn cups she used for measuring doses down off the shelf, and set them on the desk, and poured a finger of the spirit into each.

"If I'm to put it on people," she said, "I want to know what it is."

"It's alcohol. It's not for drinking."

"Nor's half of what I give people." She picked up her cup. "Go on."

He picked up his. They drank.

It was dreadful. It was thin and fierce and hot all the way down and tasted of the forge yard and the inn's sour wash and very faintly of the brass in the worm's seams, and it went down into his chest and sat there burning like a coal. Imani's face, across the desk, did something he had never seen it do before. It closed up, all of it, eyes and mouth and nose, as though somebody had pulled a string at the back of her head.

She put the cup down. She breathed out, slowly, through her teeth.

"That," she said, "is for hands."

"I did say."

"You did." She looked at the cup, where there was still a little left in the bottom. Then she picked it up again and drank the rest, and put it down, and looked at him, daring him to say anything.

"Waste," she said.

The woman by the CATCHING room snored once and woke up and looked round, and Imani told her to go home, and she went. One of the patients asked for water. Imani took it to him. Emeric washed the two horn cups in the corner and dried them and put them back on the shelf, and she let him, and did not tell him that was not where they went, though it was not.

When he left, she said from the desk, without looking up from the register, "For hands," in exactly the voice she had used the first time, and he went up the hill and was still smiling when he reached the gate.
