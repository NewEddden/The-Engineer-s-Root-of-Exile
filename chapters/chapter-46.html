Chapter 46 - Light

The first test melt came out of the crucible the colour of water, and the four people standing around it said nothing at all for a count of ten.

It was not clear. It was nearly clear, with a faint green in it that showed only edge-on, where the lump was thickest, and that was more than anybody in Randillia had managed out of a furnace in living memory. Bram held it up to the open door. Colm held it up to the open door. Ferra looked at it for a long moment and then wrote down the weight of the sand, the time in the crucible, and the words nearly colourless, and underlined nearly, because she did not believe in giving anybody a word they had not earned.

It had been made in the third furnace. Bram had said, three years before, that a town like Kaldo would never need a third furnace, and he had been right for three years.

He did not mention it. Neither did Emeric.

That was the first of the four, and it was the only one the sea did for them.

The second was the colour that was left.

Emeric knew there was a thing that took it out. In another life it had been a black mineral with a name he could not use here, and the men who made glass had called it their soap, because it washed the green away. He knew what it looked like. He knew roughly what it weighed in the hand and that it blackened the fingers. He did not know where in Randillia anybody had ever found it, or whether anybody had, and he could not go and look, because he did not know how to look for it in this ground.

So he gave it to Orrin and Vesk, as a real problem, with everything he knew and a plain statement of everything he did not.

He expected Orrin to talk about it and Vesk to write about it, and both of those things happened. What he had not expected was the method. Orrin went up to the diggings and talked to every miner who would stand still, which was all of them, because Orrin could make a man stand still. Vesk went up with him and took samples. Between them, in four days, they had a list of every kind of stone the barony's own pits had thrown away in living memory and why. On the ninth day they were running small melts in the third furnace, one spoil at a time, with a pinch of the sea sand and the same weight of each, and writing the results in two different hands in the same book.

The twelfth spoil was a black, heavy, crumbling stuff the miners called soot-stone and had been throwing on the heap for as long as there had been a heap, because it would not smelt to anything a smith could use and it ruined a man's hands for a day.

It took the green out.

Orrin carried the lump into the small room himself and put it on the table in front of Emeric, and talked for a quarter of an hour, loudly, about how they had found it, and did not once say that it had been Emeric's idea. Vesk stood by the door and said nothing at all. But Emeric saw his face when Orrin set the lump down, and it was not the face of a man who had been paid a wage. It was the face of a man who had just, for the first time in a long and indifferent life, solved a problem for a place rather than about it.

That landed on both of them harder than the wage had. Emeric could see it land and said nothing, because anything he said would have made it smaller.

The third was the heat, and the heat was not what anybody thought.

Every glassmaker in Randillia assumed that the difficulty was getting a furnace hot enough. It was not. Kaldo's furnaces had been hot enough for glass since the day they were lit. The difficulty was getting a furnace to stay where it was. The seed in Randillian glass was air that had never had time to rise out of the melt, and the only thing that gave it time was holding the melt at one steady heat for most of a day. Nobody could do that. Fuel burned unevenly. A stoker tired. A draught shifted. The heat wandered up and down all day, and the glassmaker worked it when he lost patience, and the seed stayed in.

Kaldo had the gauge.

A splinter of crystal set in the hearth wall, straining against the heat, and a reading taken off how hard it strained. Emeric had sketched it in his second autumn and Bram had built it, and it had been reading the foundry's furnaces ever since. What it had never done was hold one. That needed fire stones in the hearth, fed and kept fed, and fed by somebody who could watch a splinter of crystal for six hours and pour into the stones exactly as much as the splinter said and not a breath more.

Orrin and Vesk stood the shifts. Six hours each, turn and turn about, on a stool by the gauge, pouring mana into the fire stones by the hour. Orrin talked the whole way through his and Vesk said nothing the whole way through his, and the heat held flat as a table for most of a day, and the melt that came out of it had no seed in it at all.

Orrin came off his second shift grey in the face and sat on the yard wall and said, to nobody in particular, that it was the pan. The stones gave the heat away to the furnace, and the furnace gave it away to the room, and a man at the gauge was only ever pouring in what had gone out, the way you top up water in a leaking bucket, except that it was not water and never had been. Then he said he would teach it that way on Monday, and went to sleep sitting up.

The fourth was the one that mattered most, and Emeric said it in one sentence to Bram at the bench, and Bram wrote it on the wall with a burnt stick and left it there for the rest of his working life.

Randillia can make heat. It cannot make slow.

Flat glass cracked because it was cooled in whatever time the room took. A pane holding that much sudden cold inside itself would split on a frosty night with nobody near it, and every glazier in the kingdom lost a share of his panes that way and called it bad luck. What a pane needed was a whole day of falling heat, brought down on purpose, a little at a time. It was the held-heat trick run backward. And that was the one thing a set of fire stones and a gauge were genuinely good at.

So Bram built a second chamber off the third furnace, a long low oven with its own stones and its own splinter, and the panes went into it hot and came out a day and a night later, cool, and did not crack.

The chemistry took a fortnight. Emeric had expected that part to be the hard part, and it was not.

The hard part was a pair of hands.

Nobody in Kaldo blew glass. Nobody in Kaldo had ever blown glass. Colm tried it, because he was the nearest thing available, because he understood heat and clay and the way a soft thing holds a shape, and because nobody could stop him once he had seen the first clear melt.

The first eighty attempts were rubbish.

Blobs. Tears. Globes that sagged and folded. Panes with a wave in them like water on a windy day, and one that came off the pipe and landed on the floor of the foundry and stayed there, and was left there by general agreement as a warning. Colm burned both hands. He swore more fluently than anybody in the barony had heard him swear, and he had been known for it. He came in before light and went home after dark and came in again.

Emeric had nothing to contribute to any of it, and said so.

He could describe the method. A globe gathered on the pipe and blown. The pipe swapped for an iron rod stuck to the far side of it. The globe opened and spun, fast, until it flattened out under its own weight into a disc as wide as a man's arm is long. He knew all of that the way he knew most things from the other life, as a picture in a book. He did not know how it felt in the wrists, and nothing he could say would put it there. After the second week he stopped coming to watch, because a man who cannot help and stands in the doorway anyway is not helping.

It took Colm two months.

It took two months because there was no other way for it to take less, and at the end of them he could spin a disc. Not well, at first. Then less badly. Then, on a morning in midwinter, properly, and he did not say anything, and cut it, and brought the rounds out into the yard in a basket, and set them down, and went back in to spin another.

They were crown panes. Rounds cut from the spun disc, leaded into frames, each with a thick knot of glass at its centre where the rod had been. The bullseye. It was a flaw. It was the part of the disc that could not be made thin, and every glazier who had ever made crown glass had cut around it where he could.

Everybody in Kaldo decided within a day that it was the best part.

And it turned out, which nobody but Emeric thought about, that crown glass was the one trade in the world made for Colm. Colm had never in his life been able to make two of anything the same. He had hated the hoop gauge for four days on exactly that account. Every crown disc came off the rod a little different from the last, and every bullseye was its own shape, and there was not a single glazier anywhere who expected otherwise.

The frames were Bram's, and Bram gave the lead to the craft track. Four of the older children cut the cames and soldered them at a bench in the foundry yard, under protest from nobody, and got the angles wrong for a week and then did not. Every window in the school had a child's work holding the glass in it, and every one of those children could tell you which.

The school got its windows.

Emeric told Ferra the other thing in the small room, at night, with the last pane counted.

"You know what this is," he said.

"Glass."

"It's the one thing I've built that stops when the magic stops." He put his hand flat on the table. "Every one of those panes exists because a set of stones held a heat, and then let it fall, and two men sat on a stool for six hours at a time keeping them fed. There is no plain version. There is no version on charcoal. Take the stones and the mages away and Kaldo has no glass at all."

Ferra waited. She had learned when he was not finished.

"I have spent three years refusing to build anything like that," he said. "The wheel turns if every crystal in the barony goes dead. The foundry bellows run off the water, not off a stone. I did it that way on purpose and I would do it that way again. I'm breaking it here. Once. Knowing that I'm breaking it. For one building full of children."

"And the works," she said. Not a question. She had seen where the sentence was going before he got there.

"No works. Not like this." He looked at the ledger rather than at her. "A glassworks that only runs on stones and mages is an industry with a switch on it, and a stranger's hand near the switch. Whoever controls the mages controls the glass. Whoever sells the stones sets the price of every window. I'm not putting Kaldo's living on that."

"Then when."

"The day there's a furnace that can hold a heat and let it fall on charcoal and a good stack, with no stone in it anywhere. That day I build the works. Until then, this is one building, and it stays one building."

Ferra wrote it down.

She wrote it the way she wrote all of them. In the back of the ledger, where she had been keeping his stated rules for three years, because she had learned that the ones he said out loud were the ones worth having on paper. The date. The rule. The condition. She turned the book round so he could read it, and he read it, and it said what he had said.

He had just committed himself, in writing, in advance, to building an industry he had not yet worked out how to build. He was aware of that. He did not ask her to cross it out.

The last pane went in on a still morning, and he got the two mages into the finished room before anybody else was let in.

He did not thank them. He had thought about it and decided against it. A man thanks a hired hand.

"You built this," he said.

Orrin opened his mouth.

"The colour is yours," Emeric said. "You found the stone. The ore was yours. The hours at the gauge were yours. Every child who reads in this room is going to do it by light that the two of you made." He looked up at the windows. "So you have a stake in it. A stake isn't a contract. I can't hold you to it and neither can anybody else. It's worth a good deal more than one."

Orrin was loudly and entirely overcome. He said so. He said a great deal else, and wiped his face on his sleeve without any embarrassment, and shook Emeric's hand twice, and went out to find Colm and tell him, and could be heard telling him from the street.

Vesk said nothing at all.

He stood in the middle of the floor with the light coming down on him, and when Emeric left he was still standing there, and when the others came in an hour later he was still there, off to one side by the wall, looking up. He was the last to leave that day.

That night he opened the notebook, as he did every night. For three months every entry in it had been about the prince. Things that did not add. Things that were done without anybody seeing them practised. The notebook was a list of anomalies and it had been getting longer.

The entry he wrote that night was not about the prince. It was one line, and it was about himself.

It said that he was staying.

The room was everything Emeric had wanted it to be, and several things he had not thought of.

The high windows ran nearly clear the length of the upper wall. The bullseyes caught the low winter sun and threw bright rings down onto the floorboards, and the rings moved. Over the course of a morning they crossed the whole room, slowly, from the west wall to the benches, and the children watched them go instead of watching Orrin, and Orrin, after a day of fighting it, started timing his lessons by where the rings had got to.

The first full day was short and loud.

Orrin talked too much. He was teaching numbers, which was not his subject, because Ferra had been held up at the yard, and he was teaching them with enormous enthusiasm and a slate, and somewhere in a long sum he carried a figure he should not have carried.

Ferra came in at exactly that moment.

"No," she said, from the door. "Nine and seven is sixteen. The one goes over. Write it again."

Orrin wrote it again. The children watched him do it with deep interest.

And Ferra did not stop there. She came down the room and explained the carry to him, at the slate, in front of the children, in the voice she used for the children, all the way through, twice.

Imani came in late off a birth, with her sleeves still wet, and sat on the end of a bench and was asked by a small boy whether the baby had lived, and said yes, and was asked why, and told him. Doran sat in the corner by the crate of books with his hands folded and watched a barony teach its children in a building his church had not paid for, and did not mind in the least, and was seen by several people to be enjoying himself.

Ferra knew she had taken it too far. Emeric could see her know it, at the slate, about halfway through the second explanation. She finished anyway. Then she turned and caught him watching from the back, and the look she gave him was the one that meant she was not going to apologise, to Orrin or to anybody.

He thought about that look that evening, while he was working through the foundry's tallies. He noticed he was thinking about it, and went back to the tallies.

At noon Hollis came to the door to collect his youngest. He stood in the doorway with his bad arm against his side and looked up at the windows for longer than collecting a child takes, and then took her hand and went, and said nothing to anybody.

Emeric stood at the back of the room through the afternoon and did not speak.

He was not thinking about glass.

He had promised this on a set of steps in front of most of the town, on a morning when he needed them to believe him, and he had spent three years knowing that a promise without a date is the easiest lie a man can tell. It costs nothing on the day. It never falls due. It can be carried forever and it never once has to be paid.

He had paid it. He was standing in it. Children were reading under his windows by a light two prisoners had made.

And he knew exactly why. It had taken a burned field and a lost year and a payroll with nothing to spend itself on, and if the harvest had come in that autumn, he would almost certainly still be promising it.
