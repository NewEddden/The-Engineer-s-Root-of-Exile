Chapter 21 - The Yield

The ring closed on a Tuesday, without ceremony, because Emeric had learned.

They knocked out the last plug of clay between the western feeder and the return, and the water went round the whole thirteen miles for the first time, and it took most of the afternoon to do it because water is not in a hurry. Men walked along beside it. Wren walked beside it for the first two miles and then had to be carried back, having refused to stop.

Halloway walked the whole loop. All thirteen miles of it, from the intake to the low gates and back up the far side, and he came in after dark with his boots ruined and stood in the long room and said one thing.

"Division five's slow at the corner."

"How slow?"

"Half a hand of standing water at the head and none at the tail after four hours."

Emeric got the boards out and they found it in about twenty minutes, which was the jog in the ring where it went round Old Pell's nephew's forty acres, and the jog put two extra corners in a run that had been drawn straight, and every corner cost fall.

"Can you fix it?" Halloway said.

"Not without going through his barley."

"Then don't."

"I wasn't going to." Emeric marked it on the board, the two corners and the loss, in the same hand he used for everything. "I'll deepen the last three hundred paces and steepen the tail and we'll get most of it back. It'll want doing again in five years when it silts. That's the price of a man being allowed to say no, and I'd rather pay it every five years than not have a man who could."

Halloway looked at the board for a while.

"He'd not thank you for saying that."

"He'd not believe it either. It's still true."

Ward asked his question three days after the water went round, and he put it badly on purpose, which Emeric had begun to recognise as a method.

"There's three grey robes in Wayford's inn on any given month," he said. "Hedge men, not tower men. They'll not throw a wall down and I'd not have you frightened of them. What they'll do is stand behind a line and make the last forty paces of a charge cost more than it ought to."

"And what do I do about that."

"You tell me. You're the only man in this barony who's ever stood next to one on purpose." Ward put his gloves on the table. "I've watched you cast in a yard. I know what it costs you and how long it takes. What I do not know, and what nobody in this garrison knows, is how far a man in armour gets across open ground while it's happening, and whether the answer is far enough."

The prototype had been standing in the lower ward since the thaw and nobody had found a use for it that was not a guess.

It was the first harness off Bram's bench, plain, unfinished at the edges, made to be argued with rather than worn, with Emeric's sealed housing bolted into the breast where the crown's armourers would have put a bracket. The difference was not subtle. A bracket let a stone feed a suit the way a stream feeds a mill, at whatever rate the stream ran. A sealed housing let the suit ask and let the coupling answer, and what that meant on a man was that Rylan in the prototype could do for a quarter of an hour what Rylan out of it could do for about four seconds.

They had run him in it for six weeks and everybody in the lower ward had stopped finding it funny by the third.

The drill was simple and Emeric wrote it out the night before so that nobody could adjust it afterward. A hundred paces of graded approach with nothing on it. Emeric at the top with his back to the gate, Rylan at the whitewashed stone at the bottom, and three castings in order on Ferra's count, at nothing: a slab of stone raised a hand's breadth and dropped, a blade of moving air across the line of the run, and the slab again. He would cast at the ground and at the air beside the run and never at the man, and he said so twice, once to Ward and once to Rylan, because he had learned that a thing said to one man is a thing said to nobody.

"And I'm to reach you," Rylan said.

"Before the third. That's the whole of what I want to know. If you can't, we build the wall differently and I stand somewhere else."

The first run took him to eleven paces on the third casting. The second took him to nine paces on the second, and the whole ward made a noise, and Ward told them to be quiet in a voice that suggested he had made some of it. On the third he reached the top before the second casting was finished, and Emeric felt a hundred and ninety pounds of steel arrive beside him at the speed of a horse and stop dead without skidding.

"Again," Rylan said. "I've been running it wrong."

"You've run it better each time."

"I've run it straight each time. A man on that rise can see me the whole way and there's nothing on this ground to be behind." He was not out of breath, which was the part Emeric thought about later and not at the time. "Let me come off the line and up the left where the joint is, and don't hold the count for me."

Ferra counted. Emeric raised the slab and dropped it.

Rylan came off the line at forty paces and took the ground under the joint at an angle that put him outside where a man would look, and he was closer at the second casting than he had been at the end of the third run, and Emeric saw him coming up the blind side of his own shoulder and understood, with about a second to think about it, that the drill had stopped being about the mage.

He raised the slab for the third casting with Rylan at nine paces.

There was no sound to it. That was the thing everybody said afterward and nobody improved on. No crack, no flash, nothing that behaved the way a broken thing behaves. Between one stride and the next the most dangerous object on that approach simply stopped being one, and what was crossing the ground was a guard of eleven years carrying a suit of steel on his own legs at a speed he had been running as though the steel were not there.

He went down at four paces, hard, on his hands, and slid.

Then he got up and finished it. He came the last four paces in dead armour and put his shoulder into Emeric's chest and knocked him flat on the graded approach in front of the whole garrison, and had a hand in his collar and him half off the ground before he registered that nobody was calling the run.

"It's gone off," Rylan said. "My lord. It's gone heavy. I've no idea what I've done."

Emeric lay on the gravel and looked at the sky for a moment.

"You haven't done anything," he said. "Get off me and don't move that suit until Bram's looked at it."

Bram had the shroud off the housing inside the hour and then stopped touching it, and lifted the stone out with a rag rather than his fingers, which he had never done in front of Emeric before.

"It's flat," he said. "That's the first thing and it's not the bad thing. A stone goes flat and a stone comes back, that's what a stone does, and if we'd a wheel under load and a charging floor I'd have it back by Thursday and we'd all go home." He held it up in the doorway light. "Look at it sideways. Not at the colour."

It was not cracked. There was nothing in it a man could point at and call damage. What there was, running through the middle of it where the light came across rather than through, was a change in the grain of the thing, the way a bar that has been worked cold looks different from a bar that has not.

"I'll not tell you what happened to it, because I don't know and neither do you, and Armand's book's got forty years in it and hasn't got this." Bram set it down on the rag. "What I'll tell you is I've dressed stone and worked iron for thirty years, and there's a look a thing has when it's empty and there's a look it has when it's been pulled about, and that's been pulled about."

It sat on a shelf in the smithy for eleven days and was exactly as flat at the end of them, and it was still on that shelf in August.

Emeric got it wrong twice before he got it. The first version was that the suit had been overdriven, and it was wrong because the third run had been harder on it than the fourth and had cost nothing. The second was that the fall had done it, and it was wrong because forty men had watched the suit die before the fall.

The third came at about eleven at night, with the two facts on the sheet in front of him that he had been keeping in separate columns all week, which were that the suit had died at nine paces from a man raising a slab of stone, and that everything he had ever done with a stone drank out of the air the same way.

He made himself say it to Bram and Ward in daylight before he let himself believe it.

"There's only so much of it in a piece of ground at a given moment. That's the whole of it, I've no proof, and I'd like that on the record." He put his hand on the drawing. "I put a working up at nine paces from a housing that was live and drawing, and for about a second and a half there were two things pulling out of the same air, and the air went thin between us."

"And the suit lost."

"The suit didn't lose. That's the part that cost us." Emeric turned the sheet round. "The housing doesn't know how to lose. The air goes thin and the suit still wants what it wanted, so it doesn't take less, it pulls harder, and the only place left to pull from is the stone. It stood there for a second and a half wringing the one thing in it that can't be replaced, doing exactly what I drew it to do, because I drew a thing that asks and never drew a thing that stops asking."

Ward was quiet for a moment. "Then it's on the mage being close."

"It's on there being no floor in the coupling. Anything could thin that air. Two suits at a gate. A grey robe on the other side doing whatever a grey robe does. I don't know the list and I'm not going to know the list, and the fix isn't knowing the list." He drew it badly in the corner of the sheet and left it there. "A catch that watches the air round the housing and throws the coupling open by itself when it drops past a line. Then the suit goes dead and the man's standing in good steel holding a stone that's still worth something."

"And the man's dead in the meantime."

"Sometimes."

"Aye," Ward said. "Then say the other half of your own sentence."

"A small failure that happens sometimes instead of a total one that happens once."

Bram would not build it and said so on the spot and gave three reasons and all three were good, and the argument went nowhere, because there was nothing yet to protect and a ring to run and a crop coming up. The drawing went in the box and stayed there.

Rylan came up to the long room that evening on his own, out of harness, and stood inside the door in the way of a man who has decided the whole of what he is going to say before knocking.

"What's it worth," he said.

"There isn't a price, and that's not me being noble about it. The crown doesn't sell them and Saban can't buy what nobody puts on a cart." Emeric pushed a chair out and Rylan did not take it. "So it isn't worth money. It's worth one of the ones there are."

"Then I'd like it taken off my pay."

"It can't be taken off your pay."

"I know it can't. I've done the sums on the way up here and I'd have to live about four hundred years." Rylan was looking at the wall over his shoulder. "I'd still like it entered that I offered, and entered in her book and not in yours, and I'd not have you tell me it isn't necessary, because I'll be stood in the lower ward tomorrow with thirty-nine men who watched me break a thing this barony can't make."

So it went in Ferra's book, one line, with the date on it.

"There's a thing I'll ask and then I'll go," Rylan said, at the door. "If I go over on that wall in whatever you build next. What happens at my house."

"Nessa keeps the house and the ground and the share, and the barony pays her what you were being paid until Finn is grown, and it doesn't stop if I stop being lord, and it isn't a kindness, and nobody has to come and ask for it."

"Is that written?"

Emeric opened his mouth to say yes and found that it was not.

"It will be by Friday," he said.

"Aye," Rylan said. "That'll do."

What happened over the following six weeks was the thing Emeric had been promising for nine months, and it happened quietly, and it happened in a way he had not braced for, which was that it was visible from the road.

The crop came up.

It came up watered, on ground that had been fed, in nine divisions with three of them resting under clover, and by the middle of summer there was standing grain across two thousand acres of a floodplain that had grown a thin patchy crop on tired soil within living memory of everyone looking at it. It was not subtle. A man could stand on the rise where the coalition would eventually stand and see the whole of it at once, dark green going gold, laid out in squares with water running between them.

Halloway's own hundred and forty acres, the thirty-year slide, the dots on the board, came up in a way that made him stop talking for most of a day.

He came to find Emeric about it eventually, in the evening, and did not sit down.

"I've been doing it wrong for thirty years," he said.

"You've been doing it the way you were taught, without water, with half your hands dead, on ground nobody had fed since your grandfather."

"Don't." Halloway said it without heat. "You'll want to give me that and I'd rather you didn't, because I'll take it, and then in ten years I'll be telling some young man that it wasn't my fault, and he'll be standing in a field that's sliding." He put his hands on the back of the chair. "It was the clover. I'd heard of clover. My father had heard of clover. We didn't do it because it takes a field out for a year and we couldn't spare a field, and we couldn't spare a field because the ground was tired, and the ground was tired because we'd not done the clover." He shook his head slowly. "Thirty years I was inside that and never once saw the shape of it. You put the dots up on a board and I saw it in about four minutes and I've been angry about those four minutes ever since."

"That's not stupidity. That's what being inside a thing does."

"Aye. I know." He straightened up. "Two of the young men have asked me why we don't put clover in the resting division twice round instead of once. I've told them to work it out and come back to me with the yields, because that's what you'd have done, and I'd rather they got it off a board than off my mouth."

Then he went home, and Emeric sat there for a while with an entirely unfamiliar feeling that he eventually identified as the specific pleasure of a thing continuing without him.

He got the recruitment wrong again, for the third time, and this time he had the decency to have expected it.

He had a costing. It was a good costing. It offered a settlement bounty, a year's remission on the barony's fifth, a cottage roof at the barony's cost, and a written share of work parts from the first season, and Ferra had checked it twice and it was affordable for nineteen families and no more. He had it read out at the ford, and posted at Wayford's inn by a carter who owed Saban a favour, and he had Rylan carry a copy down the capital road.

Two families came on it. One of them left again inside a month.

Twenty-six families came on Nessa's letter and the eleven other letters that followed it, written by nine different women and two men in the town who had somebody somewhere, and the letters said almost nothing that was in Emeric's costing.

Imani, who had organised the letters and had not asked his permission to do it, explained why on a hot afternoon while re-splinting a digger's finger.

"Yours says the barony will remit a fifth for a year," she said. "Hers says the water's clean and her own two drink it. Yours says a cottage roof at the barony's cost. Hers says I've a bed for the four of you as long as you need it."

"Mine's worth more."

"Mine's a person." She tied off the splint. "You keep making the mistake of thinking a man moves his children for a better offer. He moves them because somebody he already believes tells him it'll be all right, and then he checks the offer afterward to see if he can afford to do what he's already decided."

Nessa's brother arrived in the fifth week with two children, a cart, a wife with a bad cough, and no metal at all.

Emeric met him because Nessa asked him to and because it would have been cowardly not to, and it went badly for about four minutes and then stopped going badly, because the man could not read and had come anyway on a letter read to him by his sister's own hand in a room in Wayford where he had made her read it three times.

"She said you give a fifth," he said. "Where I was, I kept a fifth."

"That's right."

"And it's written where a man can have it read to him."

"Candle shop. Fifth page after the weir. Any of about forty people in this town will read it to you and about half of them will tell you what they think of it while they're doing it."

The man nodded slowly. Then he said, "I'll want to hear it from three of them. Different ones."

"That's the correct number," Emeric said, and meant it, and went away and wrote in the list: he asked for three readings from three different people. Nobody taught him that either.

By the end of the summer the town was over eighteen hundred, up from a low he did not like to think about, and there were four hundred hands on the field where there had been two hundred and forty, and Ferra's staffing column had gone from a wound to a shortfall, which was the first time in nine months that a number had improved by enough to change what she called it.

It was, by every measure he owned, the best six weeks of his life.

He noticed that at the time, which was unusual for him, and he noticed it standing on the rise on a still evening with the whole floodplain gold in front of him and the ring running silver between the divisions, and he let himself have it for about a quarter of an hour before the other thing arrived.

The other thing arrived because Ward came up the rise and stood next to him and looked at the same view and said:

"Where's it going to go?"

"The grain?"

"All of it. Come the cutting." Ward nodded down at the town. "There's the one granary. Same one that's always been there, by the mill, because that's where granaries go."

Emeric was quiet.

"Say the number," Ward said.

"All of it. Seed for next spring, the winter reserve, the venture's grain until Saban moves it, and the field's harvest until the parts are cut and carried out to thirty-seven houses." He did the arithmetic he had been not doing for a month. "Which means for about five weeks between the cutting and the carting, everything this barony is, is under one roof, in a building with a thatch on it, forty paces from the mill."

"Aye."

"I know. I've known since the spring."

"I know you have." Ward did not sound accusing. He very rarely did, which was what made him effective. "You've a rule about it. You said it to me in the long room in front of your own boards. One of everything and nothing behind it is how this barony fails at everything, and you were right, and you built a ring with two ways round it and a furnace that works with the magic dead and a foundry with a parapet on it because I asked."

"And one granary."

"And one granary."

Emeric stood there and made himself say the whole of it out loud, because saying it was the only way he had ever been sure he had actually understood a thing rather than merely feeling bad about it.

"A second store is stone, a floor, a roof, and forty paces of distance, and it costs about what the second water wheel costs, and I haven't got either." He rubbed his eyes. "And I've spent every coin the barony had on a foundry that isn't lit, and the venture's grain doesn't exist until the cutting, and Milford's third went into the shaft in the spring. If I build a second store now I build it instead of the wheel, and without the wheel the furnace runs at half and the venture pays nothing and nineteen households who trusted me lose a quarter of their harvest."

"That's the reason," Ward said. "It's a good reason. I've heard you give it about six times this year about six different things."

"It's a real reason."

"They're all real. That's what I keep telling you and you keep hearing it as a complaint." Ward turned to look at him. "You've been deferring for nine months and every single one of them was right on the day you decided it, and you've never once gone back and counted how many you're carrying."

"Nine," Emeric said. "The second wheel. The flywheel. The deep working at the mine. Pipe instead of ditch on the eastern runs. The second granary. Roofing the western quarter. A proper drain on the low gates. The mill wheel bearing. And the wall running further than we can hold, which isn't deferred, it's just wrong and I did it on purpose."

Ward said nothing for a moment.

"You had that ready," he said.

"I have it ready every night. It's the last thing I look at." Emeric put his hands in his pockets. "I'm not deferring them because I've forgotten them. I'm deferring them because there is precisely one thing in this barony that turns into money, and it's standing in that field, and until it's cut and carted and sold I am a man with a great deal of nearly-finished. Six weeks. Everything I've put off gets built out of what comes off that ground in the next six weeks."

"And if it doesn't come off."

"Then none of the nine mattered and I'll have picked the wrong nine anyway."

They stood on the rise a while longer. The light went off the water in the ring, division by division, west to east.

"I'll put a man on the granary at night," Ward said eventually. "Two, come the cutting."

"Out of what? You've forty men and a wall you've told me twice is too long."

"Out of the wall." Ward started back down the rise. "You'll not like which stretch."

Emeric went down to the field the next morning and walked the resting division, which was under clover and full of bees and would be grain next year, and stood in the middle of nine divisions of a farm that was working exactly as designed, and did the thing he had promised Ferra he would do and wrote the good news down as well.

Ring closed. Water in all nine. Four hundred hands where there were two hundred and forty. Halloway teaching two young men off a board instead of off his own mouth. Eighteen hundred people. The best six weeks I have had in either life.

And underneath it, because he had also promised, and because the list was the only place he did not lie:

A stone off a drill field in the spring, wrung dry in a second and a half on a morning I designed, with a spell I raised while looking straight at the man wearing it. I have built eleven things in this barony with a limit in them and I drew a housing that asks for whatever the man wants and never put a floor in it, because I was thinking about the suit the entire time and never once about the stone as a thing that could be spent.

He asked what happens to his house if he goes over the wall and I had the answer straight away, in full, without thinking, and then he asked whether it was written and it was not. Eleven months I have known that man. It occurred to him on a walk up a hill on the day he cost me a stone.

Everything the barony has will sit under one thatch for five weeks and I have chosen a water wheel over a second store, on purpose, with the reasoning written out, and if that goes wrong it will not have been bad luck.
