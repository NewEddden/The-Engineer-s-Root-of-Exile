Chapter 21 - Six Days South

They were out of the outer ring before the lamps went out. Nobody stopped them and nobody watched them go.

Emeric rode. Ferra had a post-chaise, hired in the capital out of the purse, light enough to change horses at every stage with the rest of them, and a board across her knees, because seven days on post horses going north had been enough to last her, she said, until she died. Petram rode behind it on a led post horse, with the irons on and the same guard's cloak over them that he had worn north, a sentenced man now instead of a witness and not visibly different for it. Ward rode at his near side, as he had the whole of the road north. The judiciary's escort rode behind, because a sentenced man belonged to the judiciary until somebody signed for him at the other end, and the judiciary meant to see him signed for.

They had stayed two more nights while Keller's clerks wrote the ruling out in the forms the crown required. In those two nights nobody from the palace had sent for him. His father could not, and had not, and Emeric knew the reasons well enough to recite them. Waylon could have, and had not. On the last evening, crossing the inn yard, Emeric had heard a groom telling another that the third prince's brother had been at the judiciary asking which day the hearing fell on, and had been asking on the first day. Waylon had known for days that his brother was inside the walls.

Emeric paid that one the way he always had, without moving his face, and turned his horse south.

The speech would not settle.

He had expected the ruling to sit in him the way a finished calculation sits, closed and filed and done with. What sat there instead was an old man at a door, telling him about a corridor and two days and a clerk, and asking for nothing.

He tried to put it on a page that first night at the posting house, and got as far as the date.

On the second morning he gave his horse to one of the escort and climbed into the chaise. There was road dust in Ferra's hair, dulling the red of it, and she had evidently stopped bothering about it somewhere around the first posting house.

Ferra did not look up. "You want the other column."

"Yes."

She had it. She had had it since the week of the ledgers, because it sat in the same books as everything else and she did not know how to read half a book. She had kept it off Keller's table because Keller had asked where the money came from and not where it went, and a magistrate who does not ask a question has no use for the answer.

A wheel dropped into a rut and the board slid off her knees toward him. They both caught it. Her hand came down flat on the back of his and held the board where it was, and his hand with it, until the wheel was out again. Then she lifted hers off.

She turned the board round so he could read it.

Just over half. As near as paper would let her say it, a little more than half of what Wexler had taken out of Kaldo had gone back into Kaldo, through doors that were not the front one. Seed bought in the plague springs, at prices nobody would sign for in daylight. Grain in two bad winters, entered as something else and given out at the keep's gate. A roof on the almshouse. Small sums, often, and dull enough that nobody would ever look at them twice, which was the point of them.

"And the rest?"

"He kept it. It isn't much." She took the board back. "A man who wanted to be rich would have picked a better barony to rob."

Emeric looked out of the window at nothing for a while.

"He told the truth in that doorway."

"About the money," Ferra said.

That was fair, and it went exactly as far as it went, and he could not make it go any further in either direction. The man had paid a courier to have him killed on a road. The man had kept a dying town breathing on what was left. The paper agreed with both, in the same hand, on the same pages.

"Would it have changed anything, on the table?"

"Not the sentence."

"It would be easier if he had lied."

"Easier for you." She went back to her figures. "It wouldn't change a line of this."

He sat with that for a mile, and somewhere on the mile a smaller number came up underneath it.

In the week Doran came up the hill, in July, Emeric had set a wage for the rows. Half as much again as the docks paid, out of the soap money. It would go to Doran in a purse, and Doran would pay the diggers on the parish step at the end of each week, so that no man on those spades was ever seen taking a coin from the keep.

Wexler had heard about it inside a day, because Wexler heard about everything inside a day, and had found him in the side room off the hall.

"Pay that on the rows and you have set the price of a day's work in Kaldo. Every man in the barony will want it for everything else. There is nothing in this barony to pay it with."

Emeric had set it anyway, and had been faintly pleased with himself for not arguing.

He took the old man's words out now and laid them against Ferra's board, and they held. They had been true when they were said. Wexler had known what there was in that barony down to the copper, because he had spent years moving it from one column to another to keep the place standing, and he had told Emeric so in three sentences, and Emeric had heard an objection.

The rows themselves he could only think about from outside, because outside was where Doran had put him.

One family at a time. No pulpit. Nothing posted and nothing announced. And Emeric not present, not in the room, not at the door, not in the street. Doran had laid that down on his own step, in the voice he kept for burial logistics, and had added that the moment a prince was in the room a family would stop deciding whether to move its dead and start deciding whether to refuse a prince, and that a good many of them would enjoy it.

Emeric had agreed at once. Keeping it had been harder than agreeing.

He had walked the hollow once, with Imani, and watched the first line of stakes go in. After that he had kept away. The first cart had gone out by the South Gate in the week before the road, early, covered, before the market was up, and he had watched it from a window in the keep that he had no reason to be standing at, at an hour he had no reason to be awake. A big man had walked at the tail of it with one hand on the boards. From the keep Emeric could not make out his face. When he turned round, Ward was in the doorway.

Ward said nothing.

He said it for about as long as it takes to watch a cart out of sight, and went on saying it after, and Emeric went back to the ledgers and did not stand at that window again.

Three weeks of it by the time he was home, if Doran had kept the pace. He could not guess how far that came to. He wanted very badly to know, and the wanting was precisely the thing Doran had shut the door on.

He brought up the petitions that afternoon, because he could not leave them alone.

"You found them on the road."

"In the castle's outgoing register. The second posting house never saw them."

"And kept them from me."

"Yes."

"Because I would have looked at him when you read them out."

"You'd have looked at him before I got to them." She did not stop writing. "Keller would have watched you do it, and ruled on your face instead of the page."

It was the right answer. He had arrived at it himself, in that room, about two seconds after arriving at the other thing, which was that she had managed him, and done it cleanly, and that he had not felt it happen.

"Does Imani know?"

"Know what?"

"That her letters were read out to a magistrate."

"They were read out of the barony's register." Ferra turned a page. "The register is evidence. She isn't."

He let that go.

"Next time," he said, "tell me afterward."

"When there's an afterward that doesn't cost anything, I will."

"That isn't a yes."

"No."

He argued it for another mile. He came at it from three directions and lost from all three, well past the point where there was anything left to win, and got down at the next change of horses in a noticeably better temper than he had got in.

Ward put him on the ground in the yard of the posting house at dawn the next day, as he had most dawns since the spring. Two of the escort watched the first fall with the careful blankness of men who had decided not to hold an opinion about a prince, and by the third they had gone back to their horses.

Wayford came up on the third day.

Ferra went straight back up to the long room above the stables where the toll clerks kept their returns. On the way north she had come out of it with nine years of toll returns and had been annoyed about the missing two for forty miles. The case was won. Keller had ruled. No two years of tolls could move a line of it. She went in anyway, came out an hour later with one of them, which a clerk had found in the cellar after all, and was annoyed about the other for most of the evening.

Emeric asked her why, with the case finished.

"The case is finished. The books aren't."

He did not go looking for a counting house.

Somewhere in this town a man kept another merchant's accounts, because his father had poured four generations into a place that was visibly ending and the son could do arithmetic. Keller's clerk had known which house. Emeric had written to him from the inn in the capital, and the letter was in this town by now, or on the road just behind them.

He could have found the door inside a quarter of an hour.

He did not go. The letter had said the only thing Emeric had any right to say. A prince on that step, in the week the crown was killing the man's father, was nothing the man had asked for, and whatever such a visit was for, it would not have been for the son.

At supper Ward asked the question nobody else had.

"Who has Kaldo now?"

Emeric considered it.

"Apparently I do," he said, and reached for the bread.

Ferra put her cup down.

"It'll be noted," she said.

Emeric laughed. Nobody else at the table did.

On the fourth day the road went into the forest and stayed in it until dark.

Going north, Emeric had watched the treeline for most of an afternoon. Going south, Ward rode on the forest side of the chaise the whole day without being asked and without remarking on it. Emeric watched the treeline anyway. Nothing came out of it. At the posting house that night he found that his jaw ached from being held.

On the fifth morning, at the change of horses, he went back to Petram.

He had told himself in the capital that he would look in on Petram every day of the road, and he had done it twice. Petram was sitting the way he always sat, as though the irons belonged to somebody else and he was minding them.

"You're wanting to know if I'm grateful."

"No."

"Good. I'm not." Petram moved his wrists an inch. "It's a rope I'm not getting. That isn't the same as a thing I've been given."

Emeric let that stand, because it was correct.

"Where do they put me?"

"Wherever there's work. There will be a great deal of it."

Petram turned that over with the thoroughness he gave everything.

"My cousin's there."

"Yes."

"Will they let him come?"

Emeric had not decided anything about it until that moment, and found that he had.

"Yes."

Petram nodded once and looked back at the road, and said nothing else all morning. Emeric walked back to his horse unsure whether he had just done something decent or something useful, and aware that he had stopped expecting to be able to tell the difference.

He had been working on what he would say since the capital.

A town whose baron was condemned, and perhaps dead by now, and perhaps not, and he had no way to know until somebody told him. A town that had spent the summer being told a prince had come for its graves. Forty-odd armed men with nobody to answer to. He built the arguments the way he built everything, from the load backward. What they would say. What he would answer. Where each answer would fail, and what he would put under it when it did.

Ward heard the first three on the sixth morning and said, "They won't want an argument."

"What will they want?"

Ward looked at the road for a while.

"To watch."

Emeric built a fourth one anyway.

They came over the last rise in the early afternoon.

The wall first, round, with its towers, and the moat around it, and the one bridge to the one gate. The thatch packed inside so close that from here it made a single roof, and out of the middle of it the inner ward, and the keep at the high point with the green ring of the canal at its foot. The river on the east side, wide and brown, and the stone bridge to the dock quarter, and the moorings on the far bank still standing three in four empty. The aqueduct coming in from the Mere on its arches and stopping in the air where the span was down. And below all of it the long green drop running south toward a grey line at the limit of what the eye would do.

He had come over this rise once before, with his jaw still stiff from a poison and a bound man on the seat across from him. He had looked at that grey line for about a minute and had not thought about it since.

He did not think about it now. He was looking at the keep.

There was a sentence waiting for him under that roof. He could feel the shape of it the way a foot finds a stair in the dark, and he knew roughly what it would say once it had been said, and he did not say it. Not on a rise. Not with the whole valley in front of him, and the keep at the top of it, and whoever was inside the keep looking up the road.

He would carry it unsaid for as long as it took. He had carried heavier things that way for fourteen years, and he was good at it.

He rode down toward the moat bridge at a walk, with his face arranged and the sentence behind it.
