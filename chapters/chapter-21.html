Chapter 21 - The Same Water

The things he had built stopped needing him, and he took that as permission to stop looking at anything.

It was the season after the war, and the barony wore it the way a body wears a bad winter. Thinner in places, healed wrong in others, up and walking and getting on with the work in front of it. The wall stood closed. The row under the cloth had gone under the earth, uphill of every well, and the black square where the granary had burned had been cleared to bare ground and would stay bare until the covenant let him build on it again.

Everywhere else the ordinary green business of a barony that had decided to live went on, and Emeric let himself, quietly, be proud of how little of it now turned to look at him when it worked.

The crop came up. Farris walked the three sections the way Emeric had taught him and then in ways Emeric had not, reading the soil with a farmer's hands where Emeric could only read it with a chart, and the rotation held. The depleted field rested under clover. The middle section carried barley. The near field, the one the trenches served best, went to the crop that would matter at the counting, and it came up in even rows because the water came to it in even measure.

That was the part he still let himself be proud of when he passed the pump-house on the western arm. Inside sat one carved stone, his mother's Control Rune, the only thinking thing in the whole apparatus.

Everything downstream of it was blank. A hundred flat stones seated along the clay-lined trenches, dumb as gravel, holding nothing of their own and deciding nothing, pulled from the riverbed by the basketful and worth nothing apiece, waiting to be told when to hold the water and when to let it go. The Control Rune told them, all of them, without any man re-carving a single one.

A hundred stones had cost him a hundred nights' worth of registering, done alone in a cold office, one at a time, because the Control Rune would not speak to a stone that had not passed through his hands while he was under. He had proved that a network did not need a clever stone at every gate. He had also proved that it needed him at the front of it, once, for every gate, which was a different fact and one he had not yet said out loud to anybody.

No single point of failure past that. Parallel trenches, so a collapsed bank on one line only starved one line. Mundane clay doing the real work of carrying water downhill, the magic only deciding the timing. If the Control Rune died tomorrow, Farris and forty men with shovels could run the whole thing by hand. Slower, wetter, alive.

He had built it so it did not depend on him, and it did not depend on him, and so he stopped watching it and turned his attention back to the crystals and the furnace and the debt, which were the problems that still bit.

That was the mistake. Not the system. The looking away.

He heard about the sickness late and secondhand, folded into a report about something else. Farris brought it up at the end of a walk about the barley, offhand, with no more weight in it than a note on the weather. There was a flux going through the lake camp. A few of the diggers down at the works site, and some of the field hands billeted near the low water. Summer complaint. It came every year with the heat.

Emeric filed it where an engineer files a solved problem. Sanitation. He had solved sanitation. He had solved it three seasons back with the town wells and the moved latrines and the burial ground shifted off the high bank, and the sickness had broken on that solution, and the town had lived.

Water was a closed question in his ledger of Kaldo. Checked and dated and done. A closed question does not reopen. That is what closed means.

So he said something about seeing that the doctor had what she needed, and went back to the furnace, and men kept dying at the lake camp for another eleven days before he understood that a solved problem is only solved on the ground you solved it on.

Imani was already there when he finally rode down.

He had expected to find a practitioner overwhelmed, doing the crude things doctors did in this world, bleeding the weak and burning bad-smelling herbs and waiting. He had, if he was honest, expected to arrive and fix it, to walk in with the same recipe that had worked in the town and hand it down and watch it work again, because that was the shape every problem took for him. Identify, apply, resolve.

She had beaten him to all of it.

The camp was already changed. The shallow dug well the diggers had sunk in the spring, in a hurry, during the weeks when there had been no coin for anything careful, was roped off and dry. A new one stood forty paces uphill, deeper, cased in fired clay he recognized as Bram's.

The latrine trench that had sat above the old well, feeding it every time the ground drank rain, had been filled and limed and cut again far below the water, downhill, the way the town's had been cut.

There was a second fire burning at the cook tent that was not for cooking. It was for boiling. Every drop the camp drank now went over that fire first, and Imani stood over it with the flat authority of someone who had been given a rule and had decided the rule was hers.

"You moved the well," he said.

"I moved the well," Imani said. She did not look up from the man she was tending, a digger, grey and folded around his own middle but breathing, and going to keep breathing. "And the latrine. And I stopped them drinking it cold from the low ground."

She wrung the cloth out. "You gave me the town, my lord. Three seasons back. You told me the sickness rode down the water from the graves and the shit into the wells, and you told me the fix, and the fix worked. And then you went and built four new towns out here, and none of you thought to bring the fix with you."

He had not thought of the camps as towns. That was exactly the error, put in one sentence by a woman he had walked in expecting to instruct.

The field billets, the lake site, the ore road huts, the charcoal burners' clearing. He had thought of them as sites. As parts of his machine. She had looked at them and counted the only thing that mattered, which was people living close together next to water they used for everything, and she had watched the plague's geometry reassemble itself four times over while he was carving stones.

"I gave you the town," he said slowly. "You didn't have the town. You had the lake camp. Different well, different slope, different ground."

"Same water," Imani said. "That's the thing you didn't say, so I had to work it out. You told me the answer for one place. You never told me it was the same answer everywhere, because I don't think you'd said it even to yourself."

She held up a hand and counted it off like a rule, because by then it was one. "Downhill of the drinking. Downstream of the drinking. And never let them share a cup with the ground the dead are in. That's not a fix for the town wells. That's a fix for any water anybody drinks anywhere, forever."

"I wrote it down as a standing order. Every new camp, before the first man sleeps in it, the well goes uphill and the trench goes down and I get sent for. I don't wait to be told there's sickness. I don't wait to be sent for. I go before there's anyone to bury."

She had taken a recipe and made a method. He had handed her a single solved instance, and she had extracted the principle he had not bothered to state, generalized it past anything he specified, and built it into a permanent practice that would run whether he was in the barony or not.

It was the exact thing he was always claiming to want. She had taught herself off one fish and then written the fishing down for everyone who came after.

"How many?" he asked.

"Nine." She said it without softening it, which he was grateful for and did not deserve. "Before I understood it was the camps and not just the heat. Nine, and it would have been forty if I'd waited for someone to give me leave. It should have been none. It would have been none if the fix had travelled with the shovels. It didn't, because the fix belonged to you and you thought it was finished."

Nine graves, dug uphill of nothing now, because she had moved the camp's burial ground too.

Nine men who died of a thing he had already defeated once, because he had believed that defeating a thing once meant defeating it. Nine, on top of the row under the cloth by the wall. He was getting a great deal built and he was keeping a longer account than he had ever meant to keep, and the two facts were the same fact.

There was a household at the ore huts that still would not move its latrine, he learned later. Not from ignorance but from the same distrust the town had shown him at the very start. The manor said dig here, so they would dig anywhere but there.

She had not moved them. She could not be everywhere, and she was one doctor for a barony that kept growing new mouths and new wells faster than one doctor could ride between them, and some of them were going to keep dying of stubbornness and of her simply not reaching them in time.

That was the shape of it. Not a clean win. A better floor, bought with nine men and held by one woman who was going to spend the rest of her life tired.

He did the only correct thing, which was the thing his instinct fought. He did not fold her in.

It would have been easy, and it would have felt generous, to bring her up to the manor, to seat her among the ones he trusted, to make the standing order a decree that came down from the prince and his circle. Bram would have forged whatever she asked. Ferra would have funded it. It would have become one more thing the manor did, one more line in the ledger of the prince's good works.

He gave it to her instead. The standing order went out under her name and not his, sealed as her authority and not his mandate. When men asked who had stopped the summer flux, the answer he made sure travelled was Imani, the doctor, not Emeric, the lord.

She was not in his circle, and he made certain, for now, that she did not become a satellite of it either. She had earned a thing that was hers, off one piece of knowledge he had handed down almost carelessly, and the worst thing he could do to that was absorb it into his own account.

Some good in Kaldo was going to have to belong to people who were not him, or the whole project was just a prince with a longer arm, and it would die the day the prince did.

"You did this," he told her, and meant only that, and left it there without attaching himself to it.

She frowned as though he had said something slightly foolish. She had not moved the wells to have her name on them. She had moved them because men were dying forty paces from clean ground for want of anyone telling them to walk uphill, and the moving was the whole of the point and the credit nothing she had thought to want.

"Somebody had to," she said, and shrugged, and left him the odd work of insisting on a thing she plainly did not care to be handed. He insisted anyway, and put her name on the summer flux, and she let him have it the way you let a man do a harmless thing that means more to him than to you.

That was nearer to thanks than either of them had room for that day, and it was enough.

The clay workers were the one piece of the post-war labour pool he had not redirected. Three crews of them, pulling grey-blue clay from the bank above the ford where the seam ran wide and the texture was right, at it since the first buildings went up and good at it, and the only thing they had never had was a kiln. Without one they had been air-drying bricks in the sun and getting bricks that soft rain dissolved.

He had filed it under things to fix and let it sit, because the covenant did not care what order he thought things through in, only what he spent. He read the covenant's words again in the counting room one evening and found, as he had suspected, that the words said capital works and not labour or material, and that clay from a riverbed he already owned and workers already on payroll were neither.

He had Jabari's crew set the kiln on the cleared ground east of the wall. A downdraft stack, simple enough that the crew understood it from the drawing in one morning, built from the same clay it was going to fire, so the first course of bricks made itself and every course after used what the previous firing had proven out. Eight days from first stake to first burn.

Vantic walked past it on her weekly inspection of the works she had a lawful right to inspect, paused, and wrote something down in the book she always had on her. She said nothing, which from Vantic was the sound a ruling made when it landed in the borrower's favour and she intended nobody to know it.

The bricks that came out of the first firing were not beautiful. They were harder than any sun-dried piece had ever been and would not dissolve in a season's wet.

He had Farris route the first load to the latrine sites Imani had condemned and refilled with lime, where the bare earth wanted something to face it and keep the next man from digging in the wrong place out of habit. Another load went to edge the cleared granary square. Not to build on it, the covenant had that ground still, only to mark the perimeter so the bare black earth looked deliberate rather than forgotten.

It was a small thing. The covenant did not forbid small things. He was learning, without much liking it, which category every possible action in Kaldo now fell into, and the list of the small ones was longer than the list of the large, and the small ones were what the barony was going to have to build itself out of until the debt said otherwise.

Armand arrived the week the barley started to turn.

He came without ceremony, an old man on a good horse with two boxes of books and one of instruments, and he found Emeric in the pump-house with his hand on the Control Rune, and for a moment neither of them said the thing.

The founding ones were all in place by then. Bram at the forge, Ferra over the books, Ward at the door, Saban on the roads. Armand slotted in behind them, a little after, the way a man arrives after the house is already standing and does not pretend he helped raise the frame.

"You built a machine that thinks with one stone and works with a hundred," Armand said, looking at the crystal the way Emeric had once looked at the floating gallery in the tower, the day the old man had first failed to teach him. "I would have taught you why it couldn't be done. For years I would have taught you that."

"You tried to teach me a great many things," Emeric said.

"I tried to teach you the only way I knew, to a boy who needed the one thing I didn't have." Armand set down a box. "I read the problem right. I read you wrong. I knew the difference too late to fix it, and I left rather than keep being wrong at you."

He straightened and looked at his former pupil with the plainness of a man who had spent a long time deciding to say a thing. "I understand now it wasn't incompetence. It was only knowing one tool."

Emeric thought of the lake camp. Of walking in to instruct a woman who had already outrun him. Of nine graves he had dug by looking away, and a row by the wall he had dug by running out of steel.

"I know the mistake," he said. "I make it every season. I read the thing and I miss the person. I missed her." He nodded south, toward the camps. "You and I have that in common. It's nothing to build on."

"No," Armand agreed. "It isn't."

"Then here's what I need, and I'd rather ask it badly than not ask." Emeric took his hand off the crystal. "Every dumb stone in that trench had to pass through my fingers before this one would speak to it. A hundred of them. If the answer to that is that it always has to be me, then everything I've built out here is a leash with my name on it and I've been lying to this town for two years without meaning to."

Armand did not answer straight away, which Emeric had learned to recognize in other people as the sound of a man taking a question seriously.

"I don't know," he said at last. "That is an honest answer and not a comfortable one. I have never heard of the thing you are describing, so I have no received wisdom to be wrong at you with, which may be the first useful position I have ever occupied with regard to your education." Something dry moved at the corner of his mouth. "We'll find out. That's the whole of what I can promise."

"That's more than I've had."

Outside, the near field was going gold at its edges. Somewhere under all of it the calendar on the counting-house wall was still crossing out squares toward the day the harvest had to come in and pay a house that was watching the fields with a colder eye than either of them.

But that was the next problem. For one afternoon the water ran clean to the barley in even measure, and the woman who had made it cleaner than he ever thought to had done it in her own name, and the old man who had failed him stood in the door owning it, and Emeric let himself, briefly, not look away.
