Chapter 21 - Hundredweight

In February, every hearth in Kaldo was burning, and the ash came in by the cartload.

It came in at the soap yard gate every morning in buckets and sacks and once in a wheelbarrow pushed by two small boys who'd been sent with the whole winter's sweepings from a house on Foulard Street. The yard paid for all of it. It paid the price Emeric had shouted from a barrow in the lower market in June, at carrying volume. I'll go on buying ash at the same price either way.

He'd meant it. The town had believed him. That was the problem.

Ferra didn't say I told you, though she had, in June, at the scales, with chalk on her fingers. She opened her book at her table one evening and laid two pages side by side: the ash taken in during June, day by day, and the ash taken in during the last three weeks of January.

The second page had three times as many lines.

"The yard can't sell three times the soap," she said. "It can sell about a third more. The rest is sitting in the back of the shed in sacks."

"And the price?"

"The price is what you said it was." She turned the book so the bottom line faced him. "The shop's paying it. The shop's going to stop being able to pay it in about nine days."

He looked at the line. He thought about the man on the barrow who'd bought two bars on the way home and kept buying them. About doors on the lower ward that had opened to him, the first summer, because for the first time in their lives somebody had paid them for something they'd always thrown away.

"I said it in the market," he said. "In front of everybody. The first thing I ever promised this town that it believed."

Ferra waited.

"The shop pays the price," he said. "I'll cover the gap."

"From what?"

"From what's left."

He went up to his room and came back with the pouch. The same one his father had handed him in front of two hundred and eleven people, fifty Gold Rand and not one more. He hadn't counted it in a while. He counted it now, at Ferra's table, onto the wood, and she watched him do it and didn't help.

There wasn't much. The guards. The kiln. Abloh's wage, the first summer. The burned woman's month. Ferra's own wage. The households of every child in the exchange, three mornings in seven, since September. It had all come out of here. What was left made a small heap.

He pushed some of it across to her.

She wrote it in the book, and he read the line upside down.

Lent by E. to the shop, against the ash. To be repaid.

"It's not a loan," he said. "I don't want it back."

"It's a loan." She kept writing. "If I put it in as a gift, then the shop's been living on you since June and nobody knows it, and the day you stop, it falls over and nobody knows why." She blotted the line. "The scented bar pays you back. When does it start?"

"Spring."

She wrote spring in the margin, which was a threat.

The pouch, when he picked it up, was light enough that he noticed the weight of it in his hand all the way up the stairs.



She asked him the next evening, as he'd known she would.

"There's a chest in your room."

"There is."

"Full of crystals. Uncut. Your father's gift, Bram says." She had her pen in her hand and nothing under it. "Any manasmith in the kingdom would buy them. Two of them would clear the ash and pay for the guards to the summer."

"I'm not selling them."

She waited for the reason. He gave her both.

"They've got no crest and no record. My father made sure of that. The day one of them turns up in a market the size of Wayford's, somebody asks where it came from, and the answer is me." He turned his cup on the table. "And I need them. Every one. The furnace. The foundry. Things I haven't drawn yet." He looked at her. "If I sell them to pay for this winter, I've got nothing to build the next ten with."

Ferra considered that. Then she wrote one more line on the page where the barony's worth was kept: the grain, the mill share, the land. Under them, by itself:

One chest. Not on the books. Not to be.

She left it there.



The bar that would leave Kaldo couldn't be the plain one.

He'd given the plain one away. He'd stood on a barrow and told the whole lower market how to make it, twice, and by now half the kitchens in the town made their own and the other half bought it at the shop for two coppers. Anyone downriver who wanted Kaldo's plain bar could have the method for the price of asking a carter.

What left Kaldo had to be something nobody else could make. Finer. Scented. Wrapped, and marked, so a buyer in Wayford would know it on sight and ask for it by name. The kind of cake the south coast sold for a week of food apiece, though not at that price.

Abloh made it. It took him three weeks and more batches than he'd admit to, and he wouldn't let Emeric near the pots for any of them. The fat was rendered twice and strained through linen. The lye was tested with the egg and the copper, and then tested again. The scent was honey, from the four households on Ferra's census that kept bees, worked in at the very end while the soap was still warm enough to take it and cool enough not to burn it off. When it set, it was pale gold and hard and smelled faintly of summer.

The mark was Tess's. She carved it into the end of a block of pear wood on her forge mornings: one arch, the shape of the aqueduct where it came over the farms, with the water line drawn along the top. Abloh pressed it into every bar while it was still soft.

People in the shop started calling it the arch before there were a dozen of them.

The plain bars stayed what they were. The ward kept them by the crate. Sela kept them on the counter at two coppers. She was there when Emeric came in with the first box of arches to set beside them, and she went on counting change into a woman's palm and didn't look up, and he set the box down where she'd see it and went out again.



The Wayford carter came back for his hundredweight in the last week of February.

Emeric had kept his name since June, on a page at the back of the notebook, under Fletcher and above the garrison. When the yard could finally fill a hundredweight and still keep the shop stocked, in January, he'd written to him. He didn't trust it to the crown's post; nothing Emeric sent that way had ever arrived anywhere. He'd given the letter by hand to a Kaldo carter off Ferra's census who was taking hides up the Wayford road, and paid him to put it into the man's own hand and nobody else's.

The carter came down the road a month later with an empty wagon and the letter folded in his coat, as if he'd wanted to be able to prove he'd been asked.

He bought his hundredweight of the plain bars at the price he'd asked about in June. Emeric had said he'd send word. He hadn't said what the price would be, and he'd decided long ago it would be the same.

They loaded it at the yard, and Emeric loaded it with them. He'd carried water up these lanes for a summer; he could carry soap. The carter watched a prince of the blood hand crates up into his wagon and plainly didn't know where to put his face. Then it was done, and the wagon went out under the South Gate, across the moat bridge, and turned north along the road under the long line of the aqueduct, getting smaller.

Emeric wiped his hands on his coat and went back in to see what Abloh needed.

It was the first thing Kaldo had sold to anybody outside its own walls since the war.

Ferra wrote it on a line of its own in the ledger that evening. Nothing else on that line. The date, the weight, the price and the carter's name.



The spring caravan came through Kaldo in the second week of March, as it always had, even in the plague years when there'd been nothing to buy.

Seven traders, give or take, with their carts and their drivers, stopping three days on the open ground outside the South Gate to sell what they'd brought and buy whatever Kaldo had. Usually that was salt fish and not much else.

This year Kaldo had the arch, and Emeric needed someone to carry it further than one carter could.

He didn't go down to the caravan ground. He sent Ferra with full authority to choose, and a copy of her book, and Rylan to carry it, and he stayed in the keep for three days and didn't so much as look over the wall. A prince at a trader's table set a courtier's price. Everyone would agree to everything, and none of it would be true.

She asked every one of them three questions. She'd worked them out with him the week before, at her table, and they were all ordinary, and none of them was what it looked like.

First, she offered terms. Generous ones, a little too generous, so that any trader with sense ought to have wondered why. Most of them didn't wonder. They said yes, quickly, and she wrote that down.

Second, she showed them a page of figures for the first load, and three lines down, in Kaldo's favour, there was an arithmetic error. Not a small one. Several of them didn't see it. Two saw it and said nothing, and came back the next morning having thought better of it, and she wrote those two down separately.

Third, she asked what they'd charge to carry a load for the barony without being told what was in it.

Two of them named a price so fast, and so cheerfully, that she closed her book in the middle of the conversation and thanked them for their time.

One man passed all three.

He refused the generous terms before she'd finished explaining them. He found the error before she'd turned the page, and corrected it out loud, and was faintly offended that it was there at all. And when she asked about the sealed load, he looked at her for a moment and said he didn't carry anything he hadn't seen.

"Not even once?" Ferra said.

"Once is a favour," he said. "The second time it's not a favour. It's a record."

His name was Saban. He was somewhere in his early forties, with four carts and a spare, and a way of standing still while he listened. He'd built his trade one town at a time, he said, and he had names in Milford, and in Wayford, which mattered most, and in Grayrock, and in a few places Ferra had never heard of and he didn't explain.

That evening Emeric went down to her table, and she told him all of it. Then she told him the last thing.

"He's in the tithe rolls," she said. "Ours. Born here. Gone in the war years."

Emeric turned that over.

It would be useful. A man coming home to the town he'd left; a prince who knew it. You could build a whole negotiation on that. And if Saban ever found out that the prince had known, and used it, then every decent thing that passed between them afterward would go in the same drawer, filed under he knew where I was from.

"Don't use it," he said.

"I wasn't going to," said Ferra.



They met him the next day at Ferra's table, because it was where the books were.

Emeric made the offer himself. Saban would carry the arch, and only Saban: anywhere he could sell it, at whatever margin he could get. Kaldo wouldn't sell it through anyone else. The proceeds would come back as goods, from a list Ferra would give him every month: ore, tallow, timber, salt. Kaldo needed things more than it needed money, and it couldn't get most of them from Grayrock without paying Grayrock's price.

"Tallow from where?" Saban asked.

"Milford," Ferra said. "If you know anyone there."

"I know someone there."

Then Ferra opened her books for him, the real ones. Ash in against soap out, week by week since June. What a bar cost to make, broken down by fat and ash and lime and salt and wage. The week the yard had first made more than it spent. Who bought what, sorted, in a column. And at the end, a line she'd drawn herself, by hand, rising.

Saban turned the pages without speaking, then went through them again from the first.

"I've never seen a business look at itself," he said finally.

He and Ferra spent forty minutes after that on what the arch would do in summer, when nobody burned their hearth and nobody needed as much soap, and Emeric sat at the end of the table and kept out of it, and was happier than he'd been all winter.

At the end of it, Saban sat back, and looked at Ferra, and said, half joking, that if the prince ever didn't need her he'd hire her tomorrow.

"You can't have her," Emeric said.

It came out before he'd thought about it. He heard it in the room after he'd said it.

He felt his face go hot. Saban looked at him for exactly one breath, and then looked down at his sheet of figures and studied it with great attention.

Ferra didn't look up at all. She finished the line she was writing, and blotted it, and turned the page.

Nobody mentioned it again.



Saban stayed the week at his own expense, and spent it walking.

He walked the soap yard and the shop. He walked the lower ward and stood at the new tap and drank from it. He walked down to the exchange on one of Sela's mornings and sat at the back through a whole lesson on figures, until Sela told him to get out. He walked the dock quarter's empty moorings, and the next morning he took a lease, at the barony's rate, on an empty warehouse by the dead port near the foot of the east bridge, with a loading door onto the water.

On his last evening, coming up from the ward, Emeric passed him at the north well. He was looking at a house across the lane that had its shutters closed, and he didn't notice the prince go by.

"He grew up near there," Emeric said to Ferra later. "Didn't he."

"He told me why he signed," Ferra said. "It's his to tell. Not mine."

He let it go.

Saban's carts went out of the South Gate on the last morning of the caravan with the first load of arches in the bed of the lead wagon, wrapped in oiled cloth, headed for Wayford.



That evening Emeric sat at Ferra's table with the year.

It was all in her books. The shop. The ward, and its eleven beds. The exchange, half full on every one of Sela's mornings, and what it cost. The farm and the three strips under the aqueduct, winter wheat showing green in rows. The tenth of the mill that was a twentieth now. The foundry across the river with its three bays, not yet lit. The coil on Bram's wall, unpriced.

And the pouch, which had a line of its own, and very little under it.

He added it up in his head while she turned the pages, and said the total.

"Out by four," Ferra said, and showed him where.



The first Grayrock barge of the season came down the river two days later, low in the water with ore, and tied up at the dock quarter's empty moorings, and unloaded onto the bank because there was still no wharf.

The bargeman had a letter. He handed it to Ferra at the bank because she was the one standing there with a book, and stood watching the river while she read it.

The price of ore was going up. By a fifth, from this barge on. The letter was very polite about it. Grayrock regretted that its workings were going deeper this year, and that deeper workings cost more, and it trusted Kaldo would understand.

Ferra read it twice. Then she looked at the ore stacked on the bank, and picked up a piece of it, and turned it over.

"Same ore," she said. "Same colour. Same weight to the bar. Same as every barge since June." She put it down. "It's not from deeper. They've never sold anybody anything from deeper. They haven't got anything from deeper."

She opened her book on her arm and worked it, standing on the bank in the wind, while the bargeman looked at the river.

The foundry. The plowshares for the spring. The draw plate and whatever came after it. Every nail.

She said the number out loud.

Emeric didn't answer. He was looking past her, up the river, the way it came down from the north between its banks, brown and fast with the spring water. Everything Kaldo needed came down it. Ore and stone and copper and timber, all floating on the current, cheap because the river carried it for nothing.

Nothing had ever gone back up. Nothing Kaldo had was worth hauling against that current.

Until this winter: soap that sold, and a school, and a ward, and a wheel drawing wire in the dark. And somebody at the top of the river had looked down it and seen what was being built at the bottom.

He stood on the bank with Ferra's number in his ears and looked upriver.
