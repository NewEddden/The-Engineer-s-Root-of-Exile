Chapter 21 - The Crown's Judgment

He had expected a hall.

Twenty-two years in this city had given him a picture of what it looked like when the crown decided something about a man, and the picture had a gallery in it, and a rail, and seating enough for the sort of people who liked being able to say afterward that they had been there.

What he got was a room on the third floor off a corridor that smelled of floor wax. A long table. Chairs chosen by somebody who did not want anyone comfortable enough to speak for an hour. The windows were set high in the wall, well above a standing man's eye, so the light came in and nothing else did. There was no gallery. There was nowhere to put one.

Emeric took that in on the way to his seat and understood, before anybody had said a word, most of what he needed to know about the man at the head of the table.

Keller was older than the reports had made him. Grey at the temples, heavy through the shoulders, with the flat stillness of a man who has spent thirty years listening to people lie and has stopped finding it interesting. He had a stack of paper squared at his right hand, edges true, the way a stack sits when it has been read twice and put back in order both times.

He did not stand when they came in. A prince of the blood walked through his door and he did not stand, and it was not an insult, because an insult would have required him to have thought about it.

Emeric decided on the spot that he liked him and that this was going to be worse rather than better.

Petram was three streets away, in the judiciary's own holding, where Emeric had put him within an hour of the carriage stopping.

He had worked that out himself on the last day of the road, and it was the first genuinely lawyerly thought he had ever had. A confessed conspirator's word against a baron was worth nothing. Worse than nothing. Put Petram in that room and the whole afternoon becomes an argument about whether a man in chains says what he is told to say, and every hour spent on that is an hour nobody spends on the ledger. The ledger could not be frightened into anything. It had been sitting in Wexler's own strongroom for eleven years telling the truth to nobody.

So the poisoner stayed in a cell across town and the paper came to court.

Wexler arrived four minutes later and he had slept. That was the first thing Emeric noticed and it told him more than the man's face did. Two days in the capital ahead of them, a hearing he had asked for himself, and he had slept.

He spoke first, because it was his petition. He was direct about it. There was talk moving in his barony and in the neighboring ones, and talk left standing long enough stops being talk and becomes what everyone remembers having always known. He preferred to answer it in a room with a magistrate in it rather than in a market square in ten years' time.

Keller did not respond to any of that. He turned to Emeric.

"You have evidence or you do not. I am not interested in which of you says it louder."

Ferra stepped forward before Emeric could answer for her, which he preferred, and which he had half hoped for and not arranged, because arranging it would have shown.

She put three pages on the table. Barony accounts, from Wexler's own room, eleven years old and scraped. She did not say scraped. She said that the original entries had been removed with a blade and rewritten over, that the rewriting was in a different ink, and that the sheets had been thinned enough in two places to hold light through them, and then she held one up to the high window and let the room look at the shadow of a word underneath a word.

The buried entries recorded a debt discharge. Not through a merchant house, which is where a barony's money goes when a barony is paying somebody. Through a waypoint marker.

She explained what a waypoint marker was, briefly, to a magistrate who almost certainly knew, and it did not sound like flattery or condescension because she had no interest in either. A marker belongs to a dispatch route. A dispatch route belongs to a man who is licensed to run it.

This one belonged to a courier named Cobb.

Then she put down the second paper, which had not existed nine days earlier, and which Emeric had watched her build out of the back of a carriage by writing ahead to every waypoint registry on the south road and paying the couriers out of her own advance because she would not wait for authorization to be sent after her.

Cobb's payment records, taken from four registries, cross-referenced against the coin marks in the barony's own remittance log. Not a house in the middle. Not a factor, not a broker, not a cousin with a warehouse. The coin had left Wexler's treasury under Wexler's seal and arrived in the hand of a courier, and the dates sat inside a single week of an ambush on the Wayford road.

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when a thing has just become harder to argue with.

She was not finished. The same three pages carried the rest of it, because a man who alters one line in a book has usually been altering lines for years, and Wexler had.

Repairs invoiced and paid for and never done, itemized, including the outer wall and the drawbridge chain. A grain contract held for eleven years at a price no grain had ever cost anywhere. Sixteen names drawing garrison pay who had not existed since the war, by the parish book, by the tithe rolls, and by the simple test of nobody in the lower ward being able to remember their faces.

And three petitions from the barony's own healer, dated, entered by hand into the castle's outgoing register, and never forwarded to anyone.

Emeric had not known that last one was going in.

She had found it somewhere on the road and had not told him, and he understood why while she was saying it. If he had known, he would have looked at Wexler when she said it. He would not have been able to help it. She wanted Keller reacting to a document rather than to a prince's grievance, and the way to get that was to keep the prince's face empty by keeping the prince ignorant.

It was the coldest thing anyone had done to him in months and it was entirely correct.

Keller read for a while. Nobody filled the silence. Then:

"Explain the seal."

"I do not control every payment that leaves my treasury."

"You control the seal. That is what a seal is for. Try again."

"Then the pages are forged."

Ferra answered that one, and she answered it to the table rather than to Wexler, which took the heat out of it and left only the content. "Both hands on them are his. The one underneath and the one that came back over it. If they are forged, they were forged twice, eleven years apart, by a man imitating himself."

Keller turned, and the turn was not toward the baron.

"You have a grievance in this."

Emeric had a sentence ready. He put it down and used a worse one.

"Yes."

"Say it."

"He paid for men to kill me on the road and one of my escort died of it and I have wanted to be standing in a room like this since the autumn. That is the grievance. I would have brought this if I had never been on that road, because the sixteen names and the grain and the wall are on the same pages and were going to be found by somebody eventually." He put his hand flat on the table, which he had not intended to do. "Rule on the barony's own accounting disagreeing with itself in the baron's own hand. Rule on a coin trail that ends in his treasury. Do not rule on me."

"I had not planned to," Keller said.

The ruling took him under two minutes and he delivered it the way a man reads a list back to confirm he has it right. Sufficient evidence of treasonous conspiracy, specifically the financing of an attempt upon the life of a prince of the blood. Theft from the crown's own revenues, noted and subordinate. Death, by crown order.

He said the last part twice, and the second time he said it to Emeric. By crown order. Not baronial discretion, and not any other kind.

"This is the crown's judgment. Not yours. Do not confuse the two on your way home."

Emeric said that he would not, and meant it, and had already noticed that meaning it was not the same as being certain of it.

Wexler's face stopped moving somewhere in the middle of the ruling. Not dramatically. It simply went from being a face that was working to a face that was not, the way a mill goes quiet when the water is shut off upstream, and Emeric found himself watching it and taking inventory of his own chest.

He had wanted to do it himself. He had carried that for three months without once putting it on a page, which for him was as close to hiding something as he got. Standing in a small room with high windows he finally turned it over and looked at what it was made of, and it was not made of Tam.

Tam was not in the ruling anywhere. Tam was not in the evidence. There was no line of it that touched a nineteen year old on the mill road, and there never would be.

It was made of a campfire. A knife going toward a friend's back, and a man seeing the whole thing two seconds before it happened, and sitting perfectly still with his hands in his lap because the poison was already in him and his body had quit. No quantity of anybody else's blood was ever going to be accepted as payment against that. It had not occurred to him until this moment that he had been trying to make a payment.

The choice had just been taken off him by a man in a plain room who had never once looked impressed.

He found, somewhat to his disgust, that he was relieved.

At the door, Wexler turned around.

He spoke only to Emeric. The guards let him, because he was not moving and there was nothing in his hands.

"Nine years ago I rode eleven days to this city. My town was dying. I had written to everyone a man in my position is permitted to write to, and had done it for four years, and had run out. So I came myself."

"I stood in a corridor in this building for two days. Not this floor. Two down. There is a bench there. I imagine it is still there." He said it without any particular weight. "On the third morning a clerk came out and told me that the matter had been noted."

"I went home. I stopped writing letters, because a man who keeps asking has agreed to keep being told no, and I was not willing to agree to that in front of my own people for the rest of my life."

"I buried a third of them. The rest I kept breathing on what was left, and what was left was not much, and some of what I did to keep them breathing is on those pages in front of that woman. No one from this city ever came. No one from this city ever asked. In nine years no one from this city has said the word Kaldo to me except to tell me what it owed."

"And then last spring a rider arrived and told me that the crown was handing four generations of my family's work to its own embarrassment. To get him out of the capital. Not because he had earned it. Because you had become inconvenient here and Kaldo was far away."

He let that sit for a moment.

"So I paid for it. I want that plain. I am not asking you to think better of me."

"The debt cleared the same week as the road," Emeric said. "Before I ever reached Kaldo."

"Yes."

"So you never saw me. You paid for what you were told was coming."

"I paid for a drunk."

And then he said the part that did the damage.

"I have spent four months watching what actually got off that carriage." His voice did not change at all. That was the worst of it. "The wells are clean. Nobody has died of the water since the summer. There is a shop on the south row that has taken in more coin in two months than that row saw in five years. You have done more for that town since the harvest than I managed in twenty years of trying, and I have had to sit at my own table every evening and know both of those things at once."

"That is not a defense. His ruling is correct. I would have made the same one and I would have made it faster." He inclined his head very slightly toward Keller without looking at him. "It is only what I would rather you carried out of this room, instead of the version you were going to build for yourself on the ride home."

Then he went through the door, and the door was shut by somebody else, and that was all of it.

Emeric asked Keller's clerk about the family, afterward, in the corridor, because he could not think of a reason not to and because he had already decided he was going to have to know.

The clerk had it to hand, being the sort of man who has everything to hand. A wife, dead before the plague years, of something ordinary. One son, who took a merchant clerkship in Wayford eleven years ago and did not come back, on account of his father pouring four generations into a town that was visibly ending, and the son being able to do arithmetic. The letters had stopped being about coming home around the sixth year. They had gone on for a while after that, about nothing, and then they had stopped being about anything.

So there was no widow in Kaldo and no heir in the lower ward and nobody in that barony who was going to have to be dealt with kindly. The thing Wexler had paid a courier to kill for was a name his own son had declined eleven years earlier in writing.

Emeric wrote to the son once, from Kaldo, two weeks later. He never got an answer.

Petram was sentenced the following morning in a room even smaller than the first. Labor, not death, and Keller stated it the way he stated everything, which was as though the alternative had never seriously occupied his time.

"A confessed man who testifies is worth more alive than a corpse that deters nobody. The crown does not answer cooperation with a rope. It would not get any cooperation next time."

Emeric asked for the sentence to be served in Kaldo. He got it in under a minute, which told him what Petram was worth to the judiciary now that he had been written down. Nobody else wanted him. A used witness is a man everybody has finished with.

Ward found him in the yard before they left, with the horses half rigged.

"Cobb is still out there."

"I know."

"That is not what I came to say." Ward looked at the horses rather than at him, which was how he did most of his talking. "Wexler kept a garrison. Whatever else he was, he paid armed men and they answered to him, and as of this morning there is no him."

"How many?"

"Enough to matter. Not enough to feel good about." A pause. "Some of them are soldiers. Some of them were his friends. I would want to know which before I put a blade in any of their hands, and right now I could not tell you."

Emeric thought about it for less time than the question deserved, which he was aware of while he was doing it.

"After the farm. The farm eats first. The garrison keeps."

Ward accepted that the way he accepted most of Emeric's ordering of the world, which was to say that he did not argue, and went and checked the girth on a horse that did not need checking, and filed the disagreement somewhere Emeric would be able to find it later if it turned out he had been wrong.

The mage tower was on the way out of the inner wards, and Emeric had built the route so that it was.

Armand looked older than the memory, which was not fair to either of them, because the memory was fifteen years out of date and belonged to a boy who had been shorter than the man's elbow.

"Your Highness." Then, quieter, after a look at the courtyard: "Emeric."

"I read your notebook."

"All of it?"

"Including the page at the back. Eleven tests. Nine years."

"Then you know I found nothing."

"I know you were looking correctly and stopped too soon, and I am not going to explain that standing in a courtyard. I need you to see it. Not hear about it. Put your hands on it."

"That isn't how you used to talk to me."

"No. It isn't."

Armand looked at him for rather a long time. Whatever he was weighing, it was not the offer. He said that he had obligations. Two students mid-course, a bench he could not simply walk away from, and a tower that did not run itself whatever the Circle liked to imply about the matter.

It was not a no. Emeric had come prepared to hear a no and got instead an old man saying that he would settle what needed settling first, and then he would come.

Emeric said that he would write. Armand said that he would expect it.

On the way out he stopped in the lower courtyard and looked up, because he had not done it on the way in and he was not going to be back for a long time.

The Circle's gallery hung clear of the tower's spine with nothing under it but air. Ten men up there at that hour with both hands on crystals the size of a man's head, eyes half shut, and behind each of the ten another man standing and waiting to step into the gap the moment a grip failed. Twenty men on that floor, every hour, and twenty before them, and twenty before those.

Three nights ago, in a cold room in a castle four hundred miles south, he had found a thing that held its shape with nobody watching it.

He stood in the courtyard doing arithmetic he had no business being able to do and arrived, without wanting to, at the fact that every one of those twenty men was standing there because a whole order of mages had never once seriously entertained the idea that a stone might be able to remember something on its own.

He did not say it. Some things you let a man arrive at himself, if you ever want him to arrive at anything again.

The word came three days into the ride south, secondhand, in a crown dispatch handed off at a waypoint with four other items on it.

Sentence carried out.

Nothing else. No date, no manner, no note of who had been present.

He read it twice. That was habit now.

It should have closed. That was the part he kept turning over on the fourth day and the fifth. The coin trail had held. The route had held. The timing had held to a week. Ferra had built a case out of scraped paper and roadside registries that had not bent an inch under a magistrate who was indifferent to rank and actively hostile to noise, and every piece of it had done exactly what she had built it to do.

He had done everything right and the system had worked precisely as designed and Tam's name was still sitting where he had left it.

Nobody had asked about the duty roster. Nobody had asked who had been on the door that morning or who had walked out to the mill road that evening. Nobody could prove that Wexler had ordered that specifically, only that he had paid for an attempt in general, and general is more than sufficient for a treason charge and nowhere near sufficient for the only question Emeric had actually wanted answered.

Which left the thing he had been carrying since the capital without letting it form into a sentence, and which formed anyway, somewhere on the sixth day, on a bad stretch of road with the carriage complaining behind him.

The hand that reached Tam might not have been Wexler's at all. It might still be in his castle. It had watched him ride out and it was going to watch him ride back in.

And underneath that, refusing to settle anywhere he could put it, a man on a bench two floors down in a building in the capital, for two days, being told on the third morning that the matter had been noted.

Both things were true. He could not get them into the same column and he could not make either of them go away.
