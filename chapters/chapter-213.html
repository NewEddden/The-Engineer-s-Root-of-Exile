Chapter 213 - The Grey Shrub

The shed stood on the lake shore below the works, on the flat where the paper works dumped its cinders, and it had been a rope walk once, long and low, with a roof of tarred boards and a row of doors down the lake side that let the steam out and the weather in. Bram put four coppers in it, on brick hearths, and a trough of lake water down the middle, and a pair of iron rollers at the far end on a frame, turned by a belt off the works' line shaft through a hole knocked in the gable. It took him a fortnight. Nobody asked what it was for, because Bram built things in sheds by the lake every month of the year and most of them were for nothing anybody could have named.

The bales came up from Fletcher's yard at night in a covered cart, and were stacked at the shed's back end under canvas, and in the morning they were a heap of grey sticks, which was all they were.

It was filthy work, and slow, and nothing about it looked like anything.

The stems went into the coppers first, chopped short, and were boiled until the bark came loose, and the water went brown and then grey and smelled of wet sage and stewed wood. Then they were fished out with forks, steaming, and went through the rollers wet, twice, three times, until they came out the other side as a grey mash, like chewed paper, and the mash went into the long trough and the men stirred it with paddles. And then, slowly, over most of an hour, the rubber came up.

It did not come up as anything. It came up as threads, fine and pale, loosing themselves from the mash and rising through the grey water and meeting on the top, where they clung together into a scum, a skin, a floating raft of something that looked like wet wool gone wrong. The woody stuff sank. The rubber floated. That was the whole of the method, and it was the only reason it worked at all, and Emeric had stood at the trough on the first morning and watched it happen and felt something he had not felt since the first sheet of paper came off the mould: the particular satisfaction of a thing doing what it had always been going to do, in a world that had never asked it to.

A boy skimmed it with a wide flat sieve. The skimmings went on a board to drain, and were pressed by hand, and squeezed, and pressed again, and at the end of a day's work, four coppers and twelve men and a trough and a pair of rollers turned by a shaft, there was a grey-brown lump on the board the size of a loaf.

Bram weighed it. He wrote the weight on the wall of the shed in chalk, beside the weight of the bales that had gone in, and stood back and looked at the two figures together.

"That's a bad yield," he said.

"It's a good yield for a shrub nobody's ever grown," said Emeric. "We'll do better when we know what we're doing."

"When do we know what we're doing?"

"Next month. Or the month after."

Bram looked at the lump for a while. Then he picked it up off the board and dropped it on the shed floor, and it jumped back up to his knee, and he caught it, and put it back on the board, and did not say anything else about the yield.

It went wrong the way Emeric had known it would, and he let it, because a thing Bram found for himself was a thing Bram understood.

It was a warm week at the end of the spring. Bram had cut the first loaf into strips and made a gasket of it for the pump on the lake intake, a ring the size of a dinner plate between two iron flanges, bolted up, and it had held. On the third day he went down and the pump was weeping at the flange, and when he undid the bolts the gasket had gone soft and tacky, and oozed out between the iron like jam, and stuck to his fingers, and would not come off them.

He brought it up to the long room on a piece of paper and put it on the table in front of Emeric.

"It's gone like toffee," he said. "In the sun. It was fine in the cold." He looked at it with dislike. "What use is a seal that melts when it's warm?"

"Try it colder."

Bram took a strip down to the ice house and laid an ice stone on it for an hour, and brought it back and bent it, and it cracked across like a biscuit.

He came back up and put the two halves on the table beside the toffee. He did not say anything this time. He looked at Emeric and waited, with the patience of a man who had worked beside him for years and knew that when the prince said try it colder in that voice he already knew what would happen.

"Sulphur," Emeric said.

"Sulphur?"

"The yellow off the Emberfell boats. Flowers of it. Work a little into the raw stuff while it's soft, a tenth part or less, and then cook it. Not boil it. Heat it, steady, in a closed pot, at about the heat of steam, for a few hours." He turned the cracked strip over. "It'll come out different. It won't go soft in the sun and it won't crack on the ice. It'll hold its shape, whatever you do to it, and come back when you let go."

"Why?"

He did not know how to say why. In the life he had come from a man had found it by accident, dropping the stuff on a hot stove, and had died poor, and the name of it had gone round the world on the tyres of every cart. None of that was any use here. "Because the sulphur ties it together," he said. "Think of a heap of wet threads. Now think of the same threads with a knot here and there, joining one to the next. You can still stretch the heap. But it can't slide apart."

Bram thought about that for a long time. Then he took the pieces away. He came back three days later with a strip of something dark and dull and faintly yellow-grey, and put it on the table, and put an ice stone on one end of it and a lamp on the other, and left them there all afternoon, while the table argued about the Walker's second frame. At supper he picked the strip up and bent it double in his fist, and let it go, and it lay flat again on the table as if nothing had happened to it at all.

"Knots," said Bram.

After that it went everywhere, the way things did in Kaldo once they worked.

The steam works wanted it first. Every joint on every boiler and every pump in the barony was made up with hemp and red lead and swearing, and every one of them wept, a little, all the time, and was packed and repacked by boys with tins of grease whose whole work was going round packing. A sheet of the cured rubber cut to a flange and bolted up held steam at the works' pressure for a week and didn't weep at all, and the foreman of the engine house came up to the long room with the ring in his hand and asked for a hundred.

Fletcher wanted it for the ship's legs. The K Discovery stood on three legs in the yard, and the legs were on rams, and the rams were on oil, and every one of their seals was leather, and leather hardened in the band's cold and let the oil past, so that she came down from every run weeping at the knees. Fletcher took six rings and fitted them himself and brought the ship down from the next run with her knees dry, and did not come up to the long room to say so. He sent a boy up with a note that said Legs dry. More.

Then the pump hoses at the lake, which had been leather and canvas, and the belts on the line shafts in the works, which had been leather and slipped in the damp. Then the wire. The Drop House wire ran on the poles to the dock and the works, bare, on glass knobs, and wherever it came down the poles into a building it went through a hole lined with a clay pipe, and a clay pipe in the wet was a pipe for the current to find its way to the ground by, which it did. A sleeve of cured rubber round the wire where it went through the wall cured that, and Emeric stood in the works' switch room while the wire gang's boy sleeved the first one, and thought that he had now watched every single thing in his old world's kitchen drawer get invented a second time, one at a time, by men who had no idea they were doing it.

Imani wanted it for the ward, and did not ask. She came down to the shed on her own and stood at the trough and watched the threads come up, and then went to Bram and asked him how thin he could roll a sheet of it, and whether he could make a tube, and whether it could be boiled. He said yes, and he did not know, and yes. She went away. A month later there was a coil of pale tube in the ward, for draining a wound, that went into the boiling pan with the knives, and a pair of gloves on a hook by the washing trough that were so thin you could see your nails through them, and Imani said nothing about either of them to anybody. She just used them.

And Ward, when he heard, came down to the shed and asked for pads, rimmed, a hand across, for the palms of the suits and the soles of the boots, because a man in plate on a wet roof or a rock face went where the plate's weight took him and a rubber rim would give him purchase where iron slid. Bram made him a pair. Ward took them up onto the keep roof in the rain and walked about on the slates in them in his full plate for an hour while the gate watch watched, and came down, and asked for four more pairs.

Armand came down to the shed once too, and stood in the steam with his hands behind his back, and watched the rubber come up, and asked Emeric the only question about it that nobody else had asked.

"Couldn't a seal do all this?" he said. "A rune. On the flange."

"It could do the flange," said Emeric. "Better than this does, most days."

"Then why the shed?"

"Because the day the rune's in a dead place, the flange is a hole." He looked at the trough. "The rubber doesn't care where it is."

Armand thought about that for a while in the steam. Then he nodded, once, the way he nodded at a student who had said something he would not have said himself but could not fault, and went back up the hill.

The pitch came home in five casks, off the north end of the lake on the island, where the seeps ran thick and slow. Ferra had chosen the night. It was a cloudy one, eleven days after the one before, and Osric said later that they had ladled it cold like porridge and it had taken four of them all night to fill five casks, and he did not want to do it again soon.

Bram melted a pot of it in the flask room at the Drop House, with the door open and a fan going, because it smelled worse hot than cold, and sealed two flasks with it, fresh down from the band that night on hull two's racks, brimming, the lids screwed down and the island's black laid thick over the screw where Bram's own coal-tar would have gone. Then he sealed two more with his coal-tar, from the gas works lung, the way he had sealed every flask since the first one. He chalked them: I on the island two, C on the others. And put all four on the top shelf of the store, apart from the working stock, and wrote the date on the wall.

Emeric went down with Cade every week to read them. Cade was the pourer, and read a flask with his hands the way a cooper reads a cask by knocking it, and he had never once been wrong.

In the third week the coal-tar flasks were fuller than they should have been for the third week, and Bram was pleased, because they had never been so good. In the fourth week they were going. In the fifth, both of them were low enough that Cade said he would not pour from them. The coal-tar held a month. It always had. That was why the store was three days' stock and no more, and why the storm in the autumn had emptied it in a night and a day and put the dock in the dark.

The island two, in the fifth week, were full.

In the eighth week, they were full. Cade put his hands on them and stood for a long time and then took his hands away and said, "I can't feel it going," and looked at Bram as if Bram had done something to them. Bram had not. Bram had put a black seal on a screw lid, which he had done a thousand times. The only thing different was what the black was.

Emeric could feel it too, when he laid the sense on them. The coal-tar seal had been like a clay pot: whole, but not quite, so that over the weeks the charge had found its way out through it the way water finds its way out through a pot that is not glazed. The island's pitch was like glass. Whatever the ground had done to it in however many thousand years in the dark under the sand, it had closed it up, and the charge sat behind it and could not find a way through, and did not try.

He took the figures to Ferra.

She looked at them for longer than it took to read them, and then took down the store's page in the big book, the one with the three days on it, and looked at that, and put the two side by side.

"A season," she said. "Or near it. We'll know for certain at midsummer."

"Near it."

"Then we can keep them." She said it slowly, as if hearing it. "Every clear night hull two goes up, and she comes down with twelve, and we pour what we need and the rest goes on the shelf, and the shelf doesn't empty. It fills." She turned a page. "We could have a winter in that room. We'd need a bigger room."

"We'd need a lot more pitch."

"Then you'll need a lot more cloudy nights." She wrote the figure for the room at the foot of the store's page, and under it, very small, Island pitch. A season. And the date. And then, after a moment, she drew a line across the page under the last entry, which was the night of the gale in the autumn, when the shelf had gone bare and the lamps over the dock had gone out one by one because there had been nothing left to pour.

"That was the last time," she said.

She did not say it as a hope. She said it as a line in a book.

Vantic came on a Tuesday, three weeks before the midsummer quarter, which was not a quarter day and not any day she had ever come on before.

She came in the House's carriage with her clerk, as she always did, and her courier did not ride behind. She sent the clerk to the counting room to sit with Ferra's book and copy what he liked, which was a thing she had never done either. Then she asked Hobb at the stair whether the prince could give her an hour, alone, and Hobb came up to the small room at the end of the long room and said so, in the careful voice he kept for things he would be asked about later.

It was raining. It had been raining since the morning, a warm grey rain off the sea that made the lake smoke, and the small room faced north and had one window, and somebody had lit the lamp on the table against the dark, one of the old ones from the keep, a lamp stone in a brass cup under a chimney glass, that had been on that table since before Emeric came and had been topped up at the handcart every quarter since.

He stood when she came in. She did not sit. She stood at the end of the table with her gloves in one hand and her book under her arm, and looked at the lamp, and then at him.

"The engine house line," she said. "At the autumn quarter. Coal and lamps."

He had not expected that. He kept his face still. "Yes."

"I copied it. Both lines, as they stood. It went in the weekly bag and it was on the Thursday tide to the coast house, and read aloud there, at a table, I'd think within the fortnight." She said it as she said every figure, clearly, once. "I thought you should hear that from me, and not work it out. I don't dress a book. Not yours, not anybody's. Not by leaving a line off because it's awkward for somebody I like."

"I know you don't."

"Yes," she said. "I think you do." And then she sat down, at last, at the end of the table, and put the book in front of her, and her gloves on top of it, and folded her hands. "That isn't what I came for. I came to ask you for something, and I can't ask for it without telling you why I want it, and I can't tell you why without telling you who I am. So I'll tell you who I am."

He sat down too, across from her. The rain ran down the window.

"I know who you are," he said.

He said it at once, because he had decided a long time ago that if this day ever came he would say it at once. He had thought about the other way: to let her tell it, and nod, and let her believe she was telling him something. It would have cost nothing for a while. It would have cost everything, afterwards, the first time she found out, and she would find out, because finding out was what she was for.

She did not move. Her hands stayed folded on the book.

"Since when," she said.

"The winter before last. Saban read the factor roll in the capital, when we were up for the court. And Keller had the charter filing copied." He did not look away from her. "Four Vantics in sixty years. Three children in the filing. A daughter with nothing written against her name."

"And the courier."

"West past Milford. To a fishing harbour a day on. A cutter on the Thursday tide." He put his hands flat on the table. "I called Saban's men off the day he told me. Nobody has followed the bag since. Nobody will. The table was told once, in that room, and it's never been said again, and the papers are in Ferra's box. That's all that was ever done with it."

She was quiet for a long time. The lamp on the table between them burned with the low, even light that lamp stones gave, and the rain ran on the glass.

"The winter before last," she said. "That was the quarter I wouldn't move the draw."

"Yes."

"You knew then."

"Yes."

"And Kaldo asked me for a fortnight on the draw, knowing."

"Kaldo asked the House's factor for a fortnight on the draw," said Emeric. "Which is what you are, when you're at that table. I wasn't going to ask the other one for anything. I'm not now."

Something went across her face and was gone before he could see what it was. She unfolded her hands and put one of them on the cover of the book.

"Well," she said. "Then I don't have to say it, and I'd rather not, so I won't. But I'll say the rest, because you don't know the rest." She opened the book at the back, and took out a folded sheet, and unfolded it on the table, and turned it round so it faced him.

He knew it. He had seen it once, upside down, across the loan table, at the winter quarter when she had refused the draw: a sheet ruled in squares, Kaldo at the top, and under Kaldo eight or nine other names he did not know, and in every square two figures, some of them rubbed out and written again. He had not understood it then. He had thought it was something the House asked for.

He understood it now. It came together while he looked at it, the way things did, all at once: the names down the side, the years along the top, the two figures in every square, what was owed and what was paid, year after year, borrower after borrower. Not a judgment of any one of them. A record of all of them, side by side, so that you could stop looking at any one borrower's face and look instead at what borrowers like that did, taken together, over years. In the life he came from there had been rooms full of people who did nothing else, and machines that did it for them, and whole cities of money built on it. Here there was one sheet, in one woman's hand, folded in the back of a book.

"Every house in the kingdom prices a loan the way I priced you," she said. "A factor rides out and looks at it and makes up her mind. It works four times in five. The fifth time it's a disaster, and you don't know which one you've made until it breaks. That's the craft. My brothers are very good at the craft." She touched the sheet. "This isn't the craft. These are my books. The ones the House gives to people it doesn't expect anything of. Nine borrowers nobody wanted. If you look at any one of them, it's a dog. If you look at all of them together, they pay. Not every year. Not every one. But taken together, the whole class of them pays better than the House has ever priced them, for a century, because the House has always looked at them one at a time, and every factor who ever looked at one of them saw a dog."

"The House is mispricing them," said Emeric.

"The House has been mispricing them for longer than my father's been alive." She said my father as if it were a clause. "And I can't prove it with nine. Nine is a story. Nine is a woman who got lucky with her dogs. I need enough of them that nobody in a room of men who read books for a living can call it luck." She looked at him. "There's a Reading. In the autumn. The partners audit each of us in front of him, every few years, and he listens, and he doesn't say anything. The last one, I lost. I'll lose this one too if I walk in with nine."

"You want books."

"I want the books of every small borrower you can reach. Not yours. I have yours. Theirs. Milford's tenants. Grayrock's tally house. Wayford's arrears. Years of them. Who owed, and what, and whether they paid, and when." She laid her hand flat on the sheet. "Nobody in the kingdom has honest books like Ferra's. You've made three baronies keep them. That's a base rate. There isn't another one anywhere."

The lamp on the table guttered.

It did it the way a tired stone did, at the end of a quarter, when the charge in it was running down: the light dipped, and went thin and yellow, and flickered, and came back, and dipped again. Emeric glanced at it. It had been topped up at the handcart in the spring. It was due.

Vantic did not look at it. She was looking at him, and still talking, about the Reading, about what the partners asked, and while she talked she put out her left hand and laid two fingers on the brass cup of the lamp, lightly, the way you might steady a glass on a table that someone had knocked. She did not look down. The light steadied. It came up full and even and clear and stayed there, and she took her fingers away and folded her hands on the book again, and finished her sentence.

"... which is the only question he ever asks," she said. "Whether we'll say it went badly before anyone makes us."

Emeric looked at the lamp.

He had seen that done a thousand times. He had seen it done at the handcart by every mage on the flat, a hand on a stone, a moment, and the stone full. He had seen Armand do it without breaking stride on the stair. He had never once seen it done by anybody who was not a mage.

He did not ask. He looked at the lamp for as long as it took to see it, and then he looked back at her, and she was waiting for an answer to something, and he found he had lost what.

"Say that again," he said.

"I said, I'm aware what I'm asking." She had not noticed, or had noticed and would not let him see it. "To give me those books you'd have to ask other people to open their ledgers to a creditor. To me. That costs you. Paxton owes the House nothing. Bryda owes it nothing. They'd be doing it because you asked them, and they'd know it, and they'd remember it."

"Yes."

"I wouldn't ask if there were another way to ask." She stopped. Then, after a moment, in a different voice, not quite lower, only plainer: "I've never said any of that aloud. Not the Reading. Not the sheet. Not to anybody. There's nobody to say it to where I come from, and nobody at all here."

He heard it. He heard the words of it and he heard, under them, the figure he had been waiting for, which was the size of what she was asking, and he went to the figure, because that was where he always went.

"How many years do you need?" he said. "Of each. Ten? Twenty? Grayrock's tally house has Renner's books back to his father, and they're terrible, but they're honest about being terrible, which is what you'd want. Wayford's are ours for the years we've held it, and before that they're the old reeve's, and I don't know what they're like. Milford I'd have to ask."

She looked at him for a moment.

Then she smiled, very slightly, the way she had smiled at Ferra over a cup of small beer on the autumn quarter day the year the wall closed, and he did not know what the smile was for. She drew the sheet back toward her and began to fold it.

"As many as they'll give," she said. "Twenty, if there are twenty. The bad ones most. I don't need to know who paid in the good years. Anybody can pay in a good year."

"I'll ask them," said Emeric. "Livia."

Her hands stopped on the sheet.

It was the only time he said it. He had not planned to say it at all, and when it was out he did not know why he had, except that it had seemed, for a moment, like the right price for what she had said, and he had no other coin for it. She sat with her hands still on the half-folded sheet for a long moment, looking at them. She did not correct him. She did not look up.

Then she finished folding the sheet and put it in the back of the book, and put her gloves on, one finger at a time, and stood.

"Thank you," she said. "Your Highness."

At the door she stopped, with her hand on the latch, as if she were going to say something else. She did not. She went out, and down the long room, and he heard her heels on the stair, and a little later the House's carriage on the cobbles of the gate, going.

He sat for a while in the small room with the rain on the window. The lamp burned on the table between the two empty chairs, full and even and steady, brighter than it had been since the handcart in the spring, and he looked at it and did not touch it.

Bryda answered by the Grayrock carrier in four days, in her own hand, and her letter was three questions and a yes, in that order, the way she did everything.

Who sees them, beyond the factor. What she does with a name once she has it. Whether Grayrock is told what she finds. Then a line's space. Then: If the answers are none, nothing, and yes, then yes. All of it. Renner's years too; those are the ones she wants, if she knows her business, and I think she does. B.

Emeric wrote back the answers, which were none, nothing, and yes, because he had asked Vantic all three before she left the gate and she had given him all three in one breath without having to think. The tally house books went down the river road in a cart a week later, under Grayrock's seal, with a clerk of Bryda's riding on top of them who was not to let them out of his sight until they were copied, and did not.

Wayford's were the barony's own. Ferra had them copied in the counting room. It took her clerks a fortnight and she said the old reeve's hand was the worst she had ever read and his arithmetic worse, and that she would be very interested to know what a lending house made of it.

Paxton he asked himself.

He went on the train, an hour west, the way Paxton had come to him in the autumn, and sat in Paxton's hall at Milford with the rain coming in off the new bench, and asked. He did not dress it. He told Paxton who wanted the books, and roughly why, and what it would and would not mean, and that Bryda had said yes.

Paxton listened to all of it without saying anything, with his big hands flat on his table, the way he had sat at the long-room table in the autumn with the three years of the rotation in front of him. When Emeric had finished he sat for a while longer.

"No," he said.

Emeric waited.

"Bryda's hill is yours," said Paxton. "Her books are yours, when you come to it. If she gives them, she's giving them out of your house. These are mine." He did not raise his voice. "Every man on my land has a line in that book. What he owes me and what he's paid and what year he was late and why. It's the one place in Milford where a tenant's worst year is written down, and he knows it's written down, and he knows it goes no further than my steward and me." He turned one hand over on the table. "And now you've made Milford worth lending to. The wheat, and the yards, and the rail. There'll be a lender at my gate inside five years wanting to know what my land's good for. You're asking me to show her the answer before she's asked the question. Before I've decided whether I want to borrow at all."

"She wouldn't use it against you."

"Maybe not. I don't know her. You do." Paxton looked at him steadily. "And whoever comes after her in that chair, I don't know them either, and neither do you. A book once it's read stays read." He put his hand flat again. "I'm not saying she's not honest. I'm saying my tenants' bad years aren't mine to hand to anybody's honest."

Emeric sat in Paxton's hall with the rain on the windows and found that he had no answer to that, because it was right. It was exactly right. It was the thing he would have said himself if a creditor had come to Kaldo in the first year asking to read the plague houses' arrears, and he would have been right then too.

"No," he agreed. "They're not."

Paxton looked at him a moment longer, as if he had expected more of an argument and was not sure what to do without one. Then he nodded, and got up, and poured two cups of something from a jug on the sideboard, and they drank it standing at the window looking out at the bench, where the new wheat was up green on the watered ground as far as the hedge.

Emeric wrote to Vantic that night, on the train home, in pencil: Grayrock and Wayford yes. Milford no, and he is right. E. He did not soften it, because she would have noticed if he had.

Her answer came in the weekly bag, in her own hand, in the House's form. Received. Two will do to begin. He is right. V.

The rest went where it was sent, over the weeks, the way water finds the low ground.

The white crust off the western flat went to the glassworks by the sack, and Orrin stopped asking where it came from and started asking how much there would be, and the glass that came out of Kaldo's furnace that spring was clearer and ran easier than any that had come out of it before, and the glassmen stopped swearing at it and started taking it for granted, which Emeric thought was the higher praise.

The sacks from under the east cliffs went to the powder master's yard by the nitre beds, and to the fields beyond it, a little at a time; the powder master said it was worth more in a saltpeter bed than any muck in Randillia, and Hollis, who was given a sack for the top field to see, said it was the worst-smelling thing he had ever put on a field and asked for more.

The light oil burned in the lamps in the long room and the counting room and the ward, white and clean, and went on the bearings of the line shafts in the works, where it ran thinner than tallow and did not go stiff in the cold, and in the bench engine in Bram's shop, which ran on nothing else now. The lane's chandlers noticed that Kaldo's orders for lamp oil had gone down. Saban told them the barony had been economising. That was true. It had.

The stone the colour of dried blood came out of Ferra's box at last, still in its two wrappings of oilcloth, and went into a lead-lined chest in a locked room at the works that had one key, which Bram kept, with a second chest beside it for what would come after it. Emeric told Bram what it was and what it did when you heated it and what it did to the men who breathed what came off it, and Bram listened with his arms folded and then asked only when.

"When the bulbs are ready for it," Emeric said. "When there's a pump that can empty a glass of air, all of it, and keep it empty. That needs this." He put his hand on the lid of the chest. "Not before. And never in a room with a door shut."

Bram looked at the chest for a while. Then he went and got a stencil and a pot of red lead, and painted on the lid, in letters a hand high, NOT OPENED BUT BY B., and locked the door on it.

And in the shed on the lake shore the coppers boiled from first light to dark, and the grey threads came up through the grey water, and the boy with the sieve skimmed them, and at the end of every day there was a loaf of it on the board, and then two, and then, by the time the new wheat at Milford was in the ear, three. The yield chalked on the shed wall got better every week, the way Emeric had said it would, and Bram stopped calling it bad, and then stopped chalking it at all, because it was not news any more.

It was only work. It was a shrub that grew on a flat nobody wanted, on an island that belonged, on two kings' papers, to two kings, cut at night by men in black plate and boiled in a shed by a lake in the open, where anybody could see it, and nobody who saw it had the least idea what they were looking at. Emeric walked past the shed most mornings on his way down to the yard, and the steam came out of the row of doors on the lake side, and the smell of wet sage and stewed wood came with it, and it had already become the kind of smell that nobody in Kaldo noticed any more, which was the most anything in Kaldo could hope for.
