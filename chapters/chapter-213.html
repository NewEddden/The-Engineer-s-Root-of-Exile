Chapter 213 - Harrow Bridge

The toll-house went up on Harrow Bridge on the twelfth of October, while Wat was dying.

Aster sent it down the wire that afternoon, in his narrow clerk's way, as an item between the price of rope and the state of the vat road after the rain: that Lord Lisle's people had put up a timber house that morning at the south end of Harrow Bridge, a mile below the town, on the Lisle side of the road, with a bar across the road on a post and a board nailed beside it; and that the board said a toll was now taken at the bridge by the charter of the house of Lisle, for the repair of the same, on goods under the following marks; and that the marks were Kaldo's. Only Kaldo's. A Copper on a ream. Two on a crate. A Silver on a cart. Anybody else's cart went over free.

And that the bar was kept by Lisle's toll-keeper, in Lisle's green, and three of Lisle's men; and by fourteen others, in plain brown coats with no badge on them anywhere, who were not Lisle's, and who had come down the north road the night before in two wagons, and who did not drink at the posting house, and who did not say whose they were.

Emeric read it in the telegraph room with Ferra at his shoulder. Then he went back down to the end room and sat by Wat's bed, because that was where he was that week; and he did not go north until they had buried Wat in the rain.



He paid it on the seventeenth.

He came up the capital road from the ship's clearing in the beech woods past the twelfth milestone, on a horse Aster had sent down from the posting house, alone but for Ward, because he wanted to come to the bridge the way everybody else did; and at the south end of Harrow Bridge there was the toll-house, new timber, still yellow, smelling of sap, with a stovepipe through its roof and a bar across the road on a post, painted green and white. The board beside the bar was well lettered. It was on Pell's paper, pasted to a plank. By the Charter of the House of Lisle. A Copper, two Copper, a Silver. Kaldo's marks, drawn out small and correct at the foot, the stamp off a ream and the brand off a crate, so that nobody could say they had been mistaken for anybody else's.

The toll-keeper came out in his green coat. He was an elderly man with a careful face who had been Lisle's woodward for thirty years, Aster said afterward, and had never taken a toll from anybody in his life before that week. Behind him, at the toll-house door and along the bridge rail, the men in brown stood and watched. Fourteen. Emeric counted them with his sense before he could see them all: men's notes, close and hard and awake, every one of them, turned toward the road; and in the middle of them, by the stove inside the house, a fifteenth, bright and high and thin, the note of a man with a little of the faculty and a stone in his pocket.

He had a pack horse behind him with two crates on it from the works at Kaldo, Kaldo's brand burnt into the lids, that had come up in the ship's hold. He had brought them for this.

"Two crates, Highness," said the toll-keeper, without meeting his eyes. "Four Copper. If you please."

Emeric paid it. He took the coins out of his own purse and counted them into the old man's palm, four, and waited while the toll-keeper went into the house and came back with a receipt, written out in a careful woodward's hand on a printed form; and when the toll-keeper asked, as the form required, the name of the payer, Emeric gave it to him. All of it. Every name he had, in order, the whole of what the king's chaplain had said over him in the capital thirty years ago, down to the last; and watched the old man write them, one after another, slower and slower, until he had filled the line and gone onto the next, and stopped, and looked up from the paper for the first time.

"For the file," said Emeric. "Lord Lisle's. And whoever reads it after him."

The toll-keeper looked at him. Then he looked over his shoulder at the men in brown along the rail; and back; and lifted the bar.

Ward rode through behind him without paying, because Ward had no crates, and did not look at the men in brown at all, which they noticed.

"Fourteen," said Ward, a quarter of a mile up the road toward Wayford. "And the one by the stove."

"A hedge mage. With a stone."

"Yes." Ward rode on a while. "They are not here for the toll. Four Copper." He looked straight ahead up the road. "They are here so that one day a Kaldo carter will not pay it."



Imani telephoned at ten that night, from the hospital.

He was in Sethe's counting room with Aster's books when the bell went on the instrument in the corner, the brass box on the wall with its crank and its two cups that had hung there since the line came north, and he got up and took the earpiece off its hook and put it to his ear; and there was the hum, and the hiss, and under them, far down the wire, three hundred and fifty miles of it, Imani.

"Are you in bed?" she said.

"I'm in Sethe's counting room. With the rope prices."

"Liar." Her voice came thin and small and close, all at once, as it always did on the wire, as if she were whispering it from the bottom of a well into his ear. "You're in the counting room with your boots off and your feet on Sethe's table. I can hear it in your voice. You sound like a man with his feet on a table."

He took his feet off Sethe's table.

"How are they?"

"Asleep. All fourteen. Robin asked where you'd gone and I said Wayford, and he asked if Wayford had a school yet, and I said not yet, and he said then you'd better hurry up." She was quiet a moment. "Perrin's got a little colour. Not much. Armand measured him twice to be sure. He was very pleased with himself. Armand, I mean. Perrin doesn't care."

"And you?"

"I'm standing in the corridor outside the dispensary in my nightgown, with my shawl, at the telephone, in the cold, because there's a man three hundred and fifty miles away who went north on the ship without kissing me goodbye." The hum came and went. "I was asleep. You were on the slip at five, with Ward, and I was asleep, and you didn't come up."

"You'd been up with Wat's room till two."

"I know where I'd been." Her voice dropped. "That's no excuse at all. You could have woken me. I'd have let you." She was quiet. "I'd have let you do a great deal, at five in the morning, with the house asleep. I was warm. You'd have liked it."

He leaned his forehead against the wall beside the brass box.

"Imani. I've a toll-house to deal with. And Aster's books. And a..."

"Work first," said Imani down the wire, in a perfect imitation of his own voice, flat and reasonable and a little pompous, so exact that he laughed out loud in Sethe's empty counting room. "Fun later. That's what you say. That's what you always say, and then I say it to you when you come and find me in the dispensary, and then we both stand there being very good and very busy and very cross." The hum swelled. "Well. I've done my work. I did it all day. Every bit. So it's later, here. It's later in Kaldo." She lowered her voice again, very soft and very close. "Do you want to know what I'd do, if you'd come up at five? Since you didn't? I'll tell you. Exactly. Slowly. And then you can lie awake in Sethe's bed thinking about it, which is no more than you deserve. First, I'd have..."

The line died.

Not the hum. The hum went first, cut off as clean as a thread under scissors; and then the hiss, and then nothing at all. Not silence. The silence of a wire on the telephone was full of small sounds, the hum and the crackle and the far faint ghost of other voices on other lines. This was the silence of a cup held to the ear in an empty room.

He stood with the earpiece to his ear.

"Imani?"

Nothing.

He cranked the box. He cranked it again. He stood in Sethe's counting room with the dead earpiece in his hand and his forehead still warm from the wall, three hundred and fifty miles from the end of a sentence, and said, out loud, to the brass box, with feeling, a word that he had not used since he was a student in another world.



The relay post at Harrow Bridge had been shut at ten by Lisle's steward, with a padlock.

Rylan found it at first light, riding down from Wayford with two of the column to see why the line was dead, and came back up at a canter to Sethe's hall with his face very straight. The relay post was a brick hut the size of a privy on the bank below the bridge, on the Lisle side, where the wire came down from its poles and went in at one end and out at the other, and inside it, on a shelf, the pair of stones that took the line's whisper and gave it again stronger to the next hut down the road. It stood on Lisle's land. It had stood there for two years, with Lisle's leave, written, for a rent of a Silver a quarter, which Ferra had paid every quarter day to the day.

There was a padlock on its door now, a new one, and a notice nailed below the padlock in the same good lettering as the toll board. Closed for repairs. By order of the steward of the house of Lisle.

"Repairs to what?" said Emeric.

"It doesn't say," said Rylan. He kept his face straight. "I asked the toll-keeper. He said the steward said the roof. I looked at the roof. It's a good roof. Tess's men put it on." He looked at Emeric. "Imani?"

"Mid-sentence."

Rylan nodded slowly. He looked at the ceiling of Sethe's counting room, as though considering what sentence, and then looked back at Emeric, and his face was still perfectly straight, which in Rylan was the hardest work Emeric had ever seen him do.

"She'll think you hung up," said Rylan.

She did. The ship came up from Kaldo that afternoon on the regular run, and Cade put a letter into Emeric's hand on the road below the chain, sealed with the hospital's wafer, and stood back a good distance, whistling his one tune. It was one line, in Imani's quick bad hand, with the pen pressed through the paper at the end.

If you EVER do that again I'll tell you NOTHING. Not ever. I'd only got to the part with the buttons.



The charter was worse than the padlock.

Lisle's steward brought a fair copy to Sethe's hall himself, on the twentieth, very courteously, with Lisle's compliments, and laid it on the counting room table and stood back with his hat in his hands while Ferra read it; for Ferra had come up on the ship with Imani's letter, and had read the letter first, on the road below the chain, and given it back to him with no expression whatever, and asked for the charter.

It was old. Six generations, as Lisle had said. It granted to the house of Lisle and its heirs for ever the toll of the bridge at Harrow, for the keeping of the same in good repair, at such rates as the house of Lisle should judge fit; under a king Emeric had to count back on his fingers to place, with a seal he had seen only on the oldest charters in the chancery rolls.

"For the keeping of the same in good repair," Ferra read aloud. She looked up at the steward. "The bridge at Harrow was wood when this was written."

"Yes, Chancellor."

"It went down in the great flood, forty years ago. The crown rebuilt it in stone, on new piers, twenty feet downstream, and the barony of Wayford has paid to keep it since. Sethe paid. Then we did." She put her finger on the copy. "Is it the same bridge?"

The steward looked at his hat.

"My lord's view, Chancellor," he said, "is that a bridge at Harrow is the bridge at Harrow. Whatever it is made of."

"And the rates at such as the house of Lisle should judge fit. Does that let the house of Lisle judge it fit to toll one barony's carts and not another's?"

"My lord's view is that it says what it says, Chancellor."

"And the relay post? We have his lease. Written. For a Silver a quarter."

"Repairs, Chancellor," said the steward. "My lord regrets the inconvenience."

Ferra looked at him a long while across the table. Then she rolled the copy up again and gave it back to him, and thanked him, and he went.

"Only the crown can rule it," she said, when the door had shut. "Whether it's the same bridge. Whether such rates as he judges fit means he can judge them against us and nobody else. Whether a lease of a hut is broken by a padlock that says repairs." She put her hand flat on the table. "It's a crown charter, so it's the crown's to read. It'd go to the chancery. To the rolls court. To a hearing." She looked at him. "Two years. Three. And every Kaldo cart pays at the bar till it's heard, and the line's dead at Harrow till it's heard, and the fourteen men in brown stand on the bridge till it's heard."

"And if we don't pay?"

"Then we've broken a crown charter, in front of fourteen witnesses who aren't Lisle's," said Ferra. "And somebody hits somebody." She was quiet. "That's what they're for."



Corbin offered to mediate on the nineteenth.

It came in the chancery's gazette, which was printed in the capital every week and sent to every baron in the kingdom whether he wanted it or not, and which Ferra read every week, cover to cover, because the chancery's gazette was where a kingdom told itself what it wanted to be seen to think. On the second page, under the heading of the Crown Prince's office, His Highness had observed with concern a dispute arising between the houses of Kaldo and Lisle concerning the toll at Harrow, upon the capital road, by which the king's highway was obstructed and the king's peace endangered; and His Highness, desiring that the king's peace be kept between his own brother and a loyal house of the north, offered the crown's mediation to both parties, to be undertaken by an officer of the chancery at a date to be set, and invited both to accept it.

His Grace of Aldane welcomed it two days later, in a broadsheet on the same chancery steps, in a fine new fount, without a black border this time. His Grace, as a friend of the house of Lisle, welcomed the lieutenant's wise and timely offer, and trusted that the house of Kaldo would welcome it likewise, and keep the peace of the road until the crown's officer came.

"It's a trap," said Saban, at the long table on the twenty-fifth, with the gazette and the broadsheet in front of him.

"No," said Ferra. "It's a door with nobody behind it." She turned the gazette round. "There's no date. An officer of the chancery at a date to be set. There'll never be a date. And every day there isn't, the toll stands, because the crown's said it's looking into it, and the line's dead, because the crown's said it's looking into it; and if we touch either of them, we've spat in the face of the crown's own offer of peace, in the gazette, in front of every baron in the kingdom." She put her finger on Corbin's paragraph. "And if we refuse it, we've refused the crown's peace."

"So we accept," said Emeric.

Everybody at the table looked at him.

"Without conditions," he said. "In writing. Today. To the lieutenant, and a copy to the gazette, and a copy to Lisle, and a copy to Aldane. The house of Kaldo accepts the crown's mediation with gratitude, and will keep the peace of the road until the crown's officer comes, and will pay Lord Lisle's toll in full meanwhile, under protest, with a receipt for every Copper, and will hold itself ready to meet the crown's officer at Harrow on any day he names." He looked down the table. "Every day the officer doesn't come, that letter's in the gazette with Corbin's offer above it and Aldane's welcome under it, and our acceptance at the bottom with no conditions in it. And everybody who reads the gazette can count the days."

Ferra looked at him for a breath. Then she took out her pencil.

They wrote it that afternoon. It went in the gazette in the first week of November. The house of Kaldo accepted.

The crown's officer never came.



Saban took the toll receipt to Marrow.

He did not ask anybody's leave. He went down to the old strongroom under the east tower on the twenty-sixth, where Marrow had been held since September, in a stone room with a cot and a table and a lamp and a shelf of books from the keep's library, because Saban had been going down every week since September with a book and a bottle of small beer and an hour to spare, and sitting with the man, and talking about nothing; and Marrow, who had come out of the strongroom at Wayford torn, and sat for a fortnight in Kaldo without saying a word, had begun, in October, to say one true thing at a time.

Saban put the receipt on the table between them, the one with all of Emeric's names on it in the old woodward's careful hand. He told Emeric afterward that he had not said anything about it. He had simply put it there, as he put down the beer.

Marrow read it.

He read it slowly, the whole of it, the printed form and the names and the four Copper, by the light of his lamp, with his clean hands flat on either side of it; and then he sat back on his cot, and looked at Saban with the expression of mild, polite, intelligent interest that he had had in the ash in Wayford, and that he had again, now, most days. And he nodded, as if a sum had come out.

"He'll make you hit first," said Marrow.

That was all. Saban waited an hour, and drank his beer, and talked about the weather, and Marrow said nothing else.



Saban's other news came the next day, from his man in the capital, folded in a bale of cloth.

His Grace of Aldane's quartermasters were buying crystals. Not good ones. Big ones, the size of a man's fist and more, clouded, cracked, badly cut, the cheap flawed stuff that came off the edges of the old workings in the east and that no stone-cutter in the capital would touch for a lamp or a warmer because it would not hold a charge evenly and burst if it was pushed. Hundreds of them. They were buying all they could find, from every stone merchant in the capital and three on the coast, and paying above the price for it, a third above, without bargaining, which no quartermaster in the history of the world had ever done. And the stones went north out of the capital by night, in closed carts, with men in plain brown on the boxes.

"Cheap stones," said Saban. He laid the page on the long table. "Bought dear. By the hundred." He looked at the page with his commercial face, the face he priced things with, and Emeric saw that for once it had nothing to price. "I don't know what he wants them for. My man doesn't. Nobody buys bad stone at a good price, Highness. Not unless what he wants it for doesn't need it to be good."

Ward read the page, standing.

He did not say anything. He put the page down and went out of the long room, and Emeric heard him go down the keep stair, two steps at a time, which Ward did not do.



The drill began the next morning, in the quarry.

Ward had not explained. He had come back up the stair the night before with a list and given it to Declan, and in the morning the whole garrison went out through the north gate at six in full plate, the crestless plate, Bram's, a hundred and twenty men of it, with the forty of Ward's levy behind them in their department coats with the new rifles on their shoulders and Fennick's cartridges in their pouches, and Vesk walking at the back of the column with a canvas bag in his hand. Emeric went with them. Ward had told him to.

In the bottom of the quarry north of the wall, on the floor Fennick's rows had blasted flat that summer, Ward stood them in their ranks on the rubble, and turned round, and said it.

"Every mage Aldane can hire," said Ward, "can break a stone."

The quarry was quiet. The ranks stood.

"One stone. Broken, by a man who does not care what it costs him. And every stone in a field goes dark. Every lamp, every suit, every glyph, every band." He looked along the ranks. "Every knight in this quarry keeps what is inside him. Nothing else. The ship falls. The lamps go out. The wire is dead. His Highness cannot feel you." He did not look at Emeric. "And you cannot be told where to stand by anybody who can." He was quiet. "I was in one in August. Some of you were. In a yard in Wayford. I did not like what it did to us."

He nodded to Vesk.

Vesk opened his canvas bag and took out a stone. A cheap one, clouded and cracked, no bigger than a walnut, bought off a stone merchant in Wayford the week before for a few Silver: Ward had chosen its size with Vesk the night before, so that the dead air would fill the bowl of the quarry and stop at its rim, short of the lamps on the north wall. The same kind, Emeric thought, looking at it, as Saban's page; and something in his stomach turned over. Vesk held it in both hands against his chest, the way Vantic had held hers by the rack, and shut his eyes, and pushed.

It took longer than it had taken her. Vesk was a hedge mage, and the stone was a poor one and did not want to fill evenly; and for a long minute nothing happened but Vesk's face going grey and his lips drawing back from his teeth. Then the crack. Small, like a cup in another room.

And the wind that moved nothing.

It went through the quarry and through Emeric and out over the rim and away across the fields, and behind it the world went flat. Every note in the quarry, gone: a hundred and twenty men in plate and forty in coats, Ward and Declan and Rylan and Osric, gone, all of them, as if a hand had been put over his eyes. The beads on every band in the ranks went dead. He stood on the rubble in the bottom of the quarry among a hundred and sixty men he could see and not feel, and was blind.

"Again," said Ward. "The drill. From the top."

They ran it all morning. The garrison in their plate, with the handful of knights among them carrying what was inside them and nothing else, in the dead air; stepping, wheeling, holding their line by ear and by elbow, Ward's voice going up and down the ranks, flat, telling them where to stand because nothing else could. The levy behind them, kneeling on the rubble in three ranks, with the rifles. And at Ward's word, the first rank fired.

It was the loudest sound Emeric had ever heard in a dead zone. In the cellar there had been nothing; on the north road, the clash of steel. Here, a dozen rifles went off together in the bottom of a stone bowl, and the sound came back off the quarry walls a heartbeat later, and the smoke rolled up white over the kneeling men and hung there in the still air; and on the far face of the quarry, a hundred paces off, a dozen chips of stone jumped off the painted marks at once.

The second rank fired. The third. The first rank, reloaded, fired again.

No stone in the quarry worked. No suit, no lamp, no glyph, no band. And the rifles fired, every one, every time, with Fennick's powder in Bram's brass, because powder was not magic. It was saltpetre and charcoal and sulphur, and it burned because it burned, and it did not care in the slightest what anybody in a hundred miles had done to a stone.

Ward stood at the end of the ranks with his hands behind his back and watched the far face of the quarry through the smoke.

"Powder does not care," he said, to nobody in particular.

They came out of the dead air at noon, up the quarry track, and at the rim, between one step and the next, the world came back into Emeric all at once, every note, a hundred and sixty of them, like hearing after a bell; and he stopped on the track with his hand over his eyes, as he had in the yard at Wayford.

Ward had stopped beside him.

"Every week," said Ward. "A stone a week, until whatever it is happens. You will come every time." He looked down into the quarry, where the smoke was still lying on the rubble. "You will learn to fight without it. You did it on the north road for half a mile. Half a mile is not enough."



The tattoos went on the levy in the last week of October.

Forty of them, Rylan's choosing, from Ward's levy: the forty who had shot best at the butts and kept their heads best in the quarry, and who had, every one, when Rylan sat them down in the hospital's cutting room and explained it to them, plainly, the needle and the ink and the bead and the cost, said yes. Farmers' sons, most of them. A cooper's boy from the river street. A carter. Arthur, Sela's youngest, who had said yes before Rylan finished explaining, and been told to sit down and listen to the rest, and had sat down, and listened, and said yes again.

Imani drew them. She would let nobody else put a needle into a man in her barony, she had said in the fifth year when Rylan had the first, and she had not changed her mind; but forty glyphs at three hours a glyph was a hundred and twenty hours, and she had fourteen people in the end room and a hospital besides. So she drew the first ten herself, under the north window, with two of her nurses watching every line; and drew the next ten with the nurses' hands on the needles and her own over them; and the last twenty she sat beside, with her chin on her fist, and watched every stroke the nurses made, and stopped them twice, and made one of them start a forearm again from the first line, and was not sorry.

The glyph was the one Rylan wore, from Emeric's drawing, the size of a palm on the inside of the left forearm, black and raised and sore, with its thread of ink down to the wrist and under the edge of a band to the bead. The ink was the cousin of Kaldo black, cut thinner, the black that held a charge. Every man went out of the cutting room with his arm bound and his band on and a page in his pocket in Ferra's hand, which said what it was and what it cost and that he was not to show it to anybody outside the barony, and not to anybody inside it who did not already know. The table's rule. Ward's above it. A commoner with a precast glyph on his own skin, fed from a stone on his own wrist, was the thing the Church had spent a thousand years making sure no commoner had.

And then Emeric and Rylan taught them to feather it.

In the keep yard, at night, with the gate shut and Ward on it, as Rylan had first learned it under the lamp three years before: the forty in a row along the wall, and the sand sack hanging from its post at the far end, forty feet off, and Rylan in front of them with his own old glyph on his arm, much redrawn.

"Don't run," said Rylan. "Everybody runs, the first time. You feel it come and you think, now I'm fast, and you go, and you're at the sack and through it and into the wall, and I'll be the one picking you up." He held up his arm. "Ten goes in this, new. Maybe twelve. Then it's grey, and you need another drawn, and that's three hours in the cutting room with the healer looking at you as if you'd done it on purpose." He was quiet. "So you don't run. You step."

He showed them. Emeric threw the pebble, and Rylan was a foot to the left of where it went, and had not seemed to move.

"One step," said Emeric, to the row along the wall. "Half of one. The step that closes the last of the gap. Or gets you out of the way of the thing coming. That's all it's for." He looked along the row of faces in the lamplight, forty young men with sore arms and their bands on their wrists, very serious. "A man who spends his ten in running across a yard has spent them. A man who spends a tenth of one at a time has a hundred. Feather it."

They feathered it. Most of them ran, the first time, as Rylan had said they would, and two of them went through the sack and into the wall, and Rylan picked them up. By the end of the night, most of them could take the half step and stop. A few could do it twice. Arthur, at the end of the row, did it four times running, a half step and stop and a half step and stop, and his glyph was barely grey; and at the fourth Ward, at the gate, said, "Again," in the voice he used for the turn at five in the morning, and Arthur did it again.

Emeric watched Sela's son take the half step under the lamp, and stop, and stand with his arm at his side, breathing, very pleased with himself and trying not to show it; and thought of a school room on a Saturday, and a row of slates drying, and a woman watching a column of twos go by the window.



Fennick came to Saban on the twenty-ninth with a name.

He came to the counting room at noon, to Saban's end of Ferra's table, in his powder-house apron with the brass buttons, which was the only apron in Kaldo with brass buttons instead of iron, and stood there turning his cap in his maimed hand, which he did when he was going to say something that was not about powder.

A man had come to the posting house on the river street the night before, he said, and asked for him. A northern man, by his speech, decently dressed, with money. He had bought Fennick a drink, and then another, and talked about fireworks, and about the king's birthday displays, and about the old days at the royal makers, and how a man who had blown the roof off the royal powder house twice was a man with a gift, a rare gift, wasted in a southern barony making cartridges in a quarry for a prince who would never let him do anything interesting. And then he had said that there were people in the north who would let him do something very interesting indeed. A good deal of money. A house of his own, with nobody holding the key to it.

"I said I'd think," said Fennick. "Then I came and told you." He looked at Saban. "I don't want to think. I've thought. Ward gives me the key every morning and takes it every night, and I've never once asked for it, and I'm not going to start." He turned his cap. "I just wanted you to have his name. In case."

He gave it. Saban wrote it down in the small book he never let anybody see, and thanked him, and did not ask him anything else; and when Fennick had gone, Saban sat for a while with the book shut under his hand and his commercial face gone quite still.

"That's the second time somebody from the north has come asking for him," he said to Emeric. "The first was the man with the blue ring, two summers back. He knew about Abel's hand. Half the south knew about Abel's hand." He was quiet. "This one knew about the key. Nobody holding the key to it. That was said at the long table on the fifteenth of September, with the door shut, and nowhere else." He put the book in his coat. "I'd like to know who told him."



The light in the works was on at midnight on the last night of October.

Emeric saw it from the wall walk, coming back from the gate tower and the dead line, with his collar up against the wind off the river. Not the long shop, where the presses were, or the turbine house: a single window at the end of the drafting room, on the upper floor, small and yellow, where nobody had any reason to be at midnight on a Friday when the night shift was on the rolling mill at the other end of the works.

He knew whose window it was before he got there. He felt them from the bridge. Tess's note, quick and fine and particular, a woman who measured things with her own body; and beside it, very close, a big warm careless note, spilling, the way it always spilled, Osric's.

He went up the outside stair at the end of the drafting room as quietly as he had ever gone anywhere, and stopped on the landing, in the dark, at the side of the window, and looked in.

Tess was at her drafting board, in her chair, with the board tilted low the way she worked at it, and a lamp on the board's top edge, and a sheet of the school's paper pinned to the board under the lamp. Osric was on a stool beside her chair, hunched over the board with his elbows on his knees, enormous, in his shirt sleeves, with a pencil in his fist held the way a man holds a pencil who has held a sword a great deal longer. He had his tongue between his teeth.

On the sheet, in Tess's square careful hand, a row of letters across the top. And under it, in a hand that was not careful at all, and was trying very hard to be, the same row again, and again, and again, down the page.

Tess put her finger on one.

"That's a d," she said. "Not a b. The round goes the other way." She took his hand, pencil and all, in hers, and moved it, slowly, round the shape, and let go. "Like that. A d's got its belly in front of it, like you."

Osric looked at the d. He looked at her.

"That's not kind," he said.

"It's not meant to be kind. It's meant to be remembered." She tapped the sheet. "Again. And then you're going to read me the notice."

Pinned at the side of the board, under the first sheet, Emeric could see the edge of another: Ambry's notice from Wayford, the one with the black cross and the cow and the open door, and Ferra's one line in the biggest letters Ambry had. Osric looked at it, sideways, the way a man looks at a horse he has been told he will have to ride.

"I can read," he said. "My name. Figures. A muster roll and a pay sheet and a bill at the posting house. A page, if nobody's in a hurry." He was quiet. "Thirty-two years, and never needed more."

"You read Ferra's conditions out to Armand at the long table in May," said Tess, "and she corrected you twice, and you guessed the rest, and you went red to the ears." She did not look up from the sheet. "And you stood in front of that notice on the posting house door in Wayford for a quarter of an hour in August with your lips moving, and then you asked Cade what it said."

"I was checking."

"You were not checking." She pinned the sheet flatter. "And then you trod the bellows in that doorway while a boy of twelve asked the whole of Wayford if he could go to school, and you came out of it with a face." She was quiet. "So I found out. And you said, on condition nobody knows. So nobody knows." She pushed the pencil back into his fist. "And you need more. Again."

Osric looked at her a moment longer, in the lamplight.

Then he bent over the board, and put his tongue between his teeth, and made a d, slowly, with its belly in front.

Emeric went back down the stair in the dark without a sound, and along the river street to the bridge, and did not tell anybody, then or after; because it was a condition, and it was not his.
