Chapter 129 - Cargo Home

They came down on Emberfell's harbour in the middle of the afternoon, in the open, where she had gone up.

There was no reason to hide it any more. The whole town had watched her go up two days before, with its sovereign riding beside her rail in snow and bright steel, and so Ward brought her down out of the band over the inlet in full view and let her onto the harbour water in the middle of the basin, where Rowena had floated, and the bells began on the harbour wall before her screw was down. They were not alarm bells. Somebody had decided the first time that it was a thing that wanted bells, and had evidently decided it again.

Rowena stepped off at her own quay.

She went down the ladder onto the black stone in her padded jacket, with the blanket left folded on the saloon table behind her, and her knights were drawn up on the quay as they had been on the first day, two lines, helms on, hands on hilts, nothing drawn. The harbour-mistress was at the end of the nearer line. The knight-captain was beside her. Rowena walked down between the lines to them, and the harbour-mistress said something to her that Emeric could not hear from the rail, and it was short, and Rowena nodded.

Ferra told him afterward what it had been, because the harbour-mistress had said the same to her in the office an hour later, as a figure. Nothing had happened. The harbour had opened and shut. The dues had come in. The table had sat at noon both days, by office, and settled what came before it, and put off one thing until the sovereign was back, and that one thing could wait another week. Nobody had asked where she was. Nothing had happened at all.

That was the point. Emeric saw Rowena hear it, at the end of the lines, and saw her face not do anything, and saw what it cost her not to let it.

Her knights carried the suit up to the hall on its frame of poles, the way Lorcan and Hesta had brought it down, with the plume nodding and the lace still stiff with the salt of her harbour. Hesta walked beside it with her hand on the frame. Tobe had wanted to come too, to see it off. He stood at the rail and watched it go up the slope instead, and did not say anything, and went below.

Rowena came back along the quay to the foot of the ladder when the suit had gone, alone, without her tall knight, and stood there, and Emeric came down the ladder to her.

She did not make a speech. She was not a woman who made them.

"Your sulphur boats," she said. "When they come. Come with them."

"I'll come."

"Not in that." She looked past him at the ship, at the stack with nothing coming out of it. "In a boat. Slowly. Like anybody. So I can see you coming up the inlet for a day and a half and have time to decide what to say."

"I'll come in a boat."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she said it, so low that it did not reach the rail, and nobody on the rail or the quay heard it but him.

"Go home, Emmy."

She turned and went up the slope between her knights, and did not look back, and Emeric stood at the foot of the ladder until Ward said his name from the rail, and then went up.

Ferra and Imani came aboard an hour later, and Ferra would not let her go until everything that was going with her had been over a scale.

She meant everything. The casks came up out of the forepeak one at a time on the tackle, Rowena's two and the five from the ice, and the crate of pitch cakes with the seal still on it, and went over the harbour's great beam scale at the head of the quay, with the harbour-mistress's clerk at the weights and the same crowd that had watched her weigh her own people off two days before. Fletcher stood at the foot of the tackle with his arms folded while his cargo went up out of his ship and came back down into it, and said nothing, because it was Ferra, and she had signed for every rivet.

She wrote them down in a column. Seven casks, a crate. She added the column, and set it against the figure she had carried in her book since the beach: ten tons of coal left in a heap under a sail, and ten tons of room.

It did not come near the ten tons. It did not come near half.

"All of it," Ferra said. "Home."

She wrote it in, the lot, the gift in its own line with *gift* in the column beside it and the five casks in theirs with the price Saban gave her, and she did not ask where the five had come from, because Saban had given her the price and the price was where they had come from as far as her book was concerned. Nothing was left on the quay for the sulphur boats to fetch.

They lifted at the end of the afternoon, and filled, and turned east along the coast for home.

Vantic wrote her letter at the saloon table.

It was the week's letter. Emeric had known she wrote one; everybody in Kaldo knew it. She had written one line a week to her House for six years, on the same day, whatever had happened and whether anything had, and every one of them had gone, and Ferra had once used the fact of it to win an argument with the Milford road. This week's day had come and gone while she was at a port two miles over a northern sea, three hours and a night from any courier in the world. She had not written it then. She wrote it now, at the saloon table, under the lamp, with her book open beside the sheet and her small careful hand going.

She dated it for the day it should have gone.

Ferra was at the other end of the same table with the fair copies of the terms, checking them one last time against each other before they went into her strongbox. She looked up when Vantic dated the sheet. Emeric saw her look up. She looked at the date for exactly as long as it took to read it upside down, and then back at the terms, and she did not say anything, and Vantic did not look up, and did not say anything either.

Emeric read the rest of it.

He did not mean to. He was sitting at the table across from her with nothing in his hands, and she had written it in the open, at a table where anybody could read it upside down, and he had read Ferra upside down for ten years and could not stop himself doing it to anybody else. It was short. It said that the borrower's accounts were sound, and the security in good condition. It said that the borrower had concluded terms with the sovereign of Emberfell for a lane, by sea, of which the House would be given particulars when the lane opened. It said that the undersigned had been present at the signing and had found nothing in the terms that touched the covenant.

That was all.

There was nothing in it about a ship. There had never been anything in any of her letters about a ship, so far as he knew. And there was nothing in it about a night's flight to a foreign coast in the dead of the year, or a queen of Randillia at a port above three kingdoms, or five casks bought off men who did not own what they sold. There was not a line about Duvrenne. There was not a word.

*It will be in the report*, she had said, in the day room, on the way down, red to the ears. And he had said, *Will it?* And she had said no.

Vantic sanded the sheet and folded it and sealed it with her own seal, and put it in her book, and closed the book. She looked up then, at last, and found him looking at her.

Neither of them said anything. She went forward to the day room with her book held flat against her coat, and Ferra went on checking the terms, and nobody at that table said a word about any of it, then or afterward.

Ferra came and found him later, in the day room, at a port.

She did not say anything. She took a folded sheet out of her book and put it on the rail of the port in front of him, and waited, the way she did when a figure had come out the way she had wanted it to.

It was a berthing warrant. It was Emberfell's, for the ship, for the two days she had lain at the black quay without them, at the new dues under the terms. At the foot of it was a clerk's name, and under the clerk's name, in the same hand:

*for the Sovereign, in her absence.*

He read it twice.

"It's working," he said.

"It was working before we came," said Ferra. "It only needed somebody to tell her it was the thing she wanted." She took the warrant back and folded it into the book again. "The table sat both days. Nobody asked where she'd gone."

She stood beside him at the port for a while after that, looking out at the coast going by in the dusk, and she did not say anything else, and he had the sense that there was a great deal more in the book behind the warrant than the warrant, and that she was not going to give it to him tonight. He did not ask. She had never once let a thing go unanswered in her book, and whatever it was, it would be answered when she chose, in writing, dated.

It was somewhere over the coast, in the dark, that he worked out what he had said.

He was at the chart table under the caged lamp with the notebook open, meaning to write down the difference in smell between the two oils while he still had it. He had written a line of it. And then he was sitting with the pen over the page not writing, because he had come to the word *dream* in his own head on the way to something else, and it had stopped him.

*A woman I saw in a dream.*

He had said it on the third night, in the small room off her hall, with a jug on the table. He had chosen it. He had said it on purpose, easily, because it was a lie that cost nothing, and he had closed a door with it.

It had not been a door he closed. It had been a door he opened.

In ten years in Kaldo he had never once told anybody where anything came from. He had built things and let people wonder. Bram had wondered, and Ferra, and Armand, and Maren over her recipes, and every one of them had filed it and let it be, because he had never given them anything to hold. And on the third night at Emberfell he had given the first explanation he had ever offered for anything, out loud, at a dinner, to a foreign sovereign who was also one of his father's queens, with fifteen people at the table and a girl stacking plates at the end of it. And it had been a good one. Simple, and easy to carry, and the kind a person remembers. Things came to him in dreams. Women and gods and islands. A queen who put a king's head in a skin of blood.

And if a man dreamed of women and gods, a man could dream of anything.

He thought of Armand, at the end of the long table, in the voice he used for a thing everybody knew. He thought of Vesk, standing a pace behind Armand all month at the foot of the keep stair, watching the sealed letter go into Armand's coat and then looking at nothing. If it ever came to either of them, by any road, that the baron of Kaldo said things came to him in dreams, they would not think of islands. They would think of the printer. They would think of the forge that made plate with no hammer on it, and of the black ship, and of a stone that could hold a man's thought, and they would take the word *dream* and put it over every one of those, one after another, like a stone into a housing, and see whether it fitted.

His own people had heard it as a story. That was the whole of it for them. Osric had made a joke about it before the lamps were out, and Saban had asked whether the island had iron, and Ferra had counted the women in it and said so down the table, drily, and the table had laughed and gone on to the next one. Nobody on Kaldo's side of that table had taken it for an answer, because none of them had ever been given an answer before and would not have recognised one.

Rowena had taken it for an answer. She had asked for the dream and been given it and had not asked a single question about where it came from, which was not because she had stopped wondering.

And there had been a woman at the end of that table with a book beside her plate, who wrote one line a week to a House two hundred miles away, and who had heard every word of it.

And he had never given Ferra a reason for anything. Ten years, and not one, and when he finally produced one it had been for somebody else, and Ferra had heard it in passing, at a foreign table, and made a joke of it.

He turned back to the column at the back of the notebook, the one for the things he could not answer. There were a good many lines in it. The last three were about the band: a box of it, and dead air, and a thing denser than a stone.

Under them he wrote one word.

*Dream.*

He looked at it for some time. Then he closed the notebook and put it in his coat, and did not open it again that night.

They came home in the snow.

They came down at the lower water, well out, the way they always came down, a notch at a time on the levers in the dark, and they crossed Kaldo's coast in the dark and high, so that nobody on the lower water looked up and saw anything but snow falling. At the height of a man Fletcher said *now*. She sat down on the sea, and steamed in through the gap in the bar on her boiler's stones with nothing from her stack, and lay in the lagoon behind the bar where she had lain at midwinter, with snow settling on her roof.

The steamboat was at the jetty with steam up. Somebody on the beach had seen her lamps come through the gap, and the whole of Fletcher's yard was down at the water with lanterns.

He could not wait.

He knew he ought to. He ought to go up the river to the keep and see Armand first, and take back the sealed letter unopened, and hear what had happened in a week, and eat something, and sleep. Imani told him so on the jetty in exactly those words, in that order. He heard her. He had one of Rowena's casks off the steamboat's deck at the landing and onto a handcart before anybody could stop him, and was pushing it through the snow toward the foundry, with Ward walking beside him and not offering to push, because Ward had seen his face.

The forge was lit. It was always lit at night. Bram was in the foundry yard under the lamp by the door with his coat on over his apron, as though he had been waiting, and possibly he had.

He smelled it before the lid was off.

Emeric saw him smell it. The handcart came in through the gate and Bram lifted his head, the way a dog lifts its head at a gate, and his face changed, and he looked at the cask on the cart and then at Emeric.

"That's not tar," Bram said.

"No."

Emeric knocked the bung out with the heel of his hand, and the smell came up out of the cask into the cold yard, heavy and old, like a road in summer, and Bram bent over it and breathed it in, and straightened up, and looked at him.

"What is it?"

So Emeric told him, in the snow, standing by the cart, in order.

"Grease," he said. "Grease that won't burn off a bearing. Not tallow, that goes rank and runs when it's hot. This won't. For the engine, and the locomotive, and your own forge's axles, that you pack every week and swear at." He put his hand on the cask. "That's the first. The second's lamp oil. If you boil this the right way, in a still, the light part comes off it first, and the light part burns in a lamp as clean as anything you've ever seen. The glassworks can make the still. I'll draw it." He could hear himself going faster. "And a lamp of oil doesn't care what the medium's doing. It burns in a dead zone. It burns when every stone in the world has gone out."

Bram did not say anything. He was looking at the cask.

"And the heavy end," said Emeric, "the part that won't boil off, makes a road. You heat it and mix it with stone and lay it down hot and roll it, and it sets, and the rain runs off it, and it doesn't wash out in the thaw. You could lay the long street with it. You could lay the road to Grayrock."

He stopped, because he had run out of breath and not of things.

Bram looked at him.

Emeric knew what Bram was looking at. It was his own face. In ten years Bram had seen him like this perhaps twice, and he was seeing it a third time now, in the snow, by a handcart, over a cask, and Emeric could see him note it and put it away. He suspected that his face, at that moment, was doing a great many things it had not been given permission to do.

"How much is there?" said Bram.

"Not enough."

Bram looked at the cask a moment longer, and then round the yard, at the snow coming down in the lamplight, and at the dark shape of the foundry, and at the forge's glow in the pattern store window.

"Then it's a start," he said.

And he went to find a clean pail.

Emeric stood in the yard by the cart and waited for him, with the snow settling on his shoulders and on the cask, and Ward stood a little way off by the gate and watched him wait, and did not say anything, which from Ward was a great deal.

Armand was on the keep stair when he came up at last, near midnight, in the cold, with his hands folded on the head of his stick.

He took the sealed letter out of his coat and gave it back, unopened, as he had given it back after the river and after the coast. Then he said that there was nothing to report, which was not quite true, and then that there was one thing, which was.

"Declan held the wall," Armand said. "Every day. In his suit, with the cloak over it, the length of the western stretch and back, at first light and at dusk." He said it in the voice he used for a thing everybody already knew and somebody ought to say anyway. "Nobody came. But if they had, there was a man on the wall who could have been anywhere on it in the time it takes to shout, and you were five hundred miles off, in the air."

He did not say what he meant by it, and Emeric did not need him to. It was the first defence Kaldo had ever had that did not need Emeric in the room.

He went up to bed.

Far to the west, on the long grey sea between the lower water and Emberfell, a coaster with salt in her hold was beating along the coast in the winter dark, a week out still from the smoking mountain. In a case in her master's cabin, under Kaldo's seal and the Chancellor's, was a letter to the sovereign of Emberfell, in Ferra's best hand, presenting the barony's respects, and announcing that its holder would call on her at her harbour at midwinter by sea, with a small party, on a matter of trade.
