Chapter 129 - The Fourth Set

Rylan's suit had stood on its frame in the pattern store since the thirty-eighth night.

It stood at the end of the row, black, finished, with its cape furled and its toggle at safe and its helm on the shelf above it, and it had stood there through the voyage and through the rest of midwinter and on into the dead weeks after, when the snow lay on the island and the lower water froze at its edges and nobody went anywhere they did not have to. Nobody had put a name on the frame. Ferra had put a name in the forge's book, in the fourth line, in exactly the same hand as the other three, and under it there was no date, because a suit was entered in that book with the date it was fitted, and it had not been fitted.

Rylan had not come to look at it.

He had not come in the autumn, when the forge began on the plates, or at midwinter, when the others went down the river in theirs. He had stood his watches and taken his postings and eaten in the long room with the garrison, and he had said what he was asked to say and nothing else, the way he had said nothing else for a long time now. He did not ask for the suit. Osric had said he would not, twice, once in a doorway and once on the hold bench, and Osric had been right both times. Emeric had not asked him either. Very little had been said between the two of them since the stair that was not an order or the answer to one, and neither of them had ever pretended otherwise, and Emeric did not see how a suit of plate was going to be the first thing.

So it stood there.

Declan flew every day. He had flown every day since the fitting, through the whole of the voyage and every day after it, first light and dusk, the length of the western stretch of the wall and back, with the cloak drawn over the plate and the hood up. Ward and Osric had come back to theirs, and Emeric to his. The four of them went up off the wall in the snow some mornings and some evenings, and the town had stopped stopping in the street to look, and the children who had followed them to the gate in the first week followed something else now. And Rylan stood his watch on the wall under them, and did not look up, or looked up and did not let anybody see him do it.

Emeric came down to the pattern store one morning at the end of the winter before the bell.

He came most mornings at that hour, because the forge ran through the night and there was always something on it in the morning that wanted looking at before the day took him. It was dark still outside. The forge was drawing down, the glow in the cup going from white to yellow as the night's work finished, and the room smelled of hot iron and the sand, and the lamp by the door was lit.

Rylan was standing in the middle of the sand.

He was standing where the fittings were done, on the raked sand under the belay blocks, where Ward had stood, and Declan, and Osric, and Emeric himself. He was in his shirt and his breeches and his stockings, and his boots were by the wall, set side by side. He had his arms out from his sides, a little away from his body, the way a man stands to be measured. He was not looking at the door. He was looking straight ahead of him, at nothing, at the wall.

He did not say anything when Emeric came in.

Emeric stopped in the doorway. He stood there for about as long as it takes to draw a breath and let it out, and looked at Rylan standing on the sand with his arms out, and understood what it was.

It was the asking. It was the whole of it. It was all the asking there was ever going to be, and it had cost a man who did not ask for things everything he had to come this far and stand here, before the bell, in his shirt, in a cold room, where the only person who might walk in was the one person in Kaldo he would least want to walk in.

Emeric did not say anything either. He shut the door behind him against the cold, and went to the end of the row, and took the sabatons off the frame.

He fitted him plate by plate.

He did it the way the others had been fitted, in the same order, without hurrying and without slowing. The sabatons first, flat, and he knelt on the sand and did up the straps at the ankles and felt the heel nodes seat against the bone. Then the greaves. The cuisses, over the thighs, with the straps at the back. He did not say what any of it was. Rylan had never come to a fitting. But he had stood his watches on the wall under the four of them all winter, and he knew what every piece was for as well as anybody who had worn one.

The fauld, and the backplate, with the heart in the yoke behind its violet pane and the long flat pack down the spine beneath it. The breastplate over it, with the front heart, and Emeric closed it and felt the front heart wake, and felt Rylan feel it, a very small change in how he stood. The pauldrons. At each shoulder the lion's head over the cape's catch, with its glass eyes and nothing behind them. The vambraces, and the gauntlets, and he held each of Rylan's hands in turn and put it into the gauntlet and did up the cuff, and neither of them looked at the other while he did it.

It was very quiet. The forge ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside a cart went by in the snow, and a dog, and then nothing.

He was aware, the whole time, of what they were not saying.

The man on the sand had stood on a stair once, at the end of a long night, for a purpose Emeric had never reported to the Crown, and Emeric had dated that decision in the back of a book that nobody but himself would ever read. That man was standing on the sand now with his arms out while Emeric buckled a gauntlet onto his hand. Nothing had been repaired. Nothing was going to be repaired by a gauntlet. If Emeric had tried to say anything now, anything at all, it would have been a lie, or the thing itself, and he did not have a third thing, and neither did Rylan, and they both knew it. So they did not say anything, and it was the most honest the two of them had been with each other since the stair.

The cape last. He lifted it off the frame and settled it on the two catches at the shoulders, one and then the other, and felt each one take, and drew the ash battens down straight, and ran the cords down the arms to the wrists and down the legs to the heels and made them fast. He did not close it round him as a cloak. He let it hang open down Rylan's back, the way a man wears it who is about to go up.

He took the helm down off the shelf and held it out.

Rylan took it in both hands. He looked at it. It was a winged helm, like the others, black, with the violet glass in the visor. He looked at it for some time, turning it a little in his hands so that the lamp moved along the wings. Then he put it under his arm.

He moved then, for the first time, of his own accord. He found the levers without looking, both of them, the way Ward had found them the first time in this room, and his hands closed on them and opened again. He found the notch at the back of the left one. He put his hand on the pull at his breast, and on the toggle behind it, and threw the toggle to armed and back to safe, and the sound of it was very loud in the quiet room.

Then he stood still again, in the black plate, with the helm under his arm, and looked at the wall.

There was a man in the doorway.

Emeric did not know how long he had been there. The door had opened behind him at some point without his hearing it, and let a little of the cold in, and shut again, and it was Declan, in his own suit, with the cloak over it and the hood back and snow on his shoulders, come down off the wall from the first flight of the morning to put his suit on its frame. He had stopped in the doorway with his hand still on the latch.

He had seen all of it, or enough. He was looking at Rylan on the sand in the black plate, and at the lions at his shoulders, and at the helm under his arm.

Declan said nothing.

He had been fitted in this room in the winter, and he had walked the length of it three times in his suit without being asked, and tried both levers and the notch and the pull and the toggle twice over, and put his helm on and taken it off and put it on again to see how the wings looked from the side. He had looked at himself in the pane of his own front heart and in the smoked glass of the forge's port and in the bright blade of Bram's shears. He had never in his life walked into a room with something to say and not said it. He stood in the doorway now with his hand on the latch and a great deal to say, visibly, all of it at once, and did not say one word of any of it, and for Declan it was a considerable effort.

Rylan did not look at him. After a moment he walked, in the plate, across the sand to the door, and Declan stood aside to let him through, and Rylan went out into the snow in the grey light with the cape open down his back and the helm under his arm, toward the wall.

Declan looked at Emeric.

Emeric did not say anything to him either.

Declan went and stood in front of his own frame, and began to take his suit off, and did not say anything the whole time he was doing it, and went out.

The bell went.

Emeric stood alone in the pattern store with the forge ticking behind him and looked along the row.

There had been six frames in the row since the autumn. One of them was empty. It had held a suit in bright steel that had gone down the river in a crate at midwinter, and gone up a slope in a foreign town on a harbour cart, and come back down it the next morning on a frame of poles with a plume on the helm and lace at the back and snow worked along the cloak, and fallen out of the sky over a harbour, and floated. It was not coming back. It had been a gift.

The other five were black. Ward's, and Osric's, and his own, back from the voyage with the salt cleaned off them. Declan's, still wet at the shoulders from the snow off the wall. And the one at the end, whose frame stood empty now too, for an hour, because the suit that belonged on it was out on the western wall in the first light with a man inside it who had not asked for it and had stood on the sand with his arms out.

The forge had made nothing else all winter. One forge, one thing at a time. That had been the whole of the argument, in the autumn, for why there would be six of these and not fifty, and it had held.

Ferra came in at the second bell, with the forge's book under her arm, because she came in every morning at the second bell to enter the night's work.

She looked at the empty frame at the end of the row. She did not ask where the suit was. She could see the wall from the stair, and she had come down the stair.

She opened the book on the hold bench to the page with the six lines on it, and dipped her pen, and in the fourth line, against Rylan's name, in the column where the date of fitting went, which had stood empty since the thirty-eighth night, she wrote the date.

Then she blotted it, and turned to the night's work, and read him the figure.
