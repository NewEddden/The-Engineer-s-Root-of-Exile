Chapter 129 - The Night Run

They came back over the spur on the evening of the fifth with the box in Ward's saddlebag and Odo Pike on a borrowed mule, and found the boat where they had left her, lying in her bend under the birches with her fire banked and Maren's stew on the foredeck stove.

Odo Pike looked at her from the top of the spur and did not say anything. Then he looked at Emeric.

"That's got no mast," he said.

"No."

"And no horse on the bank." He looked at the funnel. "Came up against the current."

"Forty miles."

The old man sat on his mule at the top of the spur with his stick across the saddle and his crooked back very straight for once, and looked down at the boat in the bend, at the thin thread of smoke going up from her banked fire into the dusk, and his small pale eyes went bright and wet, and he did not wipe them.

"Iron road," he said, to nobody. "Iron river." And then, to the mule, very quietly: "Forty years."

They went down to her.



Fletcher would not go down the river in the dark. Then he looked at the box in Ward's saddlebag, and at Emeric's face, and at the bend they were lying in, three miles below Grayrock's landing, where any barge coming down at first light would see a coaster with no mast and a funnel and carry the news all the way to the coast, and said that he supposed there was a moon, and there was.

So they went down by night.

It was easier than the coming up. The current took her. Bram kept the fire low and the steam just high enough to give her steerage, and the screw turned slowly under her stern, and the river carried her down between the dark hills with the moon on the water ahead and the spoil heaps of Grayrock going by on the banks like sleeping animals. Fletcher stood at the wheel with a lantern hung on the post beside him, shaded on the forward side so it would not blind him, and watched the river, and did not speak. Ward was in the bow with a pole, for the shoals, which he had asked Fletcher to show him how to read, and Fletcher had shown him in four sentences, and Ward had understood all four.

The others slept. Maren below, on a pallet beside her baskets. Bram on the sacks in the engine pit with his head on his arm and one ear, Emeric suspected, still on the engine. Haddon at the firebox door, sitting up, asleep, with his scarred hands on his knees. Tess, below, in the little cabin aft that Fletcher had built into her with two bunks, because Bram had said Tess was not sleeping on a deck in October whatever she said. Ferra, in the other bunk. Odo Pike on a coil of rope in the lee of the deckhouse, wrapped in Maren's spare blanket, snoring.

Emeric could not sleep. He had the box in his head. He stood at the port rail amidships with his back to the boiler, which was warm, and watched the dark banks go by, and did not see them.

Aldane.

He had the commission folded inside his coat, against his chest, beside his mother's two stones. He could feel the edge of the heavy paper through his shirt. Fifteen years. A duke with a house as old as the king's, who had found a body of gravity stone under a hill in the south and paid a poor baron to sit on it, and waited. Waited for what? For the baron to die, and a widow to take a lease? For somebody to tell him the curse had lifted? For the price to rise? For a war?

And then the nod across the courtyard. You have it now. We will talk.

"You'll wear a hole in that rail," said Vantic.

He had not heard her. That was the thing he would think about afterward, more than anything she said. He kept his sense open all day now, thin, round him, without thinking, and he knew where everyone on the boat was without looking: Ward in the bow, Fletcher at the wheel, the sleepers below and on the deck. He had not felt her come. She was standing at the rail two arm's lengths aft of him, in her grey coat, with her gloved hands on the wood, looking at the river, and at the edge of his sense where she was there was almost nothing at all. The cupped lamp. Quieter than it had ever been. As if she had put both hands round it.

"I didn't hear you," he said.

"No," said Vantic. "People don't."

She did not explain that. She looked at the river.



They did not say anything for a while. The boat went down the river. The engine breathed. On the far bank a fox barked, twice, and was answered by another a long way off.

"The House," said Vantic at last, "will want to know what was in the box."

"I know."

"I was at the table. I saw the survey. I saw the shape at the bottom of the drawing, and the three words beside it in the crown's shorthand, which I read." She did not look at him. "I saw your face when you unrolled the last paper. I did not see the name on it. I was at the wrong end of the table." She paused. "I would like you to know that I did not try to."

He looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because whatever it was, it frightened you," said Vantic. "And I have watched you be frightened twice in two years, and both times you were right to be, and both times you did something sensible about it in your own time." She turned her gloved hand over on the rail. "The House needs to know what is under Grayrock's hill. It does not need to know who else knows. Not yet." She was quiet. "I will write the first part on Thursday. I will not write the second, because I do not know it. I would like it to stay that way until you decide otherwise."

"That's not how the House works."

"No," Vantic agreed. "It's how I work. Under the House."

The river went by.

"Ferra asked you once," Emeric said, "how you learned to keep a book. In the gate yard. The day you came."

He had not known he was going to say it. It had been in his head for two years: Ferra, at the trestle in the gate yard, with Vantic's ruled pages in front of her, asking it, and Vantic saying that she had been taught, and nothing else.

Vantic was silent for long enough that he thought she would not answer.

"An old man," she said. "The House's chief clerk. When I was small." She looked at the water. "He had a room at the top of the house, under the roof, where the old books were kept, all of them, two hundred years of them. Every loan the House ever made. Every ship. Every barony. Nobody went up there but him. It was too hot in summer and too cold in winter and it smelled of mice and glue." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "I used to go up there to get away from my brothers. They were older. They were loud. They were going to be the House, and they knew it, and they spent a great deal of time telling me so." She paused. "The old man never told me anything. He let me sit on a stool in the corner and watch him. And one day, when I was eight, he gave me a ledger that was finished, a loan to a salt merchant that had been paid off forty years before, and a pen, and told me to find the mistake."

"Was there one?"

"Three," said Vantic. "It took me a week. I found two. He showed me the third." She was quiet. "After that I was up there every day. For ten years. He taught me every book in that room. How a loan is made and how it dies. What a borrower writes when he is going to pay and what he writes when he is not. How to read what a man has left out of a column." She looked at her gloved hands on the rail. "My brothers learned to ride and to fence and to dine with dukes. I learned to read a ledger in a room under the roof that smelled of mice. And when I was twenty the House needed a factor for a debt nobody else would take, in a plague barony in the south with a disgraced prince in it, and the old man had been dead a year, and there was nobody else in the House who could read a book the way he could." She stopped. "Except me."

She had said more than she meant. He could see her know it. She stood very still at the rail with her face turned to the river, and he saw her go back over what she had said, a line at a time, the way she went over a column, looking for what she should not have written.

My brothers. The House. Up there every day. The House needed a factor.

A factor was a clerk. A factor was a hired woman sent south on a chair name to watch a bad debt. A factor's brothers did not learn to dine with dukes, and a factor did not sit in the House's oldest room as a child to get away from them, and nobody sent a factor's daughter up to the top of the house to learn every book in it.

He did not say any of it. He stood at the rail beside her and looked at the river, and did not ask, and felt her feel him not ask.

"I liked it," Vantic said, after a while. Her voice was different. Lower. "That room. I liked it more than anywhere I have ever been. Nobody in it wanted anything from me. Nobody was waiting for me to be something. There was just the old man and the books and a mistake to find." She looked down at the water going past the hull, black and silver under the moon. "I have not been anywhere like it since. Not until,"

She stopped.

He waited. She did not finish it. She stood with her hands on the rail and her face turned away from him, toward the dark bank, and he could not see it.

"The east tower," Emeric said, carefully. "In the keep. With all the books."

"It smells of damp," said Vantic, "not mice." She turned her head a little, not all the way. "And there is a man who walks past the foot of its stair every night at about the second bell after dark on his way to the line room, and stops, every night, for about as long as it takes to read a line, and then goes on." She was quiet. "I have never once asked him what line he reads."

He did not know what to say. He had not known she heard him. He had stopped at the foot of the east tower's stair every night since the spring, because there was a light under her door, always, at that hour, and he had stood there for a breath and thought nothing in particular, and gone on up.

"Your light's always on," he said.

"Yes."

"I wondered what you were reading."

"Your books," said Vantic. "What else is there in that tower?" And then, so quietly he almost did not hear it over the engine: "And wondering what you were reading at the foot of my stair."

The boat went down the river.

Neither of them said anything for a long while. The moon went behind a cloud and came out. The engine breathed. Somewhere below, Odo Pike's snoring stopped and started again. Emeric stood at the rail with his hands on the wet wood and Vantic stood two arm's lengths along it with hers, and he was aware of the distance between their hands on the rail the way he was aware of a step in the dark, exactly, without looking.

She did not close it. Neither did he.

"You should sleep," Vantic said at last.

"So should you."

"I don't, much." She took her hands off the rail. "It's a habit. From the room under the roof. The old man worked at night. He said the books were quieter." She turned to go aft, toward the hatch, and stopped. "Emeric."

It was the first time she had ever said his name. Not Highness. Not the holder. Not the borrower. His name.

"I will write Thursday's letter," she said, "with what I know and nothing else. And when you decide to tell me the rest, I will write that too, or not, as you ask." She looked at him in the moonlight. "That is not the House's rule. That is mine. You should know the difference, if you're going to keep reading lines at the foot of my stair."

She went down the hatch.

He stood at the rail. In the bow, Ward had not turned round once in the whole of it, and was very carefully sounding a shoal that, as far as Emeric could tell, was not there.



They came under the east bridge at first light on the seventh, on the last of the night's current, with the town asleep above the wall and the lamps on their posts going pale against the grey.

Fletcher brought her in to the foot of the slip. He rang the bell to the engine pit twice, and Bram shut the steam off, and she drifted in the last few yards and touched the toe of the slip, where Bram's cement held, so gently that nobody on board felt it but Fletcher.

He stood at the wheel with his hands on it after she had stopped.

"Somebody Came," he said.

Emeric looked at him.

"Her name." Fletcher did not take his hands off the wheel. "She sat in the reeds six years with moss on her keel and a gull on her stem because nobody came. Nobody ever did. I told you that, in my yard. That's what a hull with no use looks like." He looked down the deck of his boat, at the funnel, the boiler hood, the screw's shaft going aft under the planking. "And then somebody came." He took his hands off the wheel. "Somebody Came. I'll paint it on her stern this week. In white. Big enough for the dock quarter."

Tess, who had been wheeled up out of the cabin by Bram and was sitting in her chair by the boiler in the grey light, began to cry. She did it without any sound, sitting very straight, and wiped her face with her sleeve, and said that it was the smoke, and nobody contradicted her.
