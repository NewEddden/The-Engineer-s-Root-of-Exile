Chapter 142 - The Barrel

Osric came up out of the top-deck hatch with the lamp's light on his face and saw Ward, and then saw who was behind Ward, and came down the ladder on her flank without being told anything.

"She's full," he said. "Band on the way up. Every stone." He looked at Emeric. "I'm to walk?"

"To the fires. Declan has the front with you. Ward will tell you the rest."

Osric nodded and put his gloves in his belt and looked back up at the ship once, the long black shape of her standing on her legs in the flattened grass with no light anywhere on her, the way a man looks back at a horse he has ridden a long way and is leaving with somebody else. Then he went down the hill in the dark toward the meadow without a lamp, because he did not need one, and Ward watched him go.

"I don't like it," Ward said.

"I know."

"One man. An hour each way. At night." Ward did not look at him. "If the stone goes in your suit you've got nothing. Osric has the levers. You haven't even got those."

"I've got the levers. I just don't use them." Emeric put his foot on the first rung. "If the stone goes, I use them."

"And if you go?"

That was the real question and they both knew it. Emeric stood on the ladder with his hand on the rail and thought about the true answer, which was that if he went, in the dark, at the top of the band with nobody aboard, the ship would hang where she was until her stones ran down and then come down wherever the wind had put her, with nobody in her, somewhere between Wayford and Kaldo, and in the morning somebody would find a hundred feet of iron hull lying in a field with a dead prince in her armoury, and that would be the end of every secret he had, at once.

"Then you'll have a very bad week," he said, "and I won't."

Ward closed the lamp's hood. In the sudden dark his voice came from where his face had been. "Come back by the night we go in."

"I'll come back."

He went up the ladder and down through the hatch and along the dark passage to the armoury, and sat down on the stool at the bench by the forward bulkhead, and put his hand flat on the breast of his own suit on its frame, and shut his eyes. After that the levers moved when he wanted them to. The legs came up out of the grass. The hill went away beneath her, and the lamp's closed shape on it, and Ward, and a mile off the column's fires, very small, going down to embers along the meadow by the ford, and none of them looked up.

Kaldo had stopped asking how the prince came home.

He had done it all summer, a night at a time, out of a town five days north, and come down onto the lake in the dark and walked up through the water gate at the second bell as though he had been out to look at the moon, and the garrison had its own view of how, which it had arrived at by itself over a long time and a good deal of beer, and kept to itself. The man at the water gate tonight said, "Your Highness," and opened it, and did not look past him at the lake, and Emeric went up through the yard to the pattern store in the dark with the sound of the ship's stones still in the back of his head.

Bram was waiting for him. So was Jorrin, and two of the forge boys, and the bar of steel.

It lay on the bench in the lamplight where Bram had put it after the relay came, a length of rolled bar as thick as a man's two thumbs and as long as a man's arm, grey and dull and very slightly blued along one face where the rolls had caught it. It was from the fourth heat. It was the steel that had rolled whole with Colm's grit in the pot, and Bram had kept the best of it back since then without being asked, the way he kept back anything he thought might one day matter, and he put his hand on it now and did not say where it had come from, because they both knew.

"You said a tube," Bram said.

"A tube."

"I can bore you a tube. I told you that at the relay. Give me three days on Jorrin's lathe and a drill I haven't got and I'll bore you a tube you could look down and see daylight, near enough straight." He looked at the bar. "Near enough."

"Near enough is what I'm not after."

Bram grunted. It was not agreement. It was the sound he made when a thing had been said that he had expected, and he took his hand off the bar and stood back from the bench, and Jorrin and the boys stood back with him, and Emeric took the bar to the forge.

The forge stood where it had always stood, in the far bay of the pattern store behind its own wall, the plate on its four stones and the deciding stone in its bracket and the wall of fed stones behind, and it had made every suit that stood on its frame down the length of the store in the dark behind him. It had never made anything like this.

He put the bar on the plate and let the plate take it.

It rose off the iron the way things rose in the forge, without anything touching it, and hung there at the height of his chest turning very slowly on its long axis, and he sat down on the stool in front of it and put his hands on his knees and went in.

It was three things at once. That was the whole difficulty of it, and it was why he had never done it before and did not know, going in, whether he could do it tonight. He had to hold the bar where it was, which was the easy part, the part the forge's stones did for him if he let them. He had to heat it, all of it, evenly, from the skin to the heart, to the colour a smith would have wanted for drawing it out and no further, because further and the new steel would take up whatever was in the air around it and go brittle. And he had to shape it while it was hot, from the outside in, which a smith could do, and from the inside out, which nobody could, because nobody could put a hand inside a bar of steel.

He could. It was the only thing he had that Bram did not. The forge put him inside the metal the way the delve put him inside a stone, and while he was in it he could feel every grain of it, and tell it where to go.

He opened the bore first. He did not drill it. He parted the steel down the length of the bar's heart a hair at a time and pressed it outward, evenly, all round, all the way along, and the bar grew in his hands from a rod to a tube with a wall as thick as his little finger, and he held the bore true as it opened against a line he kept in his head from end to end, straighter than any rule in Kaldo, because the rule in his head was not made of anything that could bend.

Then he stopped, and held it, and breathed, and went back in for the part that mattered.

A smooth bore throws a ball like a boy throws a stone. It goes where it is thrown, more or less, and then wherever the air and its own lumps take it, and past a hundred paces it is a matter of luck. A ball that spins goes where it is pointed. He had known that since before he was Emeric. He had never in his life made the thing that made it spin, and he made it now, in the dark, in the pattern store, by feel: six shallow grooves round the inside of the bore, running the whole length of it, each one turning a sixth of the way round the tube over the length of it and a little more, evenly, all six together, cut into the wall of the bore from inside by nothing but where he told the steel to go.

He could not have told anybody how. That was what he understood about halfway along, when his hands had started to shake on his knees and the heat off the bar was drying the sweat on his face as fast as it came. There was no tool that did this. There was no drawing that could tell a tool how. He was doing it with a thing in him that had no name in any tongue in Randillia, and the barrel would be exactly as good as his attention was at every hair of its length, and when his attention went, the barrel would be as good as it was at that moment and no better, forever.

It did not go. It went thin. At the end it was a thread, and he held the six grooves together along the last hand's width of the bore by something that was not skill any more and was not strength and was closer to not letting go of a rope, and then he was at the muzzle, and there was nothing left to cut, and he let the heat go out of it, slowly, the way the new steel wanted, and brought it down onto the plate.

He opened his eyes. It was grey outside the pattern store's high windows.

Bram was still there. So was Jorrin. The boys were asleep on a pile of sacking by the door.

Bram came to the plate and picked the barrel up in a cloth, because it was still warm, and held it up to the grey light from the window and put his eye to the breech end and looked down it for a long time. Then he turned it, slowly, a little at a time, and looked again.

"It turns," he said.

"Yes."

"The light turns down it." He lowered it and looked at Emeric, and his face was not doing anything. "You cut that. In there. From inside."

"Yes."

Bram looked at the barrel again. He did not say any of the things Emeric had half expected him to say. He did not ask how. He had seen the suits come off the plate and the hearts go into the suits and had stopped asking how about the forge a long time ago; he asked whether, and how much, and how long, and he asked those about everything.

"Could you do another?"

Emeric thought about it honestly. He thought about the thread at the end, and the six grooves held together along the last hand's width by nothing, and how long it would be before he could go back into the forge and hold three things at once for a night.

"Not this week."

"Could anybody?"

"No."

Bram nodded slowly. He put the barrel back down on the plate in its cloth, square to the edge, the way Ferra put a sheet down, and went to wake the boys.

Ferra came down at the first bell of the morning with the forge's book, because the relay had come in the night before and she had read it and known what it meant and been in bed for four hours, and she wanted to see what the forge had done before anybody told her about it.

She stood at the plate and looked at the barrel. She did not touch it. Then she opened the book at the page and wrote, standing, in the forge's own hand, the one she kept for it.

One barrel. One night.

She looked at it for a moment. Then she wrote a line under it, smaller, and turned the book round on the bench so that he could read it without her having to say it.

Not to be made again without the forge's book open and the holder's reason written beside it. The barrel is a night at the limit of what he can hold. The powder is what there is until the beds give. He does not issue a thing that has not failed yet.

"That's three reasons," he said.

"You'll want all three when somebody asks." She closed the book. "And somebody will. You'll be asked why there's one. Not by me." She looked at the barrel again. "What does it do?"

"It puts a ball where I point it. Further than the rifle on the gate tower. Through more."

"What does it cost?"

"The steel was Bram's. The forge was me." He thought about it. "The powder."

She waited. He told her about the powder, which was the part he had been thinking about since the relay, and she listened without writing anything, and at the end of it she opened the book again and wrote one more line, and did not show him that one.

The lock was Bram's.

Emeric drew it for him at the long bench in the forge shop at the second bell, on a sheet of the works' paper with the master foot laid along the top for the scale, and it was not a hard thing to draw and he did not pretend it was. A hammer holding a piece of flint in its jaw. A spring behind the hammer to throw it. A plate of hard steel on a hinge for the flint to strike, standing over a little pan of fine powder beside a hole into the breech, so that when the flint struck, the sparks went into the pan, and the pan went into the hole, and the hole went into the charge. A catch to hold the hammer back, and a trigger to let it go.

Bram looked at the drawing for a long time without saying anything.

"Where's the stone?" he said.

"There isn't one."

Bram looked up.

"Nothing in it holds a charge," Emeric said. "Nothing in it needs one. Flint and steel and a spring. Powder. A ball. It'll work in a dead zone. It'll work in the rain if the pan's covered. It'll work in a hundred years for a man who's never heard of a stone."

Bram put the drawing down on the bench and flattened it with the side of his hand, and went on looking at it.

Across the shop, on the far wall, on two iron pegs where it had hung since the war, was the other one. It was the air drill with a tube round it, the thing Emeric had built for the lane in the winter before Grayrock came, with the crystal in its heart and the long brass charging stock, slow to fill and awkward to carry, and it had put a bolt through Renner at three hundred yards from the gate tower on the morning of the fight, and nobody had fired it since except at boards. Bram looked across at it now. So did Emeric.

"That one's a rifle," Bram said. "This one's a rifle."

"Yes."

"The difference." Bram said it slowly, the way he said a thing he had worked out and wanted to be sure of before it went into the world. "That one needs a charged crystal, and you have to fill it, and it goes quiet if some fool breaks a stone within a hundred feet of it. This one needs a pinch of dirt." He put his finger on the pan in the drawing. "And doesn't care."

"Yes."

Bram was quiet a while.

He had made weapons his whole life. He had made swords for men who killed other men with them, and spearheads by the hundred for the war, and the points of the bolts that had gone into the lane, and the harnesses and the plate and the hearts of the suits on their frames in the pattern store, and he had never once in all the years Emeric had known him said a word against any of it, because a smith made what the work needed and the work was the world, and the world had always been like this. He looked at the drawing of the lock and Emeric watched him understand what it was, all of it, the way he understood a joint by looking at it, and he did not say anything against this either.

"Somebody was always going to," he said at last. "If not you."

"Yes."

Bram nodded. Then he asked his one question, and Emeric had known him long enough to know that the question was how Bram said he was upset.

"How long to load it again?"

Emeric told him the truth. "A man who knows it, with the powder measured and the ball patched and ready, a minute. Most of a minute. It's slow because the ball has to be pressed down into the grooves, and the grooves fight it." He paused. "A smooth barrel loads faster. With the powder and ball wrapped together in paper, three times in a minute, for a trained man. But then it doesn't turn, and it doesn't go where it's pointed."

Bram did not say anything. He was doing the arithmetic. Emeric watched him do it: three a minute, and a line of men, and a line of men facing them, and the minutes going by, and the men in the second line falling at the rate the first line could load, with no stone in any of it to run down and no mage to tire and nothing at all to stop the thing going on until one line or the other ran out of men.

Bram picked up the drawing and folded it once and put it in his apron.

"I'll have the lock by dark," he said.

The powder took the rest of the day, and he did it himself, in the open, at the far end of the foundry yard by the old dry store, with the sun on him and nobody within forty paces.

The jar came out of the dry store with the saltpeter in it, the white crust from Grayrock's dead workings that he had boiled and cleared and boiled again in the spring until it came out of the pan in long clean needles. There was a little over three pounds of it. It was every grain of saltpeter in Kaldo, and it would be every grain in Kaldo until the beds behind the old barn gave, which Ferra's book said would be three years, and which Emeric thought might be two if the crust they had seeded the beds with was as good as it looked and nothing went wrong, and things went wrong.

He did not mix three pounds. He did not mix one.

He had thought about it on the stool in the ship on the way south, with the dark going by and his hand on his suit, and it had come out the same way every time. Mixed powder was a thing that could go up. Saltpeter in a jar and sulphur in a sack and charcoal in another sack could stand in the same dry store for ten years and never be anything but three dull substances, and the moment he put them together they became one thing, which would sit in whatever room it was kept in, waiting for a spark. He would not keep a jar of that in Kaldo, and he would not carry more of it than he meant to use. And there was the drying. Powder mixed wet and pressed and broken to grain had to dry before it would burn clean, and it had one day to dry in, and a day in the sun on a board would dry a thin spread of it and not a thick one.

So he weighed out enough for ten charges, and not a grain more.

Six parts in eight of the saltpeter, ground fine in a wooden bowl with a wooden pestle, because there would be no iron and no steel and no stone anywhere near this. One part of the charcoal, the furnace's own, from the sack in the dry store, ground separately. One part of the sulphur, the same. Then all three together, in the bowl, with water, a little at a time, worked with the pestle into a dark wet paste for the best part of two hours, until his arms ached and the paste was one colour all through. Then pressed flat on a board under another board with a stone on it. Then, when it had firmed, broken, and pushed through a sieve of Nevin's, and the grains that came through spread on a clean board in the sun at the far end of the yard to dry, with a knight at the gate of the yard to keep everybody out and a bucket of water beside the board that he did not expect to need.

It went badly at the sieve. He had known it might. The paste was too wet at one end of the board and too dry at the other, and the dry end crumbled to dust under his thumb instead of breaking to grain, and dust was no good, dust burned too fast and fouled the bore and did not do the same thing twice. He sieved the dust back out, and kept a pinch of it in a quill for the pan, where burning fast was what he wanted, and set the rest aside in a dish.

When the grain was dry in the late afternoon he measured it into charges on a scale Bram had made him that morning, and there were seven.

He looked at the seven for a while. Then he put them in a flask of horn, with a wooden stopper, and put the dish of dust in a bucket of water and poured the bucket out on the midden, and walked the empty bowl and the sieve and the boards back to the dry store himself, and washed his hands in the lake.

The candles were in the smoke house under the lake wall behind two locks, and on the suits. The suits were in Wayford in their crates, and on the ship. The flask of horn went into a locker in the ship's day room, at the far end of her from the armoury, and he locked it, and put the key on a cord round his neck.

It was the rule he had said to Ward in the spring, and he kept it because he had said it. Never the same room, never the same cart, never the same bench, never the same pocket.

The stones he did in the evening, after the lock came.

There were five of them and they were all the same thing, which was a crystal with a rune on it that did one thing once and then was finished. He had built the thing they were copied from years ago. It was over the Western Gate in its dressed-stone housing, a sound crystal and a light crystal with their releases set to dump instead of trickle, and it had never been fired in anger, and it did not kill a man standing under it. It took his eyes and his ears for the time it took two men to come down off the wall. These were that, made small enough to throw, with a pin in them for a man to pull and a count on the rune between the pin and the dump.

Two were flash: a sound crystal and a light crystal in one brass case the size of a fist, the gate in a man's hand.

One was breach: a force crystal on its own, set not to push but to strike, once, at a single point, the whole of its charge in one blow, enough to take a door off its hinges or put a hole in a wall of lath and plaster. No powder. Nothing that would burn.

And two were gravity.

The gravity stones were the ones he sat with. They were Grayrock stone from the forge's own stock, six ounces each, the same weight that went into a suit's heart, cut round and set in a cage of brass with the pin and the rune, and when the pin came out and the count ran down each of them would do one thing, which was to make everything within ten yards of it heavy, straight down, all at once, for about as long as a man could count sixty slowly. Not sideways. Gravity did not go sideways and never had. Down, the way it always went, only more.

How much more was a thing he could choose. There was a turn on the rune for it, a dial, and he sat on the stool in the pattern store with the first stone in his hand and his other hand on the dial and thought about it for the best part of an hour.

Twice a man's weight was not enough. A strong man could stand up under twice his own weight, and a frightened one would, and he would still have his sword in his hand. Six times would put anybody on the floor and keep him there, and it would also break an old man's ribs against the flags as he went down, and stop some hearts. He did not know what was in the Staple besides the five and their men. He knew some of it, from Saban. There were house men, who were young. There were clerks, who were not. There was the man who sat up all night behind the lamp, and there were servants, because there were always servants, and some of them would be old and some of them would be women and one of them, somewhere, if the world was the world, would be a man of sixty with a bad heart who had been pouring wine for the Staple's lords since before any of them were lords and who would be standing next to the wrong man when the pin came out.

He did the arithmetic on that man. His weight, four times over, arriving all at once; the fall to the floor under it; the minute on the floor with his chest trying to rise against four of himself; and his heart.

He set it at four. It would pin a strong man flat and hurt him, and hold him there, and let him breathe, just, and the sword in his hand would lie on the floor beside him weighing four times what it had and he would not lift it. It would probably not kill the man of sixty. Probably. He sat with the word for a while and could not make it into any other word, and in the end he set the second stone at four as well, and put them both in the case, and wrote the number in his own book with nothing beside it.

The mechanism was one line in the book and he wrote it plainly, because it was the line that mattered most and he would want to find it again. Each stone dumped its charge fast, faster than it had ever been meant to, but inside what the stone could hold. None of them overloaded. An overloaded stone made a hole in the world, the thing he had made three of in the winter before the war and hated making, and an overloaded stone in the Staple's hall would take his own flight with it and Ward's and Rylan's, and the lamp in the window, and anything else in the building that anybody was relying on. So they dumped, and did not overload, and left no hole. Every one of them would be a dead crystal afterward, which was the price.

Ferra came down for the price, with the book. She stood by the bench with Bram and two of his men at the far end of the store fitting the lock to the stock and wrote it standing.

Gravity stone, two cases at six ounces, at the Crown's figure of two Platinum the pound: one Platinum and five Gold. Force, sound, light: from store. Brass and pins: from the forge shop. From the fund.

She looked at the line.

"That's most of what the whole net cost," she said. "Lamp stones and brass and two printers and the saddler and all of it. For two stones you're going to throw into a room."

"Yes."

"And then they're dead."

"Yes."

She did not say anything else. She did not need to. She wrote it inside the fund's line and under it the date, and closed the book, and went back up the stair, and he sat with the two brass cases in his lap and the figure in his head, and did not like either.

Tobe made the smoke pots in the yard, which was a thing anybody could have done and Tobe did better than anybody: clay pots packed with damp straw and pitch and a wick, a dozen of them, in a crate, for the column. They would smoke like a wet bonfire for a quarter of an hour and put nobody's eyes out. There was nothing in them to explain to anybody.

The test was at dawn, in the long yard behind the foundry, with the lake at the far end of it and the mist still lying on the lake.

Bram had set up an oak board at the far end, a plank as thick as a man's hand nailed across two posts, at two hundred paces by the chain, which was further than the rifle on the gate tower had ever been asked to put a bolt through anything. There were not many of them in the yard. Bram and Jorrin, who had made the lock and fitted it. The carpenter, who had made the stock in a day out of a length of walnut he had been saving for a chair and was still unhappy about it. Tobe. Ferra, with the book, standing well back by the foundry wall. And Enno.

Emeric had sent for Enno the night before, and Enno had come to the yard at first light in his garrison coat and stood by the foundry wall a little apart from the others, not sure why he had been asked and not asking, the way he had stood in the gate tower on the morning of the fight and afterward not understood why people kept coming up to look at him. He was older now, and broader, and he still stood like a man who expected to be told he was in the wrong place. He was the only man in Kaldo who had ever killed another man at a distance with a weapon, and that was why he was there, and Emeric had not told him so, and did not think he needed to.

Tobe looked at the thing in Emeric's hands. It was long and dark and very plain, a grey barrel on a walnut stock, with Bram's lock on the side of it and two iron sights, a notch at the back and a blade at the front, and a leather sling, and nothing else at all.

"What do we call it?" Tobe said. "The tube?"

"It's a gun," said Emeric.

Tobe said it over to himself once, testing it, and apparently found that it fitted, and after that nobody in the yard called it anything else.

He loaded it the slow way, the way he had told Bram, so that they would all see how slow it was. Powder from the first of the seven charges down the muzzle. A ball of lead in a patch of greased linen, set on the muzzle and started with the heel of his hand and then pressed down the bore with the rod, hard, against the grooves, all the way, until it sat on the powder. A pinch of the dust from the quill into the pan. The steel down over the pan. The hammer back to the catch.

Enno watched every movement of it. Emeric felt him watch.

He did not lie down. He had thought about lying down, which was how a man should shoot a thing like this if he wanted to hit anything, and he did not, because he would not be lying down when it mattered. He stood, and put the stock to his shoulder, and his cheek to the stock, and found the board through the notch and the blade, and let his breath half out, and held it, and pressed.

The flint came down. The pan flashed. For the length of a blink nothing happened at all, and then the gun went off in his hands.

It was the loudest thing that had ever happened in the foundry yard, and the island had heard the converter blow. It went off the lake and off the keep wall and came back, and every bird on the island went up off every roof at once, and the smoke stood in front of him in a grey cloud that smelled of rotten eggs and hung there in the still air, and his shoulder rang. When the smoke drifted he could not see the board at all.

Tobe had his hands over his ears. The carpenter had sat down.

Jorrin walked the two hundred paces and came back with the news, which was that there was a hole in the board about a hand's width to the left of the mark and a little high, and that the ball had gone through the oak and out the back and into the post behind it and he could not find it.

Emeric loaded again. The second charge, the second ball. The pan. The hammer. He found the board. He pressed.

This time the hole was two fingers from the first.

He loaded the third. His shoulder had begun to ache in the way that meant it would ache worse tomorrow. He took the same stance, the same breath, the same press, and felt everything go right, the stock where it had been, the sight where it had been, the flint and the pan and the blink of nothing and the shove, and when Jorrin came back from the board he was not looking at Emeric.

"Low," Jorrin said. "A hand low. Straight under the other two."

Nobody said anything.

Emeric stood with the gun across his arm and the smoke going off the lake and went back over it, all of it, the way he would have gone back over a failed plate, and there was nothing. He had not moved. The stock had not moved. He had done the same thing three times and the first two had gone together and the third had not, and the only thing that had been different about the third was the third charge.

He knew what that meant. He had made seven charges out of a paste that was too wet at one end of the board and too dry at the other. He had sieved out the dust and kept the grain, and grain from the wet end and grain from the dry end looked the same in the hand and did not burn the same, and one of them had pushed the ball a little less hard than the other two, and the ball had dropped a hand in two hundred paces because of it. That was all. There was no other reason and he would never be able to prove it, because to prove it he would have to fire the other four and watch where they went, and then he would have nothing to carry north.

He wrote it down. Three at two hundred paces. Two together, a hand left and high of the mark. Third a hand low under them. Same hold. Charge, not hand. Four left.

He looked up from the book and Enno was looking at him.

Enno had been standing by the foundry wall through all three shots with his arms folded, and he had not moved at the noise, which had made the carpenter sit down in the dirt. He had watched the loading each time as though he were learning it. He had watched Jorrin walk to the board and back. And now he was looking at Emeric, and there was something in his face that Emeric had not seen in it before, which was the look of a man who has understood something about a thing and is deciding whether it is his place to say it.

"May I fire it, Your Highness?" Enno said.

"No."

Enno nodded. He did not ask why. He looked at the gun, and then down the yard at the board, and then back at Emeric's book, at the page with the three shots on it, and his mouth moved very slightly as though the first word of something had come up to it. Then he shut it, and the word went back down, and he said nothing at all.

Emeric saw him not say it. He did not know what it was. He wanted to ask, and did not, because the four charges were in the horn flask and the flask was going north tonight and there was no time left in the day to be told anything he could do nothing about, and he put the book in his coat, and Enno went back to his watch.

He flew north at dusk alone, as he had come.

The ship went up out of the lake in the last of the light, dark on dark, and climbed, and Kaldo went away beneath her to a scatter of lamps on the water and then to nothing, and at the top of the band where the stones drank he brought her round to the north and let her run. The armoury was forward. He had set her on her heading from the stool there with his hand on his suit, and then set the levers by hand to hold it, the way Ward did, the way a lighterman lashes his tiller on a straight reach, because the air at the top of the band was still tonight and she would hold a heading on the levers for the better part of an hour without anybody. Then he had walked back down her length through the dark passage to the day room, because he wanted to be at the other end of the ship from what was in the armoury, and sat down there.

The gun lay across his knees in its oilcloth. The locker with the horn flask in it was at his back, locked, and the key on its cord was inside his shirt. Somewhere forward in the armoury his own suit stood on its frame with three candles on its hip and he did not think about them.

He sat with his hands on his thighs, on either side of the long wrapped shape, and looked at the dark window and the stars very close and very hard beyond it, and did not put his hand on the gun.
