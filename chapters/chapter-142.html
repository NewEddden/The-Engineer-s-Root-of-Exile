Chapter 142 - Egg and Neck

The second failure was his own idea.

He had lain awake after the first one with the star in the glass lining in front of his eyes, and by the small hours he had decided that the star was not the lesson. The lesson was that the forge had believed him. He had said drained and it had heard drained, and it had heated a stone with a dreg in its heart as if the dreg were not there. So the question was how far that went. Would it believe anything?

On the night of the twenty-first he took a cup of river water down the narrow stair, with Bram behind him carrying the lamp and a new glass liner, and Ward behind Bram carrying nothing, because Ward never carried anything on a stair if he could help it. Bram had set the new liner that afternoon. It had taken him four hours, and he had not let Emeric help, and he had sworn at it in a low steady voice the whole time, the way some men hum.

Emeric poured the water into the feed cup and closed it, and put his hand on the control stone in the lattice, and told the forge it was iron ore. A cup of good red ore, from the Grayrock face. Melt it, take the slag off, give me the iron.

"It's water," said Bram.

"I know."

"Does it know?"

"That's what we're finding out."

It did not know. The lattice took the water up in a trembling ball in the middle of the chamber, which was very beautiful, and the chamber heated, and the forge did exactly what it had been told to do with a cup of ore. It brought it up toward a melt. It shook it, from every side at once, to make the slag rise. And then it squeezed, to drive the iron together, very hard, from twenty directions.

The water had gone to steam a good while before the squeeze. The squeeze found nothing to squeeze and squeezed it anyway.

The relief valves on the chamber top lifted with a shriek like a kettle the size of a horse. Every pipe in the vent line banged once, in order, going away from them up the wall, and somewhere far above them, outside, in the yard, something went off with a long hoarse whoosh and then went quiet.

When Emeric opened the feed cup the inside of the chamber was dry and black and smelled of scorched clay, and there was nothing in it at all. Not a drop. Not a speck of anything.

Bram looked in over his shoulder.

"Well," said Bram. "That's iron for you."

The whoosh, they learned in the morning, had been the shed. The forge's exhaust went to the shed by the north wall through the vent line, and the shed's cold plates sent what they caught into the castle's hot water, which was the whole reason the shed existed. For an hour after midnight the castle's hot water had come out of every tap as steam. Maren, who had been scalding a pan, had been very nearly scalded herself, and had said things to the tap that the scullery girl repeated to the kitchen, and the kitchen repeated to the yard, and by breakfast the whole keep knew that the prince's new boiler in the shed had a temper. Emeric let them think so. It was a good story and it was nearly true.

He started the index that night, at the table in his office, with the panel shut and the forge cooling under him. The forge could not tell water from ore because it had never been taught what either one was. It needed a book in its own stone: a list of every material it would ever be asked to work, and for each one, what that material felt like when the control stone pulsed it. A sort of smell. Steel felt one way. Copper another. Water, he now knew, felt like nothing the forge would ever be asked to melt. Every entry would cost a sampling run in the chamber and a night in the delve writing the result into the stone. Once a material was in the book, the forge would check what it was given against it, and if the two did not agree, it would refuse, and hold the run, and wait.

He wrote at the head of the first page what it was for, and under that the first entry he meant to make.

Steel.



The steel went in on the twenty-second. Not a part. A block of Bram's best cementation steel, cut rough on the saw, and declared to the forge as exactly what it was, by weight and by kind. What he asked for back was the simplest thing he could think of. A cube. One inch on every face. Every face flat and every corner square.

It took most of the night. The lattice held the block and turned it, and the chamber took it up to heat and held it there, and the stone squeezed and pared and squeezed again, and the feed bank under the chamber went dull stone by stone along its row. He kept his hand on the control stone the whole time and his attention inside it, reading what the forge read, writing it down as it came. Steel, hot. Steel, moving. Steel, at rest. When it was done he was so tired his hands shook, and he could feel the lightness behind his eyes that meant he had spent more of himself than he should have. He drank the water Ward handed him without looking at it.

The cube came out of the feed cup on Bram's tongs, still warm, grey and dull and very plain. Bram turned it over. He was a big man and the cube was very small in the tongs.

"I thought it'd be a sword," he said.

"Did you."

"Something. A first thing. You hear about these things, the first thing out of a magic forge. It's a sword, or a crown." He turned it again. "I got a brick."

He wrapped it in his apron and carried it up the narrow stair and down the hill to the tally room in the dark, with Emeric and Ward behind him, and lit the lamp there, and took down from its felt the first gauge block Kaldo had ever had: the plain square of iron Vesk had shaped and Bram had lapped on a stone two springs ago, that held the barony's inch. He wrung it onto the cube, face to face, the way he had taught every apprentice in the works to do, sliding the one onto the other until they stuck. He held them up to the lamp and looked for light along the joint.

There was none.

He took the cube off and turned it and wrung it on again, on another face. And another. He did all six. Then he got out the square, and did the corners. Then he got out his best callipers, the ones nobody else was allowed to touch, and measured it across the faces, three ways, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth. Ward leaned in the doorway.

When he had finished Bram put the callipers back in their case and shut it. He did not say anything. He put the cube down on the bench next to the old gauge block, very carefully, side by side, and stood and looked at the two of them with his hands flat on the bench.

It was the first thing that had ever come out of the cellar he believed. Emeric could see him believing it. He did not need to say so.



The rest of that week the forge made masters.

It was the thing Emeric had built it for, in his own mind, before the bands or the cells or anything with a stone in it. A forge that made perfect things was worth very little if every perfect thing had to come out of the forge. But a forge that made one perfect thing, once, that every shop in Kaldo could copy from forever after with no magic at all, was worth more than the mine. So it made a set of gauge blocks, the true ones, which went onto the tally room's shelf in a velvet-lined box that Abloh made for them, and became the barony's lengths from then on. The old inch went on the shelf beside them as the teaching set, which Bram said was where it belonged, since it had taught him everything he knew about being wrong. It made a set of master weights, in pounds and ounces, which went on the next shelf, beside Harl's old brass ones, which were very nearly right and were kept there for that reason. It made three surface plates, flat. It made a lead screw, a long bar of steel with a thread cut so true along its length that a nut run down it from end to end would move exactly the same distance with every turn.

And the same week, because that was the rule, the mundane way went into the works beside it.

Bram learned to lap. He knew it already, the rough way; now he learned it fine, with the forge's plates to check against, a lap of soft iron charged with the finest grit Ivo's glasshouse could sift, worked in long figure-of-eight strokes until the work was flat to a breath. He learned to scrape: a smear of red lead in oil on the master plate, the work rubbed on it, the high places showing red, a hardened scraper taking off a shaving finer than a hair from each red place, and again, and again, until the red came up even all over. He learned the three plates.

Emeric showed him that one with three rough plates from the foundry and a pot of red lead, at the bench, late, with the works empty. Rub two plates face to face and scrape them till they bear all over, and you have two plates that fit each other. That is all. One might be a little hollow and the other a little round, and they would fit like a cup on a ball, and both be wrong. So you take the third, and fit it to the first. Then fit the second to the third. Then the first to the second. Round and round, each one to each of the others, and if one is hollow it cannot fit both of the others, so the scraping takes the hollow out, and the round, and after enough rounds there is only one shape that three plates can all share with each other.

Flat.

Bram stood at the bench with the three rough plates in front of him and the red lead on his fingers, and thought about it for the length of time it took him to wipe his fingers on his apron.

"Two can lie to each other," he said. "Three can't."

Then he started scraping, and was still at it when Emeric went up to bed, and was at it again at dawn, and on the fourth day he brought three plates of his own to the tally room and laid them beside the forge's three and put the red lead on, and his were not as good. They were very nearly as good. He looked at them with a deep, private, sour satisfaction, and said the next set would be better, and it was.

He learned the lathe screw last. The forge's lead screw went into the old lathe, and with it the lathe cut a new screw, as true as the master over most of its length, which went into the next lathe, which cut another. By the end of the week there were three lathes in the works with true screws, and Tess was drawing a fourth, and Bram had begun to talk about the forge the way he talked about Ivo: with suspicion, and complete trust.



The forge book got its first page that week too. Emeric wrote it at his office table with the panel shut, at the head of the book, above the run list, so that anybody who ever opened it would read the rules before they read what the rules had made.

Only the forge can do it. Or it is a master, made once. Or it is a hard part the shops cannot make yet, a few of them, never by the ton. It fits the chamber. Its recipe goes into the works the same week. It is worth the cost. Nothing leaves this room looking impossible. And only one person shapes.

Under the rules he wrote the list, in order. The cube. The masters. Cells. Bands. The ship's matrix. The suits.

Ferra priced every line of it. She sat with the book open in front of her in the counting room with the door shut and her own book beside it, and against every run she wrote what it would eat: so many stones from the bank, so many charging days of Vesk's and Orrin's, so many nights of his. The cube was cheap. The masters were not. The suits were a number she wrote very small and underlined twice.

She put her pen down on the master weights.

"Why," she said, "does a box of steel blocks come before the suits? Ward's men go up the hill every day with a crossbow and a cloak. And you've put a set of weights in front of them."

"Because nothing that comes out of that room is worth anything until somebody with no magic can check it."

Ferra looked at him for a while.

"That's an answer," she said. "It isn't a price." But she did not move the line. She picked her pen up and went on to the next one.

The ship's matrix stones went in on the third night after the cube, in among everything else, the first of them, for the long frame of the hull in the shed under the north wall. They took a night each and there were going to be a great many. He did one and stopped, because the masters mattered more, and wrote in the run list how many were left. Ferra wrote the price beside it and did not underline it at all, which was worse.

She started the last schedule the same week. The mine's stones were going down the river now, a few at a time, under the House's seal, to buyers the House found and the House took its commission from; and Aldane's commission at court had gone in her locked box in the autumn and had come out again in the winter as coin. For the first time since Emeric had come south there was enough. Not enough to be comfortable. Enough to pay the House of Ducatis what the barony owed it, all of it, early, and be done.

She did not say so at the table. She wrote the heading of a new page in her book, in her small square hand, and Emeric saw it upside down across her desk and did not say anything either. Final schedule of the principal. And the date she meant to pay it on.



The chair failed for the sixth time on the twenty-third, in the dyehouse, which had not been the plan.

The plan had been Heavy. Heavy was the mode in which the stones pushed down instead of up, and planted the chair where it stood, so that nobody could wheel it off or tip it over or carry it anywhere its rider did not choose. Emeric wanted to know how hard it held. The works floor was flagged stone and told him nothing; you could have stood a cart on it. The dyehouse floor was boards. So he rolled the chair in on its motor at noon, with the dyers watching from their vats, and drew the mode lever back to Heavy, and pushed it a notch further, and a notch further, while Ward leaned on the back of the chair to see if he could shift it.

He could not shift it. That part worked.

The third notch put the left front caster through the floor. There was a crack like a branch going and the chair dropped a foot on that corner and stopped, wedged, tipped, with Emeric hanging off the right armrest and the stick in his hand and the stones still pushing down as hard as they had been told, which drove the caster deeper into the joist below with a long, grinding complaint from the timber.

Under the board, it turned out, was the old dyehouse drain. Nobody had lifted that board in forty years. It had been rotten for most of them. The caster came up out of it, when Emeric finally found Glide with his free hand and let the stones go, wearing a cuff of black slime and something that had once been blue.

The dyers did not laugh. They had too much respect for him to laugh. They went back to their vats in a body and stirred them with very great concentration, and one of them, a big woman with blue arms to the elbow, had to go out into the yard for a while.

"Floors," said Ward.

"Floors," said Emeric.

That night Heavy got graded, five notches instead of one, and the manual got a page headed Floors, in his hand, with a list under it: flagged stone, any notch; brick, any notch; planking over joists, the first two only and not near a drain; a cart, the first; a boat, none, ever. Under the list Ward wrote, in pencil, in his own square capitals, and the dyehouse.



The oath came at the end of that week, on a night the forge did not run.

All six of them came down. That was the point. Ferra came down the narrow stair first with the lamp, and Imani behind her with her skirts in one hand, and Saban after her with his hat in his hand because the stair was too low for it. Ward and Bram brought Armand down between them in his own chair with poles through the arms, a step at a time, Armand saying at every step that he had climbed a good deal further than this in his day and that if they dropped him he would haunt them, both of them, separately. At the foot of the stair they set him down in front of the stone door, and Emeric lifted it with the stones in it, straight up into its slot in the rock, and they went in.

They were pleased with it first. Emeric had wanted that and had not known how to arrange it, and it arranged itself. Bram showed them the lattice the way he would have shown a visitor a good engine, slapping the cradle with the flat of his hand. Imani walked round the chamber twice and put her palm flat on the glass lining when Bram said it was cool. Armand had his chair brought up close to the control stone and sat with both hands on his stick and looked at it a long while with his mouth slightly open, the way he had looked at the jar, and said that it was the most elegant thing he had seen made in sixty years and that he hated it. Saban picked up the cube, which Bram had brought down to show them, and weighed it in his hand, and asked what it would fetch.

"Nothing," said Bram. "It's an inch."

"Everything's worth something to somebody." Saban put it down. "That's worth a great deal to somebody, I'd think. Somebody who wanted all his inches to agree."

Then they began to say what it could do.

It started with Imani. She asked whether it could make a needle. Not a sewing needle; a hollow one, fine as a hair, for putting a thing into a vein instead of into a stomach. Emeric said it could. It could also make the thing that made them, which was better: a needle out of the forge was one needle and a night of his, and a needle-maker in Bram's shop would turn them out when nobody was in the cellar at all. She said a splint that fitted a child's arm to a hair. A clean vessel that never had a seam for anything to live in. Instruments that would not rust when she boiled them. She went down the list the way she went down a list of the sick, quickly and without raising her voice, and every item on it was something she had watched somebody die for the want of.

Bram said it would make his trade worth nothing. Then he looked at the cube on the bench and said no, it would not, because somebody still had to know what good looked like, and that was a smith. Then he was quiet and Emeric could see him thinking about his apprentices, and how long it had taken him to learn what the forge had learned in a night, and what that was worth now.

Ward said the first thing it would make, in anybody's hands but these, was a crossbow bolt that went through plate.

He said it flatly, the way he said everything, standing by the door with his arms folded. Then a better bow. Then a hundred of them, the same, every part fitting every other. Then the plate to stop them. Then a mage's gravity stone in a frame on a wall, that threw a man back off a ladder. Then a frame on a cart. He did not say he wanted any of it. He said what the first use would be, because that was his work, and because nobody at that table needed protecting from the thought.

Ferra said, "When it's found."

They looked at her.

"Not if," said Ferra. "When. Somebody sees a part that couldn't have been made. Somebody counts the stones going into this keep and the stones coming out." She looked at the bank of feed stones in their row under the chamber, some dull, some bright. "I'm the one writing the line in the book that hides this. So I'd like to know what we do on the day somebody reads the line."

"It's got a shelf life," said Saban. "Every secret does." He turned his hat in his hands. "This one, if nobody here talks and nothing leaves looking odd, I'd give it five years. Seven if you're lucky with the Church. Two if you're not." He shrugged. "What buys more time is the same as anything. Fewer people, and nothing worth the asking where they can see it."

Emeric had been waiting for his own part of it, and when it came he overreached.

He said that the world was not ready for it, and he believed that. Hand this to every crown and baron in the kingdom now and the first thing built would be Ward's bolt and the second would be a war over who owned the machines. And then he said that the Church would say the same thing about magic, and did, and that the Church kept magic scarce on purpose so that it could be the one that handed it out, and that the rite it used to make a knight was a cruelty dressed as a gift, and he went on from there into the tower and the climbs and what Armand had told him two nights before, and got some way along it before Armand stopped him.

"No," said Armand.

Emeric stopped.

"You've watched one rite," said Armand. "One. You've a scar on a young man's back, and a stone worker's sick days in a column, and a story an old man told you over a jar. And you have a priest in this town who is a good man and has done nothing but bury your dead for five years." He put both hands on the head of his stick. "That isn't an institution. That's an old man's grudge and a young man's guess. If you want to stand on something, stand on what you saw."

Emeric was quiet. Armand was right, and he knew it, and it was annoying.

"Then I'll stand on the rite," he said. "Eight mages pour into a boy until it tears him, and he didn't choose it, and nobody counts the ones who die. That I saw." He looked at the forge. "Keep the weapon back from everyone, fine. That I can understand. There's a mercy in it. But don't tell me it's to keep people safe when you're the one cutting them. That's not keeping anyone safe. You just want to be the only monkey with a stick."

Saban laughed, once, through his nose. Bram said nothing, but his eyebrows went up. Armand did not push it; he looked at Emeric, and then down at his own hands on the stick, and his face said that he thought it too and could not prove it either.

It was Imani who turned it round, and she did it inside a minute.

"Look where we are," she said.

She did not say it unkindly. She was standing by the chamber with her hand still on the cool glass, and she looked round the room, at the rock walls and the vaulted brick and the stone door in its slot and the six of them and the prince. "Six people in a hole under a castle," she said, "deciding what a kingdom's allowed to have. With a stick." She looked back at him. "You've better reasons than they have. I believe that. I don't think you've a different shape."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Emeric looked for the answer and did not find one. There was not one; or there was, and it was the one she had already given, that the only thing between this room and the thing he had just been angry at was the conduct of the people in it, and the conduct of the people in it had not been tested yet.

"No," he said. "I haven't."

And he signed.

Ferra had written the oath out on a single sheet of the plain paper in her own hand, short, with no flourishes and nothing in it a lawyer could find a door in. They signed it on the cool lid of the feed cup, one at a time. Ferra, then Imani, who read it through twice first and signed it and then stood back with her arms folded and the look of a woman who has disagreed with something and done it anyway and will remember both. Bram, with the pen held like a punch. Saban, quickly, the way he signed for goods. Armand, slowly, with Imani holding the paper flat for him, in a hand that shook and was still beautiful.

Emeric held the pen out to Ward.

He held it out the same way he had held it out to all the others. He did not say anything to go with it. There was nothing to say that would not have been worse than nothing. Ward reported to the king. He had done it since the day he rode south, in a cipher he thought nobody in Kaldo could read, in letters that never went through Hale. Emeric had known for a long while. And he was handing this man a pen and asking him to keep a thing from the man he answered to, knowing what he was, because a council you lied to was not a council, and because if he could not trust Ward with this he could not trust him with anything, and he did.

Ward looked at the pen. Then at Emeric.

It did not take long. Ward's face did not change; Ward's face very rarely changed. But he looked at Emeric for exactly as long as it took him to understand that he had been seen, and had been seen for some time, and that the pen was being held out anyway. Then he took it, and signed, in his square capitals, and handed it back.

Nobody else at the table noticed anything. Ferra did. She was writing the date under the names, and she did not look up, and her pen did not stop, and Emeric knew from the way she did not look up that she had known what Ward was for a good while longer than he had.

They went up. Ward and Bram took Armand's poles. Saban went ahead with the lamp. At the foot of the stair, getting his hat back on in the low passage, Saban said, lightly, to nobody in particular, "Well. Here's hoping we never use it the way they did."

"Mind the fourth step," said Bram, from under Armand's chair. "It's loose."

And they went up.



The third stone became a cell on the twenty-sixth.

It was the stone that had cracked the first liner, or its brother out of the same bucket. He had put it on the shelf by the forge after the first run and left it there for six days, because grey was not empty, and he wanted to see what empty felt like. Every night he put a finger on it on his way past. On the sixth night, when he touched it, there was nothing. Not grey. Not the faint warm room-somebody-has-left feeling. A hole in the shape of a stone, cold all the way through.

He put it in the chamber and told the forge what it was, and this time he told it the truth.

It shaped like cold wax. A quarter of an hour, no more. He had drawn it a dozen times on the backs of things: an egg, fat and smooth, and growing out of the narrow end of the egg a long neck, thin, straight, as long again as the egg, ending in a flat face cut square. The egg was where the charge sat. The neck was the road it came out by, and the square face at the end of the neck was where it met whatever it was feeding. He watched the stone become that in the lattice, slowly, the round of the egg swelling and the neck drawing out of it like toffee pulled on a spoon, and he held his breath without meaning to.

When it came out Bram held it up to the lamp in his tongs and turned it.

"What's the neck for?"

"The egg holds. The neck carries." Emeric touched the square face at the end of it with one finger. "A plain stone gives up its charge from all over, and you lose a lot of it on the way to where you want it. This gives it up from one place. Here. And it can sit in a slot, with the neck in a socket, and come out again, and another one goes in." He looked at it. "A flask cell."

"It'll break," said Bram. "The first time somebody drops it. That neck."

"Then case it."

Bram cased it. He made an iron sleeve that took the egg, in two halves that screwed together round it, with a collar that held the neck and left the square face bare at the end, and it took him a day, and the sleeve was a thing of great plain beauty that nobody would ever see once it was in a socket. Emeric watched him fit the two halves and add a line to the drawing: for any cell that would fly, a glass liner inside the iron, between the sleeve and the stone. Glass held mana; iron only held stone. A cell in a chair on the ground could live in iron. A cell in a heart that was going up into the sky, a long way up, was going to be glass-lined, because he did not know yet what was up there and he did not mean to find out in a dropped cell.

Bram looked at the line and at him and said nothing, which meant he agreed and thought it was expensive.



The scent was Abloh's fault, in the end, or Abloh's doing.

Emeric had said something at one of Isolde's dinners early in February, about a coast house that sold scent in tiny bottles at a price that would have built a bridge. Abloh had heard about it afterward the way Abloh heard about everything, and had come to him in the works ten days later with a cork in a bottle and asked whether it was true that the soap works' still could make a water from flowers, because the coast house's scent was, so far as Abloh could tell by smelling it, mostly a water from flowers with a lie on top.

It was true. The still had been making spirit since the second year. It had made the soap's scented water for most of that time. Nobody had asked it to make anything for a person.

He had made it in the last week of February, at night, in the soap works, with Abloh at his elbow holding the jars and correcting him. Rosemary from the hospital garden, because it had come up early under the south wall. Orange peel, dried, from the coast boat. A little pine. And at the bottom of it, after a great deal of argument with Abloh, who wanted roses, something that was not roses at all, that he had found by accident on the second night: a drop of the oil off the dyehouse's woad vats, which smelled, when you were not expecting it, faintly and very cleanly of ink.

Abloh had smelled the first finished drop on the back of his own wrist, frowning, for a long while.

"It's not pretty," he said.

"No."

"It's right, though." Abloh put his wrist down. "That's worse. Pretty, I can do. That one's her."

He gave it to her on the twenty-eighth, in the counting room, after the others had gone, in a bottle the size of his thumb with a silk cover over the stopper that Abloh had sewn and would not let him pay for. She took the stopper out and smelled it, and went still.

She did not say anything about it. Ferra did not wear scent; she never had. She looked at the bottle, and then she put a single drop on the inside of her left wrist, the one she rested on the book when she wrote, and pressed the wrist to the page in front of her and held it there. When she took it away the page smelled of it. She looked at the page, and then at him, with a face he had seen on her twice before, both times over a column that had finally come out.

"Now the book smells of it," she said.

"That was the idea."

"I know it was." She put the stopper back. "I could tell."

The next morning Imani came to the soap works. She had heard by breakfast, because by the new rule she would have heard by breakfast whatever happened. She did not want a scent. She stood in the door of the still room with her basket on her arm and said, very reasonably, to Emeric and Abloh together, that since the still was evidently taking requests, she had one. She wanted a wound wash that did not sting. Spirit stung. Salt water stung less but did very little. She had a ward full of children who fought her every time she cleaned a knee, and a fisherman's boy with a fish hook through his eyelid who had screamed so hard the day before that she had had to have Osric hold his head. There was a thing she had read of in one of Emeric's own books, in the hospital's shelf, in his hand: a wash for eyes, made from the white salt the coast boats brought for glazing. Boracic, the book called it. She wanted to know if he could make it, or if it was another of the things in his books that he had written down and never built.

He could make it. Borax and a little of the works' acid, and a crystal that came out of the cooling water white and soft as soap flakes, and a wash made from that, weak, clean, that would not sting a child's eye. It was a poor antiseptic. It was a very good kindness.

He said he would make it that week.

Abloh, who had been listening from the bench with a pine knot in one hand and a knife in the other, put both down.

"One each," said Abloh.

They both looked at him.

"Gifts." Abloh picked the knife back up and went on with the knot. "That aren't work. One each, at the same time, or it's a quarrel in a bottle. Ask any man on the coast with two." He did not look up. "I sewed her stopper. I'll sew hers."

"It's a wound wash," said Imani. "It isn't a gift. It's for the ward."

"Then make it pretty," said Abloh to the knot, "and it's both."

Ferra, when Imani told her at noon, because by the second of their terms Imani told her everything, said yes.
