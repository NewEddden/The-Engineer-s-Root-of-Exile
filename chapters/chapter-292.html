Chapter 292 - The Crown's Act

The potter came down to the stand on the third of November, because Emeric had asked him to, and because Bram had told him at the brickfield on the Sunday that the duke had broken three lengths of his best firebrick in under three minutes, and the potter had wanted to see the man who had done it.

The stand was at the yard's north end, where the slips gave out and the ground went down in a ramp of broken stone to the river's mud: a frame of iron girders on a concrete bed, under a lean-to roof of tarred boards that kept the rain off and nothing else. The can lay in the frame on its side, a steel tube the length of a man and a half and as thick through as a cider barrel, with the spare main stone's housing bolted to its back end like a fist on a forearm and the spring scale's chain running from the frame's foot to a post. Its iris was folded back, its flange unbolted. And laid out along a trestle bench under the roof, in the order they had come out of it, were the pieces of the lining.

The potter walked once round the frame with his hands behind his back, a short broad man with clay dried pale to his elbows, and looked at the steel where it had gone blue along its back and at the iris leaves where they had warped, and said nothing about any of it. Then he went to the bench and picked up a piece of the lining.

"Mine," he said. "My good brick. The white. I fired that for Bram's furnace." He ran his thumb down the crack, which went clean through from the inside face to the outside, as straight as if it had been ruled. "How fast did you bring it up."

"Cold to red in under a minute."

The potter made a sound in his nose.

"You didn't fire it," he said. "You hit it. A new kiln, I bring up over three days and nights, a little more wood every watch, and I still lose a brick in the crown sometimes and hear it go." He held the piece to the light. "Brick grows when it's hot. A hair in a foot. If it's got room, it grows and comes back when it cools and you'd never know. If it hasn't, it breaks, where it's hottest against where it's coldest." He tapped the inside face, then the outside. "And you've built a pipe. Hottest inside, coldest outside where your blown air is, and it's a ring, so it's got nowhere to go at all." He put it down. "I'd have told you that in August, if you'd asked me what brick was for, and not just for brick."

"I'm asking now."

The potter looked at the can a while, at its iron jacket and the channel where the blown air ran between the steel and where the lining had been, the way he looked at a pot on the wheel when it was going wrong and he had not decided yet whether to save it.

"Don't make it a pipe," he said. "Make it tiles. A hand across, curved to the can. Don't set them. Hang them." He held one hand flat and hooked two fingers of the other over its top edge. "An iron hook through the back of each, into the can's wall, and each one hanging a finger's width from the next, all the way round and all the way down, so every tile can grow its own hair and not lean on anybody. And your air. Let it through the gaps. The way I leave a draught between the pots when I set a kiln, so the ones at the back don't come out raw. It'll keep your hooks from sagging, and it'll lay a skin of cool air down the inside of the tiles, between them and the worst of it." He looked at the can. "That's a kiln, turned inside out and laid on its side and told to hurry. I can make you a kiln."

Emeric did not say anything.

He had it in the notebook. He had drawn it in September, the night after the second burn, when the lining had gone through with a sound like a shot and Fennick had sat down on the concrete laughing: curved tiles, the cooling air let through between them and laid along their inner faces in a film. On the other side of everything every engine that burned its fuel in a can was lined so; the drawing was a page out of a book he had read at twenty. It was in his coat. He left it there.

Tess was at the bench's far end in her chair, truing one of the iris leaves on a steel block with a mallet. The iris was the Motion Shop's; she had made it off the brass irises on the K Discovery's viewports, and it had warped in the second burn and she had taken that personally. She had stopped hammering some while ago. She had her chin on her fist.

"He's got the face," she said.

The potter turned round. "What face."

"That one. Look at him." She pointed the mallet. "He drew it in September. It's in his coat, I'd bet you a week's wage. Tiles and air through them, and he's been standing there letting you say it."

The potter looked at Emeric for a long, hard, unfriendly while.

"Not with hooks," said Emeric.

That was true. He had drawn the tiles locked into one another in rings, the way the book had them, which would have held them and cracked them in the end for exactly the reasons the potter had just given; and the potter, who had never read a book about an engine in his life, had hung them up by their backs like pans in a kitchen. Emeric took the notebook out and gave it to him open at the page.

The potter read it holding it a long way off. "Locked," he said. "In rings. They'd have gone by Christmas."

"Probably."

He gave the notebook back. Something in his face had settled, though not into anything like friendliness.

"The hooks are iron. That's Bram, and he'll tell you which iron. I'll make you the tiles. Six rings of nine, by the length of her. And I'll lose some in the firing, so I'll make seventy." He looked at Emeric as if daring him.

"Two in nine," said Emeric.

"I've told you that before."

"Twice."

"Then you know it. Ten days. They dry slow and fire slow and cool in the kiln with the door shut, or you can make them yourself." He went off up the yard with his hands behind his back and did not look round; and Tess went back to her leaf, and did not look at Emeric either, but she was smiling at it.



The K Endeavor came in from the island on the night of the seventh, and her master sent up a note before she had settled on the Mere that said he had something in his hold he wanted off his ship, and would the duke please send somebody who knew what it was.

It was a dockhand, in two halves.

The forty sworn men on the island had been lifting them off the old quay since the summer, on sheerlegs, with two of the yard's small lift stones in cradles for the heavy end, because Tess had asked for them. The first had come up out of the silt in September like a man getting up off his knees, streaming mud, and gone into the hold whole. The second had come up in October on the same slings; and as the slings took its weight it had cracked across the back from one shoulder to the opposite hip, with a sound, the island foreman wrote, like a plate dropped on a flagged floor. The third they had left where it lay until somebody could tell them how to lift it without that happening.

The whole one went to the gallery's far bay, to lie under canvas beside the golem's eleven pieces. The halves went to the Motion Shop's back bay on a flat wagon, and the bay's doors were locked behind them, and Tess and Wren cut the larger one on the eighth and ninth with the masons' wire saw: a loop of steel wire running over two wheels on a frame, a small motor, and a trickle of wet grit fed onto the wire where it bit. Wren fed the grit. She was sixteen, and a year on Ferra's books as the Motion Shop's apprentice with "Motion" underlined after her name, and she fed it exactly, without being told twice, with her tongue between her teeth; and the wire went down through the dockhand's back a finger's depth an hour, singing, with the grey slurry running off into a trough.

Emeric came at noon on the ninth, when Tess sent for him. They were washing the cut face down with a hose.

It was not stone.

A cut through a stone from the hill would have shown the hill's grain running straight across it, the way the rock had lain in its bed before anybody cut a shape out of it; the grain would not have cared what shape it had been cut to. This cared. The cut face was banded, layer on layer, each a finger thick or less and each a slightly different grey, and every band followed the curve of the dockhand's back all the way round, like the rings in an onion cut across. The outermost were the darkest and the finest. Further in they went coarser and paler. And here and there between them ran a line like a hair, paler again, where one layer met the next.

Tess held her glass close to the face and then held it out to him without looking up. Under the glass the bands were grains: finer than flour in the outer bands and like sand in the inner, set in something pale and hard that held them the way mortar holds sand, or gum holds lamp black.

"It's light," said Wren. She had lifted the slab the saw had taken off the end, the size of a roof tile, and was holding it in both hands with a look of great surprise. "I picked it up for rock. It's like lifting a loaf you thought was a brick."

Emeric put his palm flat on the cut face. Nothing at first: cold and wet and gritty, no charge in it anywhere. Then, as he let his sense go down into it slowly, each band answered. Less than a note. A push given back, the way the charging room's sweepings had answered him years ago when he stirred them in the bucket with his sense. Every grain of it had been a stone once. There were more of them under his hand than he could have counted in a year.

Bram came in at a little after one, with his coat over his shoulder, having been at the steelworks since six. Tess did not say anything. She turned her chair a quarter round, so that the cut face was toward him, and waited.

He looked at it the way he looked at a casting just out of the sand, with his whole body, bent over it with his big hands on his knees; then he ran one thumb across the bands from the outside inward, slowly, and stopped at a hair line, and went back and felt it again.

"Poured," he said. "Poured in lifts. Like a bell. You pour your skin in the mould, the fine stuff, because that's what you want the outside to be, and you let it go off, and then you pour the next against it. That's your line, where one went off before the next came on. And it follows the shape all the way round, because the mould did. Rock doesn't know what shape you've cut it." He looked at the two halves on their trestles, grey and squat, the crack across the larger one's back like a mouth. "A statue breaks," he said. "This was poured."

Emeric had said the first half of that once, under the Isle, with the cave golem coming apart at its joints in his hands. It had been true, and it had not been the whole of it.

"I knew," said Tess.

"I know you knew. You turned the chair round and didn't say a word." Bram did not look at her. "You wanted to see if I'd see it."

"I wanted to see if I'd read it right. You taught me to read a pour line."

Bram cleared his throat. "The skillet," he said.

"The skillet." Tess looked at the cut face, not at him. She glanced at Wren, and away, and said it anyway, to the dockhand. "I was twelve. I'd just come to the house and I wouldn't come in it. I was in the loft over the forge and I wouldn't come down. And he came up the ladder with a skillet that had gone wrong, and sat on the top rung because he wouldn't fit through the hatch, and showed me where the iron had gone off before the rest came in." She was quiet. "I didn't come down for a week."

"You came down for the next skillet," said Bram.

"I came down for the next skillet."

He put his hand on the back of her neck, briefly, the big hand that had brought a skillet up a loft ladder, and took it away again; and Tess went on looking at the cut face as if there were nothing in the bay but it, and Wren, at the trough with the hose, watched both of them with the greatest interest.

"Don't," said Tess, without turning round.

"I didn't say anything," said Wren.

"You were going to."

Bram weighed Wren's slab in his hand, and his eyebrows went up a little.

"It's dust," said Emeric. "Drained stones. Like what goes in the charging room's bucket. Every one of those grains was a stone once, and it's set in something. A binder."

"Like the ink," said Tess slowly.

"Like Kaldo black." He could see the pot on the size plate in the finishing loft, on the worst night of a bad winter: lamp black and gum and a pinch of the grey dust, boiled on a charged fire stone by accident, gone black as a hole and pulsing. "That's a pinch of dust in a pot of ink. This is a whole body of it, set hard. They didn't carve them. They cast them out of the dust of their own stones, in layers, on purpose."

"Why?" said Tess. "And why's it strong? The other one came off the quay whole, and that's heavier than a horse. And this one broke like a plate the minute they put a sling on it."

"I don't know," said Emeric.

Tess looked at him to see if he had the face. He did not. She took the black book out of the bag on her chair, opened it at the back, where she kept the things that were not for anybody yet, and wrote two lines in her small fast hand, and turned it round so that he could read them.

Poured. Dust in a binder. Why strong?

Why broke?

"I want the third one lifted on a frame, not a sling," she said, and shut the book. "And I want this one in twenty slices. And I want Bram to tell me what the binder is."

"I don't know what the binder is," said Bram.

"You will by Friday," said Tess, "if I give you a slice."

Bram put Wren's slab down on the trestle very carefully, as if it might be worth more than it looked.

"Mm," he said.



On the ninth of every month Imani counted Armand, and on the ninth of every month Armand asked Emeric to sit in the room while she did it, so that, he said, there would be somebody present who was not counting him.

He was in his chair by the window at four, with a rug over his knees and a book face down on the rug and the low sun across the floor; eighty-seven the spring past, by his own reckoning and the tower's, and able to walk from his chair to his bed and to the table and not much further. He did not use the motor chair in his own room. He said a man ought to be allowed to fall over in his own room if he chose.

"Mistress Imani. Your Grace. I'm told I'm to be counted."

"You're told every month," said Imani.

"And every month I'm surprised. It's one of the pleasures of being old." He unbuttoned his shirt for her without being asked. "Go on. I'll try to be interesting."

She did it as Emeric had shown her on the stool in the dispensary two winters ago: the silver cup of the stethoscope low on the left of his chest, at the tip; two fingers of her other hand inside his wrist; her eyes on the window and her lips moving. A full minute. Both at once.

Emeric did not need to count. He could feel the old man's heart from the chair by the door, going as it had gone since the Hod, out of step, the small rooms on top shivering and the big ones below going when they could, some beats so early and thin that they never reached the wrist at all. He let Imani count it, because it was hers to count.

"Ninety-four at the chest," she said. "Sixty-six at the wrist."

"Is that bad?" said Armand.

"It's twenty-eight." She wrote it on the card in her lap, under ARMAND, at the foot of a column that went back to the winter of the stethoscope. It had been twelve in the spring of last year. "It was twenty-two two months ago. It's going the wrong way."

"And the foxglove?"

"You told me on Tuesday that the lamp had a ring round it. So I came down a grain. I can't go up again." She did not look up. "Is there anything else."

"Nothing at all."

"Bettany says you sat down on the hospital stair on Thursday," said Imani, "for as long as it takes to say the Light's prayer twice through, and wouldn't let her fetch anyone."

Armand looked out of the window. "It's a long stair."

"Where did it hurt?"

He was silent long enough that Emeric thought he would not answer. Then he put two fingers flat on his breastbone, low, in the middle.

"There. When I climb. A weight, as if somebody had sat on me. If I sit down and count to a hundred, it's gone by eighty. Most days by sixty." He looked at her with his pale old eyes, amused and not sorry. "Since the summer. I'm a tower mage, Mistress Imani. We were trained young to put a thing in the right column. I put that one under stairs."

Imani wrote a short line and a date and a word Emeric could not read from the chair, and underlined it twice. Then she looked across the room at him past Armand's shoulder, and her face was perfectly calm, and her hands on the card were not.

"I want something that isn't foxglove," she said. "The foxglove slows it. It does nothing about that." She nodded at the place on Armand's chest. "And it does nothing about the other thing. The top rooms. You told me there was more."

There was. He had told her enough that winter for that winter. He told her the rest now, plainly, to both of them, because Armand had a right to it.

"Blood that stands still clots," he said. "When the top rooms shiver instead of beating, the blood in them stands about in the corners, and a clot can form there and come loose with the next beat and go up into the head." He looked at Imani. "Your old men who were well at supper and woke with half a face. That's where it comes from, most often. And the pain on the stair is the heart's own pipes, narrowed. Climbing, it asks for more than they'll carry. If a clot forms in the narrow part, the pipe shuts. That's the man who dies at his table with his spoon in his hand."

"Charming," said Armand. "Go on."

"There's a thing that makes the blood a little slower to clot. It doesn't mend anything. It won't make the stair shorter. It makes it less likely that either of those kills you tomorrow." He looked at Imani. "It's called aspirin."

She said the word under her breath, twice, as she had said the other one in the dispensary, and wrote it down.

"Where does it come from."

"From you. Your willow bark." She had been buying it by the sack since the winter of the camp fever. "The thing in the bark that works on a fever is the same thing that gives half your fever patients a bellyache. The acid works can take it out clean and change it a little, so it's kinder to the stomach and keeps. Their foreman will want a page from me. He'll have it tonight."

"How much."

"A quarter of a grown man's fever dose. Once a day, every day, with bread. And it costs: he'll bleed longer and bruise if you look at him. If his stool goes black, stop it, and tell me."

"And if I want it out of him?"

"It doesn't wash out like the foxglove. It spoils the specks in the blood that close a cut, and once it's touched one, that one's done. You wait for the body to make new ones. About a week."

Imani wrote it all, line by line, as he said it: ASPIRIN. From the willow. A quarter fever dose, daily, with bread. Bleeds, bruises. Black stool, stop. A week to clear. Then she turned the card over and wrote something small on its back that he did not see, and put it in her pocket.

"Willow," said Armand, buttoning his shirt. "I've taken foxglove on your instructions for three years, Your Grace, and seen rings round the lamps on Mistress Imani's, and grown rather fond of its bitterness. Is willow bitter?"

"Less than the bark."

"Then I shall miss the bark. One misses one's enemies."

It took the acid works five days and two spoiled batches. On the fourteenth the foreman came up the hill himself with a stoppered jar of fine white needles like frost off a window; Imani weighed them on the dispensary balance and pressed the first doses on the pill press the ward used for its chalk; and on the morning of the fifteenth Armand took the first with his breakfast bread, under her eye.

"It tastes of nothing," he said. "I'm disappointed. I was promised a cure."

"You were promised a week," said Imani.

She walked down the stair with Emeric afterward, and said nothing until the turn by the old buttery. Then she stopped on the step above him, which made her for once the taller, and looked down at him in the grey light from the stair window.

"Is it what they did," she said, low. "Where you were."

"Every old man with a heart like his. A little one, every morning. Their doctors told them to." On the other side of everything it had stood on every shelf for a hundred years; it was the first thing a doctor wrote for a heart like Armand's, before anything cleverer. "It's the right thing, Imani. It isn't a guess."

She reached down and found his wrist, two fingers inside it below the band, as she always found it, as if it were hers to take; and he felt her count three or four beats out of habit; and then she stopped counting and only held it.

"Then I'll do it," she said, and let go, and went on down the stair ahead of him to her ward.



Saban brought the council down from the capital on the twelfth, by the Wayford express, in his own head, and brought it to the wine store at seven, where the six were already at the table because his wire had asked Ferra to call them. He sat at the end with his hat in front of him and his hands folded on it, and did not take his coat off.

"The summons was laid on the tenth," he said. "By the lords spiritual, in a body, with the chancery's seal. The Duke of Kaldo to come north before the Feast of the Ascent and answer to the Church for power that did not come through the chamber. The one that's been waiting behind the prince's letter. When the prince's letter got no for an answer, they laid it."

They had all expected it. They had expected it since Ward said no, in October, in front of everyone.

"The king answered it," said Saban.

He told it flatly, as if he were reading out a bill of lading; but slowly, and Emeric understood after the first sentence that Saban had made his man tell it to him three times on the Wayford platform until he had every word.

The king had stood up, which a king did not do in council. And he had said that everything the Duke of Kaldo had done in his duchy and his four baronies, he had done under the crown's leave. The grant of Kaldo was the crown's, under the great seal. The licence for his powder was the crown's. His title was the crown's, set on his head in the crown's own hall. The soldiers he trained were the crown's, posted to him by name under the king's own seal, and the king would say so on any page anybody liked. Whatever the Church chose to call it, it had been done by the crown's man under the crown's hand; and so it was the crown's act; and the Church did not summon the crown. It had never had the power, under any law the realm had kept, and the king did not propose that it should start.

And the eldest of the lords spiritual had asked, in the silence after, whether in that case the crown had kept the oath every king of Randillia swore at his crowning, to keep the realm in the Light.

"And the king said that was the right question," said Saban. "The only one. That he would answer it before the council, as the old law says a king's oath is answered. And that so nobody need ride anywhere to fetch the answer, he would put himself into the council's keeping until it was heard." He unfolded his hands and folded them the other way. "He gave his word on it, and sat down."

"Where," said Ward.

"His own rooms. The west side of the palace, over the garden. The council's men on the door, nobody in or out without the council's leave, his letters read. My man thinks the hearing will be in the spring, when the lords are all in the city. The old law wants two parts in three of the council to find against a king. It hasn't been tried in three hundred years."

"It's not a trick," said Ferra. She had her pen in her hand and had not written anything since Saban began. "It sounds like one. It's true. Every licence has his seal on it; I've the copies in the strongroom. The grant's in his hand. The postings. He hasn't found a way round the law. He's read it out loud."

"He's put himself in a cell," said Imani. "For us."

"Not a cell," said Saban. "His own rooms."

"It's a cell if somebody else has the key," said Imani.

Saban did not argue with that.

"The prince withdrew his offer to ride south," he said. "Very graciously. There was nobody to fetch now, he said." He looked at his hat. "And Augustine said the prayer over the king's word. He's said every prayer at that table for thirty years. My man says he took the king's arm on the stair going out, the way he does on feast days."

Emeric could see it. He did not want to and he could: the long gallery stair, the low winter light, his father old and straight in his black with a hand on the rail; and at his elbow the churchman whose face had not changed in all of Emeric's memory, taking an old man's arm. The council his father had given himself to sat with the lords spiritual on its right hand and Augustine at their head; it always had. Nobody at the table said so. There was nothing in it to say. It was only where the benches were.

Ward had not moved since Saban began, except to say where. He sat with his hands flat on the oak, and his face was the face he wore at the dawn drill; and his note, under Emeric's sense, was very steady indeed, steady the way a wall is steady, with something behind it that was not steady at all and that Ward was not letting anywhere near his hands.

"Who carries his food," said Ward.

Saban looked at him.

"Who cooks it. Who brings it up. Whose men are on that door, by name. Whether he has his own taster or the council's or none." His voice did not change. "He had a taster for this one for fourteen months, when he was seven. I'll want to know he has one now."

"That's a household question," said Saban. "Not a chancery one. It costs."

"I'll pay it," said Ferra, and wrote a line and a figure, and drew a box round it.

"By the end of the month," said Ward. "The names. All of them." He took his hands off the table. "And nobody sends anything north. Not a letter, not a man." He looked down the table at Emeric for the first time. "He'll write, by my road. We wait for his letter."

"Yes," said Emeric.

Saban picked up his hat, and put it down again.

"One more thing. From my man in the household." He did not look at anybody. "The prince said it at his own table on the night of the tenth, to his old clerk, with the door open so it would be heard: that the king had just taken onto the crown, with his own hands, in front of the whole council, the one son in the kingdom who meant to end it." He turned the hat round once. "My man says he didn't sound angry. He sounded like a man proved right about something he'd hoped he was wrong about."

Nobody answered. Ferra wrote a line in her book, and looked at it, and crossed it out.



The first snow came in the night of the fifteenth, and the second Proving class rode north in it at dawn.

Forty-eight of them: the class that had stood in the base's yard in May and heard Ward read the sixty-one names; lords' sons and crown's men in grey, the girl from Milford, and the boy from the port, who had grown four inches since and needed new boots twice. They had had six months, not four, and Imani's last figures for them were better than last year's, which was all she would say about them, and she would not say it twice.

Emeric went out to the base gate in the dark, with the snow coming down slowly in the gate lamps. Ward was on his horse. Abloh's tents were on the pack horses in their bags.

The crown's men had a new sergeant this year, a long lean man from the river garrison with a cough, and he rode out of the column before it moved and pulled up by Ward's horse and saluted, and did not lower his hand.

"Captain. We're posted to you, under the king's seal. We heard yesterday in the canteen where the king is." He kept his hand where it was. "The lads want to know if the posting stands. Whose we are."

"You were posted to me by the king, under his seal, by name," said Ward. "Nobody has unposted you. I am sending you to the floor, which is where you were always going. The floor does not ask who posted you. You go in with what you've built here." He was quiet a moment. "You're his. Tell the lads. And cold water at six, on the road, every morning. I'll know."

The sergeant lowered his hand. "Yes, Captain."

And Ward said their names as they went past him, two by two, out of the gate and onto the white road north: forty-eight names without a list, a nod after each. The boy from the port went red when his came. The girl from Milford did not look round at hers, and then, a horse's length on, did. When the last of them had gone out of the lamps Ward sat a while longer and looked up the road with the snow settling on his shoulders.

"They go into a city with the king in it under the council's key," said Emeric.

"They were going to the floor before he did it. They'd be going if he was dead." Ward turned his horse. "He posted them so they'd go in with half of it built. They've got half of it built. That's his. I'm not going to take it off him because I'm frightened."

He rode back to the base at a walk. It was the only time since the carriage that Emeric had heard him say the word.



The tiles came out of the potter's kiln on the fourteenth, and the third burn was on the eighteenth, in the afternoon.

The potter had lost nine of his seventy and said so to everybody at the brickfield gate. Bram had made the hooks, and told the potter which iron and why, at length, and the potter had listened with his arms folded and said "Hm" at the end. Tess and the potter had hung the tiles themselves, lying on their backs on a board on rollers that Wren pushed into the can and pulled out: six rings of nine, each on its hook a finger's width from the next, so that when Emeric lay on the board and looked up the can's throat with a lamp it was like looking up the inside of a fir cone.

The burn had waited for the yard to be its own again. The Church's paper-collectors had gone north up the Wayford road in the third week of October with the inspector's party and their portfolios, and there had been nobody in Kaldo since who did not belong to it; Saban had said so on the Monday, and Ward had walked the yard's fence on the Tuesday to be sure.

Fennick was there by one for a burn set at three. He had been at both the others, and he walked round the frame four times with his hands in his pockets and asked twice whether it was going to crack again, in the tone of a man asking whether it might.

"Not if it's done right," said the potter.

"Was it done right last time?"

"Last time," said the potter, "it was done by him."

Ferra came down at a quarter to three with her watch and her book and stood on the bank above the stand where she had stood in September, collar up against the wind off the river. Tess had the stand's dial in front of her on the bench, the one the second bimetal strip was corded to, and a stick of chalk. Wren was at the scale.

At three Emeric lifted the brass guard and pulled the lever: the gate in the housing open, so the main stone's blast went straight down the can and not round the hub vanes; the fire stones down onto their seats in the ring; the iris open. One stroke.

The roar went up into a scream.

There was no other word for it. The stone's own roar, which the yard had stopped hearing in September, rose in one long breath as the ring took and the air in the can swelled and went out of the iris faster than it had come in, until it was a scream, high and flat and enormous, that went out over the river and the port and came back off the yard's sheds; and in the drawing office at the yard's head, two hundred yards off, every window rattled in its frame at once and the draughtsmen got up from their boards.

Wren at the scale was shouting something nobody could hear. The needle had gone round to where it had gone in September, nearly twice the stone's own push, and stood there quivering.

At the end of the first minute the iris leaves went dull red, then cherry, and the air behind them over the water shook like the air over a road in August, a long clear shimmer going away across the river's mouth, with the gulls going up off the far mud in a white sheet. No flame; fire stones burned nothing. The shell, which had gone blue down its back in September by the second minute, went a little straw-coloured at the back end, by the stone, and stayed so.

Ferra, on the bank, held up two fingers. Then three.

At three minutes in September the lining had gone through with a sound like a shot. Fennick, at the frame's corner with his hands over his ears, took them off at three without knowing he had and leaned forward, listening for it; and it did not come. Four. Five. Tess's dial crept round a hair at a time toward the chalk line where the cutoff was set. Six. Seven. The potter had sat down on the crate of spare tiles with his hands on his knees and was watching the can's lip with an expression of the deepest suspicion.

Eight. Nine.

At ten Ferra's hand went flat, and the cutoff tripped.

He felt it through his feet on the frame: a clack in the can's wall as the strip at the red line bent past its catch and the fire stones came up off their seats together; and the scream fell, in one long falling breath, back into the stone's roar. He shut the gate and took the stone off, and the hub ran down and stopped, and there was no sound at the stand but the river and the gulls coming back and a thin high ringing in everybody's ears.

Wren looked at the needle going back to nothing, and at Emeric, and sat down on the concrete exactly where she was.

"Ten," said Ferra from the bank. "Ten and a few seconds. To the cutoff."

The can was red the whole of its length now, the shell as well as the lip, the deep sullen red of a horseshoe laid on the anvil, glowing in the grey afternoon under the tarred roof; and the heat came off it in a wall, so that Fennick, who had come forward, stopped and put up his arm. It began to go dark from the back end first, where the jacket air came in coldest, and the dark crept forward along the shell a hand at a time; and the lip stayed red long after the rest had gone grey, the iris leaves glowing in their ring like the last coals in a grate.

"Again," said Fennick.

"When it's black. If I light the ring on that I'm starting from red, and I'll be at the cutoff in four minutes, and the potter will tell us why."

"I'd tell you now," said the potter, from his crate.

So they waited, all of them, round the frame in the wind, and nobody said anything, and Ferra watched her watch. The last of the red went out of the iris a leaf at a time, the way the lamps went out down a street.

"Fourteen minutes," said Ferra. "And a half."

Fennick looked at her as if she had told him a lie. Emeric had worked it out in September from the steel's weight and the jacket's flow and come to about ten.

"A quarter of an hour," he said. "Ten minutes to burn. Then a quarter of an hour, or the next one starts hot. Every time." He looked at Tess. "That's the rule."

Tess wheeled her chair to the frame's nearest upright and wrote it on the girder in chalk, large, at the height of her own eyes.

10. THEN 15.

Ferra wrote it in her book on the bank and drew a box round it, and after a while another round that.

When the can would bear a hand near its mouth the potter lay down on Wren's board with a lamp and was pushed up inside, and they heard him in there for some time, knocking softly on things with a knuckle. When she pulled him out he lay on the board looking up at the roof.

"Well?" said Tess.

"Not one," said the potter, as if somebody had cheated him. "Not a hair. Every tile on its hook and every hook as straight as I hung it." He got up and dusted his apron, which made no difference. "I'll have to find something else to complain of."

"You'll find something," said Tess.

"I always do," said the potter, with dignity, and went off up the yard; and Emeric saw him stop by the drawing office, where the draughtsmen were still standing in the doorway, and say something over his shoulder, and walk on, and every one of them laughed.

The line room's boy came running down the yard at four with a slip. The harbourmaster wished to know, with his compliments, what in the name of the Light had screamed.



The letter came that evening, a little after eight.

He felt Ward coming before he heard him: the steady note crossing the inner ward from the wine store and coming up the keep stair, not fast, the same note he had felt at the table on the twelfth, steady and holding something back behind it; so that by the time the knock came at the study door Emeric had already put down his pen.

Ward came in with his own clean copy in pencil, folded. Ferra, at the study table with the afternoon's numbers, looked at the sheet and then at Ward's face, and shut her book.

"It came on the Hod's run," said Ward. "In the spine of a ledger, for the Milford Book's binders. His hand. Written on the eleventh. The day after."

"Read it."

Ward read it standing, in his own flat voice, the cipher's dates and formulae and all, the way the cipher was read; and then the king.

The king was well. He was in his own rooms, which were warm, with more books in them than he had read in thirty years and the time to read them. He had done what he had done on purpose and in good order, and he would answer for it as the law said, and the law said he could.

He had known for two winters what the captain had written him from Kaldo. He had believed it, and had not been able to give it to anybody; he had sent his soldiers south by name and kept his mouth shut in his own council while the floor stayed open, and that had been the most a king could do. This he could do. He had chosen it at his own table with the law open in front of him, a long while before the Church laid anything, against the day it did. It was not done in haste and it was not to be undone in haste.

"Do not come north," Ward read. "Not you. Not the captain. Not the old man. Nobody of yours, on any road, for any reason, for this. I have made it mine. Let it be mine." His voice did not change. "Do not come north until I send for you."

Emeric sat very still.

He knew the sentence. He had had it whispered to him at twenty-two, at a volume that died at arm's length: do not come back until sent for. And before that he had been eight and sat in a bed while a man sat on the edge of it, which a king had never done, and wore a pleasant face over something enormous and furious the way a man holds a door shut from the inside, and told him what to be so that nothing would come through. Fourteen years his father had held that door, with his own face and his son's, with every cruel thing he said in public and every silence he kept in private; and Emeric had grown up behind it and never once seen the man's back.

He was holding it again. With his own body, this time, in his own rooms, under somebody else's key.

"There's more," said Ward. "Keller will stand for me. He came to me before I could ask. He says the law is on my side and that it will not matter, and he would like it written down somewhere that he said both." He paused. "Keep your books as you keep them. Honestly. All of them. It is the only thing you have that they cannot say you made up."

He folded the sheet in half, and in half again. "The rest is buckram prices."

Nobody said anything for a while. Ward's face was exactly as it always was, and his note was steady, and Emeric could feel what it cost him to keep it so, the way he could feel the cost of a hold in his own arms.

Then Ward sat down on the chair by the door, which he never did in the study, and took the pencil from behind his ear and began to write on the back of the copy on his knee. Short lines. Emeric could read them upside down from the desk. The rooms. The door. The food. Who tastes. Keller: when, what he needs. Hearing: spring. Two parts in three: who.

"I'll write tonight," Ward said, without looking up. "By the same road. That we heard. That the class went north at first snow, all of them, and the sergeant asked whose they were and I told him. That you'll do as he says."

"Will I?"

Ward looked up. "Yes. So will I." He looked at the lines on his knee. "He put me in it by name. He knew what I'd want to do before I knew it. He always has." He went back to his writing. "Saban's man will find out who carries his supper. Keller will have every paper we've got with the king's seal on it, copied fair, by the spring, and Ferra will see to it. And the old man will not get on a horse." He underlined the last line. "That's what I'll tell him. He'll sleep better for knowing it's being done properly than for anything you could say."

He stood up and put the copy inside his shirt.

"Ward," said Emeric.

Ward stopped with his hand on the door.

Emeric did not know what he had been going to say. He had nothing to say to a man who had guarded his mother and carried him out of a mountain and kept his father's road all those years without once using it for himself that was not either too small or too large for a study at half past eight in the evening. Ward waited, and then nodded, once, as if he had been told whatever it was anyway, and went out; and Emeric heard his note go down the stair, steady, all the way to the bottom.

Ferra came round the table. She took the pen out of his hand, which he had picked up again without knowing it and was holding without writing anything, and put it down on the blotter, and laid her own hand on his where the pen had been.

"He's read the law out loud," she said. "And he's right. And Keller's right." She was quiet. "I don't know what else there is to say about it that's true."

"Nor do I."

She looked at him a while, close, in the lamplight; then took her hand away and went back to her side of the table and picked up her book.

"Imani's on the floor at nine," she said. "Come when you come." And as he got up and went to the door: "Take your coat."

He took his coat.



The yard was dark. The arc lamps along the K Amara's frames were off for the night, and the only light was the watchman's lantern going away along the far slips and the town's glow on the cloud over the hill. There had been a frost since dusk. The concrete at the stand was white with it, and the tarred roof, and the girders of the frame, all except the can.

It lay in its frame on its side as they had left it, its iris open, grey in the dark. It had not been red for five hours. But it was still warm; he could feel it on his face from a yard off, and the frost that had come down on everything else had not come down on the can, and the ground round the frame's feet was black and wet where the frost had tried and given up. It ticked. Every little while the shell let out a small sharp sound as some part of it drew in on itself a hair's breadth; and then a long quiet; and then another, from somewhere else along it.

Tess's chalk was on the girder beside him at the height of her eyes. 10. THEN 15.

He stood beside it.

The watchman's lantern came back along the slips after a while, and stopped a little way off, and went on without coming nearer. The tide came in over the mud below the ramp with a small dry sound in the reeds. Up on the hill one window in the keep stayed lit, and then, a long time later, went dark. He did not see it go.

The can ticked in the cold, slower and slower; and he stood beside it until it stopped.
