Chapter 374 - The Chair

Ferra had drawn the chart on the back of a tide table because it was the largest sheet in the long room, and she pinned it to the table at the corners with the salt cellar, the inkwell, Saban's hat and Bram's elbow.

"That's a person," she said, "and that's a person, and those are all people. The lines are who answers to whom. Read it from the bottom."

It was the first of December, a Monday, at a little after nine at night, with the shutters closed and the fire built up and the table cleared of everything but the chart and the cups. The six were round it. Emeric sat at its end. Nobody else was in the room, and he had told the man on the door that nobody else was coming.

They read it from the bottom. It was the whole of Kaldo, as it would stand on the morning the fleet went out: every department and every desk, every works floor and every ward, with a name at each, and a line up from each to a second, and a line up from each second to a box at the top. Ilsa in Ferra's chair at the counting house, with Hobb and Wenna and Dace under her. Osric over the home knights and the fire company. Tess over Works and the long list, with Dagny's old Wayford team. Cade at the yard. Ambrose at the line room. Orla Garran at the Well. Saban's own man for Trade, whose name Saban had written in himself and whom nobody else at the table had ever met. Sela at the school. Bettany's senior nurse at the ward, since Bettany was going. The seconds. Ferra had been building them since July, one at a time, the way she built a ledger.

The box at the top was empty.

"That," said Ferra, "is what's left. Everything a second can't decide, or two seconds don't agree on, or that's bigger than a department. It goes up a line. And the line goes to that box. And in April, with the six of us and him a thousand miles out and talking to home on a box in the sky once a week if the weather's good, there's nobody in it." She put her finger on it. "Somebody's got to sit there."

"The king's father," said Bram.

"The king's father's going to be regent," said Ferra. "For the kingdom. With a seal, if Keller has his way, and Keller generally does. He'll be in the capital a week of every month signing things, and the rest of the time he'll be here with his wife, and he'll be the crown." She didn't look at Emeric. "Kaldo isn't the crown's. We've spent sixteen years saying so. The day the crown sits in Kaldo's chair as well, every lord in the kingdom will say we were lying all along, and they'll be right."

"Amara, then," said Bram, and then, before anyone else could, "No. I know."

"She's the first voice on the relays," said Ferra. "Every message that comes home comes to her first. And she can't be seen to be anything. Saban's rule. A queen who's dead can't sit in a chair."

"Waylon," said Ward.

"Has Highcairn and the Isle," said Ferra, "and is going to have a stone in him by February and a wife by next summer and the Isle's works coming up in the open, and if I put Kaldo on him as well I'll break him, and he's the only one of them who'd say yes anyway because he can't say no to his brother." She moved her finger. "Tess has Works. Osric has the knights. I'll not put the box on anybody who's already in a box under it. They'd be deciding their own appeals."

She sat back.

"So you've already decided," said Imani.

"I've decided what it isn't." Ferra looked at Saban. "You've got the rest."

Saban picked his hat up off the chart's corner, which let it curl, and turned it round once in his hands, and put it down again on the corner, which flattened it.

"Corbin," he said.

Nobody said anything. The fire spat.

"I know," said Saban. "I know. I've had him watched for two years and nine months. I've had his letters read and his tram fares counted and his confession timed. I know what he is. I know what he was." He laid his hand flat on the hat. "And I'm going to tell you why I want him in that box, and then you can all tell me I've gone soft in my old age, and I'll agree with you, and we'll still do it."

"Go on," said Ward.

"When they come," said Saban, "while we're gone, and they will come, because it's the only time they'll ever have, they'll not come with hulls. They tried hulls. They lost the hulls." He didn't look at Emeric. "They'll come the way they came last time. The way they've always come. A kind old man with his hand on somebody's head. A confessor. A friend at the door, who's very worried about the king's health, and his brother's, and wouldn't the chair like a little help." He turned the hat a quarter round. "I can watch the door. I can watch who comes through it. I can't feel what it's like when they start walking in a man. I've never had it done to me. None of us has."

He looked round the table at each of them in turn.

"He has. For fourteen years. From the inside. He knows what it feels like when they start, before they've done anything you could write down. He knows what Augustine's voice sounded like when it was being kind. He knows the clerks and the confessors and the lay brothers, and which of them took Ostrel silver and which only took a blessing; he gave me nine names the night he got here, and seven of them were right." He took his hand off the hat. "If they come for Kaldo while we're gone, I want the man in that box to be the one man in the kingdom who'd know it was happening. Not because I trust him. Because he'd feel it."

"That's an alarm," said Imani. "Not a chair."

"It's both," said Ward.

Everyone looked at him. He sat with his big hands folded on the table in front of him, looking at the chart.

"The terms," he said. "Ferra wrote them. Trust is slow, and he sets the pace by what he does. Two reviews. Watchers halved." He nodded at the empty box. "That's what slow looks like, at the end. It's not a reward. It's the next thing. If we don't give it him, we're saying the terms were a lie, and he'll know, and so will everybody who ever read them." He looked up. "And the seconds do the work. He sits on top of it. If the seconds are good, the box doesn't do very much."

"And if he does something with it," said Imani.

"Then we were wrong," said Ward, "and we'll know."

"We'll know from a thousand miles away."

"Yes."

Armand had said nothing at all. He sat by the fire with his cup on his knee and his eyes on the chart, and when Emeric looked at him he said, mildly, "He mends Doran's benches. Without being asked. He's mended every one. Doran told me in September that he never talks while he does it." He drank. "I don't know what that's worth. I find I want to know."

Ferra wrote Corbin in the empty box, in pencil, very small.

Then she looked at Emeric, at the end of the table, and waited, because he hadn't said anything yet, and he had had his hands flat on the boards in front of him the whole time, which she knew.



"Yes," he said. "Corbin."

And then he told them the rest.

He had to start further back than he wanted to. He had to tell them, first, the thing Ferra had said in October, in the bedroom with the lamp turned low, that nobody else should know: how the pope did it. The stone in the long grey hands in the water. The loop that went both ways and lost nothing. And the other end of it, the one-way piece, cut loose, carrying an order. A script, sitting in a stone. Why the cardinals obeyed. How Augustine had ended, from a vault a thousand miles away. What each one had cost him.

Ferra watched him tell it and didn't stop him. She had nodded to him across the table at the start, very slightly, which was how he knew not yet had come.

Saban heard all of it without moving, and at the end of it took his hat off the chart and held it in both hands in his lap, and said, to Ferra, "October."

"October," said Ferra.

"Seven weeks."

"I told him not to tell you. I told him not to tell anybody. It was the only thing we had that they don't know we have." She didn't apologise. "It still is."

Saban looked at her a long moment. Then he said, "Good," and meant it, and put the hat down again.

And then Emeric said what he had come to say, which was that he wanted to do it to Corbin.

He said it plainly. He'd decided, sitting on the bottom stair of the keep on Thursday night with his hand on the cold stone, that if he said it at all he'd say it plainly and not dress it in anything. A one-way piece of himself, cut loose, set in a stone. Not in Corbin; it couldn't live in a man. In the anklet. In the drain stone that already lay flat against his brother's skin and had for two years and nine months, so that nobody could take it out without taking the band off, and nobody could take the band off without Ferra's key. Set during a check. Corbin had asked for the quarter's read himself, in a letter to Imani after the first review, so that somebody would tell him if anything of Augustine's was still walking about in him; and Emeric had done it every quarter since, and the next was on Thursday.

"It wouldn't do anything," he said. "Unless. If he gives the Church a way into Kaldo, and knows he's doing it. If he gives an order meant to hurt Kaldo's people. If the anklet's cut, or forced, by anyone." He kept his voice level. "Then it does one thing. It stops his heart. The way Imani starts one, backward. And it burns its own stone to dust, so there's nothing left of it, and nothing of me in it, for anybody to find."

"Nothing of you," said Armand.

"It's a piece of me. That's what it's made of. It doesn't come back. Not if it fires, not if I take it off. To take it off I'll have to burn it." He made himself say the next part too. "I don't know which piece. I know it'll cost something. I don't know what. I don't think you get to choose."

The room was very quiet. Bram had his arms folded on the table and was looking at a knot in the board between them.

"No," said Ward. "No objection."

They looked at him.

"You can't be too careful," said Ward. "Not with a man who wanted you dead for twenty years. Not a thousand miles out. I'd have put a knight on his door with a sword, if we'd been the old kind of kingdom. This is quieter." He unfolded his hands. "That's all."

"It's the Church's trick," said Imani.

She said it to Emeric, down the length of the table, and she didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to.

"It's exactly what they did to him. The thing he came here to get away from. A man with his hand on his head, telling him it's for his own good, telling him it's a check, and putting something in him he didn't agree to." She put both hands round her cup. "You'd do it in a delve he opened for you because he trusts you to look. That's consent for one thing used for another. That's the liturgy. That's I do on the chapel step and the pope's hand in your head." She held his eyes. "I'll not be the one who tells him it's a check."

"You won't have to be."

"I know I won't. You'll be the one." She looked down at her cup. "That's worse."

"It costs him," said Armand, from the fire. "The pope. You said so. A piece every time. Two thousand years of pieces." He turned his cup on his knee. "I spent sixty years in a tower with old men who'd forgotten more than they knew. Every one of them lost it by accident, a little at a time, and hated it. He's done it on purpose, two thousand times, and called it government." He looked into the cup. "You'll be giving what he gave. Once. You'll not get it back, and you'll not know till you go looking for it." He paused. "I'd like it said, in this room, that I said so."

"It's said," said Ferra, and wrote it.

"And it can't ask what he meant," said Armand. "That's the other thing. You know it. A piece cut loose doesn't think. It does the one thing. If he gives an order on a bad night that looks like harm and isn't..."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it out loud so it's in the book with the other one."

Saban was for it. He said so in two sentences, the first of which was yes, and then he said, with real irritation, that he'd sooner it were in a stone he could look at. "I can't audit it. That's what I mind. I can't read it, I can't check it, I can't see whether it's done what it says it'll do. I've got to take your word that it's in there and does nothing till it should." He turned his hat. "I don't take anybody's word. That's my job. I'll take yours. I'll not like it."

Bram didn't say anything. He sat with his arms folded looking at the knot in the board, and when Ferra looked at him, pen up, he shook his head once, not no, but not me, and she wrote nothing beside his name.

Then it was Ferra.



She put her pen down. She did that rarely, in the long room, and they all saw her do it.

"I'm against it," she said.

Nobody spoke.

"I'm against it because Imani's right, and because Armand's right, and because Saban's right about the part he doesn't like. And I'm against it for a reason none of you have said, which is that we wrote it down." She looked at Emeric. "You stood in the square three years ago in front of the whole town and Augustine, and said what they'd done to him was the worst thing one man can do to another. We wrote down that we do things differently here. It's in the terms we read him. He signed them." She put her hand flat on the chart, on the empty box with his name pencilled in it. "And now we're going to give him the box, and the key to the Forge, and the look of every bit of trust we've got, in the same week we put a knife in his boot that he doesn't know about. That's a lie. A very good one. It's the best lie anybody at this table's ever told. And it's ours."

"Ferra..."

"I'm not finished." She didn't look away from him. "And I'm not going to stop you."

That went round the table like a cold draught.

"I could," said Ferra. "You know I could. The written veto's for a dead hand. You wrote that yourself. A thing that goes on doing what it was told after the man who told it's gone, and can't be argued with. That's what this is." She tapped the board. "A dead hand in a stone, on your brother's ankle. It's exactly what it's for. I've used it once."

"I know."

"And I'm not going to use it now." She took her hand off the chart. "Because if he'd won, we'd be dead. All of us. Or in cells under the palace, and Tess in the street with her stick, and the dome a ruin and the tree cut down for kindling. He'd have done it. He'd have done it and called it the kingdom's good. And none of us knows what he'd have done since. None of us. We like what we see. He mends benches." She looked at Armand, briefly, and back. "Wanting him to be better doesn't make him better. I want it. I want it very much. That's not a reason. That's a hope."

His father had said those words to him once, over two cups in the keep, the night before he laid down the crown. Emeric didn't know whether Ferra knew that.

"So I'm against it," said Ferra, "and I'll not stop it, and I'm going to write that down. Both halves." She picked up her pen. "In my own hand, not the clerk's. So that if it goes wrong, the one who could have stopped it is on the page. And if it doesn't go wrong, the one who was against it is on the page too."

She wrote. They watched her. It took about a minute, and it was the only sound in the room, and when she'd done she turned the book round on the table so that Emeric could read it, and he read it.

1 Dec. The leash on C. Against: F., I. No objection: W. For: S. (cannot audit). A. says what it costs. B. says nothing. F. holds the veto and does not use it. F.

"Is Amara coming?" said Imani.

She knew the answer. She was asking so that he'd have to say it.

"No," said Emeric.

"Why not?"

"Because she'd talk me out of it." He looked at the book. "She's the only one who could."

Nobody said anything to that. There wasn't anything to say. Imani looked at him for a long moment and then down at her cup, and Ferra turned the book round again and shut it on her pen.



The Forge door was at the bottom of the Works' oldest stair, a long way down, in the rock, where it had always been: a slab of grey stone without a handle, as tall as a door and as wide as two, that didn't open for anybody who hadn't been told it could.

Tess came down the stair on the Tuesday at eleven with her stick and her hand on the rail and Wren behind her, in case. Corbin came down behind Wren, with his watcher a flight behind him, and Emeric came last.

At the bottom, in the lamplight, Emeric took the key out of his pocket and gave it to Tess.

It wasn't a key. Kaldo's word was a key, because it did what a key did; it was a stone, a little flat oval one the size of a thumb, cut from the same grey stock as the citizens' cards and set in a brass ring that went over her finger. Vesk had cut the blank. Emeric had keyed it on Sunday night, the way the card bank keyed a card: to her note, and to nothing else. It wouldn't open the door for him. It wouldn't open it for anybody in the world but her.

She put it on. It was a little large. She turned it round on her finger to see the stone, and then put her palm flat on the door, and the door felt her and went back into the rock without a sound, the way a held breath goes out, and the warm air of the Forge came up the stair at them with the hum in it.

Tess didn't go in. She stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame and looked into the long lit room beyond, where she'd been a hundred times with him and never once on her own, and her face did something he'd only seen it do over a new drawing.

"It's mine," she said.

"It's yours."

"Not lent."

"Not lent. It was always going to be yours. I'm only making it so nobody can say it wasn't." He turned round to his brother, who had stopped on the bottom step with his hand on the rail and was looking at the open door and the light without any expression at all. "Corbin."

"Majesty."

"When I'm gone, she comes down here whenever she likes. Day or night. On her own or with whoever she brings. You let her. You let her, always, and you don't ask her why." He held his brother's eyes. "Whatever anybody says. Whatever the seconds say. Whatever comes up the line to you in that box with a reason on it. If she wants the Forge, she has it."

Corbin looked at Tess in the doorway, and at the ring on her finger, and then back at Emeric.

"Why would I ask her why?" he said.

"Because somebody's going to ask you."

Corbin was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, once, the way their father nodded at a paper that was correctly drawn.

"Then they'll be asking the wrong man," he said. He looked at Tess again. "I'll not ask."

Tess, in the doorway, looked round at him over her shoulder, a long considering look, the look she gave a weld that might be good.

"Thank you," she said. She said it to Corbin, not to Emeric.

"It's not a favour," said Corbin. "He's told me to."

"I know," said Tess. "I'm still saying it."

Emeric stood at the foot of the old stair in the warm air coming up out of the Forge and looked at his brother, who was looking at a woman on a stick with a ring on her finger in a doorway full of light, and who had just been handed the look of every scrap of trust Kaldo owned, and had taken it the way he took everything now, plainly, without thanks, as a thing that was correctly added.

Two days.

He went back up the stair behind them.



The check was on the Thursday, the fourth, at four in the afternoon, in the small room off the long room where it had been done every quarter since the first review: two chairs facing, a table between with nothing on it, a window onto the upper ward. Imani's nurse had been at the first two. Corbin had said, at the third, that he didn't need a nurse, and Imani had said that wasn't his decision, and then had sent the nurse anyway and told her to sit outside.

She was outside now. Nobody was in the room but the two of them.

Corbin sat down in the chair by the window, the one he always took, and put his foot up on the low stool the way he did in his own rooms, so that the anklet showed above his boot, the gold leaves on it catching the window's grey light, and held out his hands, palms up, on the table.

"Same as usual?"

"Same as usual."

Corbin nodded and shut his eyes.

He opened. He always did, now; he had learned how in two years, a little at a time, and he did it the way he did everything, carefully and completely and without comment. The door between them came open under Emeric's hand, not easy, not like Ilenne's, but open: a man who had decided to let somebody look and had gone and stood out of the way.

Emeric went in.

It was the same house it had always been, the one he'd walked through in the yard the winter before the crowning with his brother's blood on his knuckles: rooms, and halls, and the furniture worn bright where somebody else had walked. It was less worn now. That was what the checks were for. Every quarter he went through it, room by room, looking for the bright places, for anything of Augustine's still standing where it shouldn't, and every quarter there was less. The chapel room was still there. He'd never touched it, and he didn't now. But there was dust on the floor of it this time, a little, as if nobody had been in for a while.

He did the check first. He did all of it, properly, room by room, because it was a check and that was what Corbin had opened for, and he would not cheat him of the thing he'd asked for even now. It took twenty minutes. There was nothing. There had been nothing for three quarters.

Then he did the other thing.

The narration of it, afterwards, when he let himself think about it, he gave himself once. He said to himself, in the small room, with his brother's open hands under his and his brother's mind standing quietly out of the way to let him look: for Kaldo. For Tess on her stick and Imani's ward and the school and the stone at the foot of the lane. For everybody who'll be here when I'm a thousand miles off and can't come back in a day. Because if he'd won, we'd be dead, and nobody knows what he'd have done since, and wanting doesn't make it so.

He said it once. Then he stopped letting himself say it, and did it.

It wasn't hard. That was the worst of it. He'd known how since the garden wall in October; he'd seen it whole, the way you see how a lock's cut once you've seen the key. You took a piece of yourself, the way you'd take a piece of a delve to send it ahead of you into a stone. You gave it the one thing to do and the three conditions to do it on, and nothing else, no room in it for anything else, because a piece with room in it would think and a piece that thought might come home. And then you let it go. You cut the line.

He set it in the drain stone. He felt the stone against Corbin's skin, flat and cool under the gold leaves, an old stone and a dumb one, that had done one thing for two years and nine months, which was take what a man threw and give it to the air. He went into it through the mana that ran between his brother's skin and the stone, the way he'd go into any stone through a hand on it. And he put the piece there, and told it the three things, and the one thing, and let go of it.

He felt it go.

It wasn't like anything he had a word for. The nearest was a tooth drawn under ether: you felt the pull, and the root give, and then nothing, a place where there'd been something and wasn't, and you couldn't for the life of you have said which tooth. He reached for it, out of habit, the way your tongue goes to the gap. There was nothing to reach. Something was gone. He didn't know what. He went looking for it, quickly, through his own rooms, the way you'd pat your pockets for a key: the lathe, the dome, the first night in the cottage, Ferra's face at the counting house window, Imani's hands, his mother on the stair. All there. All of it there. He didn't know what wasn't.

He came out of the stone and out of his brother and sat back.

Corbin opened his eyes.

He didn't move for a moment. He sat with his hands still palm up on the table and his foot on the stool and looked at the window, at the grey light in the upper ward, at a tram going by. Then he looked down at his own hands.

"That was longer than usual," he said.

"Was it?"

"A little." Corbin turned his hands over and put them flat. He looked at them, not at Emeric. "Not much."

"There was a lot of dust in the chapel," said Emeric. "I looked at it for a while."

Corbin thought about that. His face, in the grey light, did what it did now, which was nothing that could be read, and Emeric had sat across a table from that face for two years without once knowing what was behind it, and found that he could not now, either, and that it frightened him more than it had in the yard.

"Dust," said Corbin. "Good." He took his foot off the stool. The anklet's leaves caught the light and went dull again under his trouser cuff. "Is that all, Majesty?"

"That's all."

Corbin stood up. At the door he stopped with his hand on the frame, the way he'd stopped at the door of his own rooms on the first night, two years and nine months ago, with a folded sheet of names in his other hand.

"Thank you for looking," he said. "I know it's tedious." And went out, past the nurse on her chair in the passage, who got up and went in to see whether the king needed anything, and found him sitting at the table by the window with his hands flat on it, looking at nothing, and asked, and he said no.



He went to his mother that night because he went to her every Thursday.

She was in the dome, in her own body on the mattress at the foot of the tree, asleep, which was where she was most evenings now that the made one did the days; and the made one was sitting up against the trunk beside her with its hands in its lap and its eyes open, the way it sat when she'd gone back into it to wait for him. The tree hummed. The green light came down through the tracery and lay on the gravel and on her sleeping face and on the made one's plated knees.

"You're late," said the made one, in her voice.

"I was at the long room."

"You're always at the long room." She turned her head. The seeing stones in the made face were very good, Tess's best, and they caught the tree's light and gave it back, and behind them she was looking at him. He felt her look. She was a delver; she'd taught nine people on boards how to sleep and wake; she didn't need eyes to look at him. "Come here."

He went and sat on the gravel beside her with his shoulder against the made knee, the way he had in the summer, and she put the made hand on his head, carefully, keeping it light, because it was a little too heavy and she knew it.

"What is it?" she said.

He wanted to tell her.

He'd known he would. He'd known it on the stair on Thursday night, a week ago, when he decided to do it, and on Monday at the table when Imani made him say it out loud, and this afternoon in the small room with his brother's hands under his: that at the end of the day he'd come here, and sit down by the tree with her hand on his head, and want to tell her more than he'd wanted anything in a long time. That was why he hadn't let her into the room on Monday. She'd have heard him out. She'd have asked one question, the right one, the one none of the six had asked, and he didn't know what it was, and he'd have had no answer to it, and he'd have gone to the small room this afternoon and done the check and nothing else.

He'd known that. So he had kept her out. That was the whole of it.

"Nothing," he said. "A long week."

The made hand stayed on his head. She didn't press. The tree hummed above them, and down on the mattress her own body breathed, slow and even, asleep.

"All right," said Amara, after a while, quite gently. "When you want to."

He sat there with his shoulder against her knee and her hand on his hair until the dome's lamps went down for the night, and didn't, and she let him.

On the way back up through the castle, on the keep stair, he passed the door of the rooms on the second floor. There was a light under it. Corbin was up, then; reading, or writing to Sabina, or doing whatever it was he did with his evenings that the watchers wrote down every week and that came to nothing. Emeric stopped on the stair outside it a moment without meaning to.

He had the feeling, standing there, that he had meant to remember something. It had been on the tip of his mind as he came up the stair, the way a name is, and now that he stopped for it, it wasn't there.

He stood a moment longer, waiting for it to come back, and it didn't, and it didn't seem to matter, and he went on up.
