Chapter 262 - Demons of the Isle

Ferra came at ten, and she had not brought a book.

In five years he had not once seen her come along the passage at night with nothing under her arm. She shut the door and stood with her back against it in her slate-grey dress, like a woman who had come to say a thing and decided on the stair that it would keep. Then she took the pen out of the knot of her hair and laid it on his table by the lamp, and came and sat on the end of the bed, where he sat against the wall in his shirt with a page of Bram's on his knees about the Gold Rose's crank axle, which he had read four times.

"Bram says it's true to a hair," he said.

"Bram says that about everything he hasn't finished."

He put the page on the floor.

Imani came a quarter of an hour after. He felt her note come up through the keep, quick and bright, slowing at the third step below his landing that had been loose for five years and mended in the spring, where she slowed anyway. She knocked once, which she never did, and came in without waiting, which she always did, and set her bag inside the door and did not open it, and took her boots off.

Nobody said anything about the morning. Imani turned the lamp down to a bead. Ferra opened the shutter a hand's width, because she liked to hear the river and said it was the only thing in Kaldo that never asked her for anything. Then Imani climbed over him to the wall side, all knees and elbows, and lay down along it with her head on his shoulder; and Ferra lay down on the outside, on the edge where she always said she would fall off and never had, and put her hand flat on his chest.

Imani found his wrist. She did not say the first number. He felt her lips move against his shoulder, counting, and stop, and start again from the beginning.

"Well?" he said.

"Seventy-four."

"That's high."

"It's high," said Imani.

Ferra's hand did not move. It lay over his heart under the open neck of the shirt with the fingers spread, and he could feel his heart going up into her palm, hard and even, the same beat Imani was counting at the wrist; and Ferra was counting it too, he did not doubt, in the column at the back of her head she kept for things that were not money.

"You'll each get a different figure," he said. "Then you'll want a third opinion, and it'll be Bettany's, and she'll write it in the rat book."

"We'll get the same figure," said Ferra.

"Seventy-two," said Imani. "Now."

Ferra was quiet for a few breaths. "Seventy-two."

He laughed, and they both felt it, one in her palm and the other in her two fingers, and neither of them laughed with him.



The bell in the gate tower went eleven, and later twelve.

Neither of them slept. Ferra went down into sleep all at once, most nights, like a book shut, and Imani slid into it a little at a time; tonight neither note went anywhere. They lay on either side of him with their weight on him, awake, and let him know it by not pretending otherwise.

At one Ward's boots came along the passage on his round, stopped outside the door for the length of a breath, and went on.

"Abloh wants paying for the hoods," said Ferra, into the dark. "By Friday. He's charged for the dye separately. I'll not have it. Dye's in the cloth." She was quiet. "I'll see him Friday."

She said Friday the way she said a figure she wanted read back to her.

"Your feet are cold," said Imani.

"They're always cold."

"They're colder."

He moved one foot until it found hers under the blanket, and she hissed at it and did not take hers away.

Later Ferra's pins began to come out; he heard each go down on the floorboards, a small dry tick, and counted them without meaning to, as she would have. Seven. Her hair came down over his shoulder, smelling of ink and her soap. Imani's fingers were still on his wrist, and every so often they pressed, the way a hand on a rope tells the man at the other end it is still there.

He went through the kit, because it was there to go through. Eight flasks at the secret dock, gauged twice by Tess, who had said nothing either time. Eight belts. Eight altimeters. Bren's ribs, which Imani said were knitting and Bren said did not hurt. The moon, which would be gone tomorrow night. He came to the end and started again at the top; and partway down the second time came to a woman behind a shop counter weighing out salt with a grey flake on a cord at her throat, and stopped, and started again from the flasks.

He did not say any of it. Neither did they.

Some time after three Imani turned her face into his shoulder and kissed him once, not on the mouth: on the place under the corner of his jaw where she put two fingers when she wanted a pulse in a hurry, and left her mouth there. On his other side Ferra's hand pressed once, flat, and stayed.

The window went grey a little before six. Ferra sat up first, found all seven pins on the floor by feel, put her hair up without a glass, took her pen off the table and put it back through the knot.

"Dusk," she said, and went down to the counting room.

Imani lay a while longer with her fingers on his wrist. Then she sat up with her hair coming out of its cloth on one side, and her eyes were dry and very tired.

"You'll eat breakfast," she said. "Then you'll sleep four hours. I'll know if you don't."

"You didn't sleep."

"I'm not stepping off anything tonight." She put her boots on and picked up her bag, and stopped with her hand on the latch, and did not turn round. "Seventy," she said, to the door. "When Ferra sat up. I had it the whole time."

He slept four hours, because she would have known.



The K Discovery came out of the secret dock at half past five on the Wednesday, with the sun gone behind the brickfield, and Ferra was on the slip with her book, and Tess in her chair at the foot of the plank, reading each flask's gauge once more as it went past her.

She had written the line before he came down. He read it upside down over her arm while the forty went up the plank behind him in their black plate: ISLE. 27 OCT, DUSK. And under it, BACK, and nothing after.

"You've left it open," he said.

"When you've a figure, I'll write the figure." She looked past him at the eight suits going up the plank in their frames, black and upright and empty, like men being helped aboard asleep. "Go on."

They flew north at two miles, under the Band, five hours, with the irises shut. The forty sat knee to knee along the empty racks in Bram's plate blacked with soot and oil, no crest anywhere, with Abloh's stone-grey hoods in their laps. Forward of them stood the eight suits with the Isle's rubber new at every neck and wrist. A flask hung under each left arm, the Hod's glass-lined iron filled with plain air, with a hose up into the helm to Tess's demand valve; she had chalked SCUBA SET on the rack at the dock, because it was his name for it, and underlined it, because she did not like it. Each had one of Abloh's chutes down the spine under the furled cape-wing, and on each left cuff the altimeter: a brass case like a large watch, an aneroid capsule inside it that the Forge had cut thin as a leaf, a long needle, and two short pointers Tess had set, white at six hundred feet and red at sixty. A grain of light stone behind each dial, in a shaded cup, let the wearer read it and nobody below him.

Mira had hers off her wrist in the saloon, checking it against the ship's glass for the third time.

"It's not moved," said Osric. "It didn't move the first two times."

"Then it'll not hurt to look."

Osric opened his mouth, and Declan said, "Leave her," without looking up from his own. Osric had run the purge on his demand valve twice before they were off the lake, slowly, where Tess was not, the way she had made him do it in front of her at the Mere; and Declan had watched that too and said nothing.

In the hold Noll Finch was sick into a bucket at the first climb and then sat with his head between his knees, furious. Further down the racks Osric's bandit camp outside Grayrock was being told again. It had had three men in it ten years ago and five on the road south; tonight, Emeric heard through the bulkhead, it had a palisade.

"It never had a palisade," said Declan, to nobody.

"It had the beginnings of one," came Osric's voice. "Stakes. Cut. In a pile."

Imani had Bren in the saloon's corner with his shirt up and her hand flat on his left side, low, where the ribs had cracked against the Mere. She pressed, and moved her hand, and pressed again.

"Doesn't hurt," said Bren.

"It does. Tell me how much."

Bren looked at the bulkhead. "A bit."

"Then a bit is what you've got. Don't spend it on a door you can go round." She let his shirt down. "If anybody falls on you tonight, you let him. You don't catch him on that side to show me you can."

"Yes, mistress."

"I'll not be there to see it," said Imani. "Ward will." Ward, on the step of the sealed stair with his arms folded, inclined his head a quarter of an inch, which was a contract.

Arthur sat in the hold with his back against the hull at the end of the racks, with his hands open on his knees. His note was broad and warm and steadier than it ought to have been; held, the way a man holds a full cup across a room. An hour out of Kaldo Ward went down and sat on the floor beside him with his back against the same iron, and said nothing, and was still there at the coast.



They came down on the sea at half past ten, twenty miles off Kesrath's end of the island, and ran in on her hull with every stone she had dark.

It was flat calm, the sea lying under the stars like oil in a pan. Rowena's four ships lay in a line with no lights, broad-beamed coasters out of Emberfell, two masts each and their sails furled, that had waited here two nights for the moon to go. In the dark they were only places where there were no stars. Fletcher laid her alongside the biggest as softly as a man laying a coat over a chair, and a row of faces looked down over its rail at the long bronze boat with no mast and no oars.

"That's the Kaldo boat," said one of them, not quietly enough. Somebody hit him.

The forty went over first with their hoods up. Imani went after them with three of her medics and a hand barrow: the cold-stone boxes with the pint bottles packed in straw, their letters scratched on the shoulders; Bettany's long flat case with the leveller in it; the masks, and the brown bottles she carried herself. On the coaster's deck she counted the boxes as they came over, and only then looked back at him.

Rowena was at the head of the plank in a sea coat, bareheaded, the long sword at her hip; and the grey knight stood behind her with his arms folded, who had liked none of this at Emberfell and liked it no better at sea.

"Highness," said Rowena.

"Grace."

"Your forty are seasick before they've touched the sea." She watched Noll go past with his face, under the hood, the colour of tallow. "Good. They'll have nothing left to lose over the side in the boats."

Ward went over too, to put the forty into the boats in the order they would go ashore and see every oar muffled himself. One of the coaster's sailors was helping the last of the forty over the rail, a short broad man with a ring in his ear, who treated plate as cargo that happened to walk.

Noll, at the rail beside him, had got his breath back, and was looking out over the stern away from the island, at nothing.

"What's out that way?"

"West?" The sailor looked where he was looking. "Sea. Islands, a long way out, if you keep on. The last of them a month off, they say. Folk on them." He gave the next of the forty a shove up over the rail. "Past the last islands, nothing."

"Nothing at all?"

"Nothing that comes back." He said it as he might have said the price of rope. "Ships go. Every so often a man gets it into his head. In my grandfather's time a whole fleet went out of the south with a prince in the first ship, and not a spar of it came back. So nobody goes. Not west of the last islands. Ask anybody in Emberfell."

Rowena had heard. She was three paces off, watching the last of the forty, and she did not turn her head or say anything to her sailor, who would have stopped talking at once if she had so much as looked at him. She let it stand. Then she looked at Emeric, and her face told him nothing whatever.

"Your boat's in the way of my boats, Highness," she said.



Fletcher took her off three miles on her hull and lifted her there, on the levers, as quietly as smoke goes up off a snuffed candle; and then round the island's end high and wide, and down to a thousand feet a mile off the headland on its seaward side, where the land stood up black against the stars and hid everything behind it.

From here they could not see the tower's lamp; the headland's shoulder lay between, and that was the point. Gravity stones glowed violet when they worked, and on a moonless night a violet light over the sea could be seen from a tower ten miles off. A mile out from the cove's mouth, in the cliff's lee, a man coming down out of the sky came down behind a thousand feet of rock.

Fletcher marked it on Linus's chart. "And now?"

"Now back over the boats," said Emeric. "She wants it from my mouth. On her deck."

They held five hundred feet over the flagship a little after midnight, with the trim keys clicking under Fletcher's hand by themselves, and the line went down through the floor hatch with a shaded lamp clipped near its end. He went down it in his coat, hand under hand. Halfway down he felt the grey knight's note going forward to the waist, and Rowena's, alone on her own quarterdeck, which she had cleared.

Forty feet off the deck, he let go.

He lightened as he fell, and did not think it any more than he thought the bend of his knee. He let the fall have him for a breath and then braked, a man's height off the planks, a lift under the soles like a hand catching a falling cup, with the faint violet flicker at his heels; and his legs took the rest. He came down on both feet on her quarterdeck, heavy and square and quiet, with no more sound than a sack of meal set down by a careful man, and the planks did not creak.

Rowena was standing six feet away with her hands in her pockets.

She looked at his feet. Then at his knees, and up, slowly, the way she had looked at his footwork in her yard with a blade in her hand; and the scar at the side of her face pulled at its corner.

"One year," said Rowena.

"A little under."

"Thirteen months on the chart," said Ward, from the head of the ladder, where he had come up from the waist without either of them hearing him.

Rowena did not turn round. "He wrote to me. Your knight. Two winters ago, one line. One." She took her hands out of her pockets. "I put it on the fire. I thought he'd gone soft in the head, the way old knights do." She looked over her shoulder at Ward at last. "I owe you a letter."

"Your Grace owes me nothing."

"I'll write it." She turned back. "Well. Tell me the rest, now you've shown off."

He told her at the rail, with the grey knight called back up to hear it, because the boats were his to send. The drop at half past one. The swim. The cove, and what had to be quiet in it, in what order. A shuttered lantern from the camp's landing stage, three long and one short, when the cove was held; then the forty in four boats and Imani's medics in the fifth; then the men, down to the landing and out to the ships as fast as oars would turn.

"Two hundred and forty men," said the grey knight. "Five boats. Six turns of each. An hour and a half, if nobody rows into a rock."

"We'll not have an hour and a half once they know," said Ward. "Half an hour. Then whatever's in that tower comes down the path."

"Then you'll want the ships closer." Rowena looked at her captain. "The headland's foot when the lamp shows. Half a mile. Lights out." The grey knight opened his mouth, and she said, "Half a mile," and he shut it. "And I'll come in the last boat."

"No," said Ward.

"Somebody has to be seen." She did not raise her voice. "If those men tell it as black knights out of the sea and nothing else, half of Randillia will be asking by midwinter whose knights. I'll stand on the landing in my own steel with my own lamp, and they'll tell it that Emberfell came for them. You asked me for my name, Highness. You'll have it with my face on it." The scar pulled. "And I've not stood in a fight in twenty years, and I'll not be kept on deck like a figurehead."

Ward looked at Emeric.

"The last boat," said Emeric. "When the men are coming down to the landing. Not before."

"You're very like her," said Rowena, "when you think you're giving an order." She put her hands back in her pockets. "The last boat."

Imani came up the ladder from the waist with her sleeves rolled, looked at him once from his boots up, and said to Rowena, "I'll want the main hatch cover off and the hold aired by three. And every lamp you've got that'll shade."

The bosun's chair came down out of the dark. Ward went up first. When it came down again Emeric put his foot in the strop, and the deck went away below him with the two women on it; and he did not look down after the first ten feet, because he had a thing to do with his head in the next hour and he needed all of it.



At twenty past one they stood on the open deck forward of the house, clipped to the rail, in the dark.

Two thousand feet. The air came cold through every seam the rubber did not close. Below the deck there was nothing. On every other night there had been something, a coast's lamps or a surf line or the grey of sand; tonight the sea was black and flat and the stars lay in it as well as over it, so that the ship hung in a sphere of them with no seam to say which half was water. To the south the headland cut them off in a long rough line.

Eight of them. Ward at the gap in the rail, then Rylan, Declan, Osric, Arthur, Mira, Bren; and himself last, at the house wall, where he could feel all seven notes in front of him in a row, the way he felt the fingers of his own hand.

They did not talk. They had talked on this deck every night for three weeks over the Mere, until there was nothing left that wanted saying aloud. Each man turned his altimeter to his helm: the white at six hundred, the red at sixty, the long needle on its second turn of the dial.

Ward unclipped and stood in the gap. He did not look back; he never had, at the Mere. He put his hand up flat at the height of his helm, the hand that meant on my count and not before, and stepped off into the stars with his arms going out from his sides, and was gone as completely as a stone dropped down a well.

Emeric's sense went with him. A broad slow note, falling.

Rylan, three breaths after. Declan, neatly, as if stepping off a curb. Osric said something inside his helm that might have been a prayer and was more likely about Tess, and went. Arthur went without any pause at all, as he had at the Mere every night for three weeks. Mira stood in the gap a breath longer, with her jaw set, and went. Bren went after her with his right arm held a little across his body, over his left side, and did not know he was doing it.

Then the deck was empty, and he stepped off.



The first part of a fall is quiet. For a breath there was no wind at all, only the stomach coming up and the ship going away overhead, a long black shape blotting out the stars; and then the air found him, and took hold of the suit, and began to roar.

It came up the helm's slots and round the gorget in one long rising howl that he felt in his teeth. He spread himself on it, belly to the black, arms out and knees bent, as they had learned to lie on the air above the Mere so that it held you steady; and the cold came in at the wrists. Below him the seven went down in a long uneven string, seven warm notes falling through nothing, and under them the sea. To the eye it was stars. To his sense it was a great dim field, faintly alive, full of small cold sparks that were fish; and it was coming up.

Ward's note leaned first. Six hundred feet. A moment later Emeric heard it, a long low hiss off to the south and below, no louder than a kettle starting in another room, as the air stones in Ward's calves and back and palms opened and threw their wind down flat beneath him. The air plate, Bram called it: a floor of moving air under a falling man for a count of ten, to take the speed off him a little at a time, so that the stop came on a man falling like a man off a roof and not like a stone. Braking cost more the faster you fell; the air plate paid most of the bill early, while there was room.

Rylan leaned. Declan. Osric, late by a hair and then on it. Arthur, exactly on the white. The hiss came up from below in five places, spread across the dark like breath.

Mira did not lean.

He felt it as a wrongness in the string before he could have said what it was. As the notes leaned one by one the line had gathered itself and slowed, the way a column of men slows at a bridge; and one note went on through the place where the others had slowed, at the speed of a stone. Bren leaned behind it, on his own white. Mira's note went on past Arthur's, past Osric's, down into the black alone.

He had seen it at the Mere, on Bren: a man who trusted his needle a breath too long. There was a small hard knot of attention in her note, turned in; she was looking at her wrist. It did not matter now whether it was the cold or the jar of stepping off or a hair in a pivot. Her needle had stuck, and she was waiting for the white.

She would not see the white.

He stopped lying on the air. He brought his arms in and his head down, so the air could get no hold on him, and went heavy: not the suit, whose stones were built to lift, but himself, the cast he had first made in Ward's yard on one foot to crack a cobble, made now on all of him and held, as if the sea had reached up a hand for him. A falling man ought to be slowing. The roar went up into a shriek, and the seven notes came up toward him and went by, Bren, Arthur, Osric, as if they were rising, which they were not. There was violet at his heels and shoulders, and he did not care.

Mira was below him, and he was gaining.

The fish were not sparks now but small separate cold lives; and he thought, quite clearly and without any fear in it, of his first jump at the stable wall, years ago, the one where he went up and did not know where to stop.

He knew where to stop. He had to get there first.

He let go of the heavy and flung himself wide, and the air hit him like a door, and he came down on her out of the dark. She was flat on the air as she had been taught, steady, straight and much too fast, with her helm bent to her wrist. He came down on her back the way the fourth page of Close Quarters came down on a man on a floor, only from above and moving, and got his left arm under hers and across her breastplate and his right hand into the harness strap between her shoulders, and locked them.

She fought him for half a breath, because anybody would. Then she knew him; the knot in her note let go all at once.

"Plate," he said into the roar, in case the helm carried it. "With me. Now."

She opened her air stones. He opened his.

The air plate took them both under the belly and shoved, and the roar dropped to a howl, and the howl to a wind; and now, close, he could see the stars in the water shiver where their downwash stirred its skin, a faint grey ring spreading on the black. Too fast still. He knew it the way a carter knows a hill is too steep for his brake.

He did not wait for the red. He flared.

All of it. The suit's lift through his band, every gravity node at her shoulders and hips and heels and at his own, hard over; and under it and behind it himself, the cast he had made on Rowena's deck to come down soft, made now on two bodies and a great deal of plate at a speed it had never been meant for. It cost. It went out of him all at once, out of his chest and down his arms into her and the plate; and the stop came up into both of them through the harness like a fall onto a floor.

He felt her body take it. That was the knight's half: the body a year of Ward's yard and the winter river had built round her, filling from inside all at once the way a sail fills, so that a load that would have broken a clerk's back went into her as a blow goes into a wall. His own did the same, and his heart gave one great slow knock against his breastplate like the flat of a hand on a door.

They stopped.

They hung a body's height over the water. Their air stones were still going, and under them the sea had gone white: a ring of spray tearing up off the flat black surface and blowing away from them on every side, rattling on his panes like hail on a roof, salt on his lips inside the helm where none should have reached.

He shut the stones off. They fell the last of it and went in.



The cold was enormous. It came through the plate as it comes through the walls of a house, and found him. The drop-weight belt took him down, slowly, and the black closed over his helm with a soft thud.

He bit the mouthpiece and breathed in. Tess's valve gave him air, cold and dry and tasting faintly of glass, when he pulled for it and not when he did not, with a small click in its throat each time, and his breath went out in a rush of bubbles over the crown of the helm. By the third breath his heart had begun to come down off the wall it was climbing.

Mira was still in his arm. He let go of her harness and kept a hand on her shoulder and felt her breathe, twice, too fast, and then once slowly; and then she put her hand flat on his breastplate and pushed him off, not hard, the way a woman pushes off the side of a pool. She was all right. She was telling him so.

He kicked up off the belt's weight and broke the surface with the spray still coming down round him like the end of a shower, and spat the mouthpiece out and took a breath of the night that hurt all the way down.

Six notes were coming toward them across the water, swimming. He had not had to call.

Ward was first. He came up beside Emeric with his gauntlet already reaching for the harness strap at Emeric's back, and held it, and said nothing for long enough that Emeric understood what it had looked like from above: a violet light going down past the string of them like a star falling, and a white ring on black water where there should have been nothing.

"Her needle stuck," said Emeric.

"I saw." Ward's hand did not leave the strap. "I saw you."

Mira came up on his other side and held her wrist up to her helm. The grain of light behind the glass showed the needle where it had stuck, a little under nine hundred. She looked at it. Then she rapped the glass hard with one knuckle, as a carter raps an axle box to see whether the grease has set, and the needle jumped and swung and fell all the way round to nothing.

"Tess'll want to know," said Osric, from the dark.

"Tess'll want it in her hand," said Mira. Her voice shook, and she heard it shake, and said the next thing anyway in her own voice, flat: "I'm all right."

"You are," said Ward. He let go of the strap. "Now the rest of it."

They pulled their hoods down over their helms, Abloh's thin black wool cut down across the panes to a slit, so that no lantern could catch on glass and wink, and swam in along the headland's dark side in file on the line clipped to every harness, Emeric at its head and Ward at its tail: a slow flat breaststroke under the cliff, as they had swum the Mere's north shore every night under the pole with the lantern on it. He led by sense. Once something long and slow moved off from them without hurrying.

At the point they bit their mouthpieces and went under, and swam the last of it on their tanks past the cove's mouth and the reach of the platform's lamp, and came up in the black under the cliff inside the cove with only their hooded helms above the water.



He counted the camp from there.

He went out for it slowly, holding the hurt in his left palm where he had closed it on the swim line's brass clip, and let his sense go thin up the shingle and over the fence. It came back in the order the read had drawn it, and fuller. The landing stage, with a hut at its root. The rail spur in two lines of cold iron. The gate, a lantern on a post by it, and two warm notes walking the fence, meeting at the gate and turning away. Four platforms with a man and a lamp on each, one over the cove on the cliff's edge. Six long sheds, and in them the men: a commoner's candle each and most of them low, more than two hundred laid in rows, and a great many awake, coughing, a catch in note after note.

More than a hundred awake. In the sheds; on the platforms; in a long low barracks by the gate where twenty-odd men sat round something warm; in the squat dead shape of the block, a few notes very low inside and two at its door. Sixty-odd wardens in all.

And in the warden's house, one heavy note. Not a candle: a banked fire. A mage, awake at two in the morning, sitting still, with his hands moving.

He came back to the hurt in his palm and opened his hand, and laid the count into the overlay for the seven, each note a mark where it lay; and felt them take it one after another, the way a man takes a map without looking up from the road.

"Writing," he said, very low. "Or reading. He's awake."

"As drilled," said Ward.



Rylan went first and alone, because the platform over the cove was the one that could see the water, and while it had its man nobody else could come out of the sea.

He went up the rock on the side away from the lamp, hand over hand, as he had gone up the chalked post at the Mere twenty nights running with Ward at its foot holding a watch: a quick note with a half stop in it, climbing toward the platform man's, slow and bored, with its back to the rock.

The two notes met. The bored one went down into the long slow nothing of a man held until he sleeps, the arm round the neck and the wrist turned at the place drawn on the fourth page with the word Here. Nine breaths. Emeric counted them on his own.

Then the platform's lamp rose and swung once, as a bored man swings it to look at his boots, and was hung back on its hook; and a man stood under it looking along the fence, as bored as the last, in the last one's coat.

The rest came out of the water.

The fence lantern was the second thing. Emeric lay on the wet shingle behind the hut with Declan and Osric on his left and reached for its flame. A lamp that went out with no wind would make a man look, so he did not put it out. He pulled the heat out of it a little at a time, past the glass and into the stones under his palm, as he had once pulled it out of a cone of Maren's ice cream with Imani laughing at him; and the flame went low and blue and sullen, like a lamp with bad oil. The cost came into his palm as a slow ache.

The walkers met under the starved lamp, and one of them cursed the oil and rapped the glass.

Declan and Osric took them on the same breath, as drilled until Ward stopped watching, which was Ward's way of saying it was done: one count, so that neither walker had a breath to see his fellow go. Declan close and neat; Osric rougher about it, and quieter than Emeric had ever known Osric be about anything. He laid his man down on the shingle like a child put to bed, gagged him, tied him, and sat back on his heels for the length of a breath.

Emeric let the flame go. It came back up yellow, and lit the gate, and nobody was walking under it.



The warden's house had a back door into a yard of firewood and a privy, and the door was not barred, because nobody had come in at a back door on the Isle in eighteen years.

Ward opened it. Emeric went past him down a passage that smelled of fish oil and the Isle's pitch, to a door half open on lamplight, and the mage looked up from his table.

A thin man in a warden's coat with a tower ring on his right hand and a book under the lamp, with print in it. He had felt a draft he could not place. His right hand came off the page with the fingers spread, and the ring of a glyph began over his palm, red at its edge.

Emeric crossed the room in the time it took the ring to begin, and his hand was flat on the man's chest.

He pulled. There was no time to put a stone in anybody's fist, so he pulled it into himself, the way a man drinks another's cup in one swallow before the other knows he has let go of it. The mage's mana came out in a rush, a fire mage's note, much more than a hedge man would have carried, and burned behind Emeric's breastbone like a drink taken too fast. Over the mage's palm the ring guttered, half drawn, and went out in a small flat flash and a smell of singed hair.

The man's eyes went wide, and then went somewhere else.

Emeric stopped. He felt the note going down past where it ought to sit, toward the floor Armand had warned him of, where a mage faints and the more he once held the farther he falls; and he stopped a hand's breadth above it, and held the man up by his coat while he sagged, and let his head down onto his book.

Ward put the cuffs on him: two plain iron bands with a mana stone set in each, to drink whatever a man tried to send out of his hands. They closed round the mage's wrists with a sound like a door latching, and Ward tried each clasp with his thumb, a mechanical catch and no stone in it anywhere. Then he looked at the book.

"Poetry," said Ward.

The mage's mana sat in Emeric too hot and not his own, and he let it go down slowly through his boots into the floor before it found its own way out. It took a dozen breaths.

The heavy note in the camp was gone. He laid that into the overlay, and felt Arthur, at the block, take it.



The block's door had two men at it, and a lamp, and a brazier, and they were the only men on the island whose whole work was looking at one door, so they were awake.

Arthur and Mira came at them out of the dark between the sheds, low, together, as they had come at the chalked door at the Mere in the third week, on the night Ward made Arthur go again after a first try nobody could fault. Arthur had the left-hand man before he was off his bench. The right-hand man was quicker; he was half up and turning and his mouth was opening.

Emeric, twenty paces off at the corner of the nearest shed, pulled. A commoner held almost nothing to push back with. He took the guard by the middle and drew him sideways off his feet, the way you pull a stool out from under a man, into the dark beside the block's wall where Bren stood waiting with his arms open.

Bren caught him on the right. Emeric felt him decide it, the man coming and the ache in his left side; and he turned a quarter and took the weight on his right arm and brought the left round after, and had him. Mira came round the wall and finished it. Bren let the man down onto the sand, stood up, put his right hand flat over his left side for one breath, and took it away.

Arthur had the block's keys off the first man's belt, and did not open it. Ward had said not before.

Ward took the camp's own shuttered lantern from its hook on the hut and walked out to the end of the landing stage and opened and shut it seaward: three long, and one short.



The boats came in twenty minutes later, five long shapes out of the black with no sound but the water dripping off their muffled looms; and the forty came up the shingle under Abloh's hoods and went past Emeric at the gate in pairs, each to the shed it had drilled on in chalk.

The forty went down the sheds like water down a drain, and men who had been coughing in the dark for eighteen years woke to a black shape with no face kneeling by them, a hand over their mouths and a Randillian voice saying, very low, Randillia, quiet, up, to the water. Most of them came. Some wept. A few fought, out of habit, and were held until they stopped.

In the barracks twenty-three wardens woke with the forty already inside the door. The chokes took a dozen in the first minute, and Imani's medics came in behind with the masks, leather cups over a pad of wool wetted from the brown bottles. They were slow, minutes and not seconds, so the medics knelt on wardens' chests with a knight holding each man's arms and counted under their breath, as Imani counted, until every note under them went long and flat and stayed there.

Noll Finch took his first man at the barracks' back window. The warden came out of it head first into the dark, running, and Noll was standing under it, because Ward had put him there on the first night at the Mere and nobody had ever told him to move. He took the man round the middle as he landed and went down with him, and lost him, and got him back, and held him; and when the man stopped, Noll did not let go. A medic had to tell him twice.



The round came at ten to four, and nobody had known about it.

The deserter had been a block guard and never stood a platform watch. The read had given Emeric the lamps only as he saw them from the block door, going out and coming back along the fence like men passing a word along a wall, and Emeric had seen the same from two miles up two winters ago and done nothing. Neither of them had known what the word was.

The far platform's lamp went dark and lit again. Then the next. Then the third. Rylan shut his own lantern and opened it, once, as the others had.

The third platform's lamp went dark and lit and went dark again. Twice. A question.

Rylan answered with one, because one was all he had.

And along the fence a man who had stood that watch for years, and knew the answer to the second question the way he knew his name, put a horn to his mouth.



The second half was loud.

The horn went up off the third platform in one long bray, and the second took it up, and a warden who had been sleeping in the last shed's end kicked the door open and came out with a lantern and a crossbow, and saw black armour in his own yard, a great deal of it, lit from below by his own lamp, and shouted.

Every lamp in the camp came up. The yard went from black to yellow in a few breaths, and in the middle of it stood the forty in crestless black plate with the freed men coming out of the sheds behind them in ragged lines, grey all over, grey from the shard fields as a miller is white from his flour.

Twenty-eight wardens came out of the last sheds and down off the platforms, and every one of them frightened, and frightened men with crossbows in a lit yard were worse than brave ones. The forty met them. Steel on steel, men shouting in Duvrenne's tongue and in Randillian, and the freed men shouting too, at the wardens, things they had been saving.

The last shed was Arthur's on the chalk, and Emeric's; he had put himself there at the Mere and nobody had asked him why. Arthur was at its door with his hand on the latch when a warden on the barracks' step, thirty paces off, saw one black shape alone against a lit doorway, and put his crossbow to his shoulder.

Emeric was four paces behind Arthur and to his left. He stepped across, into the line, with his hand out open at arm's length, and made the plate.

The glyph showed for its instant, faint, before his palm. Then the plate was there, a foot from his hand, a surface of his own mana between the bow and Arthur's back; and the bolt came across the yard faster than any eye could follow and struck it.

Rylan's river gravel in Ward's yard in June had hit like a hand on the breastbone. This hit like a hammer. A steel bolt off a bow wound with a windlass: a mundane thing, that had cost the man who wound it a quarter of a minute's work, so that Emeric paid for all of it, every ounce the string had put into it, out of his own chest and down his arm. It went into him through the shoulder and the ribs, and his teeth shut on it. He went back a full step. His arm dropped, and the plate went with it.

The bolt fell in the sand at Arthur's heels with its point turned over like a nail hit wrong.

Declan had the bowman off the barracks' step before he could wind again.

Arthur did not turn round. He looked down at the bolt by his heel for the length of a breath. Then he lifted the latch and went into the last shed.

Emeric stood with his right arm hanging and breathed through his teeth until it was his again. It took longer than it had in June.



It went fast after that, because it had to.

The wardens broke inside a quarter of an hour, as frightened men do, and were bound in rows by the barracks wall with their own cord. Three were hurt and would mend. One would not: a man who had come at Osric with a spear and not stopped when Osric told him to. The senior warden came up out of a cellar in his nightshirt with his hands on his head, and the camp clerk behind him holding the roll book against his chest like a child; Ward took the book from him, turned three pages under a lantern, shut it, and gave the two of them to Rylan for the boats.

A handful had got out by the gate on the tower side. Eight of them, running hard up the path toward the headland with their lanterns thrown away. Osric started after them, and Ward said, "No."

Osric stopped.

"Let them go." Ward was watching them, eight small notes going up a hill in the dark. "I want Kesrath to hear it from its own."

The block had eleven men in it, chained by the ankle to a rail in a stone room with no lamp: men who had tried to leave, or struck a warden, and been put in the dark to think about it. Emeric opened it with the guard's keys and softened the chain off the rail with heat. They would not come. They sat with their arms over their heads, and when he went toward them they pushed back into the stone. A black thing with no face at the door of the dark, saying Randillia, was exactly what a warden would send to see who would go to the door when it opened.

He pushed back the hood and took off his helm, and lit a flame on his finger, a candle's worth, beside his face, so that it lit nothing in the room but him. Fire was his public magic. These men had seen mages. They had not seen this: a face, close, and in it his father's eyes, gold, the crown's, which every levy man had been told about by his sergeant.

Nobody in the block said anything for a long time.

Then the man nearest the door, a long grey wreck with a beard to his chest, put his arms down from over his head and said, in the voice of a man who had not used it for a while, "Highness."

"Up," said Emeric. "To the water. Quietly."

They came. He put the helm back on and pulled the hood down over it at the door, before the yard's lamps reached him. Eleven men. Whatever they told afterward, in whatever port, nobody would believe it: a prince of Randillia in black plate on Kesrath's end of the Isle, out of the sea in the dark. A prince came for us. He could hear it already in a tavern in the capital, with Corbin's man at the next table, and nobody able to prove a word.



Arthur's note had not come out of the last shed.

Emeric sent the eleven down to the gate with two of the forty and went to the shed door, and stopped in it.

A lantern stood on the floor where somebody had dropped it, lighting the foot of the wall and the end of a pallet and nothing higher. On the pallet a man sat with his back to the wall and his knees drawn up and his hands, grey to the wrist and split across every knuckle, hanging between them. A big frame with nothing left on it. A cough he did not seem to know he was making.

Arthur knelt in front of him in his black plate and hood, and had said his three words, Emeric understood, more than once.

"Randillia," said Arthur again, low, exactly as drilled. "Quiet. Up."

The man did not move. "Say that again."

Arthur was still for a breath. "Up."

The man lifted his head. He was looking at the hood, at the slit where a face should have been, and his own face did a thing Emeric had seen faces do in Imani's ward when a fever broke: like a man who has held a weight up a long time and feels somebody take one corner of it.

"That's hers," he said. "That's how she says it. Up. Every morning. Up, the three of you." He was breathing very fast. "There was a baby. Three, he was. He'd be..." He stopped, and said the next thing the way a man puts his foot on ice he has been told is thin. "Arthur, is that you?"

Arthur did not move at all. Then his hands went up to the hood, and pulled it back, and to the latch of his helm, and fumbled it, which he had not done once in three weeks at the Mere; and got it off, and knelt there bareheaded in the lantern light with his face already wet.

"Edric?"

Emeric stood in the door and did not go in.

He had told Arthur not to hope, in September, as plainly as he knew how; and Arthur had been flawless the whole night through because of it, through the drop and the block door and a bolt at his back he had not turned round for. Now he put his arms round a grey man who weighed less than a sack of meal and held on, and made a sound into his brother's shoulder that Emeric did not watch. He looked at the grey dust in the grain of the floorboards, which was in everything in this place.

Ward came and stood in the door at his shoulder. He watched the two by the wall without moving, as he had watched the yard the morning Arthur took his hands off the rail. Down at the landing the oars were knocking now, careless, a boat going out loaded.

"Arthur," said Ward, at last. Not loudly.

Arthur lifted his head.

"The boats," said Ward.

Arthur got up. He wiped his face with the back of a gauntlet, which did nothing at all, and put his helm back on, and got his brother up off the pallet by the arms, as a man lifts a thing he is afraid will come apart in his hands. Edric's right leg would not take him, and he swore at it in a lower-ward voice that eighteen years had not changed. Arthur got a shoulder under that arm.

At the door Edric looked at the black plate and the hood with nothing in it.

"Who's that?"

"Kaldo," said Arthur. "Come on."

And Edric came, because Arthur was going.



Rowena was on the landing in her bright steel with the blue cloak off her left shoulder and an unshaded lantern held up, the only lamp at the water nobody had covered, saying two words over and over in the voice that had carried across her arena: Emberfell. Boats. The men came down the shingle and looked at her, and some stopped dead on the stage until the man behind walked into them; and they went down into the boats saying her duchy's name to each other like a password.

The grey knight had said an hour and a half. It took fifty minutes, because the men at the oars rowed as if Kesrath were behind them, which it was. It cost. One of Rowena's sailors took a bolt through the forearm from the second platform before Rylan got along the fence to it. Another's hand went between the stage and a gunwale as the swell came in, and would not be the same hand again. And one of the forty, a farm boy off the Milford flats, walked down to the boats on a warden's spear through the outside of his thigh without a word, because there were men who could not walk, until Noll saw the blood in his boot.

The eight went out in the last boat with Rowena and Edric, as the forty had, black shapes among the oars. On the headland path the eight wardens had stopped running. Emeric could feel them up there, high on the slope where it turned toward the tower, looking back.

"Let them look," said Ward.



The flagship's hold was full of grey men on straw and sailcloth under shaded lamps, and the smell of the camp came up out of the open hatch as if they had brought it aboard. On deck by the coaming Imani had made a ward of four lanterns and a hatch board on two casks, and on the board a man.

He was one of the block's eleven. A warden had put a spear into his side at the gate in the loud half, low, under the ribs, and he had walked to the boats on it. His lips were grey. His note was going down like a lamp running out of oil.

The white dish was on the cask beside him with a drop of his blood on it, and a row of drops beside it off the shoulders of her pint bottles, and Imani was rocking it under the lamp with her thumb.

"Nobody's ever typed these men," she said, to the dish. "Eighteen years. Nobody ever bled them for anything but work." Two of the drops went to grit while she watched; two stayed smooth. "O." She hung a bottle with an O scratched on its shoulder from the yard above the board, ran the tube down, put the needle into his arm herself with a sailor holding the lamp close, and the blood went down into him dark and slow.

Rowena came down from the quarterdeck and stood at the board's foot, and watched.

It was not enough. Emeric could feel that it was not, and so could Imani; she pressed her hand flat on the man's side and took it away and looked at it. He had a hole in him that the bottles were pouring into and out of. She hung the second and the third. Both typed sailors had given already, arm to arm, one into this man and one into Rowena's sailor with the crushed hand, and were sitting against the mainmast with their sleeves rolled down, being made to drink.

"I'll want a donor," said Imani, without looking up.

"Me," said Mira. She had come up behind Rowena with her helm under her arm.

"You'll do. I know your letter." Imani did not stop pressing. "And you're dense. You've a summer of the Hod in you and the river and the stones, and he's eighteen years of the shard fields and nothing else. Straight in, you'd kill him faster than the spear." She lifted her chin at the long flat case. "The leveller."

Her medic set it on the cask: the finger of coiled glass in its brass jacket with three spent works stones packed round it, the two dials on a board no bigger than a book, the cuff for the man's arm and the brass collar for the line. The needle went into Mira's arm; Mira looked at the sea. Her blood came down the tube into the coil, and the line's needle stood far over to the right, high, a knight's blood with all a knight's mana in it, and the medic's thumb went to the valve.

The needle came down. Slowly, a hair at a time under the thumb, across the dial toward the cuff's needle, which stood far over to the left, where a man stands who has been worked to the bone on a desert for half his life and has nearly nothing left in him of anything. It came to rest beside it.

"There," said Imani. "Now."

Rowena watched the needle all the way down. Then she looked at Mira against the casks with her blood going into a stranger through a coil of glass, and went to the rail and stood with her back to all of it for a while, looking at the island.

The man on the board lived. Emeric felt him come back up past the place where they stop: a candle gone down to almost nothing, that steadied, and stayed.

Another man died in the hold before the ships had cleared the headland, out of reach of anything Imani had.

Nobody had touched him. He was an old man from the first shed who had climbed the ship's side on his own hands and lain down on the straw; and after the four ships had their sails up and were standing out to sea, his cough became the long dragging sound of lungs that had breathed shard dust for eighteen years and had had enough. Imani was with him. She came up afterward and stood at the hatch coaming with her hands hanging at her sides, and Emeric stood beside her.

"He was free an hour," said Imani.

"An hour and a quarter."

"Don't." She was quiet. "His name's in the clerk's book. I want it."



The K Discovery came down out of the dark at a quarter to six, with the first grey just beginning low in the east and the headland a mile astern, and held at a thousand feet over the flagship, a long black shape against the last of the stars.

Rowena had the hatch cover put back on first. She had it done without a word, by her own sailors, with the men in the hold told it was against the morning cold; so that whatever went up off her deck went up in front of her people and nobody else's.

They went up in their suits one at a time, with the violet at their heels, Ward first. Mira went up in the bosun's chair on Imani's order, furious about it, with a pint gone out of her arm. Arthur went last but one, because he had asked; and he did not go until his brother had seen him go.

Edric sat on the hatch board with a blanket round him and watched Arthur rise off the deck with a faint light at his feet, straight up, as a lantern goes up a rope, until he was nothing against the grey. Emeric knelt in front of him.

"I'll take you up," he said. "Where he's gone. Held, the way he went. It'll be cold and a long way down under you, and you won't like it."

Edric looked at the hood.

He was starved and frightened, with a leg going bad and no notion what the black things out of the sea were or why his brother had gone up into the sky; and a frightened man at a thousand feet who decided to fight would be fighting a man in plate with nothing under either of them. Emeric could put him out first. Imani's mask was twenty feet away, and slow, and a starved man with lungs like these might go further under it than she could bring him back from; she would not give it to him, and she would be right. He could drain him to sleep, down below a commoner's floor until he dropped the way a mage drops; nobody had ever done that to put a man to sleep, and nobody knew where a starved man's floor was, and it would be Emeric finding out, on a deck, by trying. Or he could hit him. In every story a blow to the neck put a man to sleep for an hour and he woke with a headache. Outside stories it put him in the ground.

And the man had not said yes to any of it.

"Arthur's waiting," he said. "Hold on round my neck. Shut your eyes if you want. Say his name if you want. You can't fall. I'll not let you."

Edric looked at him a long time.

"Who are you?"

"Later."

Edric put his arms round his neck.

He rose off the deck with the man held fast against his breastplate, light, hardly anything, and the deck went away below: Rowena at the rail with her lamp out and her face turned up; Imani at the coaming with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and blood dried on them, looking up and not moving at all. Edric's arms locked round his neck so hard the gorget bit. He was saying something into the plate, Arthur's name, over and over, with his eyes shut.

The cold came at five hundred feet. In the east the grey was spreading low over the island's long dark back; under them the four coasters stood out to sea in a line with their sails grey and their wakes white, smaller, smaller, and the island beyond them with its one yellow lamp on the headland; and on the headland path, very small, eight men standing still with their faces turned up.

Ward's hand came down out of the floor hatch, and Arthur's beside it.

The bosun's chair went down once more after that, empty, and came back up with Imani in it and her bag on her knees and the clerk's page with one name copied on it folded inside her cuff. Her medics stayed with the men. She had told them what to do until Emberfell, twice, and then told them a third time, and come up the line without looking down.



Ferra was on the slip at noon, where she had been, he learned later, since six; and Tess beside her in her chair, who had been there since five and did not say so, and who looked at nothing on the plank but Osric until he was off it.

She watched them come down the plank in their shirts; and Arthur, with his brother's arm over his shoulder, a grey man in a blanket who stopped at the plank's foot and looked at the lake and the wall and the town going up the hill behind it in brick and glass, and did not say anything at all.

Then she looked at Emeric, all over, quickly, the way she looked down a column for the figure that was wrong; and opened her book, and wrote under BACK the date and the hour, and a figure. Two hundred and forty.

Imani came down the plank behind him and took the folded page out of her cuff, and put her finger on the name.

Ferra wrote a one under the figure, and the name beside it, in the same capitals as the rest.
