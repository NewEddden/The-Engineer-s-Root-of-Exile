Chapter 16 - A Working Drawing

Ferra made the tea and put it down with the air of a woman who had heard a great deal about this guest and had reserved judgment.

Emeric knew her from the ash yard and did not know her name, and had to be told it, at her father's table, four months after she had first handed him a figure.

He apologized for that. She said it was of no consequence in a tone that indicated it was of some.

She kept the forge's books. She corrected his arithmetic inside ten minutes, out loud, on a running total he had done in his head and been pleased with, and she was right and he was out by eleven.

He said so, because he was delighted by it.

She took the compliment the way a person takes a remark about the weather and went back to what she was doing, and he was left standing in a kitchen having enjoyed that exchange considerably more than she had.

He noticed it, thought nothing of it, and moved on.

The drink was not business, which was the first thing about the evening that surprised him.

Bram talked about the work, because a man will talk about his craft to anybody who actually wants to hear about it, and this one did. Heat colors and what they lie about. Why a weld takes on one day and not the next. What he had ruined at nineteen and why he had ruined it.

And Emeric was happy in a way he had not been since the fire.

Here was a thing he knew. Here was a man he could hand it to. And there was no method available to him, in this life or any other, for ever saying where he had got it.

He had an entire world in his head that he could not attribute to anyone, and the only form of ownership left open to him was giving it away.

He never said one word of that and it sat under the whole evening.

The claim came out around the second cup.

You can take iron past soft and past workable, all the way to liquid, and pour it into a mould like water.

Bram did not believe him.

Not rudely, and not the way a man humors a noble. Thirty years of working metal and he had never once seen it, and he said so, and the disbelief was the most respectful thing in the conversation, because it was being treated as a factual dispute between two men who might each be wrong.

Emeric asked for paper and ink to prove it, and both of them were terrible.

The ink was thin and grey. The paper drank it. A clean line feathered into a smear in about two seconds and a hatched section went to mud.

He worked around it badly, pressing lighter and drawing slower, and made a note somewhere in the back of his head that went onto a list that was getting long.

The drawing took most of an hour.

Charge from the top. Air in low. A tall stack, so the burn had depth to work through instead of skating over a shallow bed. Tapping at the bottom, and the whole thing running continuous rather than one batch at a time, which was the part Bram stopped him on twice to make sure he had understood.

Then the magic, seated on a mundane floor, because that was a rule Emeric had made for himself on the road and had not once broken since.

A Fire Crystal for even heat. An Air Crystal on the blast. Both out of the chest his father had handed him at the exile, both mounted so that they could be pulled and the thing would still run on charcoal and a hand bellows if every stone in it went dead in the same hour.

Slower than anything Kaldo had. Far hotter. And it does not stop when the magic does.

Bram looked at it for a long time.

"It's good."

"But."

"It's a shadow." He turned the sheet round. "You've got the whole of it somewhere and this isn't it. I can see the edges of a thing on this page that isn't on this page. I'd like to see it the way you're seeing it and I can't."

Then he put his finger on the figures along the side.

"And these are nothing."

Emeric looked.

Fourteen inches. Three feet. Eleven and a half. Every dimension on the sheet written in units that did not exist anywhere in the kingdom of Randillia and never had.

There was no inch. There was no foot. There were thumbs and paces and a crown standard that three different trades interpreted three different ways and a mason's rod that varied by the mason.

Which meant that no smith, no carpenter and no mason in this country could build one thing on that sheet from that sheet, and that every drawing he had made since he arrived had been a private document he had mistaken for an instruction.

He sat in a kitchen and worked out that his entire technical inheritance was written in a language this world did not have, and that translating it was a larger project than any single machine inside it.

He was still working it out when he dropped.

He had gone into focus trying to decide what to explain first and had gone straight through, which was the fourth time that week and the first time in company.

Bram said his name twice and got nothing.

He found a man sitting at his own table with his eyes open and nobody at all behind them, and he did not panic, because panicking has never once in his life been the first thing he reached for.

He reached over to take the shoulder.

The shoulder was, all at once, less solid than a shoulder ought to be.

And then he was somewhere else with no transition in it whatsoever.

It was an office, or near enough.

Bram's first instinct was the window, and the view mostly held, and mostly was the word for all of it. Details soft at the edges the way a memory is soft and a real room is not. Sharp where they were needed. Vague everywhere else. Lines of text and small shapes floating at the corner of his sight that meant nothing to him and evidently everything to whatever part of Emeric had built the place.

"You're not supposed to be here."

"I'm not supposed to be anywhere but my own kitchen. Where's here, exactly?"

Emeric did not have a clean answer and gave what he had without it sounding like madness, which took some doing.

The delving space. The Hollow Crystal. And the fact that he had wanted Bram to see the furnace badly enough that the wanting had done the work the stone usually did.

He watched the man's face go completely blank while he decided whether to be afraid.

He watched him land on stubborn curiosity instead, which took about four seconds and which Emeric thought about for months afterward.

"You said you can build things in here. Things that don't exist yet."

"I think so. I've never tried."

"Try."

It came together slower than he expected.

It resolved out of nothing the way a sketch fills in under a pencil, except that the pencil was attention and the paper was whatever the place was under the office it was pretending to be.

And the problem from the kitchen simply evaporated.

There were no units in here. There was no scale. There was the shape, at full size, and a blacksmith walking round it twice with his hand going through a wall that was not there, and no doubt in him anywhere at all.

He found it inside ninety seconds.

"The air line. This part. You've got it coming in at a straight angle, fast as you can push it."

"That's the design. More air through the melt, faster the impurities burn off."

"Won't work like that. Not with our iron."

Kaldo ore carried more sulfur than whatever Emeric had learned on. Blast it straight and fast and the melt cooked uneven, pockets that never purified sitting an arm's length from spots hot enough to ruin the temper, and Bram had scrapped good iron for less than what was drawn on that sheet.

Emeric looked at it properly, which he should have done the first time instead of trusting a solution that felt clean because it felt familiar.

Bram was right, and he could see exactly why the second it was said, the way a valve sized for one system fails the moment you drop it into another running at a slightly different pressure.

His information had not been wrong. It had been built for iron that was not this iron.

Angle it lower. Slower push. Wider spread across the surface instead of straight down the middle. Takes longer. Comes out even.

Bram reached for the shape as though it were something his hands could work, and in there it turned out that they could, and that was the moment the thing stopped being one man teaching and became two men building.

In the kitchen, one minute had passed.

Ferra came in and found two grown men at her table with their eyes open and nobody home in either of them, one of them her father and the other a prince of Randillia.

She checked their breathing. She checked both their faces for the droop she had seen twice in this town and knew the look of. She said both their names, got nothing, and was halfway to the door for Imani when they came back at once, mid-sentence, arguing about air angles.

The aftermath was funny for about ninety seconds and then it was not.

She was furious and entirely right to be. Bram was too full of what he had just seen to defend himself properly and kept starting sentences about furnaces. Emeric apologized and meant it and could not stop grinning, which was the wrong face to be wearing and he knew it while he was wearing it.

Then she asked how long it had lasted from the inside.

"An hour. More."

"It was under a minute. I counted."

Both of them went quiet.

She set the terms, and Emeric did not get to negotiate any of them.

Nobody does this alone in a room. Somebody who knows what it is stays awake in the house. Not in the middle of the night. And if either of them is gone longer than she can count comfortably she is going for the healer and neither of them gets a vote on it.

Then she started a book. When they went under, when they came up, in her own hand, on paper, because she did not trust either of them to report the duration honestly and had grounds for it within the hour.

Both men agreed. Because she was obviously correct, and because she was not asking.

She was the third person in Kaldo who knew, and the only one with rules.

Bram never told anybody.

Not out of loyalty, and no oath was offered or asked for. He does not talk about a thing until he has the why of it in his own hands, and he never fully got the why of this one.

He sat with the fire that night and decided not to tell his own daughter, and then she walked in on it four hours later and the decision was taken off him.

Walking back up the hill in the dark, Emeric worked through what had actually happened, and it was not the furnace.

He had brought a man in with him. On wanting alone. With no technique, no preparation and no idea how, and he could not currently repeat it.

And a blacksmith who had never left this barony had put his hand on a design out of another world and improved it inside ninety seconds.

He wrote both down, and under the second one he added a line he would spend the rest of his life proving out.

The knowledge is not the scarce part.

The furnace went on the shelf that same week. No timber. No lime to spare. No ore contract and no ore. A barony spending every copper it had on wells and a burial ground it had not yet been given permission to move.

Bram kept the sheet.

Emeric told him it was his, which was not generosity. It was the only arrangement under which the design survived anything happening to Emeric.
