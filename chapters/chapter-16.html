Chapter 16 - A Barony Without a Baron

Nobody from the palace sent for him.

They stayed two more nights in the capital while Keller's clerks wrote out the ruling in the forms the crown required, and in those two nights no page came to the inn, no letter, no summons up the hill. Emeric hadn't expected one. He noticed anyway.

On the second evening, crossing the inn yard, he heard a groom say that the third prince's brother had been asking at the judiciary which day the hearing fell on. Asking on the first day. So Waylon had known for days that he was in the city, and where, and had not come.

Emeric went up to his room and sat on the bed, and then went down again and ate his supper.



They rode home in the morning with Petram in irons in a hired carriage, and Ferra in the carriage too, because six days on a horse had been enough to last her, she said, until she died.

On the first afternoon she took out her book and opened it on her knee and turned it round so he could see.

"The other column," she said.

He looked. It was the same years, the same ledgers, the same skimming. But she'd followed the money out instead of in. Seed corn, in the plague springs, when nobody had any and the fields would otherwise have gone unsown. Grain bought in two bad winters and given out at the keep's gate. A roof on the almshouse. All of it booked as something else, repairs, contracts, garrison costs, so that it went through the books looking like theft.

"A little over half," Ferra said. "Of what he took. It went back into Kaldo."

Emeric read down the column to the bottom, and then read it again.

"You didn't show Keller."

"Keller asked where the money came from." She closed the book. "He didn't ask where it went."

The carriage jolted over a rut. Emeric thought about a man in a clean coat at a door, saying he'd buried a third of his town.

"He told the truth in that doorway," he said.

"About the money."

He let that stand, because she was right.

Somewhere past the first waystation he raised the petitions. Imani's three letters, entered and never sent, laid on Keller's table without a word to him beforehand.

"Next time," he said, "tell me. Even if it's afterward."

"When there's an afterward that doesn't cost anything, I will."

"That isn't a yes."

"No," Ferra said, and went back to her book.

He'd lost that three ways, and knew it, and rode the rest of the afternoon in a better mood than he'd had in a month.

There was one thing he hadn't said to her, because he'd only just understood it. In July, when Doran's rows move began, Emeric had set the diggers' wage at half again what the docks paid, and paid it through Doran on the parish step so nobody could say the prince was buying the graves. Wexler had told him at the time, in the side room, patiently, that a wage like that would set the day rate for every pair of hands in Kaldo, and that there was nothing in the barony to keep paying it once the rows were done. Emeric had thought he was being mean. Riding south, he could see Wexler had been right.

At Wayford, on the third day, Ferra went back up to the long room above the stables and came down two hours later with one of the two missing years of toll returns, which a clerk had found in the cellar after all.

"The case is finished," Emeric said.

"The case is finished. The books aren't."

He didn't go to see Wexler's son. He thought about it, standing in the inn yard with the letter he'd sent already somewhere in the town, and decided that a man shouldn't have to meet the prince who'd hanged his father before he'd even read the letter about it.

Ward rode up beside the carriage window as they left Wayford.

"Who has Kaldo now?" he said.

Emeric considered it.

"Apparently I do."

"It'll be noted," Ferra said, without looking up.

Only Emeric laughed. Ward looked at him as though he'd sneezed.

On the fourth day, in the forest, Ward rode on the treeline side the whole way without being asked.

On the fifth, Petram spoke for the first time since the capital.

"A rope I'm not getting," he said, to the window, "isn't a thing I've been given." Then he turned his head. "Can Corliss come? To see me. When we're back."

"Yes," Emeric said, before he'd decided whether saying yes was decent or useful. He turned that over for some miles and got nowhere.



There was a new man on the South Gate. Older, grey under his helmet, and he did it correctly: stepped out, asked their business, looked at Emeric's eyes, bowed, and stood aside. Emeric thanked him. He wanted very badly to ask his name, and did.

The town didn't riot and it didn't cheer.

The shop was open, with two customers inside. The north well's fire was burning, and the woman tending it didn't look up as they passed. Beyond the south-east wall, when the road came round, he could see the new burial ground in the hollow, three weeks of fresh earth in neat rows and stakes set out further down for the rows still to come. Doran's work, done his way, one kitchen at a time.

People stopped in doorways to watch them go by. Not hostile. Not anything. They had the faces of people waiting to learn what they lived under now.

Bram was at the forge when Emeric went down that evening. He listened to the whole of it, the hearing, the ruling, the passing of the land, without stopping work.

"Wexler was a bastard," Bram said, "and he was theirs for thirty years." He turned the bar on the anvil. "You've been here four months, and you've had one man killed."

Emeric opened his mouth to say that Keller had done it, that it was the crown's judgment and not his.

He shut it again.



Hale had a bag packed. It stood by the door of the steward's room when Emeric came in, a leather bag with a strap, and Hale stood beside it with his hands folded, waiting to be told.

Emeric looked at the bag. Then he asked Hale what he thought should be done about the drawbridge chain.

Hale didn't answer for about four seconds. Emeric watched him work out that he was being asked, which in eleven years as steward, it seemed, nobody had done.

"Iron," Hale said finally. "Not rope. It was always meant to be iron."

"Then iron."

Hale looked at the bag. Then he picked it up and put it back in the corner where it had come from.

He was the only man alive who knew which of Wexler's arrangements had been real. Emeric would have kept him for that alone.

Then everything landed at once.

A mill lease coming due. A boundary feud between two farms on the west side that had been going on since before the plague and had lately involved a fence, a dog and a broken arm. The tithe schedule, which the parish wanted settled before the harvest. The garrison. A widow who owed the barony four Silver Rand in back rent and had nothing to pay it with but a barn. And a woman whose roof had fallen in, whose landlord said the roof was the parish's because the house had once been church land, and whose parish said it was the landlord's because it wasn't any more.

The roof took him ninety minutes in the old hall with all three of them, and ended in an arrangement he knew was bad while he was making it. He gave it a year.

Three days after they came home, a single dispatch arrived from the capital. One line.

Sentence carried out.

Emeric read it, and folded it, and gave it to Hale to file, and went for a walk round the top of the wall in the rain.



Hale brought him the barony's figures the next morning.

The coin in the treasury, all of it, every chest Hale had a key for, came to a figure in Silver. The pouch in Emeric's own room was worth more than twice as much. The rest of what Kaldo was worth was grain in the barns, the mill on the river and the land itself, and none of those could be spent.

"And the crown's share?"

"Scales with what the barony produces, Your Highness." Hale didn't smile. "So, very little."

Then the guards. His four men had been paid out of his own purse since April, eight Copper a day between them. Kaldo was his now. He could put them on the barony's pay roll, where they arguably belonged, and save the purse.

He thought about it. On the barony's roll, every copper they drew would be in the books, and in Hale's hands, and in front of whoever came after Hale. If anything went wrong with them, it would be bad government. On his own purse it was only his own bad luck. And the garrison wasn't sorted yet, and wouldn't be for months, and he did not want his own four men on the same list as forty-two others he couldn't vouch for.

"I'll keep paying them," he said.

Hale wrote that down. Ferra, when she heard, worked out how many months that made and showed him the figure.



Hale knew the neighbours, and Emeric spent an evening making him talk about them.

Milford, a day west along the river's other branch: Baron Paxton, solvent, cattle and beef and wool and leather. He'd gone on selling Kaldo beef all through the plague and never once asked why the orders kept getting smaller.

Grayrock, upriver on the big river, about a day by water: ore, cut stone, and veins of crystal in the hills. The current brought Grayrock's ore down to Kaldo cheap by barge. Nothing went back up. Nothing Kaldo had was worth the haul against the current.

Wayford, at the crossroads on the capital road: inns, stables, tolls, a town that lived on other people passing through.

Wexler had let every one of those ties lapse except the ore contract.

That night Emeric took Armand's notebook and drew the region from memory, the way he'd drawn the town for Doran. The river coming down from the mountains past Wayford. The fork to Milford. The branch past Grayrock with its hills. And Kaldo at the bottom of all of it, where the river ran on to the sea, sitting on the only way out. Trade from two directions had been floating past Kaldo's dead port since the war, and the dock quarter across the east bridge was the last stop before the sea, with its door shut.

He looked at the drawing a long time. The port was the biggest fact in the barony, and it would take years, and ships, and Fletcher.

He set it aside. The farm ate first.

He rode out to the west farms with Rylan two days later.

They were exactly what he'd feared. Two fields, one sown and one left bare, swapping every year, and the bare one given nothing to eat in between. Yields the farmers took the way they took the weather, as something that happened to them. And above it all, thirty feet up on its arches, striding across the fields toward the town, the aqueduct: the Mere's water running over the heads of farmers who watered their crops by bucket from a ditch, and spilling out into the sand where the span had fallen.

It took him about twenty minutes to see what to do. It was all in a book he'd read for a college elective in another life. He didn't say any of it.

"Who do the others watch?" he asked Rylan, on the way back. "When something new turns up. Who do they wait to see try it first?"

Rylan thought about it, and gave him three names, and Emeric wrote them down.

The mines, two days out into the hills, he saw the next week. They were shallow, worked by men with hammers and wedges, and abandoned wherever the seam turned down and the hammers couldn't follow it. He asked the foreman about blasting, carefully, and the foreman didn't know the word. Nobody in Randillia, it turned out, had anything that burned fast enough to break rock. No powder anywhere.

He knew exactly what that meant, in both directions. He didn't write it down.

Iron, in the end, came from two places only. Grayrock, upriver, which held the ore. And the sea, which could bring it from anywhere, if Kaldo had a port.

Closing the notebook, he saw that he kept coming back to the same answer from every direction. Take Grayrock. Open the sea. Make the river run both ways. He didn't like how easily it came, and shut the book on it, and went to bed.



The garrison was fifty-eight men on the roll.

Sixteen of them came off at once, the names that belonged to nobody. That left forty-two names. One of them was Tam's, still drawing a wage in the book because nobody had thought to strike it. Emeric struck it himself, with Hale's pen, and then sat looking at the line.

Forty-one. Nobody could tell him which of the forty-one were real soldiers and which were men who drew a wage and stood a door and went home, and which, if any, answered to somebody who wasn't in the keep.

He went down the list with Ward in the evening. Neither of them said that one of those forty-one might have walked out to the mill road on a summer evening and come back alone.



The building across the river was Emeric's own idea, and nobody liked it.

It stood on the far bank, across the east bridge and downstream of the dock quarter, where an old works had been put up in better days and abandoned when the port died. Brick over stone and heavy timber, two storeys, with a wheel on the river that still turned when the current pushed it. Inside, the floor had been laid out for three furnaces, three bays with the footings already in.

Bram stood in the middle of it with his arms folded.

"I've been the only smith in this barony twenty years," he said. "I've never once needed two of anything."

Ferra had her book open. "Fitting this out costs about half a season of soap," she said. "Before it makes a single nail."

Emeric didn't argue need. He couldn't, yet. He argued cost. A building framed for three furnaces, with the footings laid and a wheel already on the water, would cost a fraction to fit out now of what it would cost to build later, when they needed it and the price of everything had gone up.

Ferra worked it through on the page while he talked. When he'd finished she wrote something under her figures and turned the book so he could see.

Arithmetic: agreed. Judgment: not.

Bram had wandered off while they argued. Emeric found him in the third bay, standing among the pegs somebody had driven years ago to mark where the hearth would go, looking down at them.

"I suppose," Bram said, to the pegs, "I had better find somebody to teach."

Imani had come across the bridge that afternoon to see a child in the dock quarter, and had stopped at the open door on her way back. She'd stood there through the whole argument without a word. Now she came in, looked round at the bays, the furnace footings, the wheel turning outside in the brown water.

"Where does it go?" she said.

Emeric looked at her.

"The waste. Whatever comes out of those." She nodded at the bays. "Where does it go?"

He hadn't asked. The site was downstream of the dock quarter and below every well in Kaldo, and that was luck. He had stood in this building for an hour thinking about furnaces and never once asked where the water from the quench went, or the slag, or the ash.

"I didn't ask," he said. "It's below everyone's water. By luck."

Imani looked at him a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, as if that were at least an honest answer.

Ferra wrote it down. By the end of the week it was a rule, written at the front of her book where the rules lived: nothing in Kaldo gets sited until somebody has asked where it goes.

The fire crystal and the air crystal from his father's chest would give the furnaces their heat and their blast. The wheel would work the bellows and drive a hammer. And if every stone in the kingdom went dead tomorrow, the whole thing would still run on charcoal and the wheel and men at the bellows. Slower. But it would run, and it would be Kaldo's.

"I would rather Kaldo own something slow," he said, "than rent something fast."

Bram grunted. It might have been agreement.



He gave Ferra the census the next day.

Every name in every record she could find, the parish book, the tithe lists, the garrison roll, the toll books, her father's ledgers. And next to each name, what they could do. Carpenters. Diggers. Masons. Carters. Anyone who kept bees. Anyone who'd ever built a boat. People working below their trade because their trade had died. And anyone, anywhere in the barony, who could cut a rune.

Ferra objected. It was months of work, she said, and half the records were wrong, and the other half didn't exist.

She said yes before he'd finished explaining why.



He didn't go into the soap shop.

From the capital, with the letter to Wexler's son, he'd sent money down to Imani by the same rider, for Sela, with no note. Imani had told him, in the doorway of the foundry, without looking at him, that Sela had taken it.

He didn't know what that meant. He didn't go in to find out.

That night he opened Armand's notebook to the back and wrote the list. The farms. The foundry. The garrison. The port. Petram, and Corliss's visits. Armand, coming south. The graves, half moved. The roof. The woman at the north well.

At the bottom, after a long time, he wrote three more lines.

Wexler paid for the road.

Wexler did not order Tam.

Those are not the same hand.

He held the pen over the next line for a while.

Then he put it down, and closed the book, and didn't write it.
