Chapter 16 - A Working Drawing

Bram's house stood behind the forge, close enough that the kitchen wall was warm to the hand on the side it shared with the fire. Bram opened the door himself, still in his shirtsleeves, with the smell of the day on him.

"My daughter," he said, and stood aside. "Ferra."

She was at the table with a ledger open under the lamp, and she didn't get up. She looked at him once, the way she'd looked at him over the ash scales, long enough to see who it was, and went back to the page.

Ferra.

He'd counted on the walk down, which he now regretted, because it meant he knew exactly. Six hours in the forge, with her in the back corner and her father's book in front of her, and he had spent them looking at a tuyere. The morning at the scales, when he'd had his mouth open to ask and someone had screamed. And then a month of mornings at the soap yard, with her twenty paces off with her chalk and her tally board, and him talking to Abloh about salt.

"I should have asked your name a month ago," he said. "Longer. I had a great many chances, and I took none of them, and I'm sorry."

It came out longer than he'd meant. That was the trouble with knowing the number.

"You were busy," Ferra said, without looking up. "Somebody was screaming." She turned a page. "Not that chair. The leg's bad."

He took the other one.

The lamp was between them. She'd taken off the cloth she wore against the ash at the yard, and he saw now that it had been hiding most of her hair, which was a red he'd last seen at the bottom of her father's fire, pinned up any way at all. The freckles went right across her nose and out over both cheeks. She was writing, and didn't look up, and wasn't going to, and he looked at the jug on the table instead and kept looking at it until Bram poured.



Bram asked how the yard was doing. It was the most he'd ever asked Emeric about anything, so Emeric told him properly: the salt boat late again, the kiln drawing well, the old man finally taking his wage, and the ash, bucket by bucket, from memory.

"Three hundred and forty for the month," he finished. "Near enough."

"Three hundred and fifty-one," Ferra said.

He looked at her.

"You've lost the Tuesday after the hands." She still hadn't looked up. "The yard shut at noon. I weighed the rest at the gate, after. Eleven."

He went back through the month in his head. He'd been somewhere else that afternoon, thinking about a woman's palms, and the eleven buckets had never gone in.

"You're right," he said, and he was smiling, and couldn't have said why it pleased him so much to be caught out by someone paying attention.

"I know." She dipped her pen. "It's my count. You were saying it wrong."

She took it the way a person takes a remark about the weather, and went back to the page, and he was left sitting at her table having enjoyed the exchange considerably more than she had.

That much was plain to him, and then Bram was pouring, and he let it go.



Then Bram talked about iron, and for an hour Emeric was happy.

Bram didn't talk, as a rule, and certainly not to princes. But he'd had a cup, and he was in his own kitchen, and Emeric had asked a real question about the ore he worked, and once Bram got going he went the way water goes downhill. Heat by colour: cherry, then orange, then the yellow that was nearly too late, then the white that spat. How the same bar would take a temper in winter and lose it in summer, and he didn't know why and had stopped asking. How charcoal from oak and charcoal from alder were two different animals, and anyone who said otherwise had never paid for either.

Emeric asked, and listened, and asked again. Somewhere in the middle of it he said something about carbon, the way you'd mention salt, and Bram stopped.

"Where'd you get that?"

"A book."

"Which?"

He couldn't answer. There was no answer that went anywhere but a library in another world, a shelf he'd never stand in front of again. He said it was an old one and he'd lost it, which was true in a way that didn't help either of them, and Bram looked at him a while and let it go.

It stayed with him afterward. Everything he knew had come from somewhere he could never name. Bram could say he'd learned from his master, and his master from his, and the knowledge was theirs, passed hand to hand. Emeric could only ever say trust me, and he had no right to ask it. So none of it was his to keep. The only thing he could do with it was hand it over, and then it belonged to whoever used it.

That seemed to him, on the whole, the better arrangement. He never said one word of it, and it sat under the whole evening.

"Iron can run," he said. "Like bronze. You can pour it."

Bram considered this.

"No," he said.

"Hotter than you work it. And longer. With the air forced."

"I've had iron white enough to spit at me." Bram turned his cup. "It doesn't run. It goes to mush, and then it burns. I've seen more iron than you have."

He was not being rude, and he was not humoring a noble. Thirty years of working metal and he had never once seen it, and he said so, and the disbelief was the most respectful thing in the conversation, because it treated the matter as a factual dispute between two men who might each be wrong.

"You have," Emeric agreed. "Can I draw it?"

Bram looked at his daughter. Ferra sighed, tore a sheet out of the back of a ledger as if it were costing her personally, and slid it across with the pen.

The ink feathered the moment it touched the paper. Every line he drew grew a soft grey fringe along the grain, and when he tried to hatch the walls to show they were brick, the hatching ran together into a mud that could have been anything. He tried a lighter hand and got a line that skipped.

"It's the paper," Ferra said. "It's always the paper."

He kept going. He added paper to a list that was already longer than he liked, under ink, under pens, under a straightedge that was actually straight. Before he could fix anything in Kaldo, it seemed, he was going to have to fix the things you fixed things with.

The drawing, when he'd finished it, was ugly and it was right. A tall stack, taller than the forge. Ore and charcoal and broken limestone charged in from the top in layers, so it never had to be let out and started again. Air driven in low, near the bottom, hard. And at the base, a hole you knocked open to let the iron run out, and another for the slag, and then plugged again while the stack went on burning above it, day and night.

In the air pipe he drew a mount for a crystal, and in the hearth another.

"Air and fire," he said. "From the chest my father gave me. They'd do what the bellows do, and better, and they'd keep the heat up when the charcoal lagged."

"And when your stones go flat?"

"Then you lift them out, and it runs on charcoal and a bellows. Slower. But it runs." He'd made himself that rule on the road south, watching the capital's wonders shrink behind the carriage, every one of them held up by a man who'd go home tired: nothing he built would stop working when the stones did.

Bram pulled the sheet toward him and looked at it a long time.

"It's good," he said at last.

"But."

"It's a shadow."

"It's a drawing."

"It's a shadow of a thing. I can see the edges of something on this page that isn't on this page. It's flat. I can't walk round it." He put a thick finger on the stack. "I can't tell if that's as tall as a man or as tall as the keep."

"It says." Emeric touched the numbers down the side.

Bram peered at them. "What's that?"

"Feet."

"Whose?"

Emeric opened his mouth, and shut it.

"We've got thumbs," Bram said, not unkindly. "Paces, which depend on the man. There's a crown standard in the capital, cut on a stone somewhere, and you can ask three clerks how to read it and get three answers. A mason here carries a rod his master cut for him, and his master's wasn't the same as the next master's." He tapped the column of numbers. "This could be anything."

"Bushels are worse," Ferra said.

Emeric looked down at his sheet, and saw it for the first time the way they did. Every drawing he had ever made, here or anywhere, had been in a language only he could read. A private document he had been mistaking for an instruction. To build anything at all he'd first have to teach a whole town what a foot was, and make them a foot to measure it by, and make sure the foot in the forge was the foot in the quarry and the foot at the mill.

That was bigger than the furnace, bigger than any machine he could put on paper.

He sat back with his hand in his pocket, where the stone was, because his hand was always where the stone was. He looked at the smudged stack and wanted, more than he'd wanted anything in a while, for Bram to see it. Standing. Full height, with the air going in.

The kitchen went away.



He was in the room.

It was the fourth time that week, and the first time he'd gone without meaning to, at somebody else's table.

It had changed a little since the first night. It was still plain, still soft where the walls met, still lit from nowhere. But there was more at the edges of it now: lines of his own notes hanging out of the corner of his eye, half-made shapes, the coin and the candle from a hundred repetitions. Nothing in it was sharp except whatever he was looking at.

And in the middle of it, rising, the furnace. Brick by brick, the way he'd been holding it in his head, already taller than a man.

A hand was on his shoulder.

He turned. Bram stood beside him, in his shirtsleeves, holding on, and looking at his own hand as though it had done something without asking him.

"I went blank," Emeric said. He understood it as he said it. "At the table. You touched my shoulder."

"It went soft," Bram said. "Your shoulder. Like reaching into a sack of meal." He let go and looked round. At the walls, at the light, at the lines of writing just out of sight, which he turned his head to catch and couldn't. "What've you done."

So Emeric told him, as fast and as simply as he could. The grey stone, his mother's. A room he'd found he could go into. That time ran differently here, and his body was sitting at Bram's table looking like a man who'd had a stroke, and that he hadn't meant to bring anyone. He'd only wanted Bram to see the thing whole.

Bram listened to all of it. Then he looked at the half-built stack, and Emeric watched his face go completely blank while he decided whether to be afraid, and watched him land on stubborn curiosity instead. It took about four seconds.

"Can you put it up?" Bram said. "Full size."

"I don't know."

"Try."



It went up the rest of the way, and it needed no units at all.

Bram walked to it and put his palm flat on the brick at the base and looked up, and Emeric saw him know exactly how tall it was, the way he knew the weight of a hammer by lifting it. He walked all the way round it. He crouched at the hearth and put his head in where the air pipe came through, which Emeric would never have thought to do, and stayed there.

It took him about ninety seconds.

"Your air's wrong."

"It's the standard arrangement."

"Whose standard?" Bram backed out of the hearth. "Our ore stinks when you roast it. Rotten eggs. Every smith in Kaldo knows that smell." He pointed up the stack. "You drive the air in that straight and that hard, it punches a hot road up the middle and leaves the edges raw. With clean ore you'd get away with it. With ours you'd get iron that's good in the middle and rotten at the rind. You'd never know which bar was which till it broke."

Sulfur. Of course it was sulfur. Emeric had built it from a book, and the book had been written for ore dug out of the ground on another world, and nobody in that book had ever smelled a Kaldo roasting heap.

"Lower," Bram said. "Bring it in at an angle. Slower. And spread it, more than one hole, so it comes up through the whole charge and not just the middle."

He reached out and took hold of the air pipe, and it moved under his hands.

They both stopped.

Then Bram set it lower, and the brick around it reshaped to fit, and he looked at what he'd done with an expression Emeric had never seen on him, and then went straight back to work as though that were an ordinary thing for a pipe to do.

After that there were two of them building. Emeric would put something up and Bram would pull it down a hand's width, or Bram would widen a mouth and Emeric would point out where the heat would go if he did. They argued about the angle of the air. They argued about how many mouths. They took the whole bottom of the stack apart twice and put it back a third way, and at some point Emeric noticed that he hadn't been this happy since a design office in another life, and then forgot to notice anything because Bram was wrong about the second mouth.

"Not that low," he was saying. "You'll choke the hearth, the slag'll come up over..."

And he was at the kitchen table, with the lamp in front of him and the drawing under his hand.

Ferra was in the doorway with her shawl half on.



She didn't take it off. She looked at the two of them, then came back to the table, and Emeric saw that her hands weren't quite steady and that she was making sure they were before she used them.

"You stopped," she said. "Both of you. I looked up and you were looking at nothing." She turned to her father. "Your mouth was hanging on one side. I've seen that. I checked you were breathing. Both of you were." She put the shawl over the back of her chair, carefully. "I'd got as far as the door. I was going for Imani."

"How long?" Emeric said.

"Fifty-four." She said it at once. "I was counting."

Under a minute. Inside, they'd built the bottom of the stack three times.

Bram started a sentence about the air pipe and didn't finish it. Emeric said he was sorry, and meant it, and could not stop grinning, which was the wrong face to be wearing in front of her and he knew it while he was wearing it.

"What was that," Ferra said.

Bram looked at Emeric. Emeric told her. It came out shorter the second time and no easier. When he'd finished, Bram said, "It's true," and flexed his hands on the table, slowly, one and then the other, and studied them.

Ferra didn't ask whether it was safe. She sat down, pulled a fresh ledger off the shelf behind her, opened it to the first page, and dipped her pen.

"Nobody goes in alone," she said.

"I've been going in with Ward in a chair beside me."

"Then that's not alone." She wrote it. "Alone is you in a room with the door shut and nobody. Not once." She went on without waiting for him to agree. "If two go in, somebody who knows what it is stays awake in the house. Not in the yard. Not asleep in the next room. Awake."

She wrote that too.

"Not at night."

"Why not at night?"

"Because at night people are tired and I'm asleep." She dipped the pen. "And past a count I'm comfortable with, I fetch Imani. Nobody gets a vote."

"If you fetch her, she'll know."

"Then don't make me."

Neither man argued. She was obviously right, and she was not asking.

She wrote the date at the top of the facing page, and under it Fifty-four, and both their names, and left a space beside each name, as though she already knew she was going to need the room.

"Who else knows?" she said.

"Ward."

She wrote Ward in the margin. That made three in Kaldo. Ward, and Bram, and Ferra, and she was the only one of them who had thought to make any rules.



He wrote it down too, at the other end of the table, in the back of Armand's notebook, while Ferra wrote in hers.

Brought B in. Didn't mean to. Wanted him to see it, that's all. Contact: hand on my shoulder. Can't say how. Can't repeat it on purpose yet.

B found the fault in ninety seconds. Ore he's handled all his life, a book that never saw it.

He read that back, and then wrote one more line under it.

The knowledge is not the scarce part.

They didn't build it, and nobody pretended they would. It would want timber for the forms and the charging floor that Kaldo didn't have to spare, and every bushel of lime the kiln burned was already spoken for by the soap pots, and the ore Bram had on hand would barely see him through the autumn's horseshoes. Ferra said so, in figures, before anyone asked.

Bram pulled the sheet back across the table. He studied the feathered lines, the stack he'd stood at the foot of, the air pipe he'd moved with his own hands. Then he folded it twice along its creases and put it inside his shirt.

"If something happens to you," he said, "somebody ought to have it."
