Chapter 16 - The Foundry Rises

The old forge had been the town's smithy for as long as anyone alive could remember, and moving it out to the river cost Kaldo its smith for the better part of a month.

Emeric had accounted for the machines. He had not accounted for the silence. Bram's forge in town went cold, the anvil crated, the hearth swept out, and for twenty-six days a man who wanted a hinge repaired or a share re-pointed had to wait, or ride, or do without. Emeric noted the delay as a line in a schedule. He did not, at the time, note the way it sat with people.

The new foundry rose fast, on the eastern arm where the current ran hardest, brick-faced over a core of stone and timber. Eagerness, need, and the plain efficiency of building where the water already ran carried it up in a season that would have taken the old crews a year. It stood two stories under a steep, many-planed roof, and out of that roof climbed three tall round chimneys, each one married to a furnace below.

Three.

Bram had argued for one.

"We need one now," he had said, standing in the mud where the footings were still wet. "One good furnace runs the whole town's iron and then some."

"We need one now," Emeric agreed. "We need three by the time the fields come in. The barracks want armor and every farm inside a day's ride wants a cast plow-head instead of a forged one. Building the shell for three costs a fraction more than building the shell for one. Adding a furnace to a foundry that was framed for it is a week. Adding a furnace to a foundry that wasn't is another season and another roof."

Bram had looked at the three cold hearths a long moment. "You're building for a town we don't have yet."

"I'm building for the town we're about to be."

It was, Emeric thought, unarguable. He was right about the stone, right about the framing, right about the week against the season. He was so completely right about the building that it did not occur to him to ask what it would take to feed three furnaces once they were lit.

Two waterwheels had been framed for, one on each side of the race, because a works this size would want both before the year was out. Only one had gone in. The second sat as a bare frame and a set of unmounted buckets against the wall, because the axle it wanted had to be cast in the foundry, which was not yet lit, and Emeric had decided he would rather light the works on one wheel now than wait on two.

He filed the shortfall as temporary. Nearly everything he filed lately, he filed as temporary.

The wheel that did go in stood against the wall nearest the race, open-spoked and mounted vertically, taking the current straight off the water. Ordinary work. Any millwright in the empire could have set it, and that was the point. The river turned the wheel whether or not a single crystal in the barony held charge, and if the season ran dry he had bedded a small water stone at the intake to lean on the flow when it thinned. A lift under the baseline rather than the baseline itself. Lose the stone and the foundry slowed. It did not stop.

From the wheel a line ran up to the roof ridge where a timber crane frame carried a hoist. The same rotation that drove the works below could be thrown onto the hoist drum to haul a loaded skip up to the second floor, where the charging doors opened onto the tops of the furnaces. That was the reason for the height and the reason for the crane, and Emeric was proud of both.

The furnaces were the part he could not have bought or borrowed anywhere in Kaldo. A blast furnace is not a bigger forge. He had explained it to Bram twice and drawn it three times before the shape of the idea took, and then they had stood it up whole in a room made of nothing and walked around it, and after that Bram stopped arguing.

Each furnace was a tall stone shaft, nearly twice a man's height inside, narrowing and then widening again in a profile Bram had called wrong on sight and beautiful once it ran.

You charged it from the top in courses. A measure of ore, a measure of charcoal, a measure of crushed limestone, then ore again, the whole column stacked and descending as it burned. Air went in at the bottom through clay-lined nozzles, angled low and spread wide the way Bram had insisted for Kaldo's sulfur-heavy ore, the blast driven hard and steady off the wheel's bellows instead of a boy's arms. The rising heat cooked the descending charge on its way down. By the time the ore reached the bottom it was not softened. It was liquid.

That was the line Bram had not believed. Every furnace he had ever worked gave up a bloom, a hot spongy mass of iron and slag you hauled out and beat the slag from by hand, hour after hour. Emeric's furnace never made a bloom. It made a pool. Iron running molten at the base of the stack, slag floating clear on top of it, both drawn off separately through their own tap holes. Iron you could pour. Iron you could cast straight into a mould.

The heat to hold that took more than charcoal. Set into the hearth of each furnace was a fire stone, industrial-cut, tuned to give a long even burn rather than a burst, and it did the one thing charcoal alone could never do reliably in Kaldo. It held the bottom of the stack at the temperature where iron stayed liquid long enough to work.

A reader gauged that heat. Down in the hearth sat a splinter of fire crystal, and fire crystal, born in heat, drank mana faster the hotter it got. Emeric bled it steady against that draw so the splinter always rested at a fill that matched the heat of the melt and not the hours it had burned. The control stone watched the fill.

What it did with it was the part he was proud of.

Up the face of the upper flue he had cut a scale, a tall ladder of marks etched into the stone and numbered, and in an open slot beside it a column of fire rose against those marks like the mercury in a glass he could no longer buy. Cold hearth, a low flame. Hot one, the column climbed. The tip of it stood where the heat stood, and a man on the floor read the number off it the way he would read a rule.

Emeric had built the numbers himself, fixed between two heats he could reach again on purpose, the low where the metal first went soft and the clean pour up top, the span between them split even. They were his own marks and meant nothing to a guild in the capital. They were real, because he had set them against real iron and not against a guess.

He put the whole of it at the head of the chimney, sheathed in the stone flue and standing in the draft, high above every walkway and over nothing that could catch, because an open flame in a hall full of charcoal and dust belonged in exactly one place and that was the top of it. A small pilot burned at the foot of the scale whenever the reader was alive and looking. A slider marked the heat he wanted, and when the column reached it the tip flared bright for a breath and the control stone eased the fire and held it there without a hand.

Flame on the mark meant pour. Flame low meant wait. No flame at all, not even the pilot, meant the reader had gone dark, and then a furnaceman did what furnacemen had always done and read the iron by its own colour.

That last part he built on purpose. Drain the fire stone, or drop the foundry into a dead patch, and the scale went black and the furnace did not die with it. It fell back to what a very good charcoal furnace could manage on its own. Slower, cooler, coarser iron, more of it wasted. Worse, and not nothing. The mundane furnace underneath the mana one still stood, because the barony could not afford a heart that stopped the moment a crystal did.

He gave the crew the rule in a breath and no theory. Watch the flame, keep it on your mark, and when the stone goes dark, trust your eyes.

It was the threshing floor again in a breastplate. Never trust a fixed number when you can read the real one, and here the real one had learned to light itself and climb.

Ward walked the finished shell once and said only that he did not like how much of Kaldo's worth now sat under one roof by the water, out past the wall. He had the corners built up with a parapet and merlons, more keep than workshop, and put it about that the foundry was watched at night. Emeric let him. It cost stone and it cost nothing he cared about, and Ward was rarely wrong about the things Emeric was blind to.

The wall itself had closed while the foundry climbed. He had not built a new one. He had taken the old circuit, the low patched thing he walked past his first morning in Kaldo, and had it repaired stone by stone and closed all the way around, the western and eastern arms anchoring its ends so the whole town sat behind a single line with one landward gate above the ford.

Local hands, local quarry stone, a handful of plain structural runes cut into the gate-towers to stiffen them, nothing a capital engineer would have looked at twice. It ran further than eighteen hundred people strictly needed, because it enclosed the empty quarters too, the streets the plague had hollowed out. He had chosen that on purpose and knew the cost of it. A wall too long for the men he had to hold it. He filed that with the second wheel and the deep workings, under things that were true and would have to keep.

And in the last weeks before the fields came in, he had been quietly building two things that were not for the fields at all.

He would not have called it fear. He called it exposure, which was the same thing with the feeling sanded off. He had cast out Wexler's bought men with an outdated map of Kaldo in their heads and did not fool himself that men like that rode off and simply stopped talking. Somewhere the shape of what he was doing was reaching ears, and a barony growing this fast, this visibly, with a third-born exile at the head of it and no army worth the name, was a thing the neighbors would eventually have to have an opinion about.

The first was a way to kill a mage's magic.

He had learned early, and learned again from Armand's notebook, the ugly failure that happened when you overloaded a stone past what it could vent cleanly. Draw it too hard, or route the draw wrong, and instead of paying out its charge in an orderly stream the crystal tore it loose all at once, a chaotic surge that fouled the field around it and left nearby crystals sullen and hard to draw from for a while after. Every mage was taught to avoid it the way every smith is taught not to quench a blade too fast.

Emeric had started asking instead what it would take to cause it on purpose, and aim it.

The making of it was almost insultingly simple, which was the part he kept turning over. A cheap riverbank water-stone, the kind that cost nothing and lay in the shallows by the thousand, wanted only to be told to open everything at once with nowhere clean for it to go. He cut it a single crude release channel and left out the throttle a careful mage would have added. No vent path, no stepped bleed. A couple of instructions that amounted to all of it, now.

Struck hard against the ground, the stone obeyed the only order it had. It did not pour. It ruptured, dumping its whole small charge out crooked and wrong in a single beat.

What that did to the field around it was the useful part and the ugly one. For a radius out from wherever it landed the mana went slack and unusable, wrung out, a hole punched in the thing every spell was drawn from. Any mage standing inside it reached for the mana and closed on nothing. Any charged crystal caught in it went inert, so much dead rock.

The null was strongest at the center and thinned toward the edges, a gradient rather than a wall, and it was not permanent. The surrounding field crept back in from the rim over the better part of a day, the way water seeps back into a bailed hole, quick at the edges and slow and stubborn at the heart. Left alone, the ground healed itself. He would not, in the moment he needed it, have a day.

The unnerving thing was that it barely looked like anything. He had expected the first time some visible violence to match the size of what it did. A flash, a scorch, a sound worth the name. There was almost none. The stone cracked, a flat hard report with no fire in it, a colourless shove of air pushed out and gone, and then a patch of perfectly ordinary riverbank that looked exactly like every other patch of riverbank and was, to anyone who could feel mana at all, simply absent. A quiet wrongness sitting over the grass that the eye slid right off and the mind could not.

He suspected that was the worst of it for the men he meant to use it on. Not being burned. Being made ordinary in an instant, with nothing to show them why.

It killed his own enhanced gear as readily as anyone's, which meant it was a thing you threw away from yourself and never a shield you stood inside. He built three, carried them wrapped and kept apart from every crystal he owned, and did not test them near anything he cared about.

The second was a way to hit a man across open ground with no magic at all in the blow.

He had the sketches he'd been turning over since he arrived in Kaldo, and he had air crystals, which moved existing air for free and asked nothing back. A braced tube, a rifled metal cone, an air crystal to spin it and shove it hard off the muzzle, and after that nothing but the plain dumb arithmetic of a heavy thing thrown fast.

No guidance rune, nothing clever in flight, because clever in flight was magic and magic was the thing his own deadzone stone would kill. Once the slug left the barrel it was not a spell. It was a rock, and a deadzone does nothing to a rock.

It was a personal thing, one off, braced against his own shoulder, and it could not be handed to Bram to mould and stamp because cutting its rune wanted the root-level delving that was his alone. He built one. It nearly broke his shoulder, and he built the second one better, and he was not yet willing to say in front of anyone that it was finished.

Through all of it he practiced casting the way he practiced everything, from the outcome backward. He would never be the kind of mage his eldest brother was, the kind whose raw output could level a wall on its own. That had been decided in the blood long before Marcus ever arrived to inherit this body.

But delving gave him something the raw ones didn't have, a finer hand on the mana while a spell was still forming, so that a small cheap spell cast fast and shaped exactly could do the work of a large one cast slow. He drilled the small ones until they were reflex.

The blast came on the first day of the first heat. For an hour the stack roared and did nothing anyone could see, and Bram stood with his arms crossed and his jaw set in the particular way that meant he had bet against something and was waiting to be proved right.

Then the trouble Emeric had not designed out arrived.

They needed to charge the second course, and charging meant throwing the wheel's power onto the hoist to lift the next skip. The instant the hoist drum took the load, the wheel slowed. The bellows ran off the same wheel. The blast sagged, right as the furnace was hungriest, and the reader on the flue showed the bottom of the stack shedding heat in a slow wrong slide.

Emeric saw it in the numbers before he saw it anywhere else. One wheel. Two mouths. Hoist a heavy load and you starve the blast to feed the crane, and you starve it at the worst possible moment, because you only hoist when the furnace is ready to eat again.

"Hold the skip," he said. "Don't hoist while she's drawing hard. We charge between blasts, not across them."

It worked, in the sense that it stopped the immediate ruin. It also meant the whole rhythm of the furnace was now hostage to a man watching a scale and calling when the crane was allowed to move, and it meant the throughput Emeric had built three furnaces to reach was for now impossible, because you could not run the hoist and the blast at full at the same time off one wheel.

The real fix was the second wheel he had not installed, or a heavy wheel on the drive to carry the works through the dips. And a flywheel that size had to be cast. In the foundry. Which was not yet reliably casting anything.

He wrote it down. He deferred it. He was getting very good at deferring things.

They lost part of that first heat to the sag, a skimming of scrapped iron and a lining that would want patching sooner than it should. Bram cursed and skimmed and recharged and did not say I told you so, which from Bram was its own verdict.

And then, past noon, they tapped it. The clay plug came out of the bottom and the iron came with it, running white-gold down the sand channel into the moulds, into the plow-head forms lined up waiting, and the whole crowd made a single sound Emeric felt in his teeth.

Bram crouched at the channel with the light of it on his face and watched three generations of bloom-and-beat metallurgy become a thing that simply poured, and said nothing at all for a long time.

It was, by every measure Emeric owned, a win. The furnace ran. The iron poured. The moulds filled. He had brought a piece of a world eight hundred years ahead down onto a riverbank in Kaldo and it had worked on the first real day.

In the days after, the moulds ran to more than plows. Bram set the armor forms and the foundry began stamping them out, breastplate and pauldron and greave in a uniform dark steel better than any capital knight's.

What made it better was not a secret buried in the ore, and Emeric was careful, when Bram asked, to say so plainly. The capital's smiths knew everything he knew about steel and had known it for centuries. A good capital blade was already high-carbon steel, hard and keen, and their armorers could harden the face of a plate as well as any hand alive.

The thing they could not do was do it the same way twice. A hand-worked bloom came off the fire uneven, carbon rich in one pocket and starved in the next, and a smith judged the heat by the colour of the metal and quenched by the memory in his arm, and got a fine piece four times in five and a brittle one the fifth, and never quite knew which he had made until it bent or held.

Emeric's furnace took the guessing out of the middle of it. The blast ran the iron fully molten so the melt mixed even and the carbon settled where he wanted it. The reader held the heat to a mark instead of to a man's memory, so every heat cooled down the same curve as the last. A plate stamped out of that steel came off the mould the twin of the plate stamped before it.

It was not a stronger metal than the empire had ever managed. It was the metal the empire already knew how to make, made right every single time, which no hand and eye in any capital forge could honestly promise. Twenty men in twenty near-identical harnesses, each one as good as the best the process could give rather than as good as the smith's luck that morning, was a thing only consistency could buy.

It was the threshing floor again, wearing a breastplate. Read the real number, and the work stops depending on whether the man swinging the hammer is having a good day.

They did not look like capital work, and that was deliberate. Where a Randillia knight's harness was bright etched silver, filigreed and polished to catch a hall's light, these came out of the mould a deep matte near-black, the recessed panel lines and the joints a shade greyer still, with nothing bright anywhere on them but a thin edge of oiled dark bronze where a smith's hand had dressed the plate.

Angular, flat-faced, the bevels cleaner and the seams tighter and the plates more even in thickness than any hand-beaten suit in the empire, so that a man who knew armor would read the quality without being able to name it and a man who did not would see only something plain and grim and close-fitting. Full helm, no open face, the eye-slit a narrow dark band.

Not beautiful. Better made than beautiful, which Emeric had decided somewhere along the way was a thing he preferred.

Twenty regular harnesses for the twenty, mould-stamped and near identical, a broken piece off one fitting the next, no crystal anywhere in them. Honest steel and nothing else.

And four that were not regular. The four elites, Ward, Rylan, Declan and Osric, had come out of the capital with their old harness, mediocre steel over good crystals, the reverse of everything Kaldo had. Emeric harvested the crystals out of that old capital armor and set them into four suits of the new black steel, seated deep in sealed housings sunk into the plate, teardrop insets a man had to be close enough to touch to find, with no glow bleeding out of them to tell a watcher on a night road where a body was standing.

From the outside the four looked like the twenty, one uniform dark line of them, which was the point. The difference showed only in the wearing. The four stood lighter than steel that heavy had any right to stand, moved a half beat quicker than a man in full plate should, and cost nothing to carry, right up until the day a dead patch would take all of it back at once and hand them their true weight in the middle of a swing.

So Kaldo had four Enhanced harnesses better than anything the men had worn as royal guards, and no way at all to make a fifth, because there were exactly four elites' worth of good crystals in the barony and the high-grade veins that could yield more sat under a hill in Grayrock that did not belong to him.

The elite four also looked like something out of a worse story than this one, and Emeric had allowed that on purpose. A full helm rising to a pair of horns that did nothing but tell a man across a field to reconsider. A beast-shaped guard over the right shoulder. A long black cloak, already half ruined by design, hanging from the back.

None of it bought a fingerwidth of protection the plain version lacked. It bought hesitation, and against numbers, hesitation was worth more than the crystals had cost.

The regular suits carried none of that. Clean overlapping plate, a simple visor, a high collar, no cloak and no horns, the breastplate following the body and layered heavier at the abdomen where a man takes the blows he does not see coming.

The swords came off the same heats and Emeric had listed them as an afterthought, which Bram had not. A cast blank wanted good steel, a grind wheel, and a man who knew an edge, and none of those three was scarce in a foundry with its first heat done. They came out heavier than a craftsman's work and truer than anything the garrison had carried under Wexler. He had told Bram not to waste an hour making them beautiful.

The crossbows were Emeric's idea and Ward's design, which was the right arrangement for a weapon that had to be right and cheap and learnable by a man who had never held anything longer than a croft hook.

He had thought about ordinary bows first and set them aside inside an afternoon. A war bow was the better weapon in a trained hand and would stay the better weapon for a thousand years, but a trained hand was exactly the thing Kaldo did not have and could not grow in the weeks it had left.

A bowman is not equipped, he is raised. Years of pull have to be built into the bones and the back before a man can bend a heavy bow to the ear at all, and years more spent learning to loose and mark a moving target by feel. Hand a croft farmer a war bow and he cannot draw it, and if by some gift he can draw it he cannot hold it steady, and if he can hold it he cannot put the arrow where he means it to go. None of that is a thing a single season fixes. It is a childhood, or it is nothing.

A crossbow asked for none of it. The prod did the work the archer's back was supposed to have spent a life becoming. A man spanned it with a lever and his own weight, braced the stock against his shoulder, laid it on the target the way he would point a finger, and the bolt went more or less where the tube was looking. What took a bowman a decade took a crossbowman a morning.

It shot slower, three bolts to a good archer's dozen, and Emeric weighed that cost honestly and found he did not care. On a wall, against men wading a ford, he did not need fast shooting from a handful of trained hands he had no way to conjure. He needed enough hands to put a bolt through a harness at the range Ward named, and he needed them before the month was out.

The making was as plain as the case for it. A stamped steel prod off the mill's first thin-cut run of the same steel that went into the plate, bent cold under a jig Bram made in an afternoon, bolted to a straight stock any decent carpenter could reproduce, fitted with a nut-and-roller release a man could trust with his life by the afternoon of the day he learned it in the morning. No crystal in it, nothing to fail in a dead patch, nothing a bolt-thrower had to understand past which end to point.

The gravity hammer was not for the twenty and not for the crossbows. It was for Ward, and it started as a question Emeric had been carrying for weeks. What do you give a man who already knows how to fight, so the gap between him and whatever he walks into is as wide as the steel can make it?

A man in plate expects the armor to stop the blow. He does not expect the blow to arrive at a weight his bones were not made to absorb. The answer was a gravity crystal set into the hammer's head with two channels etched beside it, one to drain the head nearly weightless on the upswing, one to cut the weightlessness at the moment of landing. Swing a baton, land a boulder.

The etch was straightforward enough for conventional rune work, no delving needed, just two clean channels and a man who knew which one to fire and when. He drilled Ward on the timing for half a morning in the foundry yard. Ward had the pattern in twenty minutes and said only that the head was heavier than he'd expected before the channel went live.

"That's the point," Emeric said.

"I know," Ward said, and swung it again.

The mismatch found him before he went looking for it, which was the only reason he caught it as early as he did.

Two of Bram's men had cut the same part off the same drawing at two different benches. An axle seat, nothing complicated, a collar a shaft was meant to drop through and turn in. One seated clean. The other bound halfway and wouldn't go, and when Emeric laid the two collars side by side on the bench they looked identical to the eye and measured a full fingernail's width apart across the bore.

He asked each man what he'd worked to. Each showed him a rule. The rules were both honest, both worn smooth with years of good use, and both wrong about where an inch ended, because they had been notched by two different hands in two different decades off two different rods nobody had ever thought to lay against each other.

He stood there with a collar in each hand and felt the whole shape of the problem open under him at once, the way a crack you have been ignoring finally runs the length of the wall.

It was not these two men. It was every man, every bench, every rule in the barony, each one a little world of its own with its own foot and its own pound. And it had never mattered before, because before, a thing was made by one pair of hands from start to finish and fitted to itself as it went. A smith who forged a hinge and the door it hung on never had to agree with anyone. He only had to agree with himself.

That world was ending, and Emeric was the one ending it, and he had walked into the ending without once accounting for the ground it stood on. He wanted parts cast at one bench to seat parts cut at another. He wanted a spray head from a mould today to thread onto a coupling from a mould next season. He wanted, when he came to it, a hundred of a thing that were each the same thing. None of that was possible in a barony where the inch changed when you crossed the yard.

"This is going to sound like the smallest thing I've ever asked you for," he told Bram that evening, "and it's going to be the one that matters most. From now on there is one rule. Not one for you and one for your father and one for whatever the last man notched by firelight. One. Twelve inches to the foot, and the inch is this inch, the one I cut tonight, and every rule in the works gets checked against it and any that disagree get burned."

Bram did not argue, which surprised him until he thought about why. Bram had scrapped good iron over exactly this and never had a name for it, only the weary certainty that two men working off two rules would sooner or later hand him two parts that hated each other.

"One foot," Bram said. "Same foot in the capital as at the gate."

"Same foot at every bench in this building. That's the whole of it. A thing you can check for yourself, that doesn't change when I leave the room, that a man ten years from now can lay against his own work and know he's right or know he's wrong and not have to argue about which."

He built the master the way he built anything he meant to outlive him. Plainly, once, and in a form any hand could copy. He set it in the foundry office in a shallow case and had two dozen true copies struck off it for the benches, and the striking of the copies was its own small lesson, because a copy of a copy drifts and a copy of the master does not, and he made sure every man understood which one he was allowed to trust.

The weights went the same way. Sixteen ounces to the pound, and the pound was this pound. He did not reach for the land measures yet. What he needed today was that a bore was a bore and a thread was a thread and the two agreed across a building, and by the end of the week they did.

He kept a second set of numbers in his own book, smaller and cleaner, built on tens instead of twelves, and did not put it on a single drawing the men would ever read. That was his, for the sums he did alone, where a figure that divided by ten every time saved him an error he could not afford in work nobody was checking. There would be a place for it later and people who would need it and a way to hand it over that didn't require explaining where he'd learned to think in it. Not here. Here the men got the foot, because the foot was what the barony already half believed in, and a standard nobody resents is a standard that holds.

He put the two collars on a shelf in the office where he would see them. Not as a trophy. As the thing he had nearly missed, sitting where it could remind him that the cleverest machine in the world is only ever as true as the least considered rule that built it, and that he had come within one bound shaft of pouring a season's work through a gap he had left in the floor.

The pipe fittings came off the same mixed run the day after the weapons. Small castings, the brass sprayer heads he had been carrying the drawing of since before the foundry stood.

The clay pipe network was already laid. He replaced the caps at each row stub with a brass threaded coupling and set the spray heads at intervals down each run, simple ball-and-spring bodies with jet arrays cut to throw water outward in a wide flat fan. No crystal needed for the basic version. Water at pressure through a small opening comes out at speed and spreads, and if the tank is high enough and the pipe run isn't kinked it does that on the river's weight alone. He had built the tank high enough.

He set the Control Rune at the main inlet and cut a single release channel into the valve body so the stone could open or close the flow on instruction. That was the floor. The rest was what the stone could do past it.

The stub valves had cost him three evenings nobody knew about. Each one was a plain river water-stone with a single open-and-close instruction, and each one had to pass through his own hands under a delve before the Control Rune would speak to it at all. Forty of them, one at a time, in a cold office, and he had come out of the third night understanding exactly why he needed Armand and exactly what he was going to have to admit to get him.

What it bought was worth the evenings. He spent an afternoon in the field running through what the drawing had promised. Close a stub valve and the section it fed went dry. Open it and it came back. He could water three rows and rest the fourth, or reverse it, or open everything at once, or run it wide and thin for a dry day and narrow and heavy for a corner that was always slow to take water.

The last thing he tested was the burst. The whole network released at once and cut in a single beat, one pressure spike through every head simultaneously, a low stuttering percussion off a hundred brass jets that left the field wet from above rather than flooded from below.

He stood in the middle of it a moment, dripping, not entirely by accident, and noted the draw on the crystal, which was small, because throwing existing water through existing openings is not a spell. It is plumbing.

He walked back to the foundry with wet boots and filed it under things that had worked, and said nothing about it to anyone, because the tank needed a full day to refill and there was iron to count.

He let himself have the win for most of an afternoon.

Saban found him at dusk, and Saban did not come out to the river for good news.

Emeric trusted the merchant's bad news for the same reason he had come to trust the merchant at all, which had not been by liking the look of him. Early on, needing factors for a barony that had none it could rely on, he'd had four local traders bidding for the lord's custom and no way to tell the honest one from the three who only looked it, and a face had never once told him the truth about a man.

So he overpaid all four. An early parcel each, coin that ran well over the agreed price, the kind of overcount that passed for a clerk's slip and could be pocketed without a word. Three of them pocketed it. Saban sent it back, counted out flat, no flourish, no angling for thanks.

Emeric cut the three from the barony's preferential contracts, one of them loudly, and gave Saban the trade, and not because the returned coin proved Saban a good man. It proved him a clever one. A merchant who skims a lord's overcount has chosen a mouthful of coin once over a supplier's standing for years. The one who returned it had grasped without being told that the barony was about to become the most valuable customer in a day's ride.

Emeric didn't need him honest against his own interest. He needed him smart about it, which holds under pressure where virtue doesn't, and Saban had never once made him revise the read.

"The seam's thinning," the merchant said, without preamble, because he'd learned Emeric preferred it that way. "Old workings. What's left near the surface is near gone. There's more ore in that hill, but it's deep, and it's poor, and getting at it means more men, more timber, more time, for worse rock." He nodded at the three chimneys still breathing heat into the dark. "You built three mouths. I can barely feed one of them past the stockpile, and the stockpile's a month, maybe less at the rate that thing eats."

"There's ore in Grayrock," Emeric said. It wasn't quite a question. Grayrock was where Kaldo's iron had always come from. One road, one town, the same road the crystal came down.

Saban's mouth went flat. "There was. Grayrock's gone quiet on us this month. Prices up, then no answer at all to the last two orders." He let that sit, because he was honest, and honesty meant not letting a lord hear only the half he wanted. "I don't like a supplier who goes quiet right when he knows how badly you need him. There's ore in Duvrenne too, and in half the baronies between here and it, and I can have it moving inside a fortnight. At Duvrenne prices. Plus the carting. Plus every hand that touches it on the way. I can find you all the ore you can burn, my lord. I can't find it cheap and I can't find it free, and you already know which of those two we haven't got."

The seam dying on one side and the coin dying on the other, both in the same week, and a straight face expected of him while a man he trusted laid them out as a courtesy.

He should have gone then. He didn't, and that was how Emeric knew the ore was not the worst thing the merchant had carried out to the river.

"There's more, and you'll want it plain," Saban said. "My factors on the Wayford road, and two men I keep quiet in Grayrock town, are all bringing back the same shape this fortnight. Ore's not the only thing those baronies are buying. They're buying iron that never sees a plow, and taking on men who've never held a scythe in their lives, and asking after the country down our way. The fords, the gate, how many bodies Kaldo can put on a wall."

He said it the way he said a price. Flat, no weight added that the words did not already carry.

"Grayrock and Wayford both, arming, and not quietly enough to miss. Milford quieter, but Milford's never loud. The word my people bring back is that the coalition means to come down on Kaldo, my lord, and soon. Before whatever you're building out here turns into something they'd have to be afraid of."

So it came down to the same place the workings did and the same place the furnaces did. Not a supply problem he could out-think with a taller stack or a cleverer blast. A money problem wearing a supply problem's coat.

And under the money problem, quieter, the thing Saban had just set in front of him that he had not yet let himself pick up. Grayrock had not gone silent by accident the same season a broke and growing barony started arming, and men who bought iron that never saw a plow were not buying it to look at.

Ferra was waiting at the hall with the ledgers open, and she did not soften it either.

"We're out," she said. "Not low. Out. The relocation, the furnaces, the fire stones, the armor, the wall, the seed and the tools and the last of the field works, all of it went out this season and none of it has come back. Payroll's due at the turn of the month. The colliers want paying for the charcoal. Saban's carters will want paying the day the ore leaves Duvrenne, not the day it arrives. And there is nothing behind any of it." She turned a page she did not need to turn. "The fields are the only thing that pays in. And the fields aren't in."

There it was, folded down into a single number that wasn't there. The foundry stood. The furnaces ran. The wall was closed, the twenty were armored, and the fields were sown and worked and wanting nothing but weather and weeks. Every coin that had bought that readiness was spent, and the only thing that could put a single coin back was a harvest that had not happened yet, from a system he had built and scaled and never once tested at the size the whole barony was now balanced on.

He found Bram still at the foundry that night, sitting on an upturned crate by the tap channel where the last iron had long since gone hard in the moulds. Emeric sat down beside him expecting to share the win. The furnace had run. Surely that was why Bram was where he was.

"Good first day," Emeric said.

Bram was quiet a while. When he spoke he didn't look up from the cold iron.

"It's the best thing I've ever put my hands to, and I'll say that to anyone." He turned a cold plow-head over with the toe of his boot. "But you asked me once why I fought you on the third furnace. It's because half this town watched you close my shop for a month and pour everything we have into three fires, and when that iron ran today they didn't cheer. They counted. They've got the same seed in the same ground we do, and they know to the bushel what happens to Kaldo if it comes up short."

He looked over.

"You read that furnace to the degree, my lord. You didn't read one face in that crowd."

And he hadn't. He had watched the blast sag and named the cause in a single breath, and he had watched a whole town all season and called their fear excitement, because excitement was what he would have felt and he was not standing where they stood.

The sound they made when the iron poured had not been triumph. It was the sound of people who had staked everything they owned and just watched the wheel begin to turn, not yet knowing where it would stop.

He could out-build the old world. He could not out-build a bad harvest, and he could not buy his way through the wait, because there was nothing left to buy it with. For once he had made a thing that worked exactly as designed, and it had left him nothing to do but sit by the cold iron and count the weeks and hope the fields were kinder than the ledger.

He was still sitting there when Ward came down the river path at a pace that was not a stroll, and stopped, and did not sit.

"Riders came in to Rylan on the west road," Ward said. "Grayrock men, and Wayford colours with them, and they weren't buying." He let it land flat, the way he delivered things he had already decided were true. "They're mustering. Both of them, and Milford behind, and the word riding with them is that Kaldo's a plague town with a mad prince and a farm, ripe for the taking before the harvest makes it anything else."

A pause.

"Somebody sold them that picture. It's a season out of date. They're coming on it anyway."

Emeric looked at the cold iron, and the moulds, and the four Enhanced harnesses drying in the dark that there would never be a fifth of, and the wall that ran further than his men could hold, and the granary that was not yet full and was the whole reason anyone would bother.

"How long," he said.

"Days. Not weeks."

He stood up off the crate. The tiredness was still there and it stopped mattering in the same instant, the way it had the night Tam died.

"Then we don't get the wait," he said. "Get me Rylan and the maps. Bram, I need every harness we've stamped on a body by the time they're in sight, and I already know it won't be every body, and I need you to help me decide which ones."
