Chapter 16 - Liquid

"How thick is the wall?"

"Thick," Emeric said.

Bram put down the drawing.

They had been at it since the forge went cold at midday and the light through the door had gone from white to gold to the flat grey that meant Emeric had lost most of a working day to a piece of paper. There were four drawings on the bench and a fifth on the floor where Bram had dropped it, and every one of them was, Emeric was prepared to admit, a picture of a furnace rather than a plan for one.

"Thick," Bram repeated.

"Thicker than a forge chimney. Considerably. It's holding molten iron at the bottom and a burning column above it and the whole thing wants to shed heat as slowly as possible, so, thick."

"Give me a number."

"I don't have a number."

"Give me a wrong number and I'll argue you down to a right one."

"That's the same problem with an extra step in it." Emeric pressed his thumbs into his eyes. "I know what it does. I know why it does it. I have never in my life stood next to one, I never had cause to learn what its wall was made of, and if I hand you a number out of nowhere you'll build to it, because you're the kind of man who builds to the number, and then it'll crack in the fourth week and we'll both know exactly whose fault that was."

"Then we're in the same trouble from opposite ends," Bram said, "because I can tell you the thickness of every wall in this town and I can't tell you the thickness of a wall around a thing I don't believe in."

"You don't believe in it."

"I believe you believe in it. That's not nothing, and it's not stone either."

Emeric pulled the fifth drawing off the floor and put it back on the bench, and they went round again.

The trouble was very specific and it had taken him two days to see it clearly, which was itself annoying, because Ferra had seen it in about four minutes and had said so and he had not listened properly.

He knew the furnace as a principle. Stack the charge from the top in courses, ore and charcoal and crushed limestone, and let the whole column descend as it burns. Blow air in at the bottom, hard and steady. The heat rising through the descending charge cooks it on the way down, so that the ore is not merely softened by the time it reaches the base, it is liquid, and the slag floats clear of it, and you draw the two off separately through their own holes. He knew why the limestone was there. He knew that the profile mattered, that the shaft had a shape rather than being a tube, that the shape was what made the column descend evenly instead of hanging up and burning hollow.

What he did not have was a single dimension. Not the height, not the bore, not the wall, not the angle of the taper, not the size or the number or the placement of the nozzles the air came in through. He had been a mechanical engineer and not a metallurgist, and the difference between those two men had been, in his old life, a matter of which floor of a building you worked on and which conference you were sent to, and here it was the difference between a working furnace and a season of Kaldo's stone spent on a hole in the ground.

And Bram, on the other side of it, had forty years of knowing exactly how iron behaved at heat, in his hands, by colour, by smell, by the noise it made when it moved. He could look at a bloom and tell you what it wanted. He had never once in his life had reason to think that iron could be got to pour, because in every furnace he or his father or his father's father had ever worked, it did not. You got a bloom, a hot spongy mass of metal and slag welded together, and you hauled it out and beat the slag out of it by hand, hour after hour, and that was iron, and that was what iron was.

"Say the pouring part again," Bram said, at some point after dark.

"It runs out of the bottom like water."

"Iron."

"Iron."

"Out of a hole. Like water."

"Like honey off a spoon, if you want it more precisely. It's thicker than water. It runs down a sand channel and fills a mould, and when it's cold you knock the sand off and you have the shape of the mould in iron, and you did not hammer it, and you did not fold it, and no man's arm was involved in any part of it."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. It was, Emeric thought later, an entirely reasonable silence.

"Then what am I for?" Bram said.

Emeric turned around.

"You said that out loud," he said.

"I did." Bram looked at the cold hearth, and at his own hands, which were the hands of a man who had spent his whole life beating slag out of metal. "Say it's true. Say it pours. Then a mould does in an hour what took me a week, and it does it the same every time, and the thing I'm best at in the world is the thing your furnace makes pointless." He shrugged, roughly. "I'm not arguing against it. I want to be clear I'm not. I'm telling you what it costs to be the man you're asking to build it."

"It doesn't make you pointless." Emeric said it too fast and heard himself say it too fast. "It makes the part of the work that's misery into something a machine does badly and cheaply, and it leaves every part that needs judgement to you, and there is going to be so much more of that than you can possibly imagine that within two years you'll be turning work away."

"You don't know that."

"I do know that. That's the one thing in this room I actually know." He put the chalk down. "The metal that comes out of a mould is a blank. It's coarse, it's uneven, it's full of nothing but potential, and every single thing that makes it any use, the edge, the temper, the fit, the finish, is you or it's nobody. What I'm building is a thing that gives you a hundred blanks a week instead of two. It doesn't replace the smith. It buries him in work."

"Hm," Bram said, which was not agreement, and picked the drawing back up.

And there it stayed. He could not make Bram see the inside of the shaft, because the inside of the shaft was the whole argument and it was not on the paper. Emeric drew it again, worse. He drew the taper twice and got the taper wrong both times and knew he had it wrong and could not say how. He tried describing the descent of the column and heard himself using the word "roughly" four times in one sentence.

What he wanted, standing there with clay dust on his hands at the end of a wasted day, was not to explain it. He wanted to take the man's head in both hands and put the thing inside it whole, the shaft and the throat and the taper and the column coming down and the pool at the bottom, all of it at once, from the inside, the way it sat in his own head.

He reached for that the way you reach for a word you know you have.

And he found it.

There was no transition worth the name. There never was. One moment he was in a forge in Kaldo with a bad drawing in his hand, and then he was standing in the place he had no word for, which was not dark and not light and had no floor except where he decided there was one, and the furnace was already there because he had been holding it, twenty feet of it, whole and quiet and turning slowly on nothing.

He had been coming here for two months. His mother had put a hollow crystal in his hands in a room in the capital when he was six and told him to look at the inside of it, and had come back every day for a year and asked him what he had seen, and had never once told him he was wrong. He had seen nothing. Every day, for a year, nothing, and she had written it down as if nothing were an answer. He had understood, by about nine, that she had loved him and that the stone was a stone, and he had gone on carrying it for sixteen years on those terms, and then a cold room in Kaldo had finished a count he had never known was running. He had not understood, until an hour ago, that it was something you could do to another person.

Bram arrived four feet to his left, with one hand still out where Emeric's shoulder had been.

Emeric said something obscene in a language that did not exist here.

Bram did not appear to hear it. Bram was standing absolutely still with his head tipped back and his mouth slightly open, and after a moment he said, in an ordinary voice, "That's it, then."

"Bram."

"That's the shape you couldn't draw." He walked forward three paces and stopped, and looked up the shaft, and then walked around it, all the way around, slowly, the way a man walks around a horse he is thinking about buying. "It swells."

"Bram, listen to me. You need to know where you are, and I need to tell you that I don't have a good word for it, and that I did not do this on purpose, and that you can leave."

"Can I?"

"I think you can. I've never had anyone here before." He heard the shape of what he had just said. "That's not an answer. I don't know. Try wanting to be in the forge."

Bram considered this for a moment and visibly did not try.

"It swells," he said again, and put a hand on the shaft, which did not feel like anything, and did not stop his hand either. "You had it as a tube. You said tube twice. It's not a tube. It's narrow at the throat and it opens out under it and then it draws in again down at the bottom." He crouched. "Why?"

"Because the charge descends. If the walls run straight the column jams and burns hollow and you lose the heat up the middle. The swell gives it room to loosen as it comes down and the draw at the bottom pulls it back together over the hearth."

"Then your swell's in the wrong place and it's not near enough." Bram stood up. "Move it down. Down. Further. There." He looked at it. "And that's too tall for that bore. You'd never get air to the middle of that. I'd not blow that with every bellows in the barony."

Emeric shortened it. It shortened, because in here things were the shape he held them in and no other shape.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've spent thirty years learning how far into a fire a blast reaches, and it's not as far as men think, and you're building a fire twice the height of a man and expecting the middle of it to be as hot as the edge." Bram walked round the base. "Where's the air come in?"

"Nozzles. Round the bottom. Clay-lined, pointed slightly down."

"How many, and how wide apart?"

"I don't know."

"Then I'll tell you, and you'll not argue, because this is the one part of this I know better than any man in three baronies." Bram set his hand flat against the hearth wall. "Kaldo's ore stinks. It's always stunk. There's sulfur through the whole of that hill and it's why my father's iron was short and why mine is, and if you put your air in hard and narrow through two big nozzles you'll drive a hot lance through the middle of it and cook the sulfur straight into the metal and it'll come out of your beautiful hole as beautiful rotten iron." He held up four fingers. "Spread it. More holes, smaller, low, and angled down and round so the blast turns as it goes in instead of stabbing. You want the whole hearth working, not a spear through the middle of it. That's not a thing you read. That's a thing that costs you eleven years and a lot of ruined stock."

Emeric changed it. Six nozzles became eight and moved down and turned, and he stood back and looked at what was now unmistakably not his design.

"Say it again," he said. "Slowly. I want to have it exactly."

Bram said it again.

They were in there a long time. That was the thing about the place that Emeric had never got used to and had stopped trying to describe to himself: time in there ran to its own arithmetic and the arithmetic was not stable. They built the whole of it. They built it four times. They put the tap holes in and Bram moved the slag hole twice and then a third time and explained, at length and with contempt, why a man who put a slag hole level with an iron hole had never in his life had to stand next to one. They walked the charging floor. They argued about the throat for what felt like an hour and a half.

And there was a wall Emeric hit, twice, and the second time he stopped and said it out loud, because it mattered and because he wanted a witness who was not himself.

"It won't tell us whether it works."

Bram looked over.

"This is geometry," Emeric said. "That's all it is. It's the shape, exactly, held still, from any side, and I can hand it to you and you can hand it back to me improved, and that is worth more than every drawing I've made this month." He put his hand through the shaft wall, which parted around it. "And it is not a furnace. It doesn't burn. There's no heat in it, no draught, no weight in the column, no iron in the hearth. I can't light it in here and watch it run, and if I convince myself I can, I'll build a thing that was perfect in a place with no physics in it." He took his hand back. "Everything about whether it works, we find out on a riverbank, in front of the whole town, with real ore in it."

"Then what's the good of it?"

"The good of it is that we've just spent a day and a half agreeing on the shape, and out there I'd have needed a season and a mason and I'd still have got the swell in the wrong place." He looked at the thing turning slowly on nothing. "It saves us the arguing. It doesn't save us the risk. I'd like to be very clear with myself about which of those two I've just been handed, because I can already feel myself deciding it was both."

He came out clumsily, which he worked out afterward was not the same thing as coming out hurt.

He had known there would be a cost and had not warned Bram, having had no opportunity to warn Bram about anything. The cost of the place came out of whoever opened it, and he had opened it for two, and when he let go the forge came back all at once and he put a hand on the bench and stood there taking stock of himself like a man patting his own pockets on a strange road.

Nothing was missing. That was the part he had not expected. He was tired in the ordinary way of a man who had been on his feet since before light, and underneath the ordinary tiredness was something he had gone twenty-two years without once having cause to notice: there was less of him than there had been at midday. Not less strength. Less of the thing nobody had ever taught him to spend. It was like reaching into a bucket he had never bothered to look into and finding the water further down than he had left it.

He put a number on it because he could not help putting numbers on things. A tenth. Call it a tenth of whatever there was. He distrusted the figure the same second he had it, since he had nothing whatever to measure the bucket against and had never in his life met a man who could tell him what full was supposed to feel like.

Bram staggered, swore, caught himself on the anvil, and was fine. Entirely fine. Fine in a way that Emeric registered immediately, because it was information. Whatever the room took, it took from the man holding the door and nothing at all from the man who walked through it.

Ferra was standing in the doorway with a lamp and her book, and her face was the wrong colour.

"How long," Emeric said.

"You've been like that for a quarter of an hour."

"A quarter of an hour."

"I came for the payroll marks at full dark. You were both standing up with your eyes open and nobody in them." Her voice was extremely level, which he had learned to read as the opposite of what it sounded like. "I said your name eleven times. I counted, because I wanted to be able to tell whoever came next exactly what I'd tried. I was about four minutes off sending Rylan for Imani and telling her the two of you had taken a stroke standing up in a cold forge."

"It felt like a day and a half," Bram said, to nobody.

Ferra turned her head and looked at him, and then back at Emeric, and Emeric watched her set the lamp down with great care and understood that whatever came next was going to be entirely deserved.

"Ask me," she said.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever the two of you have just done. You're about to explain it to me, and then you're going to want me in it, because I've watched you work for four months and there has never once been a thing you found that you didn't immediately want three more people inside." She sat down on the crate by the door. "So explain it, and then ask, and I'll answer. In that order. Not the other one."

So he explained it, sitting on the anvil block with the cold hearth at his back, because there was no reason to stand for this and he had been standing all day.

He told her what it was, which took a while, because he did not have words for it that were not borrowed from other things. He told her his mother had put a stone in his hands when he was six and had asked him every day for a year what was inside it, and had died the year after, and that he had assumed for sixteen years that she had been a sentimental woman with a sick child's keepsake, and that he had been wrong about that in the spring and had not yet finished being wrong about it. He told her what it had just done to Bram, and that he had not asked, and that he could not have asked, and that those were two different statements and he was aware only one of them was an excuse.

He told her the cost came out of the one who opened it, and what he thought that cost had been tonight, and that a tenth was a guess wearing a fraction's clothes. He told her he expected it to get cheaper, because everything got cheaper with practice, and that he had no evidence for that beyond the general behaviour of every skill he had ever learned, which he was aware was not evidence. He told her about the wall, the geometry with no physics in it, and that he was frightened of that wall specifically, because a place that showed you a shape and confirmed nothing was a place you could be very confident and very wrong in at the same time.

Then he asked her.

"What does it cost me?" she said.

"Nothing, as far as I can tell. It costs me. You'd be carried."

"What does it cost me that you don't know about?"

He made himself take the question seriously for a full ten seconds before answering it, because she deserved that and because the honest answer was worth more than a reassuring one.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the whole of it. I've been doing this two months and I've never done it with anyone, and Bram was in there for a quarter of an hour and he's standing up talking about nozzles, and that is one man and one quarter of an hour and it proves almost nothing. If there's a cost to being carried, I wouldn't find it tonight and neither would you."

Ferra thought about it. She thought about it for long enough that Bram started to say something and she held up one finger without looking at him and he stopped.

"Yes," she said.

Inside the hour, she was better at it than either of them.

He had not expected that and afterward could not think why he had not. The place answered to how exactly you held a thing. He held things approximately and then corrected, over and over, sanding a shape down toward what he meant. Bram held things by feel, the way he held heat, and got there by a route neither of the others could follow. Ferra held a number.

She stood in front of the furnace they had built, and she said, "How wide is the throat," and Emeric said a bit under three feet, and she said, "Which is it," and he said he did not know, and she said, "Then make it thirty-two inches," and it was thirty-two inches, and stayed thirty-two inches, and every part of the shaft around it moved to accommodate a throat that was exactly thirty-two inches wide.

Then she walked the whole thing, out loud, in numbers, and Emeric followed her with a growing sensation he identified, with some difficulty, as being left behind.

"You're going to want these written down," she said, "and I can't write in here."

"No."

"Then I'll hold them and you'll get us out, and I'll write for two hours, and we do this again tomorrow with the book open in front of me before we start." She turned round. "This is the most useful thing anyone has ever shown me. I'd like it noted that I said so before I say the rest of it."

"Say the rest of it."

"Tomorrow," she said. "You've been awake since the day before yesterday and I'm not having that argument with a man who'll agree to anything to get to bed."

They came out. He looked for the bucket on the way and found it lower again, and the second figure came as easily and as uselessly as the first: a fifth, all told, for a smith and a bookkeeper and most of a working furnace. He wrote that in his head under the heading he kept for things he would want the date of.

He meant to write it down that night and did not, for the ordinary reason, which was that he sat on the edge of the bed to get a boot off and lost the rest of the evening to it.

At the forge door, with the lamp guttering and the drawings still spread on the bench in the wrong shape, Bram stopped him with a hand on his arm. The same arm, Emeric noticed, and did not say.

"Forty-one years," Bram said. "Forty-one years I've beaten slag out of iron with a hammer because that's what iron is." His voice was not entirely steady and he made no attempt to fix it. "I stood next to a thing tonight that pours. I put my hand on it. Whatever that was, and whatever the priest would want to call it, thank you."

"You're welcome," Emeric said.

Ferra was two paces behind him with the lamp, and she did not say anything at all, and Emeric did not look round, and the three of them walked up the hill.
