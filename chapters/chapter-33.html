Chapter 33 - The Tradesman

The spring caravan came down the south road in the second week of March, as it had every spring that anyone in Kaldo could remember, and stopped, as it always had, because Kaldo was on the way to the sea and the sea was on the way to everywhere else.

Seven traders, this year. Their carts filled the space inside the South Gate and half the market, and their men filled the inn, and for three days the town smelled of horses and strangers. They brought what caravans brought: Wayford cloth and Milford leather, pans and knives and needles, salt fish from the coast in barrels, a cask of something the inn bought the whole of before noon on the first day, and news, which nobody paid for and everybody took.

Emeric did not go down.

A prince at a trader's table spoiled the bargain. Every man at it would start pricing the prince instead of the goods, and pricing him high, and Kaldo would pay for the difference for years. So he stayed in the keep for three days, and did the barony's business, and read petitions about boundaries, and sent Ferra down to the market with Rylan and full authority to agree to anything she liked, in writing, in his name.

She had her questions ready. She had had them ready since February.



He went down to her table each evening after dark, with Rylan, to hear how it had gone.

There were three questions, and none of them looked like a test. The first was a set of terms for carrying Kaldo's soap, offered across the table, that were quietly too generous to the trader: a little too much margin, a little too long to pay. The second was a sheet of the same terms written out with an error in the sum three lines down, an error in Kaldo's favor, not the trader's. The third was a question asked last, over the cups, almost by the way: what would he charge to carry a load for the barony without being told what was in it?

"Most of them took the terms," Ferra said on the first evening. "Smiled, and took them, and asked when they could start."

"That's not a crime."

"No. It's a man who'll take a little too much from you every time you're not looking, and smile while he does it." She turned the page. "Two of them found the error. Overnight. They came back in the morning and said so." She had them in a column of their own, separate from the ones who had not found it, and separate again from the ones who had found it at the table. "Two of them told me what they'd charge to carry a sealed load. One of them asked whether it was a body."

"What did you say?"

"I closed my book," said Ferra.

Rylan, at the door, had to go out into the lane.

On the second evening she had one name left on the sheet that had passed every question, and she put her finger on it.

"Saban."

He had read the generous terms twice and pushed them back across the table and told her she had given him too much, and named what he would take instead, which was less. He had found the error at the table, on the first reading, and corrected it out loud, and seemed mildly offended that it had been there. And when she asked him over the cups what he would charge to carry a sealed load, he had put his cup down.

"He said he wouldn't do it at any price," Ferra said. "Then he said why. Once is a favor. The second time it isn't a favor. It's a record."

Emeric turned that over.

"There's something else," Ferra said. "He's in the tithe roll."

"Kaldo's?"

"Born here. A family on the north well's own street. They're on the roll until the war years, and then they aren't, and he isn't." She closed the book. "He hasn't said so. Nobody in the market seems to know him."

Emeric could see how it would go. You sat down with a man across a table and let it slip, kindly, that you knew where he had come from, and that you were glad to see him home, and the bargain got warmer by a tenth. And then one day the man found out how you had known, and every warm thing that had ever passed across that table went into a different column, for good.

"Don't use it," he said.

"I wasn't going to."



Saban came up to Ferra's table on the third evening, because Ferra would not do it at the keep, and Emeric came down the hill to meet him there, because he wanted to meet the man he was trusting with his name.

He was in his early forties, neat and weathered, with a merchant's coat that had been good once and been mended well since, and he looked at Emeric's eyes when he came in, as everyone did, and then looked at the rest of him, which not everyone did. He had four carts and a spare, he said, and a round that ran from Wayford to Milford and down the river and along the coast, and further than that in places that were not on any map the crown had drawn, and he had built every mile of it himself, one town at a time, by being the man who came back.

The offer was simple. The arch bar was Kaldo's, and Kaldo would make it, and Saban would have it alone. Nobody else would carry it anywhere. He could sell it in any town on his round at any price he liked and keep whatever margin he could get, and what came back to Kaldo would not come back as coin but as goods, off a list Ferra would give him each time: ore, salt, timber, and tallow for the yard, which was the one thing Kaldo could not make enough of.

"Tallow," Saban said. "There's a man in Milford. Paxton's slaughter yards. He sells his fat to a chandler in Wayford for less than it's worth, because the chandler's his brother-in-law and nobody's ever offered him more." He wrote a name on the corner of his sheet. "I'll offer him more."

Then Ferra showed him the books.

Not a summary. The books: the soap yard's weeks from June to March, the ash coming in against the bars going out, what every bar had cost by every input, the week the yard had first paid for itself, the buyers in Kaldo sorted by what they bought and when, and a line she had drawn by hand across a sheet of its own, showing the bars sold rising month by month from the first free two hundred to the queue at Sela's counter.

Saban went through it without speaking, page after page.

"I've never seen a business look at itself," he said at last.

They spent forty minutes after that on what the summer would do to demand, Ferra and Saban, while Emeric listened and was not asked anything. Saban knew what soap did in a hot summer in a river town, and Ferra knew what Kaldo had done last summer to the bar, and between them they arrived at a figure for June that neither of them had had when they started, and both of them wrote it down.

When they were done, Saban sat back and put his pen down and looked at Ferra with frank appreciation.

"I'd hire you tomorrow," he said. "Name your wage. You'd see more of the world in a year on my round than in ten at this table, and I'd make my money back on you in a season."

"You can't have her," Emeric said.

It was out before he had heard it coming. He heard it afterward, in the quiet. He felt his face go hot, all the way up, the way it had not done since he was a boy.

Saban studied his sheet with great attention.

Ferra finished the line she was writing, and blotted it, and turned the page. She did not go red. She did not look at either of them.

Saban signed at the bottom of the agreement, and Ferra signed for the barony, and Emeric signed under her, and nobody mentioned it again while Saban was in the room.

When he had gone, Emeric started to say something about it. He had got about halfway through the first sentence when Ferra said, "June," without looking up, and turned back to the page with the summer's figure on it, and he understood that he had been allowed exactly that far and no further.



Saban stayed a week at his own expense, and walked the town.

He went everywhere: the tap at the top of the lower ward, the soap yard, Imani's ward, where he was let as far as the step, the old works across the bridge, the mill, where Harl let him look at the wheel and then went back to work. He asked people what they paid for things and what they earned and how long they had been earning it. He watched the bread go in at the exchange door, and on the third morning he went in after it and sat down at the back of the room on a bench meant for a child, and listened to Sela take the youngest ones through their letters.

He sat there most of an hour. Then Sela came down the room to him.

"You're not a child," she said. "And you're not paying."

Saban got up, and thanked her for the lesson, and went out. Emeric, who was across the lane, saw his face as he came out, and it was the face of a man who had been thrown out of better rooms and had not enjoyed it as much.

He took a lease on a warehouse by the dead port, at the foot of the east bridge, with its doors facing the exchange across the way. It had been empty since the war. He had his own men sweep it out the same afternoon.

On the last evening of his week Emeric saw him at the north well.

He was standing a little apart from the women at the fire, with his hands in his coat, looking at a house a few doors up the street: a narrow house with its shutters closed, the one house on that street that had never once sent anybody to the well's rota all through the boiling, because there had been nobody in it to send. He stood there until the light went. He did not go up to the door.

Emeric asked Ferra about it that night.

"It's his to tell," Ferra said. "Not mine."



He came up to the keep on the morning before he left, to take his leave properly, and stood in Hale's office with his hat in his hand and said what he had seen, because Emeric asked him.

"You pay weekly," Saban said. "Every man I asked. The yard, the kiln, the diggers. The same day every week." He turned the hat round once. "Nobody south of the capital pays weekly. It means the money's real. They'll forgive you a good deal for that."

"What else?"

"They buy things now. A cup here, two coppers there." Saban looked out of the window, down at the town. "I came through last March with this same caravan. I sold three pans and a bolt of cloth in two days, and I'd have sold the pans cheaper to be shot of them. This week I could have sold the carts." He paused. "And they talk about you like weather. Something that happens to them. They haven't decided yet whether you're a season."

"What would decide them?"

"Staying," said Saban, and put his hat on.

The first load went out on the last morning of the week, on Saban's lead wagon, under oiled canvas: crates of the arch, packed in straw, bound for Wayford and whatever lay beyond Wayford that Saban had not told anybody about.

Emeric did not go up on the wall this time. He stood in the gate yard with the others, Abloh and Ferra and Rylan and Tess, who had come to see her arch go, and watched the wagons go out under the gate one after another, five of them, and over the moat bridge, and away.

"Last thing of mine that left Kaldo was a topsail," Abloh said, when the last one had gone. "Before the war." He went back to the yard.



That evening he sat at Ferra's table and did the quarter's figures with her: the yard, the shop, the ash loan, the school, the ward, the mill's hours, the iron, the copper, the guards, the pouch.

He gave her his total for the quarter, which he had done in his head while she was doing hers on paper.

"Out by four," she said, without looking up, and corrected it.

He checked. She was right. He had dropped a carry in the copper. Nothing else happened that evening.



The first Grayrock barge of the season came down the river a week later, low in the water with ore, and tied up at the old moorings below the dock quarter, among the empty ones.

Ferra was on the bank with her book to weigh it in, and Emeric was with her, because he had nothing more useful to do on a fine morning than watch iron come into Kaldo.

The bargeman handed down the bill of lading, and with it a letter, sealed, which he gave to Ferra because she was the one with the book.

She broke the seal and read it standing on the bank, and read it again.

The price of ore was going up. By a fifth, from this barge on. The letter was very civil about it. It regretted the necessity. The good ore near the surface was worked out, and Grayrock was obliged to go after deeper workings, at greater cost, and must ask its customers to bear their share.

Ferra folded the letter and went down the plank onto the barge, and picked up a piece of ore from the top of the load, and turned it in her hand, and put it down, and picked up another from lower in the hold, and weighed the two against each other in her palms.

"Same ore," she said, when she came back up. "Same color. Same weight in the hand." She looked at the bargeman, who was looking at the river. "They haven't got anything from deeper. They've got the same ore they had in the autumn, and a reason."

Then she said the new price aloud, the number itself, flatly, so that he would hear it said and not only read it. Then she said what it did to the furnace, and to the plows in the spring, and to the wire.

Emeric was not looking at her. He was looking up the river.

It came down out of the north, wide and brown and fast with the thaw, past the dock quarter and the dead moorings and the mill and the old works and on round the bend toward the salt runs and the sea. Everything on it came down. Ore came down, and copper came down, and the sweepings in the copper came down. Nothing went back up. Nothing had gone back up for years.

Somewhere at the top of that river, a day away, the people who sent everything down had just looked at what was being built at the bottom of it.

He was still looking upriver when she stopped.
