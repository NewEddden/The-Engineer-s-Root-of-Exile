Chapter 33 - What Makes a Knight

The first thing he did was ask how they trained, and the answer was not stupid.

He had half expected it to be. It was not. Repetition, drill and sparring, six days a week, run by men who had been doing it since they were nineteen and who had a clear idea of what a soldier ought to look like at the end of it. It produced reasonable men slowly and had produced them for four hundred years, and the reason Emeric could improve it had nothing to do with anyone in that yard being a fool.

What was missing was not effort. There was more effort in that yard than in any other building in the barony. What was missing was that nobody progressed anything on purpose.

They knew that heavy work makes a man strong. Everyone knows that. What they did not do was make the work heavier on a schedule, or write down what the work had been, or lift the specific thing that the specific job required instead of just carrying general weight around in a general way. He gave them a load, a record and a ladder. Three weeks in, a man could see his own line on a slate and that was worth more than the shouting had ever been.

Endurance he split in half, and had to explain twice. Long slow work and short hard work do different things to a body. The garrison had only ever done the middle, which is the one thing that does neither well, and which happens to be the thing that feels the most like training while you are doing it.

Recovery was the largest change and the one that nearly started a fight.

"Rest is training," he said, and a man at the back made a noise.

"Say it out loud, Jarrow."

"I said, my lord, that my old sergeant would have had a word for that."

"Your old sergeant would have had a word for it. Here is mine. Every man standing in this yard has watched somebody get worse. Not injured. Worse. Drilled every day for a month and slower at the end of it than at the start, eating the same, sleeping the same, working harder, and going backward. Every one of you has seen that."

Nobody said anything.

"None of you has ever had a name for it. That is all I am giving you. A name, and a day off, and you will be better in eight weeks and I will show you the numbers."

Specificity was the last and the easiest. Crossbow crews trained the crossbow. Wall crews trained the wall. He stopped making forty men do six things badly so that a roster could look tidy, and the immediate result was that the crossbow crews got dramatically better and the men who had been quietly awful at everything discovered they were quite good at one thing, which changed how they walked.

The food was the least glamorous part of the whole year and it returned more than everything else combined.

Nobody in Randillia had ever thought about eating as a quantity. There was enough and there was not enough, and those were the only two states available, and a man who had enough did not ask further questions about it. The garrison was fed. The garrison had been fed for two years. The garrison was also, Emeric worked out over a fortnight of watching what actually went onto plates, running at roughly two thirds of what men doing that work needed, and had been for so long that they had stopped experiencing it as hunger and had started experiencing it as being forty.

He standardised it and Ferra hated it for eleven days and then became its fiercest advocate, for reasons that were entirely hers.

Protein at every meal, which was possible now and had not been two years ago: cattle out of Milford, boats on both branches, a farm in its second good year. Bread and roots in front of the hard days. Fat, because a man cannot work on lean meat and will eat himself instead. And volume, simply volume, more of everything, every day, until the complaint stopped being that they were tired and started being that the kitchen was a lot of work.

The Works crews got the same standard inside two months, and that was not charity and Emeric did not pretend it was. A fed crew laid more brick. Ferra could prove it on a sheet, in brick, against wages, and once she could prove it on a sheet the argument was over, because that was the only argument that had ever worked on her and he had known it when he set it up.

He took every man's measurements, and they found it strange, and several of them found it insulting.

Height. Weight. Reach. What he could lift and how many times. How far he could carry a hundredweight and how long it took him. How fast he covered a measured distance in harness, and then the same distance without it, and the difference between those two numbers, which turned out to be the single most useful thing on the whole sheet and which nobody in the kingdom had ever written down.

Nobody in Randillia measured men. You looked at a man and you knew. That was the system and it had the advantage of being free and the disadvantage of being wrong about half the time.

He did it anyway, and eight weeks later he did it again, and then he pinned both sheets on the wall of the yard where everybody could see them, with every man's name on his own line.

That ended the argument about the training permanently, and it did not end it the way he had expected.

He had expected the strong men to be pleased. What actually happened was that the two worst men in the yard, a boy called Enno and a thick slow man named Stott who had been the joke of the garrison since before Emeric arrived, were not the two worst men any more, and everybody could see the line, and the line was not an opinion.

Stott stood in front of that wall for a long time on the morning it went up. Emeric watched him from the other side of the yard and did not go over, because there was nothing to say that would not have made it smaller.

It was a week before Emeric understood that some of that long time had gone on finding the line at all.

Stott could read a figure. Ferra had put figures into him in the parish hall the first winter, along with three hundred other people, and they had held. He could not read the name written in front of them. So he had stood at the wall and counted down from the top, and lost his place, and counted again, because he knew roughly how far down the roll he came and had no other way in.

Emeric asked Ward, who knew without having to think, the way he knew which man favoured a knee. Nearly all of the forty could read a figure now. Nine could read a name. The rest had counted down the wall, or asked the man beside them, or waited for somebody to read it out, and not one had mentioned it, because in Randillia a soldier who could not read was the ordinary kind.

Of the four he did not need to ask. Ward read. Declan read well enough to check a roster and wrote like a man putting up a fence. Osric could write his own name and read a figure, and had stopped there, contentedly, some years ago. Rylan could not read at all and had never once pretended otherwise.

He could have made it an order, and he thought about that for most of an evening. An order would fill the parish hall for about a month. Forty men would sit in it because they had been told to, and learn what a man learns in a room he has been sent to, and the first week nobody checked, the room would empty and stay empty. He had watched that happen to a great many sensible instructions in the other life, and a fair number of them had been his.

So he changed what the yard rewarded instead.

The crews needed leads. That had been true since the crossbow crews started training only the crossbow, and he had been putting it off, because a lead is a promotion and every promotion in a garrison is a small war. He stopped putting it off, and at muster he told the yard what a lead would be for before he told anyone who.

"A lead keeps his crew," he said. "Not only on the day. Every day. How many bolts his crew drew and how many came back. Who is sick, and since when. Who is owed for an injury. Whose name goes in the book if one of you goes in the ground, because the barony pays for that now, and it pays for what is written down. Standing in a line is half the job. I am not choosing on half."

Jarrow wanted to know, from the back, whether that meant the best man on a crossbow could lose a lead to a worse one who could spell.

"It means the best man on a crossbow who can also keep a count will beat them both," Emeric said. "And it goes for every post in this garrison from here on. This barony will not be forty-four men forever. When it needs captains, I will not be choosing them only on what they did in a field. I will be choosing them on whether I can give a man a hundred soldiers and a ledger and get both back in the condition I gave them."

Then he told them what was on offer, and took care that it sounded like an offer. Doran had given him the parish hall two evenings a week, before Emeric had finished the sentence asking for it. Doran would take letters. Ferra would take figures for anyone who had missed them the first winter, and had already told Emeric precisely which of her evenings it was costing her. The hour came off the end of drill, so a man who went to the hall left the yard early. Nobody had to go. Nobody would be asked why he had not.

Eleven came the first week, most of them men who already half had it and wanted the other half. Two of them were Enno and Stott.

Doran did not bring a book. He brought the garrison roll, copied out large on slates, and the first word each man learned was his own name, and the second was the name of the man beside him.

None of the four came, and Emeric had not expected them to.

Osric raised it at the end of a day, in the yard, in the voice he used for everything, which was the voice of a man who has found something very funny and would welcome help with it.

"A knight," he said, "has a Declan. That is what Declans are for."

"I am not reading your letters again," said Declan.

"You read the last one beautifully."

"It was from your mother. I have not recovered."

Emeric let it run, because it was the first time any of the four had been willing to talk about it at all, and then said what he had walked over to say, which was not an order either.

"You are doing yourself a disservice, Osric."

"Most days, my lord."

"Every man you will ever have under you is a number before he is anything else. How many. How fed. How armed. How hurt. How much he is owed. You will have to order those men about, and an order you cannot write down changes a little every time it is passed along, and a count you cannot check is a count somebody else is keeping for you."

He nodded toward the keep, where the books were.

"The roll this garrison was paid off carried sixteen men who did not exist. For years. Nearly everyone in this yard was paid off that roll, and hardly one of them could have read it."

Osric thought about that for longer than he usually thought about anything, and then made it a joke, because that was what Osric did with things once he had thought about them.

"The last paper I put my name to was the recruiting sergeant's," he said. "He read it to me. It said two years."

"It said six," said Declan.

"It said six," Osric agreed. "I keep meaning to write to him."

Ward, who had been at the gate the whole time, said one thing without turning round.

"Everything this garrison knows about itself is in my head. It should not be."

Osric did not come to the hall that week, or the week after. Rylan, who had heard all of it from the rack with his back to them, did not come either and said nothing, which was the whole of what Rylan said to Emeric that spring. Ward did not need to come and did not, and nobody asked him where he had learned. Declan went once and sat at the back, and went again the week after with a letter folded in his coat that he did not show to anybody.

His own programme he had been running since the winter before last, and it was the one part of his life he had never discussed with a living person.

Three drills. All three deliberately unimpressive.

Water pulled thin across a flame. A blade of moving air. A slab of stone raised a hand's breadth off the ground and dropped.

He had chosen them backward, from the outcome, rather than forward from a spellbook, principally because he did not have a spellbook and had never seen one. What he had was three things he understood without having to be taught them: heat leaving a surface, pressure in a moving fluid, and force applied to a mass. Everything he did was one of those three wearing a different hat.

Nothing in any of the three would have troubled a court mage for a second. A court mage would have watched all three and been politely bored.

What would have troubled a court mage was how little of it went anywhere except where he sent it, and the reason for that was not talent and not blood. It was that he had run each of them several thousand times, in a room where a repetition cost him nothing but his own attention, and where he could do a thing wrong four hundred times in an afternoon and nobody outside would have noticed him blink.

He did not cast in front of people.

He had no reason to, and no wish whatsoever to be interesting, and by the end of that year the habit had gone so deep that he had to be careful about it in the other direction, because a man who never once does the thing everybody knows he can do is itself a fact, and facts get collected.

All of this worked on the body. None of it touched the mana, and he did not know why, and it irritated him for most of the summer.

He could make a man carry more, further, faster, and recover quicker. He could make a crossbow crew reload in two thirds the time. He could not move the number that mattered by a hair, and when he went looking for the reason he found that nobody had ever asked the question, which by now he recognised as the sound of something being about to go badly.

So he asked it directly, one evening, of the four men in the kingdom he had access to who knew.

"What actually makes a magic knight?"

He expected an answer about aptitude, or blood, or the Light.

Osric went first, because Osric always went first.

He told it as a comedy and it was a good one. Third son of a man with nothing to divide three ways, a recruiting sergeant in a market town who had lied to him about roughly everything, six years of being adequate at a job he had fallen into sideways, and then a decade of being good at it entirely by accident. He did the sergeant's voice. He did his own mother's voice, which was worse.

There was a real thing sitting underneath it that he did not say out loud and did not intend to, and Declan was the only one at the fire who caught it, and Declan looked into his cup and let it go past, which told Emeric something about both of them.

Declan's was shorter and flatter and he did not perform any of it. A house guard at sixteen. Noticed at nineteen, not for being quick or strong, but for being the one who checked things: doors, rosters, the back of the stable, the name on a delivery. A career, from there, of being the man who checks.

"It is not a noble occupation," he said. "I would not want anyone at this fire telling me it was. It is a useful one. I have never had a day where I could not say what the day was for."

"That is more than most men get," Emeric said.

"It is. I am aware."

Ward was asked and did not answer.

Not rudely. He answered a different question, briefly, something about the standard of the capital's training yard fifteen years ago compared with now, four sentences of it, all of them true and none of them about him. Then he stopped, and let the silence stand, and nobody pushed, because nobody ever pushed Ward.

Emeric sat with his back against the wall of the yard and worked out something he did not enjoy working out.

Fifteen years. In fifteen years he had never once had a sentence out of this man that was not operational. Everything Ward had ever told him had been about Emeric, or about a threat, or about a task. Where to stand. What was wrong with a room. Who had come in the gate. I am your guard. I keep you breathing. I was not given the rest of you to keep.

Nothing about Ward. Not one fact. Not where he was from, not whether he had anybody, not a single thing that would appear in a letter.

It was exactly the shape of his father, and Emeric noticed the rhyme sitting there in front of him and did not do anything about it, and let the subject drop, and thought about it on and off for a week and then stopped.

Rylan's account was the long one.

It landed harder than it should have, partly because of what was in it and partly because it was the first long thing he had said to Emeric in four months. He had been civil since the spring. Civil was the whole of it. He had answered what he was asked and he had done his work and he had not once come to the fire.

He came to the fire that night, and he sat down, and he talked.

Conscripted at nineteen, ten years back, into a unit that got used. It got used, he said, because it was the kind of unit that got used, and if Emeric wanted a longer answer than that there was not one. He came out of it. That was his entire account of two years of war and it was deliberately thin, and Emeric had enough sense not to open it, because a man who has seen a great deal does not narrate it at a training yard with a cup in his hand.

What happened next was that his captain noticed he was still alive.

"That was the qualification," Rylan said. "I want to be clear, because you are going to want to write something down. There was no test. Nobody weighed anything. He looked down a list of men who had been in it at the start and crossed off the ones who were not there any more, and there were eleven names left, and he put four of them forward."

To be made a magic knight. The greatest honour available to a man born where he was born. There is no other door. You cannot buy it, you cannot marry into it, and the only way an ordinary soldier walks out of an ordinary life is that one.

"I was proud of it," Rylan said, "for about a week."

Then he told them about the rite, and he told it plainly, which is what did it.

They were taken into a room. Stone, no windows, a drain in the floor that he did not understand the purpose of until later. They were stripped to their underclothes. There were nineteen of them that day and he had spoken to perhaps four.

A mage of the tower forced pure mana into their bodies in waves. That was the whole method. There was no preparation and no gradual part. The first wave was the first wave.

Men died.

He did not know the real figure and said so twice. Nobody publishes one. His impression, from what he saw in that room and what he was told afterward by men who had been through it in other years, was that something like one in ten came out the other side of it.

"The ones who failed did not fail quietly," he said. "I would rather not do the detail. Their bodies gave out under the pressure. It was not quick, and there was nothing in it that anybody could have called dignified, and it took as long as it took and we were all in the room for the whole of it."

The fire did something and nobody attended to it.

"And the cardinal was there," Rylan said, "because the cardinal is always there."

He had come in before it started and he had stayed for all of it, and at the end he had walked the room. Not quickly. He had stopped over each of the dead in turn, and named them, by name, out loud, in front of the men still standing. And what he named them was failures under the Light.

Not the fallen. Not the lost. Not men who had been asked for something that could not be given. Judged, and found wanting, and the wanting named first so that what followed could be made to sound like grace.

Emeric did not move at all while that was being said, and could not, afterward, entirely account for why he did not ask which cardinal.

He had the question in his mouth. He did not ask it. He never did.

"I was never devout," Rylan said. "So do not make it a loss of faith. It is not that interesting."

"What is it, then."

He thought about it for a while, and what came out took four sentences, and Emeric could not have improved a word of it in either of his lives.

"What made me special is that I lived. That is the whole of it. Some men's bodies took it and some men's bodies did not, and there was no pattern in it that anyone in that room could name, and I have asked and nobody anywhere has ever been able to name one. And a man in vestments stood over a randomness and told it it was a verdict."

He drank.

"That is not god. That is randomness, dressed by men, and used twice. Once to make the ones who lived grateful. Once to make the ones who died deserve it."

Emeric heard it correctly, and then he heard the other thing.

What Rylan had just described, with none of the vocabulary and no need for any of it, was the oldest control mechanism in the human catalogue. Survivorship dressed as selection. A cost reframed as an honour. The dead made responsible for their own dying so that the institution is never once required to answer a question about its method.

He had seen it in another world in a dozen costumes. A trial with an enrolment criterion nobody would publish. A programme whose failures were described as non-responders. A waiting room that smelled of new carpet, and a consent form eleven questions long, and a man in a good suit explaining that the ones it did not work for had probably been too far along to begin with.

He said none of that out loud. There was no version of it that could be said.

He wrote, that night, that Rylan, who could not read and had never in his life encountered an argument in a book, had taken the entire apparatus apart from the inside in four sentences.

He was careful about the note. He wrote it in the back of a book that had nothing else in it, and he was aware while writing that it was the most dangerous sentence he had put on paper since he arrived in this kingdom, and that its danger had nothing to do with the rite and everything to do with the man in vestments.

The marks he had already seen and had not asked about.

They had come up during the physical measurements, weeks earlier, when all four of them had been stripped to the waist in the yard with a rope and a slate, and he had seen them and made the deliberate decision to say nothing and take the number.

He asked now.

Every one of the four carried them. Bands and rings under the skin, following the vein lines up the inside of the forearms and across the chest, silvered and slightly raised, like something that had been stretched and had healed stretched. Osric had them worst. Declan had them on one side only and had no explanation for that either.

They were, all four agreed, the point.

In the capital a man with the marks is a man who came out. You do not have to say anything. You push your sleeves up and the room reorganises itself. Osric said he had eaten free in three cities on them.

Emeric looked at them the way he looked at a cracked weld, which is to say with no reverence whatsoever, and what he saw was tissue that had been made to grow faster than tissue grows.

Not a badge. A scar left by the method.

He said nothing at all in that yard.

He would not do it, and the reasons arrived in the right order, and he was glad about the order afterward when he went back over it.

The first was that it was vile and he was not going to do it. That was the whole of the first reason and it was settled before any arithmetic happened. There was no version of a night where he put forty men he knew in a stone room with a drain in the floor.

The second was that the numbers did not work anyway.

He ran them, because he is who he is, and he was unhappy with himself for running them and ran them to the end regardless. Forty men. One in ten. He finishes with four Enhanced and thirty-six funerals in a barony of eighteen hundred people where he had personally stood in the same room as most of their mothers. There is no barony on the other side of that. There is a hole in the ground with a town next to it.

The third was the one that made him laugh out loud, alone, which was not a good laugh. Even if it worked, even if the method were clean and the survival were universal, he could not equip them. Enhanced harness needs high-grade crystal, and he had exactly four sets' worth in the entire barony, and they were already on four men, and there was no second source anywhere he could reach.

The most obscene process in the kingdom, and the thing that actually stopped it being scaled was procurement.

The hypothesis he wrote late, alone, in the driest language he had.

If the marks are damage and not quality, then the deaths are not selection. They are a rate of application.

Something forced fast leaves marks. Something grown slowly does not. That was true of every tissue he had ever known anything about in either life, true of bone and true of muscle and true, for that matter, of steel and of concrete and of a clay pipe with too much pressure behind it at four in the morning. Nothing in the world gets stronger by being surprised.

Which would mean the rite is not a trial that reveals worth. It is a method with a bad parameter. One number, set wrong, four hundred years ago, by somebody who was not looking for a number, and then run identically for generations by men who never looked for it either, because from the very first day they had believed they were looking at a verdict rather than at a result.

If the waves came slower, the bodies might take them. If they came slower still, the marks might not form at all.

He wrote it as a question, with three things he would need to know before it was anything better than a guess, and then he wrote the fourth line, which was the wall.

He could not test it.

Not on a man. Not once. Not on a volunteer, not on a condemned man, not on somebody who asked him twice and meant it. There is no version of that experiment that he was willing to be the man who ran, and the whole value of the hypothesis was that it required exactly that experiment and nothing else would do.

So he had a theory he believed, no way to advance it by one inch, and nobody in the kingdom he could safely say it to.

He put the book away and sat there for a while.

The bird went into the window three days later.

It hit the glass in the keep's south gallery hard enough to be heard two rooms away, and when he got there it was on the stone with one wing wrong, alive, and completely out of its mind with terror.

He was going to leave it. That was the sensible thing and he had a reason ready, which was that a bird with a broken wing is a bird that is already dead and the only question is how long. He got as far as the doorway.

Then he came back and put a hand on it.

A living thing is nothing at all like a stone in there. That was the first thing and it arrived immediately and it was not a small thing. A stone is quiet. A stone is a shelf. This was not a shelf. It was moving, all of it, all the time, and it did not hold still to be looked at, and he understood inside two seconds that everything he knew about working with stones was going to be of very limited use.

But he could find the distress. It was not hidden and it was not subtle. It was the loudest thing in there by a wide margin, and he took the edge off it, carefully, the way you ease a valve rather than the way you shut one.

The bird stopped thrashing.

It did not become calm. It became a bird that was not currently screaming, and it lay in his hand and breathed, and he splinted the wing with two slivers of kindling and a strip of linen, the way an engineer splints anything, which is to say with more thought about the load path than about the bird.

Two things came out of that and neither of them was triumphant, and he made himself write both.

The first was that he had healed nothing. Not one thing. He had dulled a sensation, and then he had done ordinary mechanical work on a bone with a stick and a rag. Those are two different acts and only one of them was magic and the one that was magic had not improved the animal's body in any respect whatsoever. The wing would knit because wings knit. All he had done was stop the patient fighting him, which any physician on Earth would have recognised immediately as the oldest trick in the trade and would not have called healing.

The second was that a bird is not a man. He wrote that down too, and underlined it, because he could already feel how badly he wanted it not to be a problem.

He was aware inside a minute that he had found the beginning of a way to work on living things. He was equally aware that the distance between a sparrow's wing and a human body was enormous, and that it was very probably not as enormous as he would like it to be, and there was a third thought behind that one which he noticed arriving and declined to finish.

He told nobody. Not Imani. Certainly not Imani.

He kept the bird in a box in his rooms for three weeks and looked after it badly, in the way of a man who has never kept an animal, and it lived, and on the morning he opened the window and it went, he was a great deal more pleased about it than he had any intention of being.

In the autumn, when he took the third set of measurements, he added a line to every man's record that had not been there before.

The burial law had made the barony responsible for its dead, and a barony that pays for a man's burial has to know whom to tell. He wanted the name written down while the man was standing in front of him, not hunted for afterward. It seemed to him the most ordinary line on the sheet.

Rylan told him it was wrong, and did it the way he had once told Emeric he was wrong about the town: on a walk, without heat, and without looking at him.

"A name is not what a man needs written down."

"What does he need."

"What he would have said."

Emeric asked how it had been done in the unit, and asked it as a real question, because he had never stood in a line that expected to die and Rylan had.

"A man who knew he was not coming back told the man beside him. What to say, and to whom."

"And if the man beside him did not come back either."

"Then it was never said." They walked a while. "Most of the time the man beside you is in the same ditch. That is what beside means."

He did not tell it as a story and Emeric did not ask for one. But on the way back he gave one more piece of it, flatly, the way he had given the rite.

The night before the room, the four men from his unit had traded. What to say, and to whom, if it went the way it went. Rylan had walked out of that room holding three. One he had carried to the man's family himself, on foot, the next year. The other two he had carried in his head for so long that by the time he could have paid somebody to write them down, he no longer trusted the words, and he would not give a dead man's mother the wrong ones in a stranger's hand.

"Nobody walked out holding mine," he said. "If it had gone the other way, my mother would have had the cardinal's word for it and nothing else."

Emeric had nothing to set beside that and did not try.

He changed the line the next day. Any man who wanted to could write what he would have said, seal it, and put a name on the outside. Nobody would be made to. Nobody would open it unless the man was dead.

Declan found the hole in it inside the hour, because that was what Declan was for.

"A letter in a man's coat goes where he goes," he said. "Into the same ditch."

"Then it does not go in his coat," said Emeric, and went across to the church to ask where it should go instead.

Doran said the vestry had a box with a lid on it, and that he had been keeping things for the dead for forty years without anybody asking him to, and saw no reason to stop now that somebody had. The box had no lock. A lock, Doran said, would only suggest he was the kind of priest who needed one.

The hall filled after that. Not all at once, and not with everybody.

A tally was one thing. A letter to a wife was another, and a good many men who had shrugged at the first found that they did not want Doran, or the man beside them, or anybody at all, writing down the second, and there was only one way round that. Osric came in the autumn, for a reason he gave as a joke: Stott had read his own name off the wall in front of the whole yard, out loud, slowly, a letter at a time, and Osric had been standing beside him, and a knight of Randillia, he said, could not be out-read by Stott. By the first frost there were twenty-six in the hall on a good night.

The leads were chosen at midwinter, on both halves, and every one of them could keep his crew's count. Jarrow, who was as good on a crossbow as anyone in the yard, was not one of them, and knew why, and still did not come, and Emeric thought rather better of him for not pretending.

Rylan came last, and never to anything Emeric had set up. He went to Doran on the nights the hall was empty, and Emeric only knew because of the sheet.

Every record got a fresh page at midwinter, and every man put his own name at the top of it or had it put there for him. Rylan's was in his own hand. It ran downhill, and one of the letters had gone on backward and been corrected, and Emeric looked at it for longer than at anything else on the sheet and said nothing to anybody, because Rylan had not done it for him.

Ward's new line stayed empty. Emeric noticed and did not ask, and nobody pushed Ward.

By the turn of the year there were thirty-one letters in the box in the vestry. Doran gave Ferra the count for the burial book, because she asked, and gave her nothing else, because she did not.

At the end of that year the barony had forty-four men under harness.

Forty in plate with nothing on it, better fed and better trained and better measured than any garrison in the region, most of whom could now say their own numbers off the wall without looking, and a good many of whom could read the name above them as well.

Four in black steel over the capital's crystals, carrying the marks of a method he had decided he understood and would never use.

And one of those four was something else again, and there was no test for it.

His actual defensive plan he admitted to himself in a single sentence and to nobody at all, and it was not a plan so much as a bet, and he knew the difference.

Whoever was asking what sort of man he was had underestimated Kaldo, and was going to keep underestimating it, for exactly as long as Emeric could make that last.
