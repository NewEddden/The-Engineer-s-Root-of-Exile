Chapter 33 - Thirty-Seven Houses

What carried Kaldo from the last week of September to the middle of November was fish, and Emeric did not put that in a report anywhere, because it was not a policy and nobody had decided it.

The first house with nothing in it had been where Ferra said it would be, in the last week of September, and Ferra had given him the date on the tenth day and he had spent the intervening month with no money at all. What happened instead of a policy was that the town had a lake and a coast and about forty men who had grown up on both, and there had been a fortnight in October when every boat in the barony went out every morning and half the town stood in the yard behind the church gutting and salting under Imani's rules because Imani would not have it done any other way.

It was not enough and it was never going to be enough, and it moved the line by five weeks, which was exactly the five weeks it took a letter to reach a lending house and a factor to ride back down.

"I want it written who did that," Emeric said, on the sixth of November, with the salting yard's tally in front of him.

"It is written. I have had it written since the second day, because I could see you were going to want it in a fortnight and I was not going to reconstruct it out of memory." Ferra turned the page round. "Eleven boats, forty-one men, nine women running the salt, and Father Doran's yard, and about four hundred and sixty stone of fish which is now in barrels in a church cellar. I would like you to hear the next sentence properly. It is gone by February. Salt fish is a thing you eat instead of nothing, and a town cannot work a winter on it, and by the second month people stop being able to keep it down."

"So it's five weeks."

"It was five weeks and you have spent them, and I do not say that as a complaint, because what you spent them on arrived." She put the second sheet down. "Now the winter."

The grain came by sea.

Saban had it moving before the ink on the terms was a week old, because he had gone down the coast in the last week of September on his own money and had stood in three counting houses at a port eleven days south being told no, and had spent that fortnight making himself a man the fourth one recognised.

"November," he said. "That's the whole of the bad news and I'll say it once. Everybody on this coast bought their winter in September at September's price. You are buying in November, after a war, in a season where the whole plain knows Kaldo burned, and there is not a grain factor between here and the Neck who has not heard about it." He rubbed his face. "It's a third again on the September price. I got it under a third and I'll not tell you how because I'd have to say a man's name."

"And the weight."

Saban told him, and Ferra had it against her own sheet before he had finished the sentence, and the two of them did the arithmetic out loud across the table in a way that Emeric had learned not to interrupt.

"It is not the winter," Ferra said, at the end of it. "I want to say the true thing before anybody hopes. It is the winter for a barony of two thousand two hundred people at a ration, and it is not the winter for a barony of two thousand two hundred people eating."

"How far apart are those."

"About seven weeks and the whole of March."

The argument about how to give it out took two days and ran on the twelfth and the thirteenth in the long room, with the door open because Emeric had decided the door was going to be open.

He opened it with the wrong idea and knew within the hour that it was the wrong idea, and it was the idea he had been carrying since the sixth.

"By need," he said. "Imani knows this town better than any book. She walks it. She can tell me which houses have a nursing mother and which have an old man who has stopped eating so the children can, and those are the houses that get more, and nobody has to prove anything to anybody."

"No," Ferra said.

"Say why, not no."

"I will say why and I will say it in front of the door you have opened." She stood, which meant it was going to be quick and unpleasant. "Because on the fourth day a man will come to this table and say his neighbour is getting more than he is, and he will be right, and you will explain the reason, and he will not be able to check the reason. Every ration this town gets for five months will be a thing a prince decided about a household after somebody walked through it. That is not charity. That is the same arrangement Wexler had, run by a better man, and it lasts exactly as long as they believe you are a better man."

"And if the rule feeds a full house and starves a thin one."

"Then the rule is wrong and you will hear about it, out loud, from Maren, in a room, with a page in front of her." Ferra sat down. "That is the whole of what I am arguing for. Not that a rule is kinder. It is not kinder. It is that a rule can be argued with by somebody who is not you."

Maren came in on the thirteenth with the eleven households behind her again and a proposal that was three lines long and had been written by Dunnel, which Emeric could tell from the second word.

By household, with a head count, taken once, in the open, by two people who were not related to the household, and a floor under any house of four or fewer, because a house of three cannot halve a loaf into a working share and a house of nine can.

"And your twenty-six," Emeric said.

"Get it same as everybody. That's the point of it." Maren did not sit either. "I'll tell you the thing I've come to say and then I'll sit down. In September I came here early because a thing decided in March is decided by whoever's most frightened. It's November. People are frightened now. So I've written it in three lines and brought it while there's still a table to bring it to, and if you want it different, say it different now, because in five weeks whatever's on that wall is what this town's going to be quarrelling about and neither of us will be able to move it."

Halloway wanted the works ration and said so badly and was right.

"A man cutting stone at the quarry all day in December eats twice what his wife eats," he said. "That's not a favour and it's not a rank. That's arithmetic and any farmer in this room will tell you the same about a horse."

"Then say the part you're not saying."

"The part I'm not saying is that if you give a working ration you've made work into food, and then a man who cannot work is a man whose house eats less, and I've a cousin who cannot work." Halloway looked at the table. "And I'd still do it, because the granary wants building and the quarry wants cutting and there's four months of men with nothing to do and everything to worry about, and I'd rather they were tired."

What went on the wall on the fourteenth was four lines and had four authors and Emeric wrote none of them.

A base ration by head, counted once, in the open, by two counters not of the house. A floor under any household of four or fewer. A works addition, paid in grain, to anybody on the granary, the quarry, the foundry or the boats, open to any adult in the barony who presented themselves, with no household barred from sending whoever it liked. And a sick line, on Imani's signature and not on Emeric's, which came out of the same store and was entered in the same book with a name against it.

Imani changed one number and changed it in front of everybody.

"Your children's share is wrong," she said. "You have put the small ones at half. Halve it for the ones under about four and you are right, and for the ones between about four and ten you are badly wrong, and I am not going to argue the physic of it with a room, so I will say the part the room will understand. A child of seven with a cough in a cold house in February either has something behind him or he does not, and the ones who do not are the ones I bury, and I have buried nineteen years of them and I have never once buried a fat one."

Emeric changed it.

Vantic asked her question on the fifteenth, after everything else was decided, in the tone of a woman raising a matter of housekeeping.

"Who grants an exception," she said.

The room did the thing rooms did around her, which was to look at Emeric first.

"There aren't exceptions. That's the whole of what we've spent two days on."

"There will be exceptions by the eleventh of December and I would like to be wrong and I am not going to be." She had her case open. "I am not asking whether you will make one. I am asking, now, while nobody is standing in front of you, what an exception is, who may grant it, whether two names go on it, and where it is written. Because if it is not answered today it will be answered at ten at night by whoever is nearest the store, and the answer will be that a thing everybody knows is a thing nobody is responsible for."

"That's the second time you've said that sentence in this barony."

"It is the second time it has been the answer."

So they wrote it, and Emeric wrote this one himself, and it took four minutes and was the shortest thing on the wall. An exception is any issue of grain outside the four lines. It requires two names. One of them is Imani's or Corliss's. It is entered on the day it is made with the household, the weight and the reason, and it is read out at the store on the first of the month with everything else.

Corliss ran the issue.

Nobody appointed him to that either, and the second time it happened Emeric noticed it happening and understood that a barony had built a quartermaster out of a man who had spent sixteen years buying boxes and ground and digging out of a provisioning column, and had done it by giving him a line in a book with his name over it and then leaving him alone.

He set the store in the undercroft with one door, weighed everything in and everything out on a beam he checked against the standard bar every Monday, and put two counters at the table who were changed every fortnight and were never from the same end of the town.

The first issue went out on the seventeenth of November to a hundred and ninety-one households, and it took from before light until after dark, and it was not the disaster Emeric had braced for. What it was instead was long, and cold, and orderly, and quiet in a way that he found worse than shouting, because a queue that does not complain is a queue that has decided complaining is not available.

The thirty-seven were the easy part. They were Kaldo's own, they had been counted since the plague, and their names had been in a book on that hill for four hundred years.

The four hundred were the hard part.

They had come in April on Imani's letters and on his, out of Wayford and out of Milford and off two roads further than that, on a promise of eight parts in twenty and a roof, and they had worked a season for a harvest that burned, and every one of them was in a house that a Kaldo family had helped raise and none of them had a name on that hill.

Nessa's brother was one of them.

He had come in May with a wife and two children, out of a Wayford field where he kept a fifth of what he grew, on a letter his sister had written and had made herself write honestly, and he had eleven acres in the ring and a share on a page and a house with a green roof beam in it that Rylan had cut.

Emeric found out how that household was eating on the twenty-fourth, and he found out by accident, from Corliss, who mentioned it the way a quartermaster mentions a number.

"Rylan's not drawing his works addition," Corliss said. "Nine days now. He presents, he does the hours, and he'll not take the grain."

"Where's it going."

"He's not asked me for it to go anywhere. He just doesn't take it." Corliss shrugged with the arm that still did not shrug properly. "There's four in that house and two of them are his own and two of them are his sister-in-law's, and Nessa's not said a word to anybody, and I'd not have known if I didn't run a beam."

Emeric went down that evening.

He did not go to the house. He went to the lower ward and found Rylan coming off the wall, and asked him plainly, and got the answer he should have predicted and had not.

"They're on the four lines same as everyone," Rylan said. "I'm on the four lines same as everyone. The addition's mine to take or not take and I've not taken it, and there's no exception in that, and I'd thank you not to make one out of it."

"You're standing a watch on a winter ration."

"I'm standing a watch." Rylan pulled his gloves off. "My lord, I'll say this once and then I'd rather we didn't. I wrote to Nessa's brother in March and told him this was a place where a man keeps what he grows. He come in May with two babies on a cart. He's cut his own house and dug his own share of the ring and lost a harvest same as you did, and there's four hundred like him and not one of them's got a grandfather in that ground." He put the gloves in his belt. "If I take a soldier's extra bread and he watches me eat it, then everything I put in that letter was a lie about this town, and I'd sooner be hungry than be the man who proved that."

Emeric went back up the hill and did not sleep for a while.

What he did on the twenty-fifth he did at about nine at night, quickly, without writing anything down, and he did it because he had spent an hour failing to find a version of the four lines that reached the thing he had just been told and because the four lines were correct and he had helped write them.

He went to the undercroft with Corliss and had nine sacks put on a cart, and sent them to four houses in the new ring off the mill road, and one of the four was Nessa's brother's.

"That's an exception," Corliss said.

"That's an exception. Yours is the second name."

"Then it wants entering."

"Enter it in the morning. I want it read out on the first with everything else and I want my own name on the top of it." Emeric was already turning. "Don't let me forget it."

"Aye," Corliss said, and did not, particularly, and neither did he.

The first of December had eleven separate things in it and the reading at the store was one of them, and it was read by a counter who read what was in front of him.

Emeric got the list out on the last night of November and left the space empty at the top, and wrote underneath it:

A hundred and ninety-one households and nobody shouted. Ward says a quiet queue is a queue that has decided shouting gets you nothing, and that it is the single worst sound in a town, and that he has heard it twice and both times in a place that was about to stop being a town. I have been telling myself all week that it went well.

I opened with need and I was wrong inside an hour. Ferra did not say it was unkind. She said it could not be checked by anybody who is not me, which is the same sentence I have been saying to this barony since March about wells and about iron and about a stick on a wall, and I did not recognise it coming the other way until she had finished.

Four lines and four authors and none of them mine. I have written that down twice now this year and been pleased about it both times, and I would like to note that being pleased about it is not the same as it being safe, because a thing with four authors is a thing nobody will change in February when it turns out to be wrong.

Rylan has not drawn his works addition in nine days and will not, and the reason is a letter he wrote in March. I have built a barony on the idea that a man will hold a line if you feed his family. He is holding the line by not eating. Those are not the same thing and I have been counting them as the same thing all year.
