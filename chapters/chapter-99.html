Chapter 99 - Four Nights

The first of it came on the twenty-fourth, at the exchange window, from a wool buyer from the coast who had come up the river road to see about Kaldo's soap and stayed to talk about everything else.

He was a stout cheerful man with a cold, and he told it the way men told the road's news, between other things, as a thing that had happened to somebody else. Two of Milford's wool carts had been taken on the western road. A few nights back, he thought, or the week before, he had heard it at an inn. The carters had been beaten and left in the road and the wool was gone, carts and all, and the horses. Bad business. The western road had been bad since the plague. He had heard of it happening twice the winter before. Then he asked what Kaldo wanted for its soap by the hundredweight at the coast, and Ferra told him, and he coughed and said that was very steep, and bought some.

Ferra wrote it in her book when he had gone, on the page for the day, as she wrote everything that came through the window that touched the barony's trade. Milford wool carts, two, taken on the western road. Heard from the coast buyer. And the date she had heard it, which was the date of the page.

Emeric was sorry for the carters. He said so. He thought of the forest past Wayford last winter, the felled tree and the four hungry men climbing over it with a billhook and a cudgel, and thought that the western road in November was a road with hungry men on it, and that wool was easy to sell and hard to trace. He did not think anything else.



The second came two days later, with Hollis.

Hollis had a cousin who farmed on the Milford side of the border, three fields from the line, and the cousin had come over that morning with a cart for seed corn and the news. Hollis brought it up to the exchange himself, because it touched the barony's grain, and Hollis had opinions about the barony's grain that he liked to have heard.

The barn at the border had burned. Paxton's big barn, the stone one with the timber roof, on the Milford side of the boundary stone, a mile from the nearest house. Burned to its walls, one night the week before, or about then; the cousin had seen the glow from his bed and thought it was the sky. Nobody knew how. There was nobody in it. There never was, in November, except the grain.

Ferra put her pen down.

There had been two hundred sacks of the barony's wheat in that barn. Paxton had bought them in October at the posted price, after the harvest, for his own winter, because Milford grew grass and not grain and the harvest had been Kaldo's best. They had gone over the border on the barony's carts and into his barn to wait for his own carts to lift them to Milford town, and they were to be paid for when they were lifted. Ferra had drawn the terms. Until Paxton's carts came, every sack of it was the barony's.

"Was it lightning?" said Vantic.

She had come down from the east tower at Ferra's word, because it was the barony's stock and therefore the House's, and she stood in the back room in her grey coat and listened to Hollis tell it again, and asked her questions in the order she always asked them.

"In November?" said Hollis.

"A lamp, then."

"Nobody in it to carry a lamp."

Vantic considered that. Then she asked Ferra for the terms of the sale, and read them, and asked whether the grain had been lifted, and Ferra said it had not, and Vantic asked whether any of it had been paid for, and Ferra said none.

"Then it was the barony's grain," Vantic said, "and it is the barony's loss."

She took out her own book. She did not open it at the front, where the weekly figures were, but at the back, at the column she had opened in April for Wayford's toll board, where she wrote whatever the neighbours cost the barony and the date it cost it. Emeric had seen the column before and taken it for House practice. He watched her write in it now, small, in her grey hand: the barony's wheat, two hundred sacks, sold and not lifted, lost by fire in Milford's border barn, and the date it was heard of, and her initial.

The column had more lines in it than he had expected. He did not read them.

Ferra wrote it in her own book, on the page for the day. Paxton's border barn burned, with the barony's two hundred sacks. Heard from Hollis's cousin. The date of the page.

It was a bad loss. It was most of what Kaldo had sold over the border that autumn. Emeric said that Paxton would need grain still, and that they should offer him more at the same price if he wanted it, and Ferra said she had already thought of that, and Vantic said it would be a new sale and could be sold at the posted price, and went back up the hill. Nobody said how a stone barn with nobody in it caught fire in November.



The third came at dusk on the twenty-eighth, with Saban's man from the coast road.

Saban brought him up to the keep himself. Saban did not do that. He sent his men to the exchange, or to the back room, or he came alone and turned his hat in his hands and told it himself. He came up the keep stair to Emeric's door with a man behind him who was grey with the road and had not eaten, and stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands and did not turn it.

"Dunnel," Saban said.

The man told it. He told it plainly, because Saban had told him to and because he was too tired to tell it any other way. A carter going up from the coast had come on Dunnel's cart in the road a dozen miles short of the coast house, on the stretch where the road went through the scrub oak before it came down to the shore. The cart was burned. Burned to its axles, standing in the road, with the bale in the back of it gone to ash, and the horse gone. Dunnel was in the ditch beside it. He had been dead some days. His purse was gone, and his rings, and his good brown coat. The red scarf was still round his neck.

The boy was further on, a long way further on, face down in the ditch on the other side of the road. As though he had run.

Emeric did not sit down. He stood at the window where he had been standing when Saban knocked. On the table under the window, where he had put it on the fifteenth and not touched it since, was Dunnel's sister's cheese, still in its cloth.

"When?" he said.

"Some days," the man said. "The carter couldn't say. Some days. The coast house had been waiting on him. They'd thought he'd stopped at a farm."

"Who did it?"

The man looked at Saban.

"Nobody saw," Saban said. "There's nobody on that stretch to see. The coast road's had men on it this autumn. Hungry ones. Everybody on the coast says so." He looked at his hat. "He carried coin. Everybody on the western circuit knew he carried coin, because he told them. He told everybody everything."

It was true. It was the truest thing anybody could have said about Dunnel.

Emeric thought about the forest past Wayford, and the four men in the road, and how close a hungry man came to killing you for a coat. He thought about the western road, which had been bad since the plague. A factor with a fat purse and a cart, on a lonely stretch, in November, with winter coming. It needed nothing more than that. It was the oldest crime there was on a road, and the commonest, and the most likely by a long way.

He had decided what it was before the man had finished telling it, and he knew he had, and he let it stand.

Ferra came up from the exchange as soon as she heard, which was within the quarter hour, because Saban's man had been seen on the keep stair. She stood in the doorway beside Saban and heard it again from the beginning, and when it was told she did not say anything for a while.

"The bale," she said.

"Burned."

She nodded. She did not say anything about the twin that had been in the middle of it, deep among the fleece, and neither did he. She looked at the cheese on the table.

Ward was in the door behind her by then. He had come up the stair without anyone hearing him and stood behind Ferra and Saban and heard all of it, and when it was finished he looked at Emeric for a while, and said nothing at all, and went back down. That night there were two men on every gate instead of one, and a third walking pair on the wall, and nobody was told why, and nobody asked.



The fourth came on the thirtieth, from a hide seller.

He was a Milford man, a drover's son, come down with a cart of hides for Bram's leather and the soap works' fat, and he sold them at the works gate and then stood about in the yard telling the yard boys the news, because there was a good deal of it. Milford had had a bad month, he said. The western herds had been driven off, the other week, the outlying ones on the grazing toward the hills. A good many head. Nobody knew who. Cattle thieves came down out of the hills every winter, everybody knew that, though not so many as this. And the border barn had burned, they'd heard that in Kaldo, had they. And Paxton's wool carts. And poor Dunnel, the Light keep him. Milford had had a very bad month.

Bram heard it from the yard boys and came up to the exchange at noon and stood in the door of the back room and told Ferra, and Ferra wrote it in her book on the page for the day. Milford's western herds driven off. Heard from the hide seller. The date of the page.

Then she turned back through the book, one page and then another, to the twenty-fourth, and looked at the four lines on four pages, and turned forward again.

"Milford's had a bad month," she said.

"The western road's been bad since the plague."

She looked at him across the trestle.

"Yes," she said. "It has."



She drew the payments that afternoon.

There were two. Dunnel's wife had died before the plague; he had told everyone so, at length, with great tenderness, generally within an hour of meeting them. He had a sister in Milford town, the one who had not believed it, the one who had sent the cheese. The boy had a mother, who kept the inn at the coast end of the western circuit, where Dunnel had stopped every time he went through.

There was no rate in any book for what the barony owed the sister of its carrier, or the mother of the boy who drove his cart. Ferra had looked. There was a rate for a lost horse and a rate for a lost cart and a rate for a lost bale of fleece, and the barony would pay those too, to the sister, as Dunnel's heir, at the full value, because they had been lost in the barony's service. And then there was the other thing, which had no rate.

"I've made one," Ferra said.

She did not say how. She had made it out of something, Emeric could see, out of Dunnel's fee for the run and the years he might have had and the inn's trade and the boy's wage, and it was in her book, worked down a whole page in her small plain hand, and at the foot of it there were two figures, and they were a great deal of money.

She wrote the two orders on the barony and put them in front of him to sign.

He signed them. He dipped the pen and signed the first and blotted it and signed the second, and gave them back to her, and he had not read either of them.

Ferra took them. She looked at his name at the foot of each, and then at him. In three years he had never once put his name to a thing in her book without reading every line of it first, and sometimes arguing with half of them, and she had never once let anybody else do it either.

She did not say so. She folded the orders and put them with the letters she had written to go with them, one to Milford and one to the coast, and sealed them.

Then she reached for the index.

It was the long book she kept for the twin year, one line for every pair since January, with the date the pair went out, and the slip from the gate clerk, and the date the coast house's confirmation came home, each in its column. Every line so far was full across. She found the line for the fifteenth of November. In the first column she had already written the date, and in the second, the gate clerk's slip, held. She took the slip out of the left-hand box on the trestle, where it lay on top of the others, and looked at it, and put it back.

In the third column, where the coast house's confirmation would have gone, she wrote: The carrier dead on the western road. The twin burned with the bale. And in the last column, which had never been used, she wrote one word.

Broken.



He went down to bay three that night after the keep was asleep, because he could not stay in his room with the cheese.

He had a key to the bay. Bram had given it to him in the first year and never asked for it back. He let himself in at the side door and lit nothing, and closed the shutters on the yard side and hung his coat over the crack, and stood in the dark of the long bay with the cold of the stone floor coming up through his boots and the drum's empty bed of brick at the far end, where the boiler had been riveted, and the anvil in the middle, black on black.

He put a flame in the air over the anvil.

Not from his hand. His hands were at his sides. He looked at the place a foot above the anvil's face, and only at that, and put the fire there, the way he had put it on the candle's wick a month before, by looking. A thumb of flame, yellow, standing on nothing, lighting the anvil's horn and the scale on the floor round it.

He let it go out. He did it again.

He did it ten times. It came every time. A thumb of flame and no more, wherever he looked, as long as he could see the place: on the anvil, on the edge of the quench trough, on the head of Bram's big hammer in its rack, on the far wall. The headache came with the third and he went on through it. On the tenth he put the flame on the end of a bar of iron stock lying on the bench across the bay, a small bright thumb of it on the iron's end, and held it there until the iron's end began to glow, and let it go.

Then he stood at the door end of the bay and took his own weight off his feet.

It was nothing now. It had been nothing for months. He hung a hand's width above the stone in the dark, with the cold coming up at him, and reached back past himself into the air at the far end of the bay, where it lay still against Bram's back wall, a long way off, the whole length of the bay, forty feet of it, and leaned on it, the way Linnet had said, gently, and let it lean on the rest.

The air moved. It came down the bay behind him like a door opening at the end of a long house, and took him with it.

He went down the bay. Hanging, a hand off the floor, slowly and then not slowly, past the anvil and the quench trough and the bench with the iron bar still glowing faintly on the end of it, past the bending rolls and the punch with its long lever, with the air at his back and his own weight gone, the whole length of the bay, farther than he had ever gone like this, farther than he had thought he could. The brick bed of the boiler came up at him out of the dark at the far end.

And the air went out of him.

It did not run down. It went out, all at once, the way the light goes out of a lamp stone that has given its last. The hold he had on his own weight went with it. He dropped the hand's width to the floor and the floor was not where his feet expected it and he went down on his side on the stone with his shoulder under him and his head after it, hard, in the dark, at the foot of the empty brick bed where the boiler had been.

He lay there.

The cold came up out of the stone into his face and his ribs. The bay was quite dark. Across it the end of the iron bar on the bench had gone out. His head rang, and his shoulder, and under both of them, steadily, in the dark, he could hear a boy's voice asking whether it was true that it did fourteen horses, and his own voice saying, come in the spring.

He lay on the floor of Bram's bay until the cold had got all the way into him. Then he got up, and found his coat, and went home.
