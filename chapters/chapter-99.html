Chapter 99 - What the Road Paid

They began laying between planting and haying, for the same reason the road had been mended between planting and haying six years before: it was the only stretch of the year when a man with a spade cost less than he would cost at any other time, and the only stretch when the barony could spare him.

Bram hired sixty. Ten coppers a day and a meal at noon, the meal cooked on the road in a cauldron on a cart that moved up with the gang, which Imani had asked for and Ferra had priced and nobody had argued with. Every man's name went into Imani's book on the first morning, with his work and his village, in a boy's hand while Imani stood over him, and every man was told on the first morning that if he was hurt he went to the cauldron cart and not home, and that he would be paid the day.

The work was the same work all the way along, and after the first week the gang did it without being told.

The ballast went down first, the grey spoil off the Grayrock hill that had been coming down in empty carts all winter and lying in heaps by the road, raked out in a bed a foot deep and wider than the rails by a yard on either side. The blocks went into the bed, two to every length, bedded and beaten down and sighted along by Bram with his eye an inch off the ground. Oak pins went into the drilled holes in the blocks, and the chairs went down over the pins and were driven, and then the rails. Six men to a rail, three a side, with iron tongs that gripped the head, walking it off the wagon and down into the chairs on a count. Iron wedges driven in beside the rail to hold it. And at every joint Bram's gauge bar, a length of flat iron with a notch at each end that he had cut off the ruts in the cutting in the autumn, laid across the two rails, and not a wedge driven home until it sat.

The wagons brought the rails up behind them. That was the whole trick of it, and the gang saw it before anybody had to tell them. A length of iron laid in the morning carried the wagon that brought the next length in the afternoon, so that the loaded wagon rolled up the new iron to where the iron stopped, and was unloaded there, and the empty went back down, and the gang never walked further than the end of the line to fetch anything. The line fed itself. Bram watched it do that on the second day with his arms folded and said nothing, which from Bram was a speech.

On the third day a rail slipped.

It came off the wagon on the count and the man at the back left of it, a Kaldo man called Tully with a wife and three children in the lower ward, lost his grip on the tongs as the rail tilted, and it came down on the side of his foot. He did not cry out. He sat down in the ballast very suddenly and looked at his boot.

Imani was there within the quarter-hour, off a horse she rode badly, because the boy with the book had been told to run. She had the boot off him on the cauldron cart and looked at the foot and felt it bone by bone with her thumbs while Tully looked at the sky. Two bones broken, she said, the small ones, and they would knit if he stayed off it for six weeks and did not try to be clever. She splinted it there, on the cart, and wrote it in the book in her own hand: the day, the name, the work, the rail, the two bones. Then she stood by the gang with her arms folded while they carried the next length, and watched the tongs.

"They're too short," she said to Bram.

Bram looked at the tongs. He looked at them for some time.

"They are," he said, and by the next morning the tongs had handles a foot longer, and a strap across each handle to go round a man's wrist, and nobody dropped a rail again that summer.

Tully was paid the six weeks. Ferra entered it under the line's own heading, not under the barony's, so that when anybody asked what the line had cost it would be there with the iron and the blocks. Imani read the entry over her shoulder and nodded and went back to her ward.

The first mile was down by the second week, and on the morning after they finished it Emeric had the four lettered wagons brought up to the end of the iron and loaded from the ore carts, and a horse put to them.

It was one of Farris's. Farris had lent it for the day, grudgingly, because he wanted to see for himself and did not want to be seen wanting to, and he stood at the side of the road with his arms folded and his hat pulled down, among the thirty or forty people from the town who had walked out along the road to see a thing they had been told about in the market. Four wagons of ore, roped and loaded, the weight of four carts. One horse in front of them, walking between the rails on the gravel path the gang had raked for it, with a boy at its head.

The boy clicked his tongue. The horse leaned into the collar, and the first wagon moved, and the second, and the others behind them with a sound like a long iron sentence being read, and the horse straightened up out of its lean and walked. It walked at the pace a horse walks when it is pulling nothing much. The four wagons followed it down the mile to the foundry yard without a sound except the wheels on the joints, and the town walked beside them, and some of the children ran ahead.

Farris walked beside them too, and at the yard, when the horse stopped, he went and felt its chest, and looked at its legs, and at its mouth, and came back to Emeric.

"She's not blowing," he said.

"No."

"Four carts."

"Four carts, on the level. On the road she'd pull one."

Farris looked back down the line at the rails going away up the road in the sun, two thin bright lines on the grey ballast, a mile of them, and then stopping.

"That's all very well for the horse," he said, and went to take her home.

Saban's list had come to the table in the winter, as he had promised. Thirty-one men with forty-four carts on the Grayrock road, near enough all of them hauling ore down and grain up for Kaldo on the contract rate, which was a silver and four coppers the cartload to the foundry yard, and sixty-two horses between them. Some had one horse. Two had six. Saban had put against each name what he knew: which ones owned their carts outright, which ones were paying for them, which ones had sons old enough to drive. It was the kind of list Ferra kept on the census, and Saban had kept it in the same form without being asked, and Emeric had read it in the winter and had not known what to do with it then either.

Garrow came down the road in the third week of the laying, in the middle of the afternoon, walking at the head of his own lead horse with three carts behind him and three sons driving them. He brought his carts to the end of the iron, where the carts now stopped and unloaded into wagons, and stood and watched his ore go off his carts and onto the wagons and away down the iron behind a single horse that was not his. Then he asked the gang where the prince was, and was told, and walked the mile down to the yard.

He was a broad, weathered man of fifty or so, with a carter's shoulders and a carter's hands, and he did not take his hat off, and he did not shout.

"You paid me to keep four horses standing," he said. "Six years back. Against the days the ruts took one. I kept them, and I was paid for it, and then you mended the road and I put them to work, and I've worked them since. I've ten horses now. I've three sons driving." He looked down the yard at the four wagons. "That horse there does what four of mine do."

"On the level. Yes."

"So you'll want one man in four." Garrow said it without any expression. "Which one?"

Emeric had known the question was coming since the table in the autumn and he had turned it over for the whole of the winter, and he did not have an answer that was any good, and he found that he was not going to pretend to one.

"The wagons will want teams," he said. "The teams go to the carters first, on a rate Saban sets with you, by the ton and the mile. The road past the end of the iron is still carts, the whole way up to your hill, and it will be carts for years. Every season the iron goes a little further and the carts have a little less of it."

"How many years?"

"Six. Maybe seven. Maybe less, if the forge gets bigger, and I mean it to."

"Six years," Garrow said. "And then?"

"Then I don't know."

Garrow looked at him for a long moment. It was not a hostile look. It was the look of a man checking a load that somebody else had roped, to see if it would hold, and deciding that it would not, and deciding that it was not his rope.

"That's honest," he said. "I'll give you that. It's more than Renner ever gave anybody." He turned his hat round in his hands, once, the only thing he had done with his hands the whole time. "I'll not drive your wagons. I'll cart what the iron doesn't reach, for as long as there's any of it the iron doesn't reach. My sons can do as they like. They're grown."

He went back up the road that afternoon with his three empty carts and his three sons. Two of the sons came back down the road a week later and asked Saban about the wagon teams.

Saban did the rest of it over the next month, one man at a time, at the end of the iron and in the tap-house on the quay and once in a barn in the rain. Nineteen of the thirty-one took the wagon rate, or took it for a son, or took it and went on carting past the end of the iron as well. Seven went on carting and would not talk about the iron at all. Three sold horses to Farris, who had wanted horses. Two went up the road to Grayrock and asked Hask for work in the deep ground, and got it. Saban brought the list back to the long room at midsummer with every name on it marked, and he did not say anything about it, and neither did anybody else.

Ferra ruled a new column in the line's book, beside the iron and the blocks and Tully's six weeks. She headed it Carters, and under it she entered what Saban had given her: the names that had lost carriage to the iron, and what each had been carrying in a year, and what each carried now. It was not coin the barony had paid out. It was coin that other men had stopped being paid. She entered it anyway, in the same hand as everything else, and nobody asked her why, because everybody at the table had heard Saban say in the autumn that there was a price that would not be in her book, and now it was.

The second mile went down before the haying, and then the gang went back to the fields, and the laying stopped for want of both men and iron. Bram kept six of the best on through the summer, to lay what the mill made as it made it, a few lengths at a time.

Ilsa's column came down the river road at midsummer with the ore tallies. At the pithead, not carried. It was still growing. It had been growing every month since the deep ground opened and it grew that month too. But for the first time since the autumn it grew by less than the month before, and Ilsa had noticed that, because Ilsa noticed everything, and at the foot of the column, under the sum, she had written one word in her small upright hand and underlined it.

Slower.

Emeric read it standing at the window of the long room, with the light coming in over the sheet, and then went down to the yard and walked out along the iron in the evening, the two miles of it, to the place where it stopped. It stopped at a stake, in a field beside the road. The road went on past it, up the valley toward the lake and the hills, with the evening coming down on it and a cart a long way up it, coming down, one horse, loaded.
