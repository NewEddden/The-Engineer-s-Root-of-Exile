Chapter 31 - Wire

Harl sent for him three days after he had said no, by a boy with flour on his sleeve, who said that the miller knew now and the prince could come down.

Emeric went down that afternoon. Harl was at the hopper, as before, and did not stop, as before, and told him over his shoulder what he would take.

The mill stayed his. The name stayed on the lintel. Emeric kept his tenth, since he had paid for it fairly.

"And that's all of it you'll have while I'm alive," Harl said, to the hopper.

But the wheel turned all night and all winter whether the stones wanted it or not, and Harl had been listening to it do that since his boys went into the ground. If the prince wanted the wheel, the prince could have the hours the stones did not want: nights, and the dead weeks of the winter, by a belt to whatever he liked in the back room, and pay for every hour of it, by the hour, the same as anybody would pay for anything.

"Paid to you," Emeric said.

"Paid to the mill." Harl tipped the last of a sack into the hopper and watched it go down. "It's the mill's wheel."

Emeric agreed before he had finished the sentence. It was a better arrangement than the one he had drawn in the flour three days before, and it had cost Harl nothing he minded losing, and it had cost Emeric his idea of how the thing was going to go, which was the only part of it he minded.

"He had terms," Rylan said on the way back up, with enormous satisfaction. "The miller had terms."

"Everybody in Kaldo has terms."

"Except you," said Rylan, who had not let it go since October and did not intend to.

Pell, the widow Brannock and the moneylender's nephew got their share in the end, though not of Harl's mill. They got half of Emeric's tenth, split three ways, at the price his standing on the bank had set. It came to more than the whole tenth had cost him. Ferra entered all three sales and the difference without a word about any of them, and against the difference, in the column where it would be spent, she wrote copper.



The draw plate they built in the room.

It was Bram's kind of problem and Emeric's kind of problem at once, which was why it had to be done in there. A plate of hard steel with a row of holes through it, each one tapered like a funnel and each a little smaller than the one before, so that a rod of copper pulled through the first came out a little thinner and a little longer, and through the second thinner again, and so on down the row until it came out as wire. Emeric knew how much smaller each hole could be than the last before the copper tore instead of stretching. Bram knew how to cut a taper in steel, and how steel behaved when you did, and what it did to a drill in the first hour.

They sat at Bram's table on a Sunday afternoon with the shutters open and the snow bright outside, and Ferra opened the timing book and put her finger on the page, and Emeric reached for the room and Bram's shoulder at the same time, and went in.

Inside, there was all the time they wanted. They built the plate at full size on a bench that was not there, and Bram cut the first hole with his own hands, slowly, and then stopped and said the taper was wrong at the mouth, it would score the copper going in, and opened it out. Emeric gave him the next hole's size as a fraction of the first. Bram made it, and the next, and the next. They argued about whether fourteen holes was too many, and Bram won on fourteen, because twelve would make each step too big and sixteen would make the plate too long for the steel he had.

The holes had no sizes. There was nothing in Kaldo to size them by. There were thumbs, which were not the same as each other, and Bram's rule, which was not the same as the mason's. So the holes were numbered instead, one to fourteen, by order, and each was exactly as big as it was, and the plate itself was the measure.

Bram did not like the room, and said so, in there, with his hands in a steel plate that did not exist: that a thing a man could not drop on his foot was not a thing he trusted. But his hands went on working while he said it, and when he had the fourteenth hole cut he turned the plate over and held it up to a light that was not there, and looked down the row of holes, one after another, each smaller than the last, and was quiet longer than Bram was usually quiet about anything.

When they came out Ferra had a hundred and twelve written in the book, well under her ceiling, and was looking at them both over the top of it to make sure they were all there.

"Fourteen," Bram said, to her, as though she had asked.

It took him nine days in the forge to make it real, out of the last bar of good steel he had been keeping since before the war, and a file he would not let anybody else touch. When it was done, Tess took it away to the back of the forge and came back with a strip of scrap iron with fourteen holes drilled through it to match, one for each hole in the plate, so that anyone could put a length of wire through the strip and know by the first hole it would not go through which hole it had come out of. She had scratched a word across the top of the strip with a nail. Then she had scratched it out again, and the word under the scratches was thumb.



The copper came down from Grayrock on the last barge before the river iced at its edges, in rough bars the color of a worn Copper Rand, and Ferra wrote down what it had cost and then wrote it down again in the margin, the way she wrote down everything that came from Grayrock now.

Bram drew a bar down to a rod at the forge, and they took the rod and the plate down to the mill, where the carpenter had built a drum in the back room and Harl had run a belt to it off the wheel's shaft. The first time Harl put the belt on, the drum went round fast enough to take a man's arm off at the shoulder, and the carpenter spent two days making it a pulley twice the size so that it would turn slowly enough to pull and not to snatch. Then the drum wound. The rod went through the first hole and came out thinner. It went through the second. It went through the third and broke clean across in the middle of the hole, with a small flat snap like a twig.

Emeric knew what had happened. Copper pulled through a die grows harder with every pass, and brittle, until it stops stretching and starts breaking, and the cure was to heat it between passes, red, and let it cool, and start again soft. He had known that in the other life. He could have done it himself, properly, slowly, thinking through every heat, and he started to say so.

Bram had already taken the rod off the drum. He heated the next length in the little hearth they had built in the back room by eye, to a color he did not name and did not need to, let it cool on the stone, and fed it back to the plate, and it went through the third hole and the fourth and the fifth. He did it in about a tenth of the time Emeric would have taken, and he did not think about it at all.

At the seventh hole it broke again.

Bram picked up the broken end and turned it in the light from the hearth, and then broke it again with his fingers, deliberately, and looked at the break.

"Grey," he said.

Emeric looked. There were grey streaks in the break, dull threads running through the pink of the copper like dirt in a cut.

"Slag," Bram said. "Or worse." He threw the end into the scrap box. "Grayrock's sending us their sweepings."

So they melted all of it down again in the forge and skimmed it, Bram standing over the crucible with a long iron spoon and taking the scum off the top until what was left ran clean. It cost them a fifth of the copper. Ferra wrote that down as well, and under it, in very small letters, the name of the barge.

"Write to them," Emeric said.

"And say what?" She did not look up. "That their copper's dirty. They'll say it was clean when it left them and the river got into it. And next time it'll be dirtier, and dearer, and they'll know we noticed." She blotted the line. "I'll know we noticed. That'll do for now."



Harl had his school morning in the first week of January.

He had not wanted one. Sela had chalked him up anyway, on the wall between Farris and the mason, and Harl had looked at the chalk and then said that if they were coming they would come to the mill, because he was not carrying a wheel up the hill for anybody. So a dozen of them came down to the river in the snow, with Tess among them, and stood in the stone room under the floor where the great shaft came in from the wheel through the wall, turning, dripping, as thick as a man's body.

Harl made them put their hands on the wall beside it. The whole wall trembled.

"That's the river," he said. "All of it, pushing. It doesn't stop. It's been pushing that wheel round since my grandfather cut his name over the door, and it was pushing before that, with nothing on it." He took them up the ladder and showed them the gears, wood on wood, the big wheel driving the little one, and the little one turning the stones above it, and the sound the stones made when they were running right and the sound they made when they were not, and which sound meant you had a quarter of an hour and which meant you had none.

Tess had gone up the ladder ahead of the rest before anybody told her she could, and was lying flat on the boards with her head over the edge, watching the little wheel take the big wheel's teeth. Harl looked at her, and let her stay.

A girl asked whether the river could turn anything else.

Harl looked down at her.

"Anything you can hang on a shaft," he said. Then he looked past her at the back room, where the drum stood with its belt off for the morning, and the prince's plate was bolted to its bench. "Seems somebody's trying."



Tess drew the first full length in the second week of January, at night, on the wheel's hours, with Bram watching and Emeric watching Bram.

The rod went into the first hole and came out of the fourteenth as wire, thin enough to bend round a finger and still copper all the way through, with no grey in it. It went on coming. The drum wound it in slow turns, and Tess fed the rod and annealed it at the little hearth between passes the way Bram had shown her, by eye, red and then rest, and did not once look at either of the men behind her. She went quiet the way she did when a thing mattered to her, and stayed quiet for most of an hour, and when the rod was gone there was a coil of wire on the drum as long as several houses.

Abloh had come up from the soap yard to see it, because Emeric had asked him to bring thread. He brought linen thread, waxed, run through a cake of beeswax until it was stiff and shone, and he sat on an upturned box and began wrapping the wire in it, turn against turn, close and even, as he would have whipped the end of a rope, while the others watched him.

"What's it for?" he said, without looking up.

"Later," said Emeric.

Abloh grunted, and went on wrapping. He had the look of a man who had been told later by princes before and had been paid every time, and could wait.

Ferra had the cost of it before the coil was off the drum. Copper by the length, less the fifth they had skimmed, and the wheel's hours at Harl's rate, and Tess's night, and Bram's, and the thread. Emeric gave her his own figure for it. She corrected it, by a good deal, and then asked him what it would bring back.

He opened his mouth, and had nothing to put in it that was a number.

She looked at him, then wrote, against the coil, in the column where things went when they were sold or used: Unpriced.

Tess took the coil off the drum and hung it on a nail on the back-room wall. Then she took a cord she had knotted that afternoon, a knot at the end of every stretch of her arms, and ran it along the wire from end to end, counting under her breath, and when she reached the end she took a stub of chalk out of her pocket and wrote a number on the wall beside the nail.

Under the number she wrote knots.
