Chapter 31 - The Writ

The covenant arrived as a person at seven in the morning on the twentieth of October, which was the first thing Emeric learned about it.

He had understood it on the nineteenth as a document. Eleven pages, two of them lists, a rate he could recite, a schedule with the second granary in it and a completion date in June. He had read it four times looking for the unfair thing and had gone to bed at some hour with the settled feeling of a man who has bought an unpleasant object and knows what it cost him.

What he had bought was a woman in a serviceable coat sitting at the end of the long room with a writing case open, at seven, before the fire was lit, who stood when he came in and sat down again and did not say good morning.

"Ferra is bringing the venture book," Vantic said. "I have asked for the venture book and the barony book and the foundry's charge log, and she has told me she will bring them and that I am not to touch the third one, because the third one has a man's wages in it who has not yet been told he is being kept on. I thought you should hear that from me rather than discover it."

"You could have asked me for them."

"I could. Then the arrangement is that I ask you, and you ask her, and in four months when you are busy the arrangement is that I ask you and you say you will get to it." She turned a page over. "It is easier for everybody if the awkwardness happens in the first week, while it is only awkward."

Emeric got the fire going himself, because the boy who did it was late and because he wanted a minute.

"Do you sleep," he said.

"On the mill road. Since the fourteenth."

"That wasn't the question I was asking."

"I know what question you were asking, and it was a pleasantry with a hook in it, and I would rather you did not do those, because I will have to answer about four hundred of them before this is over and I have a limited supply." She looked up for the first time. "You may have one a day. I am not being unkind. I have been resident in five baronies and the ones that went badly went badly because somebody in the first month decided it was going to be a friendship and then felt betrayed in the spring."

Bram found out about her on the third day, in the foundry, when he came up the charging floor with a broken tap-bar in his fist and found a stranger at the wall copying the notches off the standard onto a card.

"That comes off that wall for nobody," he said.

"It has not come off the wall. I have my own iron and my own file and I am cutting a copy against it, which is the procedure painted over your head, and I have taken a blank off the pile and entered it as taken." Vantic did not stop cutting. "I intend to keep it in my case. On the first of the month I will bring it back and have it checked with everybody else's, and when it drifts I would like it cut up in front of the floor with the others, and I would like that done by the shift man and not by you, because the instruction says the shift man and until last week the instruction did not say anything at all."

Bram stood there for a while.

"You'll not get a straight copy with that file," he said eventually. "It's cut for brass."

"Then I have wasted a blank and I will enter that as well."

He took the file off her, cut the copy in about ninety seconds, stamped it, handed it back, and told Emeric about it that evening in a tone that Emeric could not place at the time and identified two days later as the tone Bram used about apprentices he intended to keep.

The schedule of security took six days.

Emeric had thought of the security as a sentence in the terms. It turned out to be an act, performed with a beam and a set of weights and two clerks, on every object in the barony that the house now had a claim against, because a claim against a thing requires the thing to be described in a way that a stranger in a room eleven days away can recognise.

So they weighed the foundry. The pig iron in the yard by the ton, the bar stock, the forty harnesses in the armoury by suit and by piece, the wheel and its shaft measured rather than weighed, the second wheel's frame lying in the grass with its axle still uncast, the third furnace standing cold and entered as brick and labour with a note that it had never been lit. Ferra walked it with them and corrected the clerks eleven times and was wrong once, and made them enter the once.

On the sixth evening Vantic put two sheets side by side on the long table.

"The crown's roll assesses this barony at a figure I am not going to say out loud in front of the window," she said. "It is signed by the Chancellor's office and it is dated four years ago and it describes a floodplain with a dead mill on it, about eleven hundred living people, and no industry of any kind. My schedule of your pledged estate, which excludes the ground, the standing timber, the town, and everything I could not put a beam under, comes to rather more than that. I would like you to look at both numbers and tell me you have understood the difference."

Emeric looked at both numbers.

"How much more."

"A multiple. I will not give you the multiple this week because I have not finished and I do not hand people numbers I intend to revise." She squared the sheets. "My house will have both figures on a desk in the capital inside a fortnight, because the difference between what a debtor is assessed at and what a debtor is worth is the single most useful fact a lending house can hold, and there is no version of my employment in which I do not send it."

"And who else reads what your house holds."

"Nobody, in the ordinary way. My house is not a market crier and a house that gossips about its debtors has no debtors." She closed the case. "That is the honest answer and you should not take much comfort from it. The crown's number is four years old. It will not be four years old forever. Somebody will ride out here with a roll and a beam eventually because that is what the Chancellor's office is for, and on the day they do, this barony stops being a place nobody has thought about since the plague. I am not warning you. I am telling you that I am the first person to write it down and I will not be the last."

The thing he wanted to build came up on the second of November and it took a fortnight for the covenant to stop being a page.

Doran raised it, and raised it badly on purpose, which Emeric worked out afterward.

"I want to know when I have my church back," the priest said, in the long room, with his hat in his hands like a tenant. "I am not asking for it. I want you to hear that I have not asked. I am asking when."

Twenty-two men were still on the stone floor at the beginning of November. Nine of them were Kaldo's. Two of those were the men off the granary, who were nine weeks into the six weeks Imani had promised them and who wanted their dressings changed twice a day by somebody who boiled the cloth, and would want it until the new year at the earliest.

Imani came up that afternoon with a list she had clearly written in stages over a fortnight.

"Nine beds," she said. "In the whole barony. Nine. I counted them in June and told nobody because there was nothing to be done about it in June. There are two thousand two hundred people here now and four hundred of them came in the spring and are living six to a room in houses that were built for a town that had fewer of them, and it is going to be a wet winter, and I would like you to know what that is before it happens rather than after."

"Say what it is."

"Chest. Every year, every town, from the first frost to the thaw, and it goes through a full house in eleven days and it kills the old and the very small and nobody anywhere calls it a plague because it comes every year." She put the list down. "I want a room. Stone floor, because you have taught me what a floor is for. Two rows, a boiling yard, a door at each end so the ones coming in do not walk past the ones going out, and a warm floor if you can do it, and I know you can do it because you did it for grain in a shed in June."

"How big."

"Forty beds. And I will tell you now that forty is a number I have made up out of nineteen years of watching, and if you ask me to defend it I cannot, and it is still the right number."

Emeric had the sheet drawn by the following evening, and he enjoyed drawing it, and that was the part he thought about later. Stone off the quarry the second granary was already going to use. Timber out of the same fall. A floor over a flue off a single fire-stone in a hearth at the north end, which was the drying shed done again and done better, and which he had already built once and could therefore build for about a third of what a man who had never built one would pay.

He put it in front of the room on the fourth, with the cost written at the bottom in Ferra's hand because he had asked her to price it before he showed it to anybody, having learned that much in July.

Vantic read it for a long time. Then she took the terms out of her case and found the second page and turned it round.

"No," she said.

"It's a hospital."

"It is a new work. Clause four. Kaldo may repair, replace and maintain, and may build the granary because the granary is in the schedule, and may not commence any new works, trade, manufacture or undertaking not listed here while any part of the principal is outstanding." She did not look away from him. "It is not a trap and it is not aimed at this. The clause is aimed at glasshouses and kilns and a second foundry on the western arm, and if you had brought me any of those I would have said the same word in the same voice and you would have been angry at me for four days and it would have passed. I am saying it about a building for sick people, in front of your doctor, and I would like it understood that I know exactly what I am doing and that I am doing it anyway."

"Then find me the reading where it isn't a new work."

"There isn't one, and if I found you one I would be worth nothing to my house and nothing to you, because a factor who finds a debtor a clever reading in November finds his creditor a better one in March." She squared the page. "There are two honest roads. The first is that you write to my house asking for an amendment to the second page. I will carry it and I will write my own letter beside it saying the works are small, that the sum is trivial against the principal, and that in my judgment the barony's labour is idle in winter and the thing costs less now than it will ever cost again. That is all true and I will put my name on it."

"And the answer."

"Is no. I want to be exact, because you will hear the shape of hope in the first half of what I said and I would rather kill it now. The men who read it have never been here. They will read a barony four weeks into an eleven year debt asking to build something, and the fact that it is a hospital will make them more careful and not less, because a lender who grants the first amendment in the first month is a lender who has taught the debtor how to ask." She put the terms back in the case. "Six weeks up the road and six weeks back if the post behaves, and the post on that road has behaved badly all autumn. Ask in November, hear no in February, and the winter you are asking about will be over."

"And the second road."

"Repair, replace and maintain. You have a building with a stone floor two hundred feet long, and you may put anything into it you like, and the only thing you may not do is own it." Vantic looked at Doran, who had not moved. "Which is not my sentence to write. It is his."

Emeric said the thing he said next to Ferra, which was the wrong person by a distance he understood before the end of the sentence and could not stop.

"You wrote the deferral column," he said. "Nine things I wasn't doing and why. She read it in two days and barred six of them and now she's holding the seventh, and I asked you to keep that column, and it is the only page in this barony that has ever been used against me."

The room went the particular quiet of people deciding not to look at each other.

"Yes," Ferra said. "I did write it."

He apologised for it at noon, in the same room, in front of the same five people including the factor, because Ward had said something to him on the stairs that was not a rebuke and was worse than one.

"That was mine and I put it on her," he said. "The column is the reason there was a loan at all and she wrote it because I asked her to and she wrote it honestly, which is the whole of what she has ever done wrong in this barony. If anybody repeats this morning outside this room I would like them to repeat both halves of it."

Ferra let him finish, then went back to the book, which he had learned was the largest thing she did.

So the hospital went on the deferral column, in her hand, dated the fourth of November, with the drawing folded into the back of the venture book and the cost written under it, and a note that it was barred by clause four and not by money, because Emeric made her write the reason and she made him say the reason out loud before she would.

Imani read the entry when it was done and did not argue, which was the part that stayed with him.

"Eleven years," she said.

"Or until the debt clears. It won't be eleven."

"How many winters is it going to be."

"I don't know."

"I do," Imani said, and picked up her bag, and went back down to the church.

Emeric got the list out that night and left the space empty at the top, and wrote underneath it:

Sixteen days. I signed it on the nineteenth believing I had bought money on hard terms and what I bought was a person in the room, and the difference between those is that a page cannot say no to a thing that is not on it and a person can and has.

She is right about the clause. I have read it eleven times today looking for a door and there is no door and I would not respect the door if there were one. I have spent a year telling this town that a rule which bends for a good reason is a rule that has already been broken, and the first rule I have wanted to bend was somebody else's, and it took me until the afternoon to notice that this is exactly the shape of every argument I have won here.

I put it on Ferra in front of five people because Vantic was sitting three feet away and I could not put it on her. That is not a thing I did not choose. I chose it in about a second and a half and I knew while I was doing it.

The crown thinks this barony is worth what it was worth four years ago. That number is wrong by a multiple, and the person who found that out is being paid by a house eleven days away to write it down, and she was polite enough to tell me she was doing it. Ward has been saying for a year that the danger is being interesting. I have been treating that as a mood.

Imani asked me how many winters and I said I did not know and she said she did. I have written down what I think she meant and I have torn it up twice, because putting a number on it makes it a thing I have decided to spend, and I have not decided anything. It is being spent anyway.
