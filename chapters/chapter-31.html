Chapter 31 - Head of Water

The difference showed up first in nails.

For two and a half years Kaldo had counted them. A keg came upriver from Wayford at a price that had nothing whatever to do with iron and everything to do with there being one seller, and when a crew ran short the crew stopped. Emeric had once watched a roof wait nine days for eleven nails.

In the second week of spring the foundry poured for four hours and Bram's younger apprentice carried a keg out into the yard and set it down and stood next to it, and nobody said anything, because nobody had a sentence ready.

That was the whole change and it was not a small one. A barony that can pour is not a richer version of a barony that cannot. It is a different kind of place, and it took Emeric most of a month to stop noticing it every hour.

Plow heads came off a master mold, forty of them, every one the same weight and the same angle, which meant that for the first time in living memory two men in two different divisions were turning the same furrow. Cart tyres. Hinges and hasps by the crate. Sluice gates for the trenches, ten castings off one pattern, and when the eleventh was wanted there was no discussion about it, there was simply an eleventh. Bearings for the mill, which had been running on wood and grease and an understanding with God.

And in the third week, the axle for the foundry's own second wheel.

Emeric stood in the doorway while they set it and thought, with a satisfaction he recognised as slightly childish, that the building had just finished building itself. He made the mistake of saying it out loud.

Bram did not find it as pleasing as he did.

He had been on the floor since dawn, and what he had been doing since dawn was cutting a pattern. Not a hinge. The thing a hinge comes off. He had cut nineteen of them that month and had personally made almost nothing, and when Emeric finally asked, four days later, in the way he had learned to ask Bram things, which was sideways and without looking at him, he got an answer.

"Eleven years," Bram said, "I have been the reason a thing exists."

He turned the pattern over and looked at the underside of it.

"Now I am the reason a thing can be copied. And the copies are better than what I was making, most of them, because I was tired half the time and the pattern is never tired." He put it down. "I have not decided what I think about that."

Emeric did not fill the silence. He had learned that on this floor, over six hours, at some cost.

"It is the same hands," he said eventually. "They are just further from the thing."

"Aye." Bram picked the pattern back up. "That is what I have not decided what I think about."

The other thing that had arrived with spring was data, and the data said the farm was losing two thirds of its water on the way to the crop.

He had suspected it since the autumn and had made himself wait, because a suspicion measured badly is worse than no suspicion at all. So he measured it badly for a month and then measured it properly. A notched pole at the pump-house, a second at the head of the fourth division, a man walking the line at first light with a slate, every day, for three weeks, in every condition the weather offered.

Roughly a third of what left the wheel reached a root. The rest went into the ditch walls, into the air off a mile of open surface, and into the low corner of the fourth division, which had spent eighteen months quietly converting itself into a bog that nobody had asked for and nobody could farm.

Farris put it in one sentence, the way he put most things.

"We are watering the ground on the way to the ground."

The drawing had been in the chest since the first summer, folded twice, on the back of a bill of lading for lime. He had not been able to build one foot of it then. He had dug the ditches deep anyway.

He took it out, unfolded it on the table in the pump-house, and found that past-him had made a note in the corner that present-him had entirely forgotten, which read dig deep now, cheaper than twice, and he sat with that for a second and then got on with it.

The pipe was Colm's, and the pipe was not invention. It was production, which is a different discipline and a harder one to be good at.

They had clay, they had a kiln crew that had spent two years making drainage tile and brick, and they had a master form: a ring at one end, a socket at the other, a spigot to sit in it. Two hundred and eleven pipes, each one the same length, each one the same bore, each one interchangeable with any other. The kiln crew made them the way the kiln crew made anything, which was steadily and without being interesting about it, and by the eighth day the yard behind the kiln looked like the inside of a very organised mind.

The pipes were not the problem. The problem, as it always is, was where two of them meet.

Butt-jointed and mortared cracked on the first cold night, all along the run, in eleven places. Wrapped in tarred cloth held beautifully and held right up until there was pressure in it, at which point it did not. They lost a fortnight to joints, which Emeric had not budgeted for and which he recorded in the book in the flat language he used when he was angry with himself.

On the ninth day, Colm, who had been on his knees in wet clay since before dawn and had stopped being polite about three hours previously, said:

"Stop joining the pipes."

Emeric looked up.

"Cut the socket deeper. Pack the gap round the spigot with tow and clay and drive it home with an iron. You're not joining two pipes, you're filling the hole between them, and the hole doesn't care about the cold." He wiped his hands on his thighs, which did not improve them. "That's all I've got. I'm going to eat something."

"That will work," Emeric said.

He said it about half a second too fast, and he heard himself do it, and it was already out.

Colm stopped in the doorway and turned round and looked at him for a moment with an expression Emeric had seen before and had never been able to get a clean read on. Not suspicion. Something more like a man noticing that a door he walks past every day has a second lock on it.

Then he said, "Aye, well," and went to eat something, and that was the end of it.

It was the fourth or fifth time. Emeric had stopped counting at three because counting had begun to feel like an admission. He sat in the pump-house with the folded drawing in front of him and thought, not for the first time, that the strangest thing about the whole business was not that people noticed.

It was that nobody had ever asked.

He did not know what he would say if they did.

The tower went up on the western rise, which was the only piece of high ground west of the bridge that was not either farmed or drowned, and which nobody had ever found a use for.

Stone base, timber tank, banded and pitched, high enough that the main ran downhill the whole way to the far edge of the ninth division without anything helping it. It took three weeks and a great deal of rope and one afternoon Emeric would prefer not to discuss.

The whole town came out to look at it. Not in a body, but steadily, over a week, in ones and twos, walking out over the Left Bridge and up the rise in their good boots to stand and regard it. Emeric had expected some of that. He had not expected the verdict.

The prince, it was generally agreed, had built a very large bucket on a hill.

It got as far as the market inside two days. He heard a child say it to another child by the well, word for word, in the tone of a thing the child had heard an adult say and did not understand but knew was funny, and Emeric walked on and let it happen, because there is no version of explaining a reservoir to a market square that ends well.

The explanation, for what it was worth, was that storage was not the point. Storage was a side effect.

The point was that the western arm ran slack in dry weeks, and the wheel fouled on weed roughly twice a month, and an hour of dead water in the arm had always meant an hour of no water on the fields. A tower turns a pump that works sometimes into a system that works always. The wheel fills the tank whenever the current runs, at whatever rate it manages; the farm draws off the tank and never meets the wheel at all. The tank does not care when the water arrived. It only cares that it did.

Nobody in Kaldo had ever seen such a thing, and the great virtue of a very large bucket on a hill is that it is extremely difficult to be frightened of one.

The heads were fixed, not rotating, and that was a decision rather than a limitation. A rotating head means bearings, and bearings mean a moving part in a field where the nearest man who can repair a moving part is nine divisions away and it is August.

So: a cast bronze face, a slot cut across it at an angle, and the water leaves it as a fan. Bram cut the master. The foundry poured the rest.

Two hundred of them, off one pattern, identical. It was the first object in the history of the barony that existed two hundred times, and Emeric noticed that nobody else found this remarkable, and enjoyed it privately for about four days.

Then they began to clog.

They clogged constantly, because they were being fed river water, and river water is not water. It is water and silt and weed and one third of a snail. The heads plugged, the fans went crooked, the fourth division got a stripe down it that was green and a stripe down it that was not, and for a week the whole system required a man walking the line with a length of wire, which is precisely the kind of thing Emeric built systems in order to stop doing.

The fix took a day and a half. A settling box at the tower inlet, water in at one end and out at the other with the long way round in between so the heavy matter drops, and a graded screen after it, coarse then fine.

He was halfway through cutting the fine screen when he stopped, because he had built this before. It was the seed sieve. It was the same object doing the same job in a different costume, and he had been treating it as a new problem for two days because it was wearing different clothes.

He wrote it down that night, and he did not write down put a screen on the tower, because that was worth nothing. He wrote:

Anything that moves a fluid needs something upstream that takes out what the fluid is carrying. This is not a fix. It is a class of fix. Look for it before the thing clogs, not after.

He was more pleased with that than he had been with the tower, which told him something about himself that he filed and did not examine.

The control post stood at the head of the farm and was the most ordinary object in the barony.

This had cost him some effort. A water stone the size of a fist, set in a waist-high stone housing with a timber roof over it against the rain, and on the stone, cut by hand, a piece of runework that any hedge carver in the kingdom could have cut and which the one in this barony effectively had.

Nevin had come out of the diggings the previous autumn, a careful man in his forties who had spent eleven years cutting charge-and-hold into mine lamps and had never in his life been asked to cut anything else. Ferra had found him in the work rolls. He had taken four days to believe he was not in trouble.

His work on the post was legible, competent, and completely unremarkable. That was the entire brief and Emeric had given it to him in those words, and Nevin had been faintly insulted for about an hour and then got on with it.

A farmhand put his hand on the stone and pushed into it the trickle of mana that almost anyone has and almost nobody can do anything with, and the stone let a charge down the line, and the line tripped the latches.

The latches were the cheap part and there were a great many of them. Each one was a mundane catch on a section valve, held shut by a spring and released by a small water stone set beside it. The stones cost nothing. The branches were full of them, because a stone that has sat in running water for a hundred years takes affinity from what it sits in, and Kaldo had two branches and a century of nobody bothering to pick anything up. A boy could fill a sack in an afternoon, and one had.

Onto each of those stones Emeric cut the shallowest working he was capable of cutting. Hold charge. Let go on signal. Nothing else. No conditions, no ordering, no judgment of any kind, because judgment is what costs and all the judgment in this system lived a mile away in the pump-house.

There were a hundred and fourteen of them and they went under four feet of spoil along the line of the main.

Laying the eleventh, he noticed something and then decided not to have noticed it.

His work did not look like Nevin's work. Nevin's cuts had a hand in them, the small consistent wander of a man holding an iron against a curved surface for eleven years. There were tool marks in them. You could see where he had started and where he had rested.

Emeric's had none of that. No entry, no exit, no tremor, no tool. They were not cut so much as present, the way a vein is present in a rock, and set side by side with one of Nevin's they were so plainly not the same act that a man who knew anything at all about etching would need about four seconds.

He held the stone and thought about it for the length of a breath.

Then he put it in the trench, because it was going under four feet of spoil in the middle of a farm in the poorest barony in the kingdom, and nobody was ever going to dig up a latch.

Two men were trained to run the post inside a week. They were shown the sequence, the order of the divisions, and what to do if a section did not answer, which was hit the housing with a hammer and then send for Colm. There was nothing in the whole visible system that required Emeric to exist, and that was not an accident, and it was not humility either.

The other layer he built alone, at night, and told nobody about at all.

He took one of Corbin's stones out of the box, having not touched them since the banquet, which was one of a number of things from that evening he had elected not to touch.

An unbound stone is a blank machine. Anyone with the faculty can pick it up and use it and it carries nothing of the man who held it before. Binding makes it yours. After that it holds your work the way a shelf holds what you put on it, which is convenient and which cuts both ways, because a bound stone that leaves your possession is a bound stone somebody else can read.

The binding was quiet and slow and took most of two nights and was, he thought, the closest thing to a private act he had performed since he arrived in this world.

What went onto the stone was not power. He was careful about that, in the writing and in his own head. It was sequencing and timing. Which sections open, in what order, for how long, without a man walking a mile of line with a slate. The far divisions get theirs at dawn when the wind is down. The third gets a short pass because the third drains. The whole cycle runs while everybody is asleep and nobody is required to be anywhere.

The stone went on a shelf behind the wheel housing, in the dark, in a building that already smelled of wet timber and grease and that nobody entered voluntarily. It addressed the line at range and touched nothing that anybody would ever look at.

The post still worked exactly as it had. Half the farm crew never learned that there was anything else, and the ones who half-noticed that the sections sometimes ran themselves assumed it was something to do with the tower, because the tower was the strange thing and the strange thing gets the credit.

He was clear with himself about why he was hiding it, because the honest version was available and he did not enjoy the dishonest one.

It was not modesty. Nobody in this barony was knowledgeable enough or nosy enough to tell his work from ordinary etching, and ordinary etching was everywhere in the kingdom, on lamps and locks and mill gates and cattle gates. The camouflage cost him nothing and covered him completely.

What he was hiding from was in the other direction, and Saban's report was a month old and sitting behind his eyes the entire time he did it. Somebody had asked a carter, pleasantly, who the soap belonged to.

So he sat in the pump-house and made a list of the things he was not going to do, which is a miserable sort of list. A stone on the mill that evened the load. A stone on the kiln that held a temperature. Four on the foundry, one of which would have paid for itself in a season. Something on the bridge. Twelve altogether, and every single one of them would have worked, and every single one of them would have been an object sitting in plain view for a visitor to stop in front of and ask a question about.

He crossed all twelve out and went to bed.

The main burst nine days later, and that one was his.

He had set the stone's output high. The reasoning had been sound at the time and was still sound in isolation: he wanted the far divisions covered in a single pass, because a single pass is one decision and two passes is two decisions and two decisions is where a system starts needing a man to think about it.

Clay pipe is not steel pipe. He knew that. He knew it in the way you know a thing you have read rather than a thing you have broken, which is not the same kind of knowing at all.

The main split forty feet below the tower base at four in the morning and put out most of the tank through a hole in a field before anybody found it. Nobody was hurt. Two hundred feet of pipe had to come up and go back down. The fourth division acquired its second bog.

They lost nine days.

Farris walked out to look at the new bog and stood at the edge of it with his hands behind his back and did not say anything at all, which was considerably worse than the sentence he could have said, and which Emeric suspected he knew perfectly well.

What he wrote afterward took him two hours and came out as four lines.

He capped the powered mode at what the pipe survived, which turned out to be roughly what the tower's own head gave it standing there doing nothing. The stone was not there to push harder than the tower. The stone was there to hold that pressure steady through a dry fortnight and reach the far sections in sequence while everyone slept.

The backup's capability sets the ceiling for the primary.

If the powered mode can do something the unpowered mode cannot survive, then the day the power fails is the day you discover your pipes are already gone. You will not discover it gradually. You will discover it at four in the morning in a field.

He was annoyed by this for about a week, and the annoyance was instructive. It meant the stone bought him consistency and reach and not strength, and strength was what he had wanted, because strength is a better story. Nobody walks out over a bridge in their good boots to look at consistency.

It was also the better system, and he knew it while he was being annoyed.

So the farm had three ways to be watered.

With the stone: sequenced, set once, running in the dark, ten sections at a time, nobody present.

Without the stone: a farmhand with his hand on the post at tower pressure, two sections at a time, all day, which is a worse day for the farmhand and the same day for the crop.

Without any of it: pull the sluices and let the old trenches flood on grade, exactly the way they had done it the first year, badly, losing two thirds, which is not nothing. Two thirds lost is still a third delivered, and a third delivered is a harvest.

Bram asked him about the ditches directly, which was the only way Bram asked anything. They were standing over the second division and the ditch beside them was dry and weeded and plainly redundant, and Bram wanted to know why a man who had just buried two hundred pipes was paying a boy to keep a hole in the ground tidy.

"Because the ditches cost nothing to leave alone," Emeric said. "And the day I need them I will not have a season to dig them again."

Bram thought about it.

"That's a soldier's answer," he said, "out of a man who isn't one."

"I read a great deal."

"Aye," Bram said. "So you keep saying."

Ferra came out to walk the main with him at the end of it, late, after the water had gone off and the light was going.

She had the whole thing costed in a book she was not carrying, which was her way of telling him she had the whole thing costed. They walked the line from the tower down, over a mile of buried pipe that looked from the surface like a faint scar in a field, and she told him the farm was fine.

She meant it, and she did not say it warmly, because she never said anything warmly.

"It has a post any fool can work," she said. "It has a ditch underneath it if the post dies. If the tower falls down, which it will not, the trenches are still there, and if the trenches fill in, the ground is still ground. You have built the only thing in this barony that does not need you."

"That was the intention."

"I know. That is not what I came out here to say."

They walked another thirty feet.

"Every binding in this barony came out of one head," she said. "Every working on every stone. The corrections to the drum. The mark on the drying shed. The spreader, which I have now seen four men use and none of them could tell me why it works. The furnace, which I am told was redesigned in a way that nobody who was present can describe to me."

"Bram can describe the furnace."

"Bram can describe what it does. He cannot describe how the two of you arrived at it, and I have asked him, and he changed the subject, and Bram does not change subjects." She stopped and turned round. "You cannot teach it. Not to me. Not to Nevin, who is the only trained hand we have and who you use to cut the one piece of work that does not matter. Not to Bram, who is the cleverest man in this town and who you keep four feet away from the interesting part."

It was Bram's complaint from the harvest, arriving from a completely different direction, in the mouth of someone who kept records.

"I know," Emeric said.

"Then what do you intend to do about it?"

"Armand is coming."

She let that sit for a second, and when she answered it was not unkind, which was somehow worse.

"A plan that depends on one old man deciding to travel is not a plan. It is a hope with a name attached to it." She started walking again. "What does this barony do if he dies on the road? He is not young and it is twelve days on a road he has never travelled. What does it do if he simply does not come? He has a life. He may have looked at the distance and the season and his own knees and decided he would rather not."

Emeric did not have a reply.

He waited for one to arrive, the way it usually did, and it did not, and after a while it became obvious to both of them that it was not going to.

She did not push it.

They walked the rest of the line in the dark without talking, all the way down to the ninth division and back up to the bridge, and the not-talking was comfortable in a way that neither of them examined, then or afterward.

Nowhere in any of it did either of them raise the possibility that he had never actually written to the man.

He had not.

He did not say so.

By August the system was four things and a boy.

A very large bucket on a hill. Two hundred and eleven identical pipes under a mile of field. A post that a farmhand worked with his hand and a hammer, cut by a careful man from the diggings who had taken four days to believe he was not in trouble. A rock on a shelf behind a wheel housing, in the dark, that nobody would ever have cause to look at.

And a boy on the western arm, sitting on a frame a cartwright had built two summers ago, who had exactly one job, which was to pull weed off the wheel when the wheel fouled, and who spent the rest of every afternoon filling a tank on a hill without once being required to understand why.

The fourth division came in that autumn at better than double what it had given the year Emeric arrived.

Including the bog. Both of them.
