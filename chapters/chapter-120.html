Chapter 120 - The Road to Court

Ferra went north in a chaise, because she could not ride and would not learn on the king's road with the case in a box behind her.

It was the barony's, or had been Wexler's, which came to the same thing: a light two-wheeled thing with a hood of oiled leather that folded back and a seat for two, one of whom was meant to drive. Ferra did not drive either. Rylan drove, with the reins in one big hand and the other on his knee, and talked to the horse, and Ferra sat beside him on the seat with the three deal boxes roped in behind and the barony's book on her lap and the pen in her hand, and wrote. She wrote at a walk and at a trot and on the cobbles at Wayford and on the long straight stretches of the south road where nothing happened for hours. Emeric did not know what she was writing. He rode on the near side of the chaise most of the way and once, on the second day, looked across and saw a column of figures going down the page in her small upright hand as steady as if she had been at her trestle, while the chaise bounced on a rut and Rylan swore at it and the horse, and the figures did not.

The carriage stayed at home. Ferra had said so in the hall. The carriage with the good springs was for the boxes when the boxes went alone, and the boxes were going with her this time, and she did not want them out of her reach in a coach that four people in Kaldo knew was odd and nobody on the south road had looked at twice. Not yet. So the chaise, which bounced.

Ward rode in front. On the fourth day, at the fork above Wayford where the south road went on north and a lesser road turned off east into the hills, he pulled up and sat his horse in the middle of the road for some time looking back the way they had come, and then rode on, and said nothing about it until noon.

"Two men," he said, at noon, at the inn where they changed horses. He said it to Emeric, quietly, by the trough. "On grey horses. They were at the posting house in Wayford when we came through. They were at the fork when we came to it, going the other way, east." He drank from his cup. "They are behind us now."

"Going north."

"Going north, a mile behind, at our pace." Ward put the cup down. "If they were going east this morning they have changed their minds."

"Sethe?"

"Somebody." Ward did not look round at the road. "We will see if they are still there at the next house."

They were.



The wheel went on the fifth day, in the hills.

The south road climbed out of the river country on the fifth day and went up over the high downs, a long bare country of short grass and sheep and old grey stones standing in the turf, with the wind coming across it from the west all day and nothing to stop it. In the morning it had been fine. By the middle of the afternoon the sky in the west had gone the colour of slate, and then the wind had come up off it cold, much colder than it had any right to be in July, and then the rain had come, not hard, but blown, a fine grey rain driven flat across the downs, that got under hoods and into collars and found every gap in a coat.

The chaise's near wheel went into a rut hidden under a sheet of running water at the bottom of a dip, and there was a crack like a bough breaking, and the chaise lurched and sat down on its axle on one side, and Ferra put out her hand and caught the edge of the hood, and the barony's book went into the road.

Rylan was down in the water before the horse had stopped. Emeric got the book. It had landed open, face down, in two inches of muddy rain, and he picked it up and shook it and held it out to her, and she took it and opened it on her knee and looked at the page she had been writing on, and the column of figures had run into a long grey smear down to the bottom of the sheet.

She looked at it.

"I'll do it again," she said.

Rylan came up out of the dip and stood by the hood with the rain running off him.

"Spokes," he said. "Three gone. And the felloe's split." He wiped his face with his sleeve, which made no difference. "She'll not go on it. Not to the next house. Not to anywhere."

Ward had ridden back. He looked at the wheel, and then up the road ahead of them, north, where it went up over the next long rise of the down into the grey.

"The house is six miles," he said. "Beyond that ridge, and the next. In a valley, where the road comes down off the downs." He looked at the light, what there was of it. "Two hours to dark."

"We can't leave the boxes," said Ferra.

"Rylan and the other stay with the chaise and the boxes," Ward said. "I will send a wheelwright back from the house tonight with a cart." He looked at the second garrison man, a quiet one from the levy, who was sitting his horse in the rain with his cloak over his crestless plate and his crossbow under the cloak, and the man nodded. Then Ward looked at Ferra. "You go on to the house."

"I can't ride."

"No," said Ward. He did not look at Emeric. "You will not have to."

It took Emeric a moment to understand him. Then he did, and so did Ferra, and he saw her understand it, sitting under the hood of the broken chaise in the driving rain with a ruined page in her lap, and he saw her look at Ward's back, which was already turned, and then at Emeric's horse, and then, for a long breath, at him.

"Behind you," she said.

"Behind me."

"On that."

"It's a good horse."

"I'm sure it's a very good horse." Ferra looked at the horse, which looked back at her with rain dripping off its ears. "I've never been on a horse in my life. My father shoes them. I've held their feet." She put the book inside her coat, against her chest, and buttoned the coat over it. "That's the closest I've ever wanted to be."

But she got down out of the chaise.



Rylan put her up. He did it the way he did everything with his hands, without fuss: a knee for her foot and his hands at her waist and up, and she was sitting sideways behind Emeric's saddle on the horse's broad wet back before she had decided to be, with her skirts bunched and her hands out on either side of her in the air with nothing to hold.

"Hold on to him," said Rylan.

"To what?"

"His belt," said Rylan. "Or him. Whichever you'd rather fall off with." And he went back down into the dip to see to the horse in the shafts.

Ferra's hands found his belt. Both of them, one either side, at the back, her fingers hooked under the leather, and held on, and he felt her hold on, and felt her sit very straight behind him with as much air between her and his back as there was room for on a horse.

"Walk," she said. "Don't trot. If you trot I'll get off and walk six miles."

"Walk," he agreed, and they walked.

Ward went ahead. He went far enough ahead that they could see him, a grey shape on a grey horse in the grey rain on the road where it went up over the rise, and not so far that he could hear anything said behind him. Emeric noticed it, and noticed that he was meant to notice it, and did not know what to do with that, so he did nothing.

For the first mile Ferra did sums.

She did them aloud. He did not think she knew she was doing it at first. The wheel. Three spokes and a felloe, at the wheelwright's rate on the south road, which was robbery, and the cart to fetch the chaise, and a night at the house for two men they had not counted on, and the horses' feed, and she had budgeted the journey to the copper in the summer and there had been nothing in it for wheels. She went down it, line by line, in a low steady voice behind his shoulder, in the rain, the way she would have read a column back to herself at the trestle to make sure she had it right, and every time the horse put its foot down in a rut her hands tightened on his belt and the sum stopped, and then went on.

He did not say anything. He listened to her carry the copper.

Then, at the top of the first rise, the sum stopped and did not go on.

The wind had them there. It came over the crest of the down from the west with nothing between it and them for twenty miles, and the rain came with it, flat and hard and cold, and went straight through his coat as if the coat had not been there. He heard her breath go in behind him. He felt her hands on his belt go tight and stay tight.

"It's July," Ferra said.

Her voice was not quite steady.

"It's the downs."

"It was warm this morning." He could hear her teeth. "I put on the thin coat. I didn't think. I've got the good one in the box. In the chaise. Six miles back." She stopped. "I didn't think it'd be this cold. Not today. Not in July." She was shaking. He could feel it through her hands on his belt, a fine hard tremor that came and went with the wind. "I didn't count it."

He thought of Armand by the fire.

Not on the road a day either side of the walls. They were five days from Kaldo and three from the capital and in the middle of the high downs in the rain with nobody nearer than Ward, a quarter of a mile ahead with his back to them, and two men on grey horses who were a mile behind if they were anywhere at all.

He let it go into himself.

Not outward. Not a glyph, nothing that would glow in the grey light and show to a man on a hill. Down, into his own body, the way he had been letting it go behind the shutters since the spring, into his chest and his back and his shoulders and his arms, and told it nothing, only to be there; and then told it what a fire was.

The cold went out of him.

It went from his chest first, and then from his back, as though a stove had been lit somewhere under his ribs, and the heat came up through him and out into the skin and through the wet shirt and the wet coat, and the rain that hit his shoulders did not feel cold any more, it felt like rain on a roof. He let it go further. He let it fill his back, all of it, from his neck to his belt, where her hands were, and hold there, steady, the way a good grate holds a fire.

Behind him Ferra stopped shaking.

Not at once. Her hands on his belt, first, the tremor going out of them a little at a time, the way it goes out of a man's hands by a fire. Then the rest of her. He felt her notice. He felt her go very still on the horse's back behind him, with her fingers hooked under the leather, and then he felt her, slowly, carefully, as if she thought he might not notice if she did it slowly enough, close the air between them.

She leaned in. Her forehead came against his back, between his shoulders, and then her cheek, and then the whole of her, along his back, in the rain, and she let go of his belt with one hand and put that arm round him instead, flat against the front of his coat, and held on, and did not say anything at all.

He rode down off the crest of the down in the rain with his whole back burning like a hearth and Ferra against it.

It was a long time before she spoke. Long enough that the road had gone down into the next dip and up the other side of it, and the rain had eased to a drizzle, and Ward had disappeared over the second ridge ahead and come back into sight on it, waiting.

"You're warm," she said, into his back.

"Yes."

"You were cold. At the top. I could feel it through your coat. You were as cold as I was." She did not move her cheek. "And then you weren't."

He did not answer.

"Ward does that," Ferra said. "On the wall. In the winter. I've seen the steam come off him." She was quiet for a while. "My father says the knights can do it because of the rite. The silver lines. He says it's the first thing they learn, before they learn to lift a horse." Her arm was still round him, flat. "You haven't got any silver lines."

"No."

"I've seen your arms. In the yard. With your sleeves up." She was not asking. She was laying it out, the way she laid a column out. "You haven't had the rite. You're a mage. You do fire. Mages can't do that. My father says that too." She stopped. "You're doing it now."

The horse walked on. The road went up toward Ward on the ridge.

He had stood in Imani's ward in February with her face a hand's breadth from his and not answered, and she had stepped back. He waited for Ferra to step back. He waited to feel her sit up, and the air come back between them, and her hand go back to his belt.

She did not.

She stayed where she was, against his back, warm, with her arm round him. And after a while he felt her let her breath out, long and slow, the way she let it out when she closed a book on a sum that had come out.

"Then don't stop," she said.

That was all. She did not ask what he was. She did not say anything else about the rite or the silver lines or what mages could do. She put her cheek more comfortably against his shoulder blade, and closed her eyes, he thought, though he could not see, and they rode up the second ridge in the drizzle toward Ward with the light going, and he did not stop.



A mile short of the house she made him stop.

They had come down off the downs into a valley with a stream in it and trees, and the rain had given over, and below them in the dusk there were lights: the posting house, a long low building with a yard and a lamp over its gate, and two or three cottages round it, and smoke going straight up from its chimneys in the still air of the valley bottom.

"Stop," said Ferra.

He stopped. She sat up. He felt her sit up, the warm length of her coming away from his back, and the air come in cold where she had been, and her arm go from round him back to his belt.

"I'll walk from here," she said.

"It's a mile."

"I know how far it is." She was looking down at the lights. "There's a yard. There'll be ostlers in it. And a man at the gate, and the woman who keeps the house, and whoever's in the long room by the fire, and every one of them will come to the window when they hear horses after dark." She was very straight on the horse's back behind him. "And they'll see the holder of Kaldo ride into the yard with his Chancellor up behind him with her arms round him, and they'll tell it at every house between here and the capital before we get there." She paused. "And Sethe's men will be in one of those houses."

"Ward's ahead of us. He'll have told them we're coming."

"Ward will have told them a party from the south is coming with a broken chaise," said Ferra. "Ward knows what to tell a yard." She let go of his belt. "Help me down."

He got down first and helped her down. She came off the horse awkwardly, the way anyone does the first time, and he caught her at the waist as she came, and set her on her feet in the wet road, and let go, and she stood there with her hand on the horse's shoulder getting her legs back.

She did not look at him. She straightened her coat. She took the barony's book out from inside it, where it had been against her chest the whole six miles, and looked at it, and it was dry.

"You walk the horse," she said. "I'll walk beside it. Like a clerk who was in a chaise that broke a wheel, and walked the last mile because the prince's horse was tired." She started down the road. "Which is true."

He walked the horse. She walked beside it, on the other side, with the book under her arm, in the dusk, down into the valley toward the lights of the posting house, and neither of them said anything for most of the mile.

At the bottom, where the road crossed the stream on a plank bridge a hundred yards short of the gate, she stopped.

"You were cold," she said, "at the top. And then you made yourself warm. For me." She was looking at the stream. "I counted. It was four miles you held it."

"About that."

"It cost you something." She did not ask it. "I could feel your back. It was like leaning on a bread oven. Nobody keeps an oven lit four miles for nothing."

"It costs about what it costs to walk four miles."

Ferra looked at him then, across the horse's neck, in the last of the light.

"That's a lie," she said. "A small one. I'll let it go." She turned back to the bridge. "Thank you."

And she went on across the plank bridge and up to the gate of the posting house, ahead of him, with the book under her arm and her back very straight, and the lamp over the gate lit her red hair, wet and coming down out of its pins, as bright as the coals at her father's back, and then she was in the yard.



Rylan and the levy man came in after midnight with the chaise on a cart, and the boxes on the chaise, and the wheelwright cursing in the dark.

Ward was in the yard at dawn, as he always was, with the practice blades.

Emeric came down to him with his right hand still stiff from the night and his back aching in a deep dull way it had never ached from riding, and took the blade Ward gave him, and they drilled in the wet yard of the posting house with the ostlers watching from the stable door.

He held back. He held everything back, all of it, close in, the way Armand had said. He let his guard come up exactly as fast as a man's guard comes up who has been drilling at dawn with his knight for four years and is no better at it than he should be, and not one breath faster.

Ward put him on the ground three times.

The third time, standing over him in the wet yard with the blade's point resting on the cobbles beside his head, Ward looked down at him longer than he needed to.

"Slower than yesterday," Ward said.

"I'm tired."

"Yes." Ward held out his hand and pulled him up. He did not let go at once. He held Emeric's hand in his, and Emeric felt him feel it: the heat still in it, from the inside, hours after, like a stone that has been in a fire. Ward let go. "Drink something cold before we ride." He turned away to the horses. "The two men on grey horses slept in the house last night," he said, over his shoulder. "In the room over the yard. They left an hour before dawn. North."
