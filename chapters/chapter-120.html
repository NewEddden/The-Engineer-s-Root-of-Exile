Chapter 120 - Emberfell

The inlet ran inland for the best part of an hour at the pace she steamed, and the heat came out to meet them long before the port did.

It came off the land. Kaldo had been under snow that morning and the lower water under frost, and here, five hundred miles and more west along the coast, there was no snow at all on the hills either side of the water, and no frost, and the air coming in at the ports was the air of a spring afternoon. Where the hills came down to the inlet the rock was black, and in places along the foot of it steam stood up out of the ground in thin white threads and leaned away in the wind. Nobody aboard had ever seen ground that breathed. Imani stood at a port and watched a thread of it for a long time, and then said that she would want to know what was in the water there before anybody drank it.

There was no woodsmoke anywhere. Emeric noticed that last, and then could not stop noticing it. Along the shores there were villages, and yards, and the long low sheds of works of some kind, and not one of them had a chimney going. The furnaces that must be in those sheds were not burning anything he could see. The ground was doing it.

The port was at the end of the water, under the mountain.

It was bigger than Kaldo. It was bigger than all of Kaldo, island and quays and long street and the new streets on the bank together, and it went up the slopes on three sides in tiers of dark stone with the harbour at its foot and the mountain behind it, smoking. There were quays of black stone the length of the lower town, and ships at them, real ships, coasters and three-masted traders and a great many smaller boats, and cranes, and the smell that Saban had put his head up at in the inlet mouth was everywhere now, sharp and yellow, a smell like struck matches that had been left in a hot room. Sulphur, Saban said, as if he were saying a price. There was sulphur on the ground here the way there was ash round a hearth.

They came up the inlet toward all of that in a black iron hull with a stack that had nothing coming out of it, and no sail set, and no banner, and no name on her anywhere.

The bells began before they were halfway.

It was one bell first, on the harbour wall, and then two more answered it from somewhere in the town, and then there were boats putting out from the quays, pulling hard, a dozen of them, and on the harbour wall there were men running. Fletcher brought her down to a crawl. He did it without being told. He had come into a great many strange harbours in his life, he said, and he had never yet been hanged in one, and the way not to be was to come in slower than anybody expected.

The boats came round her and lay off, a long cable away, and the men in them stared at her. They did not hail. They stared at her plating and her roof and her stack with nothing coming out of it, and at the ports along her sides with faces in them, and one of them stood up in his boat, which no man who lives in boats ever does, and shouted something across the water to the next boat that Emeric could not hear.

Saban could. Saban had spent his life hearing what men shouted across water.

"The story off the coasters," he said. "The one out of our water. A black ship that went up off the sea." He did not look round from the port. "They've heard it here. Nobody believed it. He's just told the man in the next boat that he believes it now."

By the time she came alongside there were knights on the quay.

There were forty of them at least, in plate, drawn up in two lines along the black stone with their helms on and their hands on their hilts and nothing drawn, and on the harbour wall behind them there were archers along the parapet, with their bows strung and held low. They were ready to welcome her or to fight her, and they had not yet been told which, and they stood very still.

A good many of them were women. Emeric saw it as the lines came closer under the port, and was not surprised, because he knew whose knights they were.

Fletcher laid her alongside the black quay so gently that her fenders hardly creaked, and men on the quay who had not been told whether to take her lines took them anyway, because they were harbour men and a line thrown was a line caught.

Then Emeric saw her.

She was standing at the end of the nearer line, a pace in front of it, as a captain stands, and she was in the armour he remembered.

It was the capital's armour, bright steel, engraved from the gorget to the knee with a fine running pattern that caught the grey light off the water. A half-cloak of blue hung from one shoulder. Her hair was black and pulled back hard from her face into a long tail that fell down her back, bound at intervals with rings of steel. There were two swords at her belt, one long and one short, and her hands were nowhere near either of them.

She was less changed than he had any right to expect. Ward had said she would not have changed, and Ward had been right. To the eye he had brought with him from another life she might have been forty, a hard forty, lean, weathered, with lines at the eyes. To her own people she would look exactly the age she was, which was a good deal more. She was broad through the shoulders, broader than a man would expect before he had seen her, and she stood with her weight on her back foot and square to nothing, not to the ship and not to him.

And she was shorter than he remembered her. She was exactly as tall as Ward had put her in the room, not a finger more or less.

He looked at Ward. Ward was looking at the quay. Emeric said nothing.

There was a scar down one cheek. It ran from below the eye to the line of the jaw, pale and straight and a little raised, and it had not been there when he was sixteen. He saw it and did not ask about it, and did not let his eyes stay on it, and his eyes went back to it anyway.

They went ashore cloaked.

He went first, and Ward a pace behind him, and Osric a pace behind Ward, the three of them in black cloaks with the hoods drawn up and closed at the throat, so that there was nothing to see of what was under them but the shape of a man and the toes of his boots. The helms were on their shelves in the hold. Their swords were at their hips under the cloth, as every knight's on that quay was at theirs, and nothing was drawn.

Behind them came Ferra, and Saban, and Imani.

He had wondered what the quay would make of the beads, and now he saw. They were meant to be looked at, and they were. Ferra's collar at her throat, a row of small lights against the dark of her coat; Saban's chain; the cuffs on Imani's wrists; they burned in the grey afternoon the way nothing else on that quay did, and every face in the harbour crowd behind the knights turned to them. People who had come down to see a black ship stood and looked at a woman's collar instead, because a woman wearing lit stones round her neck like pearls was a thing they had never seen either, and could understand better. There were people in that crowd, Emeric saw, who knew exactly what a lamp stone cost, and were pricing Ferra's collar in their heads, and could not make the sum come out.

Rowena did not give the beads a glance.

Her eyes were on the three hooded men in front. They were not on their faces, which she could not see, or on their hands. They were on their feet: on where each of them put his boots on the black stone, and how, and how much weight came down on it, as though she were reading through the cloaks the thing under them that was heavier than a man.

Saban saw it. Emeric saw him see it. Saban's face did nothing at all, and Emeric knew that it had gone into him and been filed as the first thing he knew about her.

Vantic had come ashore last, without beads, with her book held flat against her, and stood a little apart from them all on the quay, as if she had come off a different ship.

Emeric opened the sense.

He did it without thinking, because it was what he did on any ground he did not know. The quay was stone and the harbour was water and in the middle of all of it there were a great many people, and he wanted the lay of it: where they stood, how many, who was holding what, where the archers were on the wall and how far apart. So he let the medium open out of him, the way he had in the boat's hold at the Deep Channel and on every night since, and it went out from him across the black stone in a widening ring, finding bodies as it went, dense places in the thin air of the ground, forty knights and a crowd and the bows on the parapet and the men in the boats on the water behind.

The ring reached Rowena.

She stepped back.

It was half a pace. It was nothing that anybody else on that quay would have seen as anything but a woman shifting her weight. But Emeric felt the ring go over her, and felt her move under it, and he saw her hand go to the hilt of the long sword and close on it and come off it again, all in the time it takes to draw a breath.

She was looking straight at him, through the hood.

"Didn't know you'd gotten so skilled, Emeric," she said.

He had not given his name. He had not had time to give anything. She had known him on the gangplank, under a hood, ten paces off, after sixteen years; and he, looking at her, had only half known her until Ward's figure told him who she was.

And she had felt it. He had held the medium open near Vesk for years, and near Orrin and Armand, listening, and none of them had ever turned their heads. But he had never once pushed it out across a crowd with a mage-knight standing in it. She had felt it pass over her the way a man feels a draught from a door he did not see open, and had known what it was, or near enough, and had put her hand to her sword.

It was not covert. Not against the gifted. He put that away where he would find it again, and closed the medium down to nothing, and did not open it again on that quay.

The watch captain was a thick grey man in a leather coat who had been standing behind her with his mouth slightly open since the gangplank came down, and he found his voice now.

"Your Grace knows this man?"

"He's a prince of Randillia," said Rowena. She said it without taking her eyes off Emeric. "Harrold's third son. Amara's boy."

The watch captain looked at Emeric, and at the black ship behind him with no smoke from its stack, and at the three hooded men, and had plainly never in his life had a prince of Randillia arrive at his quay without a letter, and did not know what the book said to do about it.

"There's a letter," said Saban pleasantly, from behind. "It's in a coaster. It left our water a week ago, and it's about a fortnight behind us."

The watch captain looked at Saban, and then at the ship again, and did the sum, and did not like where it came out.

Rowena let go of whatever she had been holding in her face. It was not much. It was the kind of thing a captain lets go on a parade ground when she has decided a thing is not going to be a fight.

"They're mine," she said to the watch captain. "Put them in the harbour house. Under guard." She looked at Emeric one moment longer. "I'll send for you."

Then she turned and walked away down the black quay between the two lines of her knights, and they turned after her one by one without being told, and the bells on the wall stopped.

They were walked to quarters under guard, which Ward said was correct and Osric said was flattering.

The harbour house stood on the slope above the quays, and the way to it went through the edge of a yard. It was her practice yard. Emeric knew it the moment he came into it, because every knights' yard in the world was like every other: a long square of flagged stone behind a wall, with racks of blunt steel along one side and a water butt at the corner and sand in the joints between the flags, and at the far end of it two women in padded jackets working a drill slowly with long blades, over and over, while a grey knight watched them with his arms folded. It was old. The flags were worn hollow in the places where a hundred years of feet had planted.

He put his hand on the wall as they went along it.

It was nothing anybody would have seen. A man's hand trailing along a wall as he walked. But he let the sense out again, very low this time and very tight, down through his palm into the wall and through the wall into the flags of the yard, and nowhere else, the way he would have run a fingertip along a seam. Not across the yard. Not over anybody. Only into the ground. It told him what the stone told it: where the flags were bedded solid and where they were not, a loose one near the water butt that would rock under a heel, a patch by the butt itself that stayed damp and would be slick, a fall in the whole of the yard of half an inch toward a drain in the far corner that nobody walking on it every day would ever feel. He took it all, the way he took a map, and shut the sense again before the yard ended.

The quay had taught him that she could feel it. The yard had to have taught him something too, and it had.

From the harbour house's upper windows he could see the works.

They were along the foot of the mountain, a mile off, long sheds of black stone with their roofs blackened, and he could see heat going up off them in the air, shaking, the way it goes up off a road in summer. No smoke. No stack. No carts of charcoal on the road to them, and no woodyard, and no hillside stripped bare behind them for a day's ride, the way every hillside round Kaldo's foundry had been stripped before the coal came down from Grayrock.

He had come here for sulphur. He had known since the autumn that the sulphur would be here, lying on the ground round the mountain the way ash lies round a hearth, and that it would come down the coast in boats, and that it was worth the voyage.

What he had not expected to envy was the fire.

Every cell he had ever made, every stone Orrin had ever filled on his stool, the whole of the forge, the band, the ship standing at the quay below with nothing coming out of her stack, all of it was fuel, at the bottom. It was ten years of solving the one problem that everybody in the world had and nobody ever talked about, which was what to burn. And these people had never had to solve it. The ground burned for them. It had been burning for them since before there were people here to be grateful, and they built their furnaces against it the way a man builds his house against a hill, and did not think about it at all.

He stood at the window for a long time.

Imani came up behind him. She had been out on the balcony at the side.

"Drains," she said.

"What about them?"

"There aren't any. Not the kind you mean." She said it without heat. "There are channels in the streets, open, going downhill, and everything goes in them. I smelled it before I saw it, coming up from the quay. In this heat, in a town this crowded, on a hillside." She looked out at the tiers of dark stone going up toward the mountain. "They'll be burying children every summer, and they'll think it's the heat."

Ferra had gone to the harbour office.

It was her business, and she went to it as soon as the guard had shown them their rooms, taking one of the guard with her because she was not allowed to go without one. The office was at the head of the black quay, a long room with a counter and a clerk behind it and books on shelves behind the clerk, and it was the most familiar room she had been in all day.

The clerk was young and very correct and had plainly been told who the black ship belonged to, by somebody who had been told by the watch captain. He opened the harbour book and turned it round on the counter for her.

She wrote: *K Discovery. Out of Kaldo. Master, Fletcher. Eleven souls.* And the date, and her own name under it, and her office.

It was the first book the name had gone into but her own. She looked at it on the page for a moment, in a foreign book, in her hand.

Then the clerk wrote out a berthing warrant for the ship. It said where she might lie, and for how long, and under what dues, and it was in a form Ferra could have written herself in her sleep, and she read it through anyway because she read everything through. At the foot of it the clerk signed. And under his name, in the same hand, he wrote: *for the Treasurer, in her absence.*

Ferra looked at that line.

The Treasurer of Emberfell was not in the room. She was not on the quay. She was, the clerk said when Ferra asked, at her house in the upper town, and the clerk had signed for her, as he signed for her every day she was not there, because that was what the clerk's office was for.

It was a very small thing. It was a berthing warrant for a ship at a quay, and a clerk's name on it, and four words under the name. Ferra folded it and put it in her book.

She thought of the room at the end of the passage in Kaldo, with the window that faced south, and the three of them in it at their tables, copying. And of the long list in the drawer with the lock, of everything that waited for her, on top of everything else that waited. None of them could have written those four words. No clerk in Kaldo had ever signed anything for anybody in her absence, because there was nothing in Kaldo that could be signed in her absence at all.

The day before, in Kaldo, she had thought of it as a place where Kaldo stopped. Standing at a counter in a foreign harbour with a berthing warrant in her book, she thought of it, for the first time, as a problem, and not as a thing she was proud of.

She did not write that down either. She thanked the clerk and went back up the slope with her guard.

The herald came at dusk.

He came to the harbour house in a coat of dark red with a staff in his hand, and stood in the door of the long room where they were all sitting, and waited until everybody had stopped talking, which took some time because Osric was in the middle of a story.

Emeric stood.

The knight on the quay had been one woman, in bright steel with her hand on her sword. He did not know yet who would be sitting at the head of a table in her hall, and he understood, looking at the herald in his dark red coat drawing breath to speak, that he was about to find out, and that they would not be the same woman at all.
