Chapter 247 - The Bigger Room

Ferra priced the room on the twelfth of February, at the counting room table, the day after the stewards went home, with the page she had headed THE BIGGER ROOM in August open in front of her and nothing under the heading but a date.

"You said," she said, "that when there was a feed that came down every week in a tank to a pipe in that wall, and not before, you could cut it."

"You said."

"I said." She turned the gauge book round so that he could see it: the Hod's runs since November, one line a week, every week, without a gap, the vials and the canisters and the litres that had gone down the cellar stair and into the niche and through the pipe in the wall into the reservoir. Fifteen weeks. "That's a feed. I've counted it. It comes down every week in a tank to a pipe in that wall." She turned the book back. "So cut it."

She wrote the figure under the heading, the whole of it, labour and glass and brass and iron and Ivo's time and the barge and the lamps, in one line; and against it the line for what the old chamber had cost in stones drained to dust in its four years, which was longer. She ruled under both. She did not write anything in the margin.

"Nights," she said. "And you'll tell me when you start."



They started on the fifteenth, at the late bell, the three of them: Emeric, Ward and Bram, as they had cut the first room four years before, with the same picks and the same baskets and the same rule, that nothing came up the keep stair that two men could not carry and nobody in the castle should hear a sound.

The first room had been a wine cellar before it was anything, a barrel vault under the old keep's east end that the baron before last had filled with casks and the baron before that had used for salt. They had taken it down to its brick and put the forge in it and lived with it for four years: a chamber the size of a wine barrel in an iron frame, and the rails, and the bank of stones against the far wall that Ferra hated. Behind the bank the vault's end wall was rock, the keep's own rock, the spine of grey stone the castle stood on; and beyond the rock, thirty yards on and a little lower, was the old canal shaft, the long sloping tunnel the castle's builders had cut down through the hill to the canal ring for their stone barges, and that the works had cleared in the autumn to bring the plant's crates up in the dark.

The bigger room went between them.

It took three weeks. Emeric did the rock, a little each night, the way he had learned from Paskin's one grain at the Isle and Armand's rule about heat, and his own long years of moving small things with weight: he did not break the rock. He could not break rock; it was not his. He found the cracks in it with his sense, the old seams where the hill had settled a thousand years ago, and put his weight into them, along the seam, a little at a time, so that a block the size of a cask let go of the face and came away into his hands and he lowered it to the floor without a sound. Then the next. Low draw. A block an hour, some nights two, and a headache by three in the morning that Imani could read on his face at breakfast and did not mention.

Bram shored it as it went. He came down every night at the late bell with timbers on his shoulder and a mallet in his belt beside the crooked hammer, and set the props and the head boards so that nothing above them moved, and checked them with his palm every night when he came in, before he did anything else. Ward carried. The blocks went out by the canal shaft, not the stair, on a handcart, down to the canal ring and onto the castle-works barge, and the barge took them out in the dark to the new wharf below the works, where Bram's men found a pile of good grey building stone every morning and did not ask where it had come from, because the works had been finding things it did not ask about for nine years.

By the first week of March there was a room.

It was long and high and arched. Bram had lined it in brick in the last week himself, by lamplight, with Ward carrying the hod, a barrel vault the height of two men and a half, with a floor of dressed stone laid level to Declan's board; and where the vault met the old cellar's end, the wine cellar's vault and the new one opening out of it like a corridor into a hall, Bram had set a bulkhead of riveted iron into the arch, bolted to the rock, with a door in it.

The old door, the gravity door, the heavy plate set with its stones that only one man in Kaldo could open, still stood where it had always stood, at the top of the cellar's stair off the office. Nobody had touched it. Same door, Emeric had said, on the cellar step in August. Same key.



The forge went into the new room in March's second week, and it went in as a different thing.

The old chamber came down first. He took it apart himself on the eighth of March, the barrel-sized pot of iron and the frame it hung in and the rails and the lattice of stones in their anchors round it; and the one thing in all of it that mattered, the grey seed that sat in the brass seat under the floor and remembered every run he had ever written into it, he lifted out of its seat with his own hands and carried through the iron door into the bigger room in his two palms, the way a man carries a bird. The index. It went into a new seat under the new floor, in the middle, exactly, and he sat on the floor beside it in the lamplight for a while with his hand on the floor over it, feeling it wake to its new place, before he let anyone bring anything else in.

Then the rest. Over five nights.

A ring of gunmetal on the floor, round the seat, nine feet across, bolted to the stone. A grate over the catch basin inside it. Above it, the height of a tall man and a half, at the vault's spring, a second ring, the same, bolted through into Bram's iron bulkhead behind it. And between the two rings, standing on the one and hanging from the other, glass. Curved panes of it, each the height of the room from ring to ring, each a slice of a great cylinder, each of Ivo's toughened glass, the kind he poured in the works' annealing oven and cooled for a week so slowly that a man could stand on a pane laid flat and it would not crack. Ivo had been told they were for the winter garden, the trial bay, a column for the cartridge rig to stand in. He had made them to Bram's pattern without asking anything at all except how the curve was to be checked, and had checked it himself with a template on every pane.

The panes stood in the rings like the staves of a barrel, held by slim brass ribs between them and collars of gunmetal top and bottom with brass bolts through them; and one pane, at the front, was not a pane but a door: a curved glass leaf the full height of the tube, hung on brass hinges, with a brass hand wheel at its middle that drew a ring of bolts into the ribs when it was turned. It shut on a felt seal. It shut, Bram had made sure, of its own weight if nobody held it, and could not be opened from inside without the wheel.

Inside the tube, at the height of a man's chest, the gravity stones sat in brass cradles on brackets off the ribs, four of them, round the space where a billet would hang, cupped like hands. Below them, near the grate, two fire stones in iron cradles. Under the top collar, running round the inside of the glass, the gantry's circular rail, and on it the carriage with its arm and its head that went where the index sent it. Up through the top collar, the flue and the relief valves.

And against the brick at the tube's foot, wired into the base ring, a cabinet of brass the height of a man's waist with a gauge on its top and a row of small brass levers behind a glass front: the controls a man without the index could see and touch, so that what the forge was doing could be read off a dial by somebody who was not inside it.

Nine feet across. Nine feet high. Floor to vault. A glass room you could walk into, if the door was open and the stones were cold, and stand in the middle of, and look up through the gantry rail at the iron of the bulkhead.

Ferra came down to see it on the fourteenth of March, with her book under her arm, and stood in the arch of the iron door and did not come in for some time.

"It's glass," she said.

"All the way round."

"Why glass?"

"Because glass holds mana. Iron doesn't. The old chamber leaked; any mage who stood in the office above it on a run day could feel it through the floor, if he was paying attention. Armand could. Orrin could, once, and asked me if the cellar was damp." He put his hand flat on a pane. "This keeps it all in. What goes in to make the thing stays round the thing, and doesn't go through the floor into the office. And it means the stones can stand off from the work on every side, instead of pressing against the iron the way they had to in the old one." He took his hand away. "And it means you can see."

Ferra looked at the tube, and the room round it, and the cabinet with its levers, and the workbench Bram had built against the side wall with the tool rack over it, and the brick vault going away into the lamplight.

"It's beautiful," she said, which she did not say about things; and then, before he could answer: "What does the wheel cost? The brass one on the door. That's not in my figure."

"Bram made it out of the old chamber's frame."

"Good," said Ferra, and came in.



The inducers stood against the right-hand wall, in a row, three of them. Mana inducers, he had written at the top of the drawing in October, because what they did, when it came to it, was put mana back into a place that had lost it.

He had had them built over the winter in Bram's long shop and brought down in pieces, for this room, from the drawing he had made in October: three tall cylinders each the height of a man, a head of domed gunmetal on each, bolted down, with a gauge on top and a copper pipe going up from it into the vault's pipework; and under the head, a body of thick glass in brass bands, full to its shoulder of the blue. Liquid mana, the Band brought down a vial at a time. In the cellar, where nobody came who was not sworn, they wore no iron jackets; they stood bare against the brick and glowed, a soft cold blue that came up off the floor and lay on the curve of the forge's glass, and made the room look as if it stood at the bottom of a clear sea.

From their bases, braided copper hoses ran down to brass valves in a lidded trench in the floor, and under the floor to the forge's base ring. That was what they were for, most days: a store. The forge drew from them through the valves, as the old chamber had drawn from a flask, and they were filled again from the niche's reservoir through the same valves, from the other side, when the Hod came down.

The other thing they were for was on each head, beside the gauge: a little dome of brass, and under it a flared brass horn coming out of the cap's side.

"Show me," said Ferra.

"Everybody out," said Emeric.

They went out onto the cellar stair: Ferra, Bram, Ward, and Imani, who had come down at four because Ferra had sent a runner. He shut the iron door on them, and stood alone in the bigger room with the forge cold and the inducers glowing, and took a stone out of his pocket.

It was a cheap stone. A lamp stone, small and cloudy, the kind that sold at Grayrock's market for a copper and lit a privy. He held it in his palm and pushed his mana into it.

A stone took what it could take, and stopped; that was the rule of every stone ever cut. Nothing overloaded on its own. It had to be made to. He made it. He pushed past the place where the stone said full, past where it began to hum in his palm and go hot and bright, past where it began to shake; he pushed the way the grey mages had pushed theirs on the stream ground in the war, three hundred of them in a quarter of an hour, and the world had gone out round him. The stone went white. It sang.

He opened his hand and let it go, and stepped back.

It burst on the floor with a crack like a dropped plate, and a wind went through the room that moved nothing. Not a hair. Not the lamp flames, not the dust on Bram's bench. The cellar's lamps were stone lamps, every one; and every one of them went out at once, and the room was dark except for the inducers.

Then the inducers began to count.

He could hear it in the dark. Each head had its latch under the brass dome: a little air stone that kept a leather bellows full, and the bellows held a spring-loaded steel spike cocked back from a disc of glass in the throat of the tank. In a dead zone the air stone stopped. The bellows began to go slack, slowly, with a small sighing sound in the dark, one, two, three. On the third count the catch on each head let go, all three together, with a snap; and three steel spikes drove forward on their springs, and three discs of glass broke.

The blue came out of the horns.

It came out as a liquid for the space of a breath and then it was not a liquid, it was the air: three plumes of it going up out of three brass horns and spreading into the room, a cold blue mist that went bright as it met the dark and then faded as it spread, into all of the room, the vault and the floor and the glass tube and him; and he felt it go into him like breath on a cold morning, and felt the dead place the stone had made fill up round him, and fill, the way a hole in wet sand fills when the tide comes.

The lamps came on. All of them, one after another along the brick, as the mist reached them. The room was blue, all of it, every surface, for the length of a breath, as if he were standing inside the inducers' glass; and then the blue faded into the lamplight, and the room was the room again, and the lamps burned yellow on their brackets, and the three tanks against the wall had each lost a hand's breadth of their blue, and their horns dripped.

He went and opened the iron door.

They were on the stair in a row, where he had left them, and they had felt it: the wind that moved nothing had gone through the iron and up the stair past them, and then the other thing after it.

"How long?" said Ferra.

"From the stone to the lamps? The count of ten."

"And without them?"

"A day. Less, here, with the rock all round." He looked back at the tanks. "But a day in a dead zone with a run on is a ruined run, and the index sleeping, and the door that only I can open stuck shut on whoever's inside."

Ferra wrote. She was writing before he had finished: the level the tanks had dropped, which she had read off the gauges as she came in, and what that was in litres, and what a litre was in Hod runs.

"That's a litre and a half," she said. "For one stone. A copper's worth of stone cost a litre and a half." She looked up. "Don't do it for fun."

Bram was not looking at the gauges. He was looking at the broken discs in their throats, where the spikes had gone through them.

"How fast," said Bram, "can Ivo make those?"



The first thing the bigger room made was not a wheel.

He had meant it to be. He had meant the first run in the new tube to be the thing that could not have been made in the old one: a wheel for the express, or the matrix of the ship in Fletcher's slip, the big things, to show what nine feet across was for. Ferra had asked him which it would be, at the table, and he had said the matrix, and Imani, at the end of the table, had said nothing at all.

Then he had gone down to the ward on the ninth with the gloves' formers, and seen the tray.

It was the instrument tray in the cutting room, the one the surgeons took from, and it was half empty. He had stood at the cutting room door and looked at it, and at the nurse sharpening a blade on a stone at the bench because there was not another one to take from the tray, and counted. The war had used half of what the ward had. The smallpox had used half of what was left. Bram's shop made a good blade, by hand, one at a time, a day for a good one, and the shop was building a ship.

So the first run was the ward's.

He wrote it into the index on the afternoon of the fifteenth: one build plate, a sheet of steel the size of a door, laid on the grate; and on it, in rows, every instrument the ward used, laid out side by side like type in a printer's forme. Two hundred scalpel blades in four sizes and a curved one. Forceps, long and short, toothed and smooth. Needle holders. Artery clamps, three patterns, in fifties. Retractors. Probes. Scissors. Every one of them out of the stainless the open works was now pouring by the hundredweight, laid on the plate as powder and wire, in the shape it was to be.

Then he shut the glass door and turned the wheel and put his hand on the cabinet's plate, and the tube filled.

It went blue, as the inducers' mist had gone blue, from the floor up, a cold light rising inside the glass; and the gravity stones in their cradles woke and the fire stones under the grate woke, and the build plate rose off the grate and hung in the middle of the tube, level, still, a hand's breadth from nothing; and the light inside the glass went from blue to white to a colour that had no name; and the gantry head went round on its rail above the plate, and round, and round, and the plate under it glowed.

It took an hour. Imani stood beside him at the cabinet the whole hour, with her arms folded and her eyes on the glass.

When the light went down and the plate came down onto the grate and he opened the door on the cooling air, the plate was covered. Row on row, side by side, bright, sharp, every edge true. Two hundred blades. A hundred and fifty clamps. Everything. A year of the ward's instruments, in an hour, on a sheet of steel the size of a door.

Imani went into the tube before it was cool enough to and picked up a blade by its handle with a cloth round her fingers and looked along its edge in the lamplight, and then at the plate, at all of it, and then at him.

"Every year," she said. "Like this. The ward's whole year. On this plate. In an hour."

Ferra, behind her at the door, had already turned to a clean page.

"I could," said Emeric.

Imani waited.

"And that's the trap." He looked at the plate. "If I make the ward's blades in here every year, the day this room breaks, or somebody takes it, or a stone goes over in the castle above and puts a dead zone in it for a week, Kaldo doesn't know how to make a scalpel. Bram's shop will have stopped. The man who knew how will be dead. The ward will be sharpening the last blade on a stone at the bench, the way your nurse was this morning." He picked up a blade himself. "This plate's because the ward's short now. Today. The master blade goes up to Bram's shop tomorrow, and his men cut a die from it, and the next thousand come off the die in the open works, by people who've never been in this cellar and never will be. Then the ward's blades don't need me."

"And the next time we're short?"

"Then it's here," he said. "For when you can't wait."

Imani looked at the blade in her cloth for a while longer. Then she put it back on the plate in its row, exactly where it had been, as she put her own instruments back in their case.

"For when we can't wait," she said, and took the plate.



Bram replayed the first run that night.

It was the thing the old index could not have done, and the new one could, because he had rebuilt the index's seat and taught the seed in it one new trick in the delve while the room was being dug: to keep. Every run he wrote into it now, it kept, whole, as a run, and not only as a memory of how he had done it. And a kept run could be started again. Not by him. By any delver's hand on the cabinet's plate, who knew which one to ask for.

Bram had been the first of the six to delve, before any of the others; he had been in the room more than anybody but Emeric. He put his black-seamed hand on the brass plate at nine that night with a fresh build plate on the grate and a heap of the open works' powder and wire on it, and shut his eyes, and asked.

The tube went blue.

Emeric stood back against the brick with his arms folded and did nothing at all, and the run went exactly as it had gone that afternoon, an hour, the light and the gantry and the glow; and at the end of the hour the plate came down covered, and Bram opened his eyes and looked at it through the glass and did not say anything for a while.

"Without you," he said.

"Without me."

"It doesn't need you here." Bram took his hand off the plate and looked at it, his own hand. "Four years I've been waiting on your nights. A part wants making, I write it on the board, and it waits till you've a night. Some of them waited a month." He looked at the tube. "It doesn't need you."

"Only to write it the first time."

"Only to write it the first time," said Bram, and Emeric saw him understand what that meant for the board in his shop, with its list of parts in chalk that had waited a month; and saw something come into the old smith's face that had not been in it since the first night he had walked into the delve and seen the room.

He put that second plate on the open works' shelf in the morning for the Grayrock trainees to learn the instruments on, when they came.



The gloves were the same week.

He had made the formers first, before any of it, on the old chamber's last night before he took it down: two hands of fired clay, glazed white, on stands, each one the shape of Imani's own two hands to a hair's breadth, from the outlines he had drawn round them in November on the long room's bench while she slept and from her measurements along each finger. The forge had cast them in a quarter of an hour. He had taken them down to the ward the next morning, with the pail from the Isle, and that was how he had seen the tray.

The rest was Tess's.

She did it in bay three, because the bay had the warm room for the drying and the sulphur from the powder works and nobody else wanted to be near the carbide; and because Imani, when he had told her what the sap could do, had said that she did not want him doing it, she wanted it done by somebody who would do it again next month without him, and had looked at Tess. The sap from the Isle had been kept in its pail with a little ammonia from the gas works stirred in, as he had told Ferra to have it kept, so that it would stay milk and not set. Tess warmed the formers in the oven, and dipped them in a bath of something that made the milk cling, and then into the milk, slowly, to the wrist, and lifted them out, slowly, so that the white skin on them ran even, and dried them; and dipped them again; and then cooked them, in the drying room, with the sulphur, for an hour, so that the white skin went from a thing that would go sticky in the sun and brittle in the cold to a thing that would do neither.

Three dips. An hour's cure. She peeled them off the formers on the twentieth with a dusting of chalk, turning them inside out as they came, and they came off whole.

Two gloves. Thin, the colour of cream, so thin that the lamp shone through them, and they stretched.

Imani put them on in the dispensary that evening with the door shut. She put them on slowly, the right and then the left, and drew them up over her wrists, and held her hands up in front of her face in the lamplight and turned them over, and flexed her fingers, and picked up a needle off the bench, the finest she had, and threaded it, first time, without looking.

"I can feel the eye," she said.

"They're thin enough to."

"I can feel the eye of the needle. Through it." She put the needle down and pressed her gloved fingertips together, and apart, and together. "Nothing gets through?"

"Nothing. Not blood. Not water. Not what you can't see in either."

She looked at her hands a while longer. Then she took the gloves off, carefully, inside out, as Tess had taken them off the formers, and laid them on the bench side by side, and looked at them there.

"Spring," she said. "You said spring. On the card."

"It's the first day of spring."

"It's the first day of spring," said Imani, and took the card out of her bodice, where she had carried it since midwinter, and put it on the bench beside the gloves; and he saw that it was soft at the corners now, and that the pencil of her two hands on it had worn faint, from being carried against her, for three months.

"Twelve pairs," she said. "For a start. And Tess teaches two of my nurses to dip them, so that when the pail's empty and the next one comes, it's not Tess either." She looked at him. "And you'll not use them on anybody till I've had them on for a week and found what's wrong with them."

"Something will be wrong with them."

"Something always is," said Imani. "That's why it's a week."



Vantic's letter came on the twenty-sixth, by the post from the capital, in the House's grey wax with the House's mark.

It was a single sheet, in her small, perfectly level hand, and it was addressed to him and not to Ferra, which was the first thing he noticed; and it did not begin with a greeting, which was the second.

The House understood, she wrote, from its correspondent in Kaldo, that Kaldo had installed in a place she would not name a set of three vessels, each holding a quantity of a liquid she would not name, each made to discharge itself entirely in an emergency she would not name, at a cost per discharge that the House's correspondent put at a litre and a half for the trial alone. The House did not ask what any of it was. It asked what it saved. A vessel that cost a known sum to fill and discharged itself in an unknown circumstance was a liability on the books until somebody could say what loss it prevented and how often; and if His Grace could put a figure to the second, the House would be glad to know it, for its own understanding, and would not repeat it.

She was, she wrote, his servant in all things that could be priced.

And under her signature, after a space, one more line, in the same level hand:

You will not know the figure. You will have built three of them anyway, because the first time it fires and saves the thing it saves you will not want to have been the man who priced it first. I would have built them too. I would not have told the House. L.

He read it twice at the counting room table. Then he put it down and looked at it.

Ferra read it over his shoulder. She had come in behind him as he opened it, as she came in behind him at most letters now, and read every line of it with her chin nearly on his shoulder, and when she reached the last line she straightened up.

"Who's her correspondent?" she said.

"I don't know."

"I do," said Ferra. "It's me. I wrote the litre and a half in the gauge book. She's had a man reading my gauge book." She did not sound angry. She sounded, if anything, impressed. "Through the House's audit. She's entitled. I signed the terms." She looked at the letter again. "She'd have asked the same question if she were sitting here. What it saves."

"Would you?"

"I did ask it," said Ferra. "On the stair, on the fourteenth. You told me a ruined run and the index asleep and the door shut on whoever's inside. That's not a figure." She took the letter off the table and read the last line again, and her mouth did something at the corner. "She reads you better than you'd like."

"She always did."

"Write back," said Ferra, "and tell her she's right, and you'll send her a figure the first time it fires and saves something. And thank her for the line." She put the letter down. "And then I'll find out which of my clerks lets a House man look at my book on audit days without telling me."



The yard had been waiting for the bigger room since February, and the bigger room had been waiting for the yard.

Hull three was to be the first of a class. Not a ship; a set of lines, one hull drawn once and built again and again, two hundred and forty-six feet from stem to stern and forty-eight across, longer than the old shed; and she was to be built not plank by plank on a slip as Fletcher had built every hull in his life but in blocks, welded, the hull cut into great slices on the yard floor and each slice built on its own and the slices brought together on the slip and joined, so that two slips could work at once and the class could come off them like loaves. And she was to be a freighter, first, before any of the others were anything else, so that every mistake the class had in it was made on a cargo ship that could carry sulphur along the sea lane in the daytime, and not on a ship that had to fight.

The secret dock could not hold her. The covered shed by the lake could hold her keel and nothing else. So in March the yard grew: two new slips on the lake's shore beside the covered shed, open to the sky, where any ship could be built that any shipwright on the coast might build; with the secret dock beyond them, round the point under the cliff, as the third slip that nobody counted.

The shore under the new slips was a shelf of rock, and Fennick blasted it.

He blasted the first half on the fourth of March, a long careful day of holes and charges and sand-bag tamping, and it went exactly as he said it would and the rubble went where he had said, and he stood on the shore afterward with his short fingers laced over his belly and looked at it with deep content. On the sixth, Paskin came down from the railhead for two days on his way to the holdings' first water survey and did the second half of the shelf, the seaward half, with his hands, in an afternoon.

Fennick stood on the shore and watched him do it.

He did not say anything at all. He went home at the end of the afternoon without a word to anybody, and the next morning his crew said he had sat up till three drawing a better charge pattern for a rock shelf, a pattern that would have done the seaward half in a morning; and when Emeric saw it, on the board in the quarry hut, it would have.

The keel of hull three went down in the covered shed on the twentieth. Gas-cut plate. Every frame of her was cut from plate with the torch Tess had lit in bay three a month before, along lines chalked from Fletcher's patterns, by yard hands who had been put on the torch from their first week and had learned it from Tess and from each other: a bright fan of sparks along a chalk line, and the plate falling open, and the edge clean enough to weld without dressing. It took a day to cut what would have taken a fortnight with saws.

She had a name before her keel did. Ferra had written it in the book in February under the class's heading, without asking anybody, because a ship that carried sulphur in the daytime would need a name the coast could say without wondering about it. The K Endeavor. Fletcher read it, and said it was a good name for a freighter and a better one for a man who had to build two hundred and forty-six feet of hull in a shed made for a ship a third that length, and went back to his patterns.



It was Fletcher who saw the roof.

He saw it on the twenty-second, two days after the keel, standing on the shed floor at the keel's after end with his hands in his pockets, looking up. He was looking up because a shipwright looked along every line he could see, always, even a roof's; and he stood there through the whole of a bell, and then he walked to the shed's other end, and looked up from there, and then walked back to the middle and lay down on his back on the keel blocks and looked up from there.

Emeric found him there.

"It's dropped," said Fletcher, from the blocks.

Emeric looked up.

The shed's roof was carried on trusses, a row of them across the shed from wall to wall, great triangles of timber that Fletcher had built in the fourth year for one timber hull, with a tie beam along the bottom of each and the rafters above. Since then a great deal had been hung off them that nobody had drawn into them. The sail loft, which Abloh had built into the roof's space in the second summer of the shed because there was room, and filled with canvas and felt and cutting tables. The hoist rails, three of them, that ran the length of the shed under the tie beams and carried the chain hoists that had lifted every engine and every stone matrix into every hull. The lamp lines. The K Discovery's tackle, when she was in.

The middle trusses had sagged. From the floor, standing, he could not see it. Lying on the keel blocks beside Fletcher, sighting along the tie beams from one end of the shed to the other against the lamps, he could: the middle of the row lower than the ends, a long shallow belly, as if the roof had taken a breath and not let it out.

"A hand," said Fletcher. "Near enough. In the middle. Since the loft went up, I'd say, and more since the hoists." He did not move from his back. "Abloh's chalk's been rolling across his loft floor since November. He said his floor had grown a slope. I said he'd had too much of Maren's pudding." He looked at the roof. "He hadn't."

"Posts," said Emeric. "A row down the middle. Under the tie beams. Iron, on pads."

Fletcher turned his head on the keel block and looked at him.

"In my slip," he said.

"Down the middle."

"Down the middle of my slip. Where two hundred and forty-six feet of hull's going to be built in slices the width of the shed, and swung on the hoists, and brought together." Fletcher looked back up at the roof. "You'll not put a post in my slip. Not one. I'd sooner build her in the rain."

Bram had come into the shed while they were lying there, with a coil of rope over his shoulder for the hoists, and was standing over them, looking down at the two of them on their backs on the keel blocks, and then up at the roof.

"Pull it up," said Bram.

They both looked at him.

"From underneath." Bram took the rope off his shoulder. "A bowstring. You don't hold a bow up from the floor, you string it, and the string holds it in its shape." He held the rope out between his two fists, slack, and then pulled it taut. "A rod. Under each beam. Fast at both ends, at the walls, where the truss sits. Down under the middle of the beam, on a little stool of iron, so it pushes up there. Then you tighten the rod, and the rod pulls the ends in, and the stool pushes the middle up." He let the rope go slack. "A quarter turn a day on the nuts. Till it's level." He looked down at Fletcher. "No post."

Fletcher looked up at the roof for a while.

"No post," he said.

"Tension rod reinforcement," said Emeric, from the keel blocks. "That's what it's called. A trussed beam." He looked up at the roof, and saw it, all of it, in a breath, as Bram had seen it with a rope: the rod under each beam, the iron saddle in the middle, the anchors at the ends. "A pair of iron plates at each end of every beam, wedged against the wood, with a strap over the top. A rod through them, threaded, with two nuts on each end so one locks the other. And in the middle, a hook bolt through the beam and a saddle of iron for the rod to bear on, so it pushes up where it bends." He looked at Bram. "It's yours. You thought of it."

"I thought of a bow," said Bram.

"That's what it is."

They put it in over the last week of March, beam by beam, Bram's men on ladders with the plates and the rods, Fletcher's men holding the beams; and when the plates were on and the rods were through and the saddles were bolted, they began on the nuts. A quarter turn a day, every nut, every morning, at seven, by Bram's watch.

The collars that held the rods' anchor plates together at the beam ends were welded, by the yard's two new welders, in the bright torch light up on the ladders; and Emeric went up a ladder beside one of them on the second morning, with Fletcher's leave, and laid one seam himself on the last collar of the middle truss, with his own fire. Not the torch. A thread of flame off the end of his finger, held, steady, the way he had held a candle flame steady behind a shutter in the first winter, run along the joint between two plates of iron until the edges ran together and joined.

The welder beside him on the next ladder watched him do it, and then looked at his own seam on the next collar, laid with the torch, and then at Emeric's.

"Yours is better, Your Grace," he said. "Smoother."

"Yours is the one that'll be laid next year," said Emeric, "when I'm not on a ladder."

The welder considered that, and nodded, and went back to his torch; and Emeric came down the ladder with his finger sore to the second knuckle and a headache starting, and stood on the floor and looked up at the two seams side by side. Both would hold. His had cost him a morning. The welder's had cost a bottle of gas and nobody's headache.

Tess hung a plumb line from the ridge on the first day, from the middle truss, a cord with a brass bob on its end that hung down to a mark on the floor; and every day, after the quarter turn, she rolled her chair out onto the shed floor and looked at where the bob hung against the mark, and drew a chalk line on the floor beside it.

The lines crept. A hair a day. In a fortnight the bob hung over the first mark, and the belly was gone from the roof, and lying on the keel blocks sighting along the tie beams against the lamps, Fletcher could see nothing but a straight line from one end of the shed to the other.

From the lane, where the town walked past the shed every day on its way to the lake, they had seen men on ladders mending an old roof. Nobody stopped to look.



The matrix went through the glass on the twenty-ninth of March.

It was the run he had meant to be first. Hull three's whole gravity matrix: every stone mount and every sleeved rail and every bracket of the lattice that would hold her stones in the shape that would lift two hundred and forty-six feet of loaded freighter, all of it in one load, in one run, in the nine-foot tube, where the old chamber had made it one stone mount at a time over a month for the K Discovery and over two months for hull two.

It took a night. He was at the cabinet the whole of it with his hand on the plate and Bram beside him; and Ferra on the cellar step outside the iron door with her book on her knees, because she had said that if a run was going to cost what this one was going to cost she would be in the building when it did; and at four in the morning the tube's light went down and the build plate came down onto the grate, and on it, in a tangle that only made sense when he looked at it with the index's eye, lay a ship's skeleton of mounts and rails, folded on itself, every piece true.

He told the index to keep it. A run kept was a run that did not need him to be written again. Every hull of the class would have this matrix, to the hair; Bram could put his hand on the plate and ask for hull four's, and hull five's, and have them.

After it, before he let the room go cold, he wrote one more, small, that had been on the board since February: the head tanks' glass bodies. Four of them, each the size of a cask, with a neck and a flange, in the glass that held mana; for the K Discovery and hull two and the capital lift hull and the Hod, so that each could have its own inducer at its latch the way the cellar had three against the wall. Ivo could blow a vial and a flask and a lamp glass. He could not blow a vessel that size without a seam, and a seam was where a vessel failed. The tube made the four of them in an hour, seamless, in a row on the plate, and they came out clear and thick and ringing faintly when Bram tapped one with his crooked hammer.

Ferra came in through the iron door at a quarter to five with the book under her arm and the dawn still an hour off, and looked at the plate with the matrix on it, and at the four glass bodies in their row.

"What did it cost?" she said.

He told her. She wrote it down.

"Per run," she said. "Not per piece. Per run. I want every run costed from tonight, in its own line, so that I can tell the difference between a run that's worth it and one that's a man in a cellar showing off."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's the book." She closed it. Then she looked past the tube, at the arch of the old wine cellar, and the stair going up out of it to the office, and the iron door, and came back to the plate, where the matrix lay folded on itself, the length of two men when it was opened out.

"How," said Ferra, "are you going to get that out of a wine cellar?"

"Down the canal shaft. On the barge. At night."

"And a wheel? When you make the express's wheels? You said that's what nine feet was for." She looked at the iron door, which was the width of one man. "Those come out of here on the barge too?"

"Everything does. That's why the room's at this end."

Ferra looked at the room's far end, where the brick vault ran on past the tube toward the rock, and where, he knew she knew, thirty yards of rock away, the canal shaft came down through the hill.

"You've cut a door," she said slowly, "into the canal shaft."

"Not yet. Next week. Bram's planned it. An iron door, like the bulkhead, in the rock, that looks like rock from the shaft."

"That's not in my figure."

"No."

Ferra opened the book again, and turned back to the page headed THE BIGGER ROOM, and looked at the line she had written on the ninth of March, the whole of it, labour and glass and brass and iron, ruled off. Then she wrote under the ruled line, small, a second figure, and against it, for the first time on that page, something in the margin.

He leaned over to read it.

A DOOR. BRAM'S. CHEAP, OR ELSE.
