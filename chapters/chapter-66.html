Chapter 66 - Ash

Ferra had the count by the evening of the first day, and she read it to him at her own table, with the book open between them and Bram's chair empty by the stove, because Bram was still across the river at bay three, putting out the last of the sparks in his rafters. She read it straight through, line by line, and did not look up until she had finished.

Kaldo's dead were nine.

The gate-crew man from the first cart. Two of the townsmen Ward had given spears to, a carter's son and a man who had kept bees on the west side, whose names she read out in full, because she had them. Two of the six at the powerhouse. Harl. The second man from the hut at the bend, found that afternoon by the men Ward sent up the road, on the floor inside the door with the crossbow beside him and three bolts gone out of the quiver. And two of the nine who had stayed in the dock quarter, an old net-mender and his brother, found together in the ashes of their loft, which had been their father's.

The coast man from the powerhouse door made ten, and Ferra had him on a line of his own, under the nine, because he was nobody's in Kaldo and she had not known where else to put him.

The wounded were thirty-four, by her count, and she had the count by name: burns, mostly, from the bucket line and the bridge foot; cuts; two broken arms; Declan's leg; the townsman with the side wound; the children coughing in their borrowed houses from the smoke. And Tess.

The wall-crew man from the hut was not on the list of the dead. He had walked into the gate yard at noon, down the river road, with his left arm bound up in his shirt and the telegraph key in his pocket. He had held it until they broke the door, he said, and sent what he could, and then gone out through the back of the hut, where the shepherd's old wall had fallen, and lain in the reeds until they left. He had not seen the second man die. He had heard it.

Grayrock's dead, carried or counted, were fifty-two. Three were the hired mages the bows had found by their rings, and Ferra had them on a line of their own, without names. There were thirty-one prisoners in the old guardroom under the gate, most of them levies from Grayrock's farms who had sat down in the mud of the moorings and waited, and three of them were boys. Two were the other hired mages, the ones who had held their hands up.

Ferra closed the book.

"Seven hundred and ninety-eight," she said. "Living in the town. That's the seven hundred and ninety-one, and the seven of the nine. They've nowhere to go back to. There's nothing there." She looked at the book, shut, under her hands. "I walked it this afternoon. You can stand at the river and see the old works from the bridge. There's nothing in between."

Then she turned to a fresh page.



That night, and every night after it, the arc lamps came on along the wall at dusk.

Somebody had to be at the lever. Emeric went down to the powerhouse the first night to do it himself, and found the river door hung again on new hinges, and the floor scrubbed, and the belt laced where it had been cut, and a stool inside the door between two buckets, full. Bram was sitting on it.

He did not get up when Emeric came in. He looked at the dynamo, humming on its bed, and at the two white arrows on the post, and at the lever.

"She'll want to know it ran," he said. "Every night. When she asks."

"She'll ask."

"Then it'll have run." Bram folded his arms. "Go home."



Ward took him across the bridge on the second morning to see where the stone had broken.

It was in the road at the far end, a few paces short of where the bridge came onto the bank, where the levies had formed in the night. There was nothing there to show for it but a ring of grey dust on the cobbles, about the width of a cartwheel, fine as flour, that the wind had not yet taken. In the middle of the ring a few grains of something glittered, very faintly, and then did not.

"A big one," Ward said.

Emeric crouched and touched the dust with one wrapped finger. It was cold. A stone drained to nothing went to dust. He had never seen one that had been made to.

"He brought one," Ward said. "Next time somebody will bring three."

He did not wait for an answer. He walked on up into the burned lanes, slowly, looking at everything, and Emeric followed him. There was not much to look at. Chimneys standing up out of black rubble. The ore yard wall. The wharf, scorched along its landward edge and whole along the river, its pale deck gone grey with ash. Here and there a family from the town, picking through what had been a house, with a sack. The barge the coast woman had put against the middle pier was still there, on its side, with the river running through it. A boy from the net lofts was standing in the middle of a black square that had been somebody's floor, holding his slate against his chest, looking at nothing. Emeric did not know whether it was the same boy. It might have been any of them.



Tess woke on the second day, and did not say anything for most of it.

She lay on her side on a bed by the far wall of the ward, where Imani had moved her, with the long row of stitches under its linen down her back and a rolled sack behind her to keep her off it. She drank what she was given. She slept. When she was awake she looked at the wall.

On the afternoon of the second day she asked for her bench book.

Emeric brought it up from the powerhouse himself, where it had lain inside the door all night, with her pen and a pot of ink, and put them on the bed by her hand, and she opened the book to the first clean page and wrote the date at the top, in her careful hand, and under it, after looking at the page, she wrote: Feet. Nothing.

She showed it to him.

"I can't feel them," she said. "Since I woke. Not the feet. Not the legs. I've been trying all day." She said it as she would have reported that a coil had come out a turn short. "I can't move them either."

Imani was at the next bed. She had heard. She came and sat on the edge of Tess's bed, and took one of Tess's feet in her hand, and pressed the sole of it with her thumb, hard, and then pricked it with the point of a needle from her sleeve, and watched Tess's face. Tess watched the wall.

"It may come back," Imani said. "I've seen it come back. When the swelling goes down. Weeks, sometimes."

Tess turned her head on the pillow and looked at Emeric.

He had seen the inside of the knuckle where the blade had stopped. He had felt where the blade stopped, and in the room he had built what lay behind it, and Imani had watched him build it. He did not know everything a book could have told him, and he did not know what this world's mana did to a body that the other world's books had never had to account for. He knew what the blade had reached.

"I don't know," he said. It was true, and it was not enough, and Tess heard both of those things.

She held his eyes. Then she turned back to the page, and under those two words she wrote the date again, and a line, and left the space under it empty, for tomorrow.



Sela had the dock quarter's children in the school's long room on the third morning, all twenty-two of them, on the front benches where she had sat them in May to give each of them a house. Emeric stood at the back and watched her count them, aloud, one name at a time, and every name answered.

Three were still coughing from the smoke. A girl had her hand bound where a spark had caught it at the bucket line, where she should not have been and had been anyway. The boy from the net lofts sat at the end of the bench with his slate on his knees and his house's number on the back of it.

None of them had a house to go back to across the river. Every one of them had a door in the town with a number on Ferra's list, and the doors had held.

"Twenty-two," Sela said, and wrote it on her own slate, under the number from May, which was the same.



They buried Kaldo's dead on the third day, in the churchyard, in daylight, at the barony's cost, with their names in Doran's book.

Doran said the words over each of them, and the same words over the coast man, who had no name in the book but the one the woman with the grey plait gave Doran at the graveside, and Doran wrote it in. The woman stood at the edge of the grave with the long thin one beside her, and did not cry, and when it was done she came to Emeric at the gate and said that her friend's days were owed to the end of the week, and that she would take them to his mother, who lived at the coast and would want them more than she wanted him back in a sack. Ferra paid them that afternoon, to the day.

Harl went into the ground last. Bram stood at the head of the grave in a clean shirt Emeric had never seen him wear, with his hands hanging, and did not speak, and when Doran asked whether anybody would, Bram shook his head. The mill's men stood behind him. A few of the farmers had come in from the west side, and Hollis was among them, with his hat in his hands. At the gate afterward Hollis said, to nobody in particular, that Harl had ground his corn thirty years and never once given him short weight, and put his hat on.

That evening Emeric saw Doran going down the lane into the lower ward with a sealed letter in his hand.

He thought he knew whose it was. Doran had not told him; Doran would not have. But he had seen the gate-crew man in the church on the night of the warning, bent over his slate, and he had seen him fall back against the wheel. Doran went down the lane without hurrying and stopped at a door near the bottom of it, a door like any other, and knocked, and waited, and when it opened he went in.

Emeric did not follow him. He went back up the hill.



Renner went home on the fourth day.

They had laid him in the church, in the side chapel, on a trestle, washed and in a clean shirt, with his burned coat and his broken plate set beside him. The bolt was not with them. Ward had worked it out of the plate himself and put it away in the guardroom, and said that Kaldo kept its iron. Emeric had looked at the plate. The hole the bolt had made was square and clean, as small as a thumbnail on the front, and on the back it was not small. Anybody who looked at it would see a fire mage's bolt, and wonder how a fire mage had put it through steel at that range. He thought about keeping the plate back, and decided that keeping it would say more than sending it. He hoped they would go on wondering.

Hale wrote the letter. There was a form for it, he said, for sending a lord's body home to his house after a private war, and there had been no occasion to use it in his time or his predecessor's, and he wrote it out correct to the last stroke, addressed to the baron's widow at Grayrock, with the barony's seal. It said what had happened, and when, and where, and by whose order. It did not say how.

Hale wrote the other letter too, the one to the crown, as Emeric asked him to, which was what the page in the drawer said anyway. Emeric read it twice before it was sealed. It was plain, and long, and every line of it was true, and it went north by the road that afternoon, with a copy to go down the river by the salt boatman and a third to go north again by the road a week behind the first.

The body went up the river road on the barony's cart, with the prisoners walking behind it, unbound, and a garrison man in front with a white cloth on a pole. Ward had wanted to keep the prisoners. Emeric had asked him what for.

"Witnesses," Ward said.

Emeric said that Hale's letter was witness enough, and twenty-nine more mouths to feed were not, and Ward did not argue, and they went.

The two hired mages did not go with them. Nobody at Grayrock had paid to have them back, and a mage sent home unbound was a mage you met again. They stayed in the guardroom under the gate with their hands tied behind them. One of them had talked since the moorings, to anybody, about the money he had been promised and would not now be paid. The other had not said a word.



On the fourth night Emeric went to the ward late, because he could not sleep, and because Tess had stopped writing the date under the empty space and he wanted to know whether that meant anything.

He had no yes for it. There was nobody awake at the side door to give him one, and he went in anyway.

The ward was dark except for one lamp turned low on the register desk by the side door. Imani had sent her women home at dusk, all of them, to sleep in their own beds for the first time since the barges. Every bed but one was full, and every pallet on the floor between them, and every man on them was asleep: Declan in the bed by the window, with his leg up on a folded coat, breathing slow and heavy; the townsman with the side wound; the men from the bucket line with their burned hands wrapped on their chests. Tess, on her side, by the far wall. Nobody stirred when he came in.

Imani was on the floor.

She was sitting against the wall beside Tess's bed, with her knees drawn up and her arms round them and her head down on her arms, and she was asleep. He did not think she had meant to be. There was a cup of something gone cold on the floor beside her, and her hair had come out of its strip of cloth and fallen forward over her arms. She had not slept, as far as anybody could tell him, for two days and two nights.

The bed next to Tess's was empty. A man from the bucket line had gone home from it that afternoon, walking, with his hands bound, and nobody had been put in it yet. It had a straw mattress on it and nothing else. Every blanket in the ward had gone to the dock quarter's people in their borrowed houses, the first night, because they had come over the bridge with what they could carry and most of them had not carried blankets in May.

He stood in the dim ward and looked at her, and at the bed.

He could have woken her. She would have told him to go home, and gone back to the wall, and slept on the floor till morning, and been stiff all the next day and not said so. He knew that as well as he knew anything about her.

He looked round the ward once more. Every face he could see was turned away, or asleep, or both. Declan's breathing went on, slow and heavy, by the window.

He put out his hand toward her, and pointed, and the small ring came up over his fingers, dim, in the dark of the ward, where nobody was awake to see it.

She was light. That was the first thing. A sleeping woman with no more mana in her than anybody, and nothing in her pushing back, nothing at all, and she came up off the floor in one slow piece, knees and arms and the fallen hair, as if she were lying on a board that somebody was lifting, and she did not wake. It was still a strain. He had not slept either. His hand shook, and he held it, and brought her across the gap between the wall and the empty bed, a foot above the floor, slowly, and let her down onto the straw, and let go.

She sighed, and turned onto her side, toward Tess, and was still.

He stood there with his hand down and his heart going and waited for anybody in the ward to say anything. Nobody did. Declan breathed by the window.

He took off his coat. It was the only thing he had. He laid it over her, up to the shoulder, and the collar of it fell against her cheek, and she did not wake for that either.

Then he went out by the side door, and shut it behind him without a sound, and was halfway up the hill in his shirt before he thought about what she would make of the coat.
