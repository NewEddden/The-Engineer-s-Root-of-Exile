Chapter 60 - The Flood

Nobody stopped it. That was the part Emeric had the most difficulty with afterward, and it was not the part that mattered, and he knew both of those things at once for the rest of that year.

What happened was that a young man in a clerk's coat, in a building six hundred kilometres north, opened a folder with a number on it and began doing the first thing a clerk does with a matter, which is to write to everybody whose name appears anywhere near it and ask them dull questions in the ordinary form.

He wrote to eleven waypoints on the southern road. He asked each of them for its consolidation returns for the past three years, for the names of the men who held the sorting warrant in each of those years, for the schedule of bags received and bags forwarded, and for any record of items reported missing, damaged or delayed. He asked it in the language such requests are always written in, over a magistrate's seal, with a number and a return address and a clerk's initials at the bottom, and he sent the same letter to all eleven because that is how such letters are sent.

He was not investigating anybody. He had been assigned a matter three weeks earlier and this was the first of what he expected to be several hundred dull letters, and he did not know what Kaldo was, and would have described the whole business, if anybody had asked him at the inn, as post going astray in the south.

The letter reached the crossing at Wayford in the second week of that season.

The man who held the sorting warrant there had been taking Kaldo's crown items out of the bag for three years. He was paid four crowns a quarter for it and he had another man under him who was paid two, and neither of them had ever been told why, and neither of them had asked, because a man who asks why is a man who has made himself worth removing.

It was a good arrangement. It required nothing of him except doing slightly less work eleven times a year, and there was no victim he could see, and the items he took went into the stove in the back room and not one person in three years had ever come to the crossing asking after a single one of them.

He read the clerk's letter twice.

Then he sat in his own office with it for about a quarter of an hour doing the only piece of arithmetic that anybody in this entire business ever did correctly on the first attempt.

Four crowns a quarter.

A magistrate's office, with a number on it, asking for three years of consolidation returns and the name of the man holding the warrant in each of those years. His name. In his own hand, in the returns, against every one of the dates.

Burning a letter for a baron is a crime for a wage. Burning a letter while a court clerk is asking after that letter, by number, in writing, with the date already in a register, is a crime for a wage and a rope, and the rope does not scale with the wage.

He did not tell anybody. He did not write to Wayford. He did not stop taking the four crowns, because refusing the money was itself a thing somebody would notice.

He simply stopped taking the letters out of the bag.

The man under him worked it out inside a fortnight without a word being exchanged between them, because it is not possible to conceal from your own subordinate that you have stopped doing a thing, and the subordinate had a rope of his own to think about.

And that was the whole of it. No raid. No arrest. No confrontation. Two men in a sorting house on a river crossing, separately and silently, deciding that four crowns was no longer the correct price, and a bag of southern post going north in the ordinary way for the first time since the war.

Sethe found out five weeks later, in his own hall, from a man who did not understand what he was reporting.

The man was a steward of the middle sort who handled, among a great many other things, the small standing payments the household made to people in other places for reasons the steward had never been told. He came in with a quarter's list to be approved, as he did every quarter, and he mentioned, as one item among nine, that the crossing man had sent no return this quarter.

"No return."

"The note he sends. He's sent it every quarter for three years and it came late twice and it hasn't come at all this time." The steward turned the sheet round. "I've left the payment in. Shall I hold it?"

Sethe's first question was about the road. He asked whether there had been weather at the crossing, and then whether the man was ill, and then whether anybody had gone through Wayford from that direction in the last month who might be asked. All three were the questions of a man managing a logistical fault, and he asked them in the ordinary voice he used for logistical faults, and the steward answered two of the three and went away to find out about the third.

Sethe sat in the hall for a while after he had gone.

Then he did the second piece of arithmetic, and it took him considerably longer than a quarter of an hour, because he had more to lose and therefore more reasons to arrive somewhere comfortable.

He arrived nowhere comfortable.

If the crossing had stopped, the crossing had stopped for a reason, and the only reasons available were that the man was dead, that the man had been bought by somebody else, or that the man had become frightened. And a frightened man does not send a letter explaining that he has become frightened.

And then the part he could not get out from under, and which he turned over for the rest of that evening and a good deal of the following month.

A mercenary can be paid off and ride away. A steward can be dismissed and will be in another county inside a fortnight. Money can be moved, and had been, and most of his was. Renner, who had been an idiot, had at least had the decency to commit his offence with a body and a levy, both of which had come home or gone into the ground and were finished.

What Sethe had done was to a crossing.

It was a stone building with a yard and a warrant and a book in it, on a river, on his ground, with his family's name over the door and three years of his own household's payment records running out to it in nine-crown intervals in a steward's tidy hand. He could dismiss the man. He could burn the returns. He could not pick up the crossing and carry it somewhere else, and he could not make the road go around it, and every letter that had ever been taken out of a bag in that building had been taken out of it on a date, and the dates were in somebody else's books at both ends of a road he did not own.

He had committed a crime that was fixed to a piece of ground.

He sat with that, and he did not panic, because he was not a man who panicked; he was a man who bought things. He spent the following six weeks buying things, and none of them helped, and one of them turned out later to have been the single worst purchase of his life, which was the retention of a very competent advocate three months earlier than he needed one.

The pile reached the capital across nine days in the middle of that season, and it was received by a second-tier clerk in the crown's southern correspondence office named Aldous Reeve, who had held that desk for eleven years and who had, in all eleven of them, never once had a problem with it.

The southern desk was the desk you were given when nobody was angry with you and nobody had noticed you. It covered nine baronies, four of which corresponded twice a year, three of which corresponded when they wanted money, and two of which had sent nothing at all in living memory. Reeve's principal professional skill was knowing which of the nine could be left in a tray until a Thursday.

Kaldo arrived on a Monday in four bags and did not stop arriving until the following Tuesday week.

He opened the first bag expecting arrears, because a barony that sends nothing for four years and then sends four bags is a barony with a grievance and an overdue tithe. What he found was eight quarter accounts in date order, each one ruled, footed and cross-referenced, each one with a duplicate attached, and each duplicate carrying a crown post lodgment receipt in the hand of a clerk at a southern gate, dated, stamped, stating the weight of the item and the seal device.

He had never in eleven years seen a lodgment receipt attached to anything. He had not, if he was honest, been entirely certain until that morning that the gates issued them.

He worked through the four bags over nine days and then through the ones that kept coming, and what assembled itself on the long table in the back of that office was the following.

A census correction, with the wrong figure, the corrected figure, the method by which the correction was made, and the date of each.

A full account of a war. Written within the month of its ending, four years previously, in the holder's own hand, with a bookkeeper's figures attached and the coalition's strength stated in both the version Kaldo had believed at the time and the version it had established afterward, with the difference explained and the error attributed to the writer by name.

Three petitions, all of them unanswered, all of them reasonable, one of them about a school.

A query, four years old, as to whether a toll levied at a river crossing was lawful.

An exclusive commercial instrument with a neighbouring barony, lodged for the record in the proper form and within the proper period, which practically nobody ever did.

A request for a physician's licence.

A request for permission to build a hospital of forty-one beds, with the reasons costed.

And underneath all of it, in the ordinary quarterly returns, the thing that actually unsettled him: tonnages. Foundry output, quarter on quarter, rising. Ore under a fixed-price contract from a barony that everybody in that building believed had fought a war against this one. A soap trade running out to three coastal ports. A drainage programme. Eleven hundred people at assignment and something over two thousand now. A debt to a chartered lending house being serviced, on schedule, out of earnings, with the house's own resident factor's countersignature on the schedule.

Reeve took it to his superior on the eleventh day, which he had not done about anything in four years.

"What is it?"

"It's Kaldo," Reeve said. "The plague one. The one we sent the third son to."

"What do they want?"

Reeve stood there for a moment with his hand on the top of the stack, which came to the height of his elbow.

"Nothing," he said. "Not one thing. It's four years of reports."

The last piece of it arrived through Saban, in four lines, in the ordinary way he reported a market.

Two of the four companies Sethe had on a wage had given notice inside a fortnight of the quarter day and one of them had gone north without waiting to be paid out, which is what a company does when it expects the pay to stop rather than be late. The grain in store had been offered quietly at the crossing. Two houses that had been extending Wayford short credit against the autumn had declined to roll it, and neither of them gave a reason, and neither of them needed to.

"He can't afford the war any more," Saban said. "It went in about a week. That's usually how it goes, with credit. Nobody pulls first and then everybody has."

There was no battle. There was never going to be one. A thing that Ward had put a garrison on a war footing for, and that eleven hundred people had been quietly bracing themselves against since the spring, stopped existing because four men in three counting houses independently decided that a barony under inquiry was not a barony to be owed money by.

Emeric was glad. That was true and it stayed true and he would have said so under oath.

He was also, for about three weeks, flat in a way he could not account for and did not like, and when he finally sat down and made himself account for it the answer was small and unpleasant. He had built an instrument. He had spent two years on it, and a man had died carrying it, and he had believed it was going to be the thing that ended this. What had actually ended it was a clerk he had never met sending a form letter to eleven waypoints, in the ordinary course, three weeks into a job, without knowing what any of it was about. His instrument had not stopped one letter being burned. It had only ever been for afterward.

Which was exactly what he had told Ferra it was for, in a room, with the first eleven copies stacked and ready, and she had said it back to him better than he had said it, and he had agreed with her.

And he had still wanted it to be him.

He noticed that. He turned it over for the length of one evening, and he could not make it into anything he liked, and he did not tell anybody about it, and it did not go away.

The summons came down at the end of that season, both of them by the same courier, on the same road, through the same crossing.

They were not summonses to a trial. Keller's office did not use the word, and the document did not contain it. Both men were required to attend upon a matter, by number, on a stated day, to answer such questions as the court might put.

Everybody who read either of them understood exactly what it was.

He took Ferra, because the paper was hers and had always been hers, and because a case that arrives without the person who made the index is a case that spends its first morning explaining an index.

He took Ward.

He left Armand with the barony's numbers, which Armand accepted with an air of having been asked to hold a coat, and Imani with everything else, which she accepted by asking two practical questions about who could sign what and then going back to the ward.

Vantic wrote to her house that the borrower had been summoned before a magistrate, because a resident factor is obliged to report any proceeding touching the borrower within seven days and she had never in six years been a day late with anything.

She wrote it the day the summons arrived. And then she took it down to the counting room, unsealed, and put it in front of Ferra and asked her to read it.

It was eleven lines. It was accurate. It stated the matter number, the date of attendance, the two parties summoned, and the fact that the proceeding concerned interference with crown post and did not concern the borrower's conduct or the security. It contained no opinion of any kind.

Ferra read it twice and said it was correct.

Vantic said thank you, sealed it, and sent it.

She had not shown Ferra a letter before. Not once in six years, on anything. Ferra noticed, and said nothing about it to anybody, including Vantic, and including Emeric.

A letter came down from Grayrock in the week before they left.

It was about the summer schedule and the condition of the upper workings after the thaw, and it proposed a small adjustment to the carriage rate in Kaldo's favour on the grounds that the new road had changed what a horse could do and the old rate had been built on the old road.

There was one sentence in the middle of the third paragraph. She had dismissed the captain of the lower seam and put another man in. She had done it because she had taken Grayrock's own weights at the pithead for two quarters and set them against Kaldo's receiving figures at the gate, and the two had not agreed, and the disagreement had gone one way every time. She noted the new man's name so that Kaldo's gate would know who to raise a query with.

She did not say where she had got the idea of setting two sets of figures against each other. She did not say it had taken her four months. She did not ask him what he thought of it, and she did not mention that a mine captain of nineteen years' standing had been her husband's man.

Emeric read the letter twice. The second time he read it for what was not in it.

He found nothing that was not in it, and thought, as he had thought for two years, that a woman who was holding something back would write precisely this letter, and put it in the box with the others.

They went out of the Western Gate on a clear morning at the start of summer, three of them, with a carriage for the boxes and two riders.

Ferra had the index in a case on her knees and would not put it in the boot. Ward rode. Emeric spent the first two days re-reading Keller's four requirements in the notebook, in the hand he had written them in, in a room in the capital, three years earlier.

It was the second time in his life that he had gone up this road to put a baron in front of a magistrate.

The first time he had been twenty-three, and had been entirely certain, and had been right about the man and wrong about nearly everything behind him, and had come home believing that a thing was finished which had not been finished at all and was not finished now.

He thought about that for a while somewhere north of the fork, and then he stopped thinking about it, because there was nothing to do with it except arrive.
