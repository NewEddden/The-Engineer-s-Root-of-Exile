Chapter 385 - Twelve Berths

Imani read the Band cards on the twenty-third of February, a Monday, in the ward's long room, at a table by the window, one at a time, in alphabetical order, because she'd said that if she did them in any other order somebody would read something into it.

Eight hundred and thirty-one cards. She'd done the knights' in January; they all passed, every one, as she'd known they would, because the knights had been flying since the war. The war fleet's crews the same. That left the ones who'd started in September on the Hod's Tuesday runs, the civilians who were needed and had never flown: the sail-makers and the cooks and the carpenters and the radio hands and the seed boy from Milford and the second medic and the second engineer. Sixty-three of them. She'd had them up at the Band's foot every week for five months under her clock with the scrubber shut, and eaten Kaldo's mana food at every meal, and she had five months of dots.

The line was the line. No bleeding at the foot, the scrubber shut, the whole watch. The veterans' line from the two years of numbers, from before the war. It had never been argued with, because nobody had ever found anything wrong with it.

Sixty-two of them had reached it. Some late; Abloh in the second week of February, so late that Imani had had his card on the table in front of her twice and put it back. Bettany in December. Dagny in November, and very pleased about it. Fletcher, who'd said at the start he didn't hold with lying down in the daytime and didn't hold with sitting in the sky either, in January, the week after his stone; he'd have passed without it, Imani thought, but the stone hadn't hurt. Linnet Shaw. Tobin Marsh, who'd been sick over the Hod's rail on his first run and had never bled at all.

And one hadn't.

Imani held the card for a long time. It was a good card. White, Imani's own stock, ruled in the margin, the name at the top in her small slanted hand and under it five months of Tuesdays. A dot on every one of them. Forty-four minutes, fifty, forty-eight, the coffee, no coffee. Forty-nine in December, the day of the wheat. Fifty-one at the end of January, the best. Fifty-three on the seventeenth of February, the last, better still.

Still a dot.

She put the card face down on the table and went to find her.

Maren was in her kitchen, as she always was at eleven in the morning, with the range up and the girls at the long table shelling peas and the smell of the day's bread coming out of the oven, and she looked up when Imani came in at the yard door with the card in her hand and wiped her hands on her apron and said, "Well, come in, then, you're letting the cold in."

Imani came in and shut the door.

"Out," said Maren to the girls, without looking at them. "All of you. Go and see if the coffee house wants peas."

The girls went. They went quickly, and quietly, which was not like them, and the youngest one looked back at the door.

When they'd gone Maren didn't sit down. She stood at the end of the long table with her hands on the back of a chair, and Imani stood by the range, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"Go on," said Maren.

"Fifty-three minutes," said Imani. "On the seventeenth."

"I know. I was there."

"It was the best you've done."

"I know that too." Maren's hands tightened on the chair. "Say it."

"You don't make the line," said Imani. "You've not made it once. You won't make it by the end of March. I've looked at every dot and I've looked at your body every week since September and I've tried everything I know, and you bleed at the foot, and past the wall the water's thicker than the foot by the end of the first week." She didn't look away. "You'd be bleeding on the deck before we'd seen the first of her people. Every day. And getting worse. And I'd have to choose between keeping you in a cabin for six months in a mask, and sending you home on the first hull back, alone, and I'll not do either of those to you." She put the card down on the table. "So you stay. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, Maren."

Maren looked at the card. She didn't pick it up.

"I knew in October," she said.

"I know you did."

"I knew the second Tuesday in October. I sat on that bench with your rag up my nose and looked at the cloud and I knew." She let go of the chair. "And I thought, she'll find something. She always finds something. That woman found a way to keep a dead queen's mind in a stone and put it back in her body. She'll find a way to get a cook to sit in the sky for an hour without her nose running." Her voice didn't rise. It got harder, and flatter, the way it did when one of the girls had let the stock boil. "And you didn't."

"No."

"You didn't."

"There wasn't anything to find. It's not a disease. It's your body. It's the body you've got, and it's a good one, and it's carried you through every kitchen in this town, and it doesn't want to be past the wall." Imani's voice wasn't quite steady. "I could give you a stone. If the Board would. They wouldn't. And I wouldn't ask them, because a stone for a cook so she can go on a voyage is a stone a ropemaker's daughter doesn't get for her eyes, and you'd say that yourself, and you'd be right."

"I'd say it," said Maren. "I'd not like it."

She went to the range and opened the oven door and looked at the bread, which was done, and took it out, loaf by loaf, with the cloth, and set it on the rack, as if Imani weren't there. Eight loaves. She did it slowly. She did it exactly right.

"I've been feeding him for seventeen years," she said, to the bread. "Since that first winter. Since he came in that door with the cart and the chest and nothing in his belly and sat at that end of that table and ate four bowls of my mother's soup and didn't say thank you because he'd forgotten how. Seventeen years." She shut the oven. "And now he's going to the end of the world and I'll be here, and somebody else'll be putting his plate in front of him, and he'll not eat it, because he never eats it when he's working, and she'll not know to stand there."

"She'll know," said Imani. "You've written it down."

Maren turned round.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody told me. I've known you seventeen years." Imani picked up the card again. "You've been marking that ledger since the night of the third Tuesday. You've marked every page. You've written a list on the back pages of things that aren't on anybody's list." She held the card out. "You knew in October and you've been getting ready since October to hand it to somebody. And you've known who."

Maren took the card. She looked at it for a while: five months of dots in Imani's hand.

"She doesn't know how to make bread," she said, at last. "The island woman. Not proper bread. She makes that flat thing on a stone."

"She'll learn."

"She'll not learn it from me. I'll be here." Maren put the card in her apron pocket, quite carefully, the way you'd put away a letter. "She knows the fish. I'll say that for her. Ilenne says she knows the fish. She put the long leaf with the comb fish and I'd never have thought of it." She took a breath. "And she's not had to feed him. Nobody's had to feed him but me."

"I know."

"Then you know." Maren looked at the door the girls had gone out of. "Go on. Go and tell the next one. I've the dinner to do."

Imani went to the door. At the door she stopped, and turned back, and Maren had already picked up a knife and an onion and was standing at the board with her back to the room, cutting, very fast, very fine, the way she did when she didn't want anybody to see her face.

"It's the onions," said Maren, without turning round.

"I know," said Imani, and went out.

She gave the ledger to the island's cook on the Wednesday, in the kitchen, in person, with Ilenne there, because Ilenne had asked to be.

The island's cook had come over from South Sentinel on the morning boat. Her name was Dorrit Hake, and nobody on the Sentinel had used it in years. She was a short broad weathered woman of sixty with forearms like a stevedore's and a face that had been out in the wind on the Sentinel's rocks every day of her life, and she'd never been in Maren's kitchen before, and she stood just inside the yard door with her hands folded over her stomach and her eyes going round it, the range, the hanging pans, the jars, the long table, the way a sailor's eyes go round another man's ship.

"Sit," said Maren.

The island's cook sat. Maren put the green ledger down on the table in front of her. Then she put a cup of the strong tea beside it, and sat down across the table, and folded her arms.

"That's mine," she said. "Nineteen years of it. My mother's in the front. The crosses are what keeps. The circles are what'll do at sea if you've got the hens. The lines are don't." She nodded at the book. "The back's the stores and what's in them. And the people. What they eat. What they don't. Who you stand over."

The island's cook opened it. She turned the pages slowly, one at a time, with a thick careful finger. She didn't say anything for a long time. Maren watched her do it with a face like a stone.

"You've written in the margins," said the island's cook at last.

"I've written everywhere."

The island's cook found a page and turned it round on the table. It was the page for a fish stew, an old one, Maren's mother's, and down the margin in Maren's square pencil there was a column of remarks, cramped and furious, that had nothing to do with the recipe at all: Not with the shellfish, I don't care what anybody says. Not with cream either. Zale will ask for cream. Zale is wrong. The king likes it the second day better and will tell you he doesn't. Salt at the END. If you salt it at the start I'll know.

"You'll know," said the island's cook.

"I'll know."

"From here."

"I'll know," said Maren again, "from wherever I am."

The island's cook looked at the margin for a while longer. Then she shut the ledger and put both hands flat on its cover.

"I'll salt it at the end," she said.

And then, after a moment, as if it cost her something: "My mother put it in at the start. I've always put it in at the start. She'd have said you were wrong."

"Your mother," said Maren, "never cooked for a king."

"No," said the island's cook. "She cooked for fishermen. They're harder." And for the first time she looked straight at Maren across the table, and something passed between the two of them that Emeric, standing in the yard doorway with his coat on, didn't understand at all and wasn't meant to.

Ilenne was at the end of the table by the window. She'd been very quiet. She was trying very hard to be quiet. Her hood was down, because it was only them, and her face was arranged in the expression Emeric had seen her wear at the long table when somebody she didn't like said something stupid and she'd decided to be polite about it.

It wasn't working. Her colour had gone up from the throat to the jaw in a soft warm gold he'd never seen on her before and Imani hadn't got a line for, and it was getting brighter, and she knew it was getting brighter, and she put her hand up over her throat as if she could hide it, which made it worse.

Maren looked at her.

"You're pleased," she said.

"No," said Ilenne.

"You've gone yellow."

"It is the cold," said Ilenne.

"It's August in here. I've the range up." Maren looked at the island's cook, and at Ilenne, and at the gold going up Ilenne's jaw, and something in her face cracked, very slightly, at one corner. "She does the long flat fish for you with the leaves."

"Yes," said Ilenne. In a very small voice.

"And you like it better than mine."

Ilenne took her hand down off her throat. She looked at Maren for a long moment, steadily, with the gold up to her cheekbones now and no way on earth to stop it.

"Yes," she said. "Only the fish. Everything else, you."

Maren looked at her. Then she put her head back and laughed, one great bark of it, so loud the island's cook jumped, and wiped her eyes with the corner of her apron, and said, "Well, that's honest, at least," and got up and went to the range and began, furiously, to make all three of them something to eat.

Maeve asked on the Tuesday, properly, at the long table, standing up.

She'd been going to ask in February since December. She'd told him so at the tram stop, after the yard, after her mother: that she'd ask in February, and that if he said no she'd find a way to follow him anyway, because she'd spent thirty years being kept somewhere safe and wasn't doing another. It was the twenty-fourth. She'd left it to the last week on purpose, Ferra said afterward, so that she'd have had the whole winter in Kaldo first and nobody could say she didn't know what she was asking for.

She stood at the foot of the table in her own clothes, not Mira's jacket, with her short hair brushed and her hands at her sides, and said what she'd come to say. She didn't make a speech. She said she'd been at the knights' yard every Tuesday and Friday since December learning the spear from Mira and was not good but was better. That she'd been to the Band's foot every week since November and passed Imani's line in January. That she'd eat her stone in March, on the date Imani had given her, the ninth, and sail with it. That she'd sit the K Amara's deck and do what Ward told her and not get under anybody's feet. That she had the lightning, the base of it, and three weeks of his lessons, and she'd go on with them aboard. And that she was his sister and he was going to meet a queen.

"That's all," she said. "That's why."

Emeric looked at her. He'd known since the tram stop what he was going to say. He'd known since the yard, when she'd laughed at their mother's hand on his chest, and he'd thought then that he'd been hearing that laugh, a little, his whole life, from somewhere off to one side, and had never once heard it properly.

"Yes," he said.

Maeve opened her mouth. She'd had another speech ready, he saw, for the no.

"Yes," he said again. "Sit down. You're making the table nervous."

She sat down. Ward, beside her, didn't look at her. He moved the water jug half an inch nearer her elbow, which from Ward was an embrace.

Armand won his place the same afternoon, and he didn't ask for it. He took it.

"I'm going," he said, when the roster came to his line on Ferra's sheet and Ferra's pen paused over it. "You'll write yes and you'll not write a question mark after it."

"You're ninety-two," said Imani.

"I'm ninety-two and I look thirty-two and I can run up the castle stair faster than Saban." Armand didn't raise his voice. He'd stopped raising it in the tower, sixty years ago, when he'd found out it made old men listen less. "And I'm the only person in this kingdom who's read the old grammar. Who knows the colour layer from the inside. Who's looked at the bottom of a stair and come back up. Ilenne's language is half the old races' and her mother's court is older than this town. You'll want somebody at that door who knows what a thousand years of manners sounds like when it's being polite to you and when it's being polite at you." He folded his hands on the table. "Lysa has my chairs. Lysa's better than me with the stones. She's not better than me with the past. Nobody is. That's not vanity. It's arithmetic."

"It's a little bit vanity," said Saban.

"It's a little bit vanity," Armand agreed. "Write yes, Ferra."

Ferra wrote yes. She didn't write a question mark. She wrote, under it, small, He insisted, and Armand read it upside down and nodded as if it were a compliment.

And then the roster, in the long room, on the Wednesday, the whole of it at last: eight ships, eight hundred and thirty, every name on Ferra's sheets in the order they'd board. The K Discovery on point, unarmed, twenty, with Emeric and Sorrel and the relays. The K Amara at the centre with Ward and Ferra and Armand and Maeve and Arthur's group and the knights' aprons. The K Endeavor with the Challenger slung under her and the hospital deck and Imani and Bettany and Fletcher and the greenhouse and Tobin Marsh's empty jars and Finn. Hull four with Bram and the workshop deck and Dagny. The two escorts in their pair. Zale's ship. The K Tam, built on her pattern, named for Sela's middle son, launched at the end of January and flown and sailed twice since and no worse than her sister.

The island's cook in the K Amara's galley. Maren's name on the stays-home sheet, in Ferra's hand, between Lysa Orme and Mattie Dunne, with nothing written after it.

The sapling stayed home too. Ferra put it on the list for the sake of saying no to it out loud: the merfolk had their own grove under the sea, and a Hollow Crystal past the wall was a risk with nothing to gain, and a seed from her people's grove would be the better trade. Ilenne nodded. Nobody argued.

Then the berths, which Saban had been waiting for since Ferra read out the K Discovery's line.

"Nine hundred and thirty," said Ferra, and wrote it at the foot of the column, and drew a line under it.

"You've eight hundred and thirty names."

"I have."

"Then you've counted wrong," said Saban, pleasantly, "and I'd like to be in the room when somebody tells the bank."

"I've counted three times." Ferra didn't look up. "A hundred berths with nobody's name on them. Four on the K Discovery, forty on the K Amara, the rest spread over the carriers and the escorts. Made up, aired, a blanket on every one. And hull four's after hold swept and limed and empty, with the hatch boards marked so nobody stows in it by accident, and if they do, Dagny's said she'll have them out by the ears."

"She will," said Bram.

"Why?"

That was Zale. He'd sailed a ship west with four people on her and room for nothing, and it was an honest question, and he asked it as one.

"Because we don't know what's out there," said Emeric. "Ore. Timber. A seed nobody's grown here. A stone we haven't got a name for. If we find it and there's no room to bring it home, we've found it for nothing."

"Or who," said Saban.

Emeric looked at him.

"Or who," he agreed. "People get on ships. Somebody out there's going to want to see where we come from. I'd sooner have a berth for them than tell them to wait for the next one."

"Two hundred and forty tonnes is for eight hundred and thirty," said Ferra, to the page. "Whoever comes back with us eats fish." She wrote that too.

And then she turned the sheet over to the other side, which was the one she'd been putting off, and read it out the way she read out the coal: the K Amara's forty-centimetre guns and what she carried for them; the escorts' twin turrets, fore and aft, and the flak; the close-in mount on escort one's quarter with its four barrels on the spindle, and the cases of dead shard flakes Ruan had fired over the sea the week before last; the Dragon Net in escort one's weapons bay, on its rack with his white paint on it; the cloud seeder on the K Amara's stern; rifles and the special rounds for every man who'd been taught them, and about a hundred and twenty knights in flight armour with their kit; harpoon guns; drain cuffs, in case of a mage. She read it all, flat, and when she'd finished she put the pencil down on the sheet and looked at the room over it.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"I've seen fleets go to war lighter than that," said Zale.

"Good," said Ward.

"It's a trade voyage," said Saban. "That's what I've been telling the lanes."

"It's a trade voyage with guns." Ward sat back. "I like being prepared. You never know. And we know less than most."

"We know one thing," said Armand.

He hadn't spoken since his own line on the roster. He was sitting with his long new hands folded on the table and his eyes on Ilenne, not unkindly.

"We know what our side did," he said. "We drove them off this land, and then we forgot it, so we'd not have to be sorry. She said so on Hollow Night, in the lane." Ilenne didn't move. Her colour didn't either. "Every people out there that's old enough to remember will remember it. They've had a long while to tell it to their children. I'd not count on many friends."

"No," said Emeric.

He'd been thinking it since Hollow Night, since she'd stood in the lane and put her foot down flat on Kaldo's stones; and he'd been thinking, too, about every border he'd ever read about, in books nobody here would ever read, and how none of them had ever been clean. Somebody's daughter married across it. Somebody's trader stayed. Somebody wouldn't march, and went the other way instead, with the people he was meant to be marching against.

"Unless they don't take us for the ones who did it," he said. "When they went, some of ours must have gone with them. They always do. Families. Traders. Men who wouldn't fight." He turned to Ilenne. "Did any? Are there people like us out there?"

Ilenne thought about it for long enough that Ferra's pen came up off the page.

"Yes," she said. "Some. Not in the sea. Far." She moved her hand in a long flat line away from her, west, which was where she pointed for everything that was a long way off. "I never see one."

"Then that's the only way we slip under," said Emeric. "If they think we're theirs. The ones who went with them, and wanted to."

"Not my mother," said Ilenne.

"No. Your mother knows exactly where we've come from." He almost smiled. "We came with you."

"Where nobody knows us, then," said Ferra, writing. "Nowhere else." She underlined nowhere.

"And if they don't believe it," said Ward, "we've the guns." He said it the way he'd said everything else that morning, as a fact about the stores. "I'd as soon not need them. That's the point of having them."

"And the twelve," said Ferra.

The Challenger had twelve bunks. That had been fixed since she was drawn: twelve, in two rows of six, under the deckhead, a foot and a half apart, with a curtain each that Abloh had wanted to be blue and Bram had made grey. Twelve people could go down in her. Down past the reef and the basin's edge to the door of a hall nobody from the land had seen in a thousand years. Twelve and no more. And no tank for Ilenne aboard, because she'd not need one where they were going; she'd be going home.

"Ilenne picks," said Emeric.

Everybody looked at him.

"It's her door," he said. "It's her mother. She knows who her mother needs to see and who her mother would sooner not." He turned to Ilenne. "You don't have to say why. You can if you want."

Ilenne was sitting by Zale at the table's side, where she'd sat since the autumn. She had a pencil. She'd been drawing on the back of one of Ferra's sheets while they talked about the hulls, and now she turned the sheet over, and on the back, in her careful capitals, in two rows of six, she'd already written them.

"I pick yesterday," she said. "In the bath."

She turned it round so they could read it.

ILENNE. ZALE. EMERIC. FERRA. IMANI. ARMAND.

NOLL. NED. FINN. ABLOH. FLETCHER.

And the twelfth space, at the end of the second row, empty.

Ward read it. His face didn't change. Then it did, a little.

"I'm not on it," he said.

"No," said Ilenne.

"Nor any knight."

"No." She looked at him, not unkindly. "You are the soldier. My mother sees a soldier come to her door from the land, what does she think?" She waited. Nobody answered. "She thinks war. Then her old men say, see, war, we told you. Then the house I..." She stopped. The colour at her throat went, briefly, a hard dark slate, and came back. "Then it is war. So. No soldiers." She put her finger on each name. "Me, I am her daughter. Zale, she must see. The king, she must see, because his paper says a war with me is a war with his country, and she must see the man who wrote the paper. Ferra has the words, and the counting. Imani is my doctor. Armand knows the old ways, she will like him, he is old." Armand inclined his head. "Noll and Ned, the boat. Finn swims with the suits. Abloh makes my dress. Fletcher made my tank." She paused. "Fletcher is my friend."

"And the twelfth?" said Ferra.

Ilenne looked at the empty space for a moment.

"For coming back," she said. "Somebody from there comes up with us. Maybe. I do not know who." Her colour shifted, very faintly, at the throat. "I leave it empty. Then it is there."

Ferra wrote the eleven names in the book, and drew a box after them, and left it empty, and wrote under it Hers to fill. I.'s door.

He asked his father on the Thursday, in the dome, at night.

It was their place now, the three of them. His mother's body asleep on its mattress against the tree's trunk, breathing slowly, with the blanket up to its chin; the made one sitting beside it on the gravel with its knees drawn up, awake, looking up through the branches at the glass. Harrold on the bench by the north door with his coat on, because he felt the dome's cold more than he admitted. And Emeric, standing, because he'd come to ask a thing and found he couldn't sit down to do it.

The seal was on the bench beside Harrold in its box. Keller had brought it down from the capital on the morning train, the old regency seal, which had lain in a drawer in the records hall since the last regency, longer ago than anybody living could remember. Keller had his way. He usually did. A regency by seal, written, as law, so that the regent's signature bound and nobody could say afterwards that the old king had only been minding the shop.

"You know why I'm asking," said Emeric.

"I know what you're asking. I'm waiting to hear why."

So he told him why. Why the king went himself, and didn't send a man.

"Because it's first contact," he said. "With a people nobody on the land has spoken to in a thousand years. At her door. If I send an envoy, I've sent a man. If I go, the country's gone." He'd said it at the top of the ride-up in October, to himself, with the whole of the realms laid out under him; he said it now out loud, to the one person who'd have to live with it. "And because of the paper. The envoy's commission names the convoy Randillia's. It names me. It says that what's done to Ilenne's door, and to her, is done to us. That a war with her is a war with Randillia. That's what makes her safe to walk into her mother's hall on her own legs. Her enemies have to look at it and know the land will come." He stopped. "It's only true if the king's standing behind it. A paper with my name on it in a drawer in Kaldo is just a paper. Me at the door is the country at the door."

Harrold didn't say anything for a long time. Across the gravel the made body had turned its head and was watching them both.

"That's a good reason," said Harrold at last.

"It's the reason."

"It's a good reason. I'll give it you." He looked at the box on the bench beside him. "It's not why I'll do it."

"I know."

"You don't know." Harrold put his hand on the box. He had his father's hands, big and square, and his son's, Emeric thought, and a stone's, now, young in a way that still looked odd on him at the end of an old man's arms. "I gave you the crown in the square with half the kingdom watching because the kingdom needed it given, and I didn't want it, and I've not wanted it since. And now you're going to the bottom of the sea for most of a year and leaving me to sit on it in all but name and take the train up to the capital once a month and listen to old men who knew your mother tell me I've never looked better, and then complain to me about your trenches." He didn't raise his voice. He never did. "I'll do it. I'll seal it. I'll sit there. I'll do it for her." He tilted his head, very slightly, at the made body on the gravel. "Not for you."

"I know," said Emeric. "That's all right."

"It's not all right. It's what it is." Harrold opened the box. The seal was a heavy round thing of dark bronze on an ivory handle gone yellow with age, and he took it out and held it in his palm and looked at it. "Your mother's back. I can stand a great deal for that. I can stand being regent for a year from Kaldo with the bed I've got here and the train to take me away from it one week in four. I can stand you going." He turned the seal over. "I'll not pretend I can stand it gladly."

"I'd not believe you if you did."

"Good," said Harrold.

And then, because the box was open and the seal was in his hand and his son was standing in front of him in a dome at night asking him to hold a kingdom, he said the other thing. He didn't look at Emeric when he said it. He looked at the seal.

"Ward wrote to me," he said. "Seven years ago. When you were still only the duke. A long letter, in his own hand. I don't suppose he told you."

Emeric was very still.

"He told me what you meant to do. Not the stones. The other thing. The bank the king doesn't own and the limit and the audit, and what came after. The Assembly. The day the crown's just a chair somebody sits in for a while and gets up out of." Harrold turned the seal over again. "He told me it was meant to end. The crown. That you'd take it, if you had to, and spend the rest of your life taking it apart." He put the seal down on the bench, carefully. "I've known for seven years. I gave it you anyway. I'm sealing this anyway." He looked up, at last. "So when you're at the bottom of the sea, and some old fool in the capital asks me what the new king's playing at, I'll know, and I'll say nothing, and I'll go on sitting in his chair for him. That's what I'm giving you. Don't make a speech about it."

Emeric didn't make a speech about it. He found he couldn't have if he'd wanted to.

On the gravel, the made body got up, and came across to the bench, and sat down beside Harrold with her knees nearly touching his, and put her made hand over his on the seal's box, and left it there. Behind them on the mattress against the trunk her own body breathed in, and out, in its sleep, with the tree humming over it.

"For me," she said. "Not for him. I heard."

"You were meant to," said Harrold.

Keller sealed it on the Friday morning, the twenty-seventh, in the castle's hall, with Harrold's hand and Emeric's and the old bronze seal pressed into a disc of red wax the size of a saucer, and Ferra's clerk and Keller's clerk and two members of the Assembly to witness, and the thing was law.

By the afternoon it was printed. By the evening it was on the doors of the counting house and the records hall and every church in Kaldo, and on the train to the capital in Keller's case, and on the telegraph to Wayford and down the home net to every steward's desk, where the stewards' clerks took it down and wrote it out fair and nailed it up in their own market squares by the morning. The king of Randillia was going abroad. To the west, past the last islands, on the king's own business as the kingdom's envoy, for most of a year. The regent was his father, Harrold, under seal, from Kaldo.

That was all it said. Saban had written it. Saban had made it say as little as a proclamation could and still be one.

"They'll answer it," he said, that night, in the long room, with the lamps going down and the printed sheet on the table in front of him under his hat. "From the pulpits. Within the week."

"Saying what?"

"Saying that a king who leaves his kingdom for a year to go to the bottom of the sea on a heathen's business isn't a king. Saying the old one's been made young by sorcery and is keeping company with a woman everybody says looks like his dead wife. Saying the Light weeps." Saban turned the hat once. "They'll not say anything they can be hanged for. They never do. They'll say it in a bishop's voice with a hand on a child's head, in every town between here and Ostrel, and by Lantern Night half the kingdom'll have heard it three times." He looked at Emeric. "That's how they fight now. Since the air battle. Pulpits and envoys and letters. This is what they've been waiting for. You've just given them a sermon."

"Could I have not?"

"No," said Saban. "But you've given it them. I'm saying so it's said." He picked up his hat. "And there'll be somebody, after the sermon, who comes to the chair. Not to you. To the man in the chair, while you're packing. A kind old man, or a kind young one, with something to say about how things could be different." He put the hat on. "I'd like Corbin to have heard it from me first."

"Then tell him."

"I told him this morning," said Saban, "before Keller had the wax hot," and went out.
