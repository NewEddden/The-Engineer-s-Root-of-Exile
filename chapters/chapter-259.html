Chapter 259 - The Veto

It took him nine nights to write it, and on the Monday, the sixteenth, a little before eleven, he wrote the last line and came up out of the stone and sat back in his chair and looked at what he had made.

It looked like nothing. A grey stone the size of the top joint of his thumb, dull, with the fine net over every face of it that the court had called carving for three hundred years, lying on a bed of old flannel in a box of plain wood. Corbin's box. There had been nine in it once, set down in front of two hundred people at a banquet with both hands, as an insult. The other eight had gone out of it one at a time over the years, into bands and the printer and the Forge's index and the South Gate, and this one had lain on the flannel by itself a long while since, the last grey stone in Kaldo that was not already at work. The sprout under the dome's glass would not bear before the spring. Until it did, there was no other.

He had not decided to use it. He had drawn the thing first, the night Old Edrin had his new band, on a page he showed nobody; and on the next night he had found the box open on his table and the stone in his hand and his room open round him, and himself already writing.

What he had written was short, for what it did. It listened. Each of the six wore a band with a grey bead at its clasp that knew its wearer and nobody else, and would call through the bond if it was cut off a living arm, or carried out of a dead zone on a wrist that had stopped answering it; his own did the same. The stone heard all seven. If his band called, and any three of theirs, it would wait a day and a night. If every band went quiet at once and stayed quiet a day and a night, it would wait the same. And then it would speak, once, in his note: the one note that every stone he had written in nine years had been made to answer and no other. It would tell each of them to drink.

A stone that drank past what it could hold burst. Every quarryman in the four baronies knew it, every miner who had ever watched a cheap stone blown at a face so that the good ones beside it would stop guarding themselves. The lamp stones and the cold stones and the racks in the ships. The Forge's rails, the gate, the bands themselves. Every stone in every kitchen in the town. He had built them all so that nobody but he could ever tell them anything, and so they would believe him.

Whoever had taken the six would wake on the second morning holding a town that worked by hand, and seven people who could tell them how it had been done once, with stones that were now dust.

On his other world there had been a thing like it, and men who had sat in rooms under the ground for forty years making certain it stayed warm. He had read about them once and thought them mad. He thought about that, with the stone on its flannel under the lamp, and found that it did not change anything.

A dead hand. He wrote the name on the page under the drawing, small, the way he named everything; and put the stone in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left, and went down the hill.

He had told both of them what he was afraid of, a fortnight before. Ferra four miles up, in the dark of the top cockpit with the probe glowing between them; Imani in her kitchen a few hours after, at her stove with her arms folded. Nobody had a thing the other did not. So he would show them both what he had done about it, and Imani's lamp was nearer, and still lit.



She was at her bench in the dispensary with the register and the day book side by side, copying the one into the other with her shoulders up round her ears, the way she sat when a day had gone on too long. Beyond the door the ward was quiet. A child coughing at the far end; Bettany's slippers going down between the beds and stopping and coming back.

She did not look up. "You're late for supper and early for breakfast."

"I've eaten."

"Maren says you haven't. Maren sent a boy to tell me, as if I'd a cure for it." She finished the line and blotted it. Then she looked at him, and then she looked at his left hand, which was inside his coat.

He had not known it was. He took it out.

"Put it back," said Imani, "and bring out whatever you had hold of."

He put the stone on the bench between the register and the day book. She did not touch it. She had worn one at her wrist for years and knew exactly what it was, and she looked at it the way she looked at a thing a child had swallowed and been sick up again: with interest, and without any wish to pick it up.

"That was going to be a band," she said.

"It was going to be something. It's the last one."

"And now it's something."

He told her. He told her the way she had made him learn to tell her things, in the eighth year: what it was first, and what it did, and only after that why, and the why short. She heard all of it with her two hands flat on the register, the way she heard a history from a man who had walked in late with something he had carried a week before he came. When he stopped, she asked her questions in the order she would have asked them at a bed.

"What does it hear?"

"The bands. Seven."

"And if it hears nothing?"

"It waits a day and a night."

"Lisle's wood was dead for seventeen hours," said Imani. "Hull two lay in it in a field with every stone she had asleep, and Ned and Garrick sat on her and waited for the air to come back." She tilted her head. "Somebody blows a wide one over the long room on a Tuesday, away from the river, in a dry month. All seven of us in it at the table, and our bands gone quiet together. Is that a day and a night?"

"Nobody's ever held one that long."

"Nobody had ever blown one on purpose until a quarryman did it to see what happened." She looked at the stone. "What's in my ward that it tells to drink?"

He told her that too. The cold press. The two boxes in the passage where the rested blood waited for whoever needed it next. The two stones on the leveller's board, and the stone lamps in the end room that the night nurse lit when the line went down in a storm. She counted them on her fingers as he said them and did not say anything until he had finished.

"And whoever's in my end room that night," she said, "with somebody's blood going into her through a board that's just been told to die."

"If the six are taken, Imani, there's no end room. There's whoever took us, reading what's in our heads and walking about in Kaldo with it."

"I know what you're afraid of. You told me in my kitchen." She did not raise her voice; she had never once raised it in the ward. "I said I knew. I didn't say go and make something."

"It's the only answer there is."

"It's the only answer that's yours." She put one finger on the bench beside the stone, not on it. "In May you sat in that chair with three dead boys in my ward and wanted it to be your fault, because then at least you'd be holding it. I told you it was theirs. And here you are again, only larger. Somebody takes you, and you can't bear him having any of it, not a lamp, not a cold box. So you've made yourself a way to be holding all of it still, after you're gone." She took the finger away. "You're wrong. I'll say it plainer if you like. It's wrong. It kills my patients to keep you from being read."

He did not answer. There was a great deal he could have said, and all of it was in the drawing on his table, and he could hear already how it would sound in this room.

Imani looked at him for some time.

"And you'll take it up to the table tomorrow and put it down in front of them anyway," she said. "I can see you're going to."

"They should see it."

"They should." She pulled the register back toward her and found her place in it with her finger. "Ferra has a pen," she said, to the page.

He picked up the stone and put it back in his coat. She let him go as far as the door.

"Go up the hill," she said, without turning round. "Sleep, if you can. You'll want to be awake tomorrow."



The table sat at ten, in the long room, with the windows open at the far end on the inner ward and the noise of ward 2 going up beyond it: the crane, the hods, a foreman calling a course. It was an ordinary Tuesday and the first hour of it was ordinary business, and he let it be.

The mill went first, because Ferra had put it first. Paxton's flax figure from Milford, larger than Ferra's guess and smaller than Paxton's hope; wool off the hill grazing in the autumn clip; Bram's ring frames drawn and the first power loom in pieces on his floor. The paper mill had wanted rags since the winter of the shirts, and Ferra read the rag price out of her book, doubled in two years, and said that the mill's offcuts would go to the paper mill by the sack, and that this was the first time in Kaldo's history a shirt would be worth something twice.

"And write that nobody under the school-leaving age goes near a frame," said Imani. "Not to sweep under it. Not to tie a thread. I had the Staple's sorters through my ward, coughing, who went into that room at eleven, and I'll not have Milford's children at a frame for Candlemas."

Ferra wrote it. Bram, who had been about to say that a child's hands were the only ones small enough to piece a broken end on a ring frame, looked at Imani's face and decided to say it to his drawing instead, and wrote something on the drawing.

The rail's August figures. Saban on the salt price at Wayford, which had nothing whatever to do with salt. Then Ferra turned the page, and looked down the table.

"Anything else?"

He took the stone out of his coat and put it on the table in front of him.

Nobody said anything at first. They all knew what it was. Bram's eyes went to his own wrist and back. Saban, at the far side, sat forward a little and put his hat on his knee. Armand, at the head in his chair, did not move at all.

He told them as he had told Imani, in the same order; and he told the why longer, because the table had not been in the top cockpit or the kitchen. Old Edrin, walking down a lane behind the capital mooring in a wind that moved nothing, and coming to on the cobbles with his wrist bare. A grey bead cut from inside, in somebody's hand in the north, telling whoever could read it that Kaldo delved. A lamp watchman who had sold a ship's hour for a Silver. The same quiet in a wood. A man who could do that to Old Edrin could do it to Ward. To Armand. To any one of them on the road to Grayrock with a clerk and a bag.

"Take one of you," he said. " and they have the Forge, or the Band, or the seed, or the cellar, whichever one you hold. Take me, and they have all of it. Delve us and drain us and they have it working, by spring, in somebody else's town." He put his finger beside the stone. "With this, they don't. They have us, and nothing we built works for them, or for anybody. Nobody can take it, because it isn't there to take."

The crane outside swung its pallet up past the window, and a man on the scaffold called down for mortar.

Armand spoke first, from the head of the table. He did not raise his voice; he had stopped needing to some years ago.

"How many stones in Kaldo answer you?"

"Every one I've written."

"How many is that?"

"I don't know. Ferra knows."

"Some thousands," said Ferra, without looking at her book.

"Some thousands," said Armand. "Each one a dead zone of its own, the size of a room or a house, all in the same quarter of an hour, all in the same few miles." He looked at the stone. "Nobody in the history of the tower has ever made two on purpose in the same place. I've read every page there is on it. I wrote some of them. I don't know what some thousands do, laid one over another. Does the town close in a day? A week? Does the river carry it back in, or does the river go dead downstream as far as the sea?" He looked at Emeric. "You don't know either. You can't try it. There's no trying it. So you'd build a thing nobody can know will work, or will stop."

"Then it's a warehouse," said Saban, slowly.

They looked at him.

"When the bailiff comes for a merchant's stock," said Saban, "there's always a man in the trade who fires his own warehouse the night before. I knew two. The bailiff gets ashes and the man gets the satisfaction." He turned his hat round on his knee. "I understood them. I always thought they'd have been better selling the stock the week before and keeping the money." He looked at the stone, and then at Emeric, and his face was not unkind. "And afterward they had no warehouse."

"Who carries it?" said Ward, from the door.

Emeric turned his head.

"Nobody carries it. It goes in the cellar, behind the Forge's door."

"Then it's in a room." Ward had not moved from the door. He said it in the voice he used for a roll, every word given the same weight. "A man who wants Kaldo, takes us, and gets dust. A man who doesn't want Kaldo, who wants it gone, knows that now as well as you do. He doesn't need the cellar. He only needs to cut four bands." He let that sit. "You've made a thing that does what Kaldo's worst enemy would want, to stop him doing what he wants. And you've told him how to fire it."

"Nobody outside this room knows it exists."

"Nobody outside the room knew what Old Edrin's band was, either."

Bram said nothing. Bram sat with his big hands folded on the table in front of him and looked at the grey stone the way he looked at a casting with a flaw in it that he had not put there, and said nothing at all.

Imani said nothing either. She had said hers. She was looking down the table at Ferra.

Ferra had not written anything since he took the stone out of his coat. She had sat with her book open at the morning's page and the pen in her hand, and listened, and he had watched her not write; and now, while Ward was still at the door and Saban still turning his hat, she turned the book's pages back to the front, past the morning and the month and the year, to the place near the front where the clauses were copied fair, and read one of them through. Then she turned forward again, to a clean page, and ruled a line across the top of it.

The pen was the one from the stationer's by the posting house. It had been in her hair all morning, through the knot at the back, as it was most mornings; she had written with it every day for six years, and he had stopped seeing it. Dark wood. The gold band where the fingers went, worn a little brighter on one side than the other. She dipped it.

She wrote slowly, for her, in the hand she kept for things that were meant to be read by somebody who was not her: larger than her working hand, every letter separate. Nobody at the table spoke while she did it. The crane came round again outside and went away.

When she had finished she blotted it, and turned the book round on the table, and pushed it down to him the right way up.

Stayed, by the ninth clause: the stone on this table, and any stone, line or wire made to the same end, now or after.

Reasons. It fires on our own. It brings down every ship in the air that night with every soul aboard; the Hod's book alone has six names, N. Tallis, Tobe, Cade, Arthur, M. Holt, B. Tallis. It empties the ward. It can be fired by anybody who wants Kaldo destroyed and not taken, by doing the very thing it is meant to stop. It cannot be tried, and so nobody can know it will work, or that it will stop. It answers a fear with everything we have.

F. The seventeenth of August, year ten.

He read it twice.

He had written the clause himself. He had written it at his own table in the winter of the fourth year, and given it to Hale to copy fair, and stood in the hall while Hale read it out to a room that went white; and when she had told him across that table that it meant if she wrote it, it stood, even if she was frightened, even if she was wrong, he had told her he was giving it to her because one day he would be. He remembered every word of that, sitting in the long room six years later with her stay in front of him. It did not help at all. He found that he was angrier than he had been since the Staple's cellar, and that there was nobody in the room he could be angry at but the one woman who had done exactly what he had asked her to.

"Lift it," he said.

"No."

"Ferra."

"A stay so written holds until the Chancellor lifts it," said Ferra. She was very pale, and her voice was quite level, and her hand was flat on the table beside the book, pressed down. "The holder may not set it aside. You wrote that. I didn't."

He looked at her down the length of the table, and she looked back, and did not drop her eyes.

He put his hand over the stone.

He did not decide to. He felt the whole table see him do it and go still, Ward's note at the door going still as a wall, and he did not care. He had drained stones since the first winter, to the bottom, a little at a time; he had never once drained one to nothing on purpose, because a stone drained to nothing was gone. He drained this one. It came up into his palm all at once, nine nights of writing and a seed's whole charge with it, hot, a buzz that went up his forearm into his shoulder and his teeth like a knight's first morning; and under his hand the grey went paler, and paler, and then he felt it give, like a crust of ash on a pipe when you put your thumb to it.

He took his hand away.

A small heap of fine grey dust lay on the table where the stone had been, the colour of a winter sky, with the lattice's last pattern still on the top of it for the space of a breath before it slid.

For that same breath his sense found nothing over the heap at all, a hole in the room's mana no bigger than a thumbnail, and then the room filled it.

"There," he said. "Write that."

Ferra drew the book back toward her and wrote it. She wrote it in her working hand, small and upright, on the line under the stay, and he read it upside down, as she knew he could.

One Hollow Crystal, the last not at work. Drained by the holder, at the table, in front of the six. Dust. No pod due before spring. None to be had.

She blotted it.



She did not close the book. He had expected her to, and to say the business was done, and he had been ready to get up; and instead she took a second book out of the pocket of her apron, a small one, plain board, that was not Kaldo's, and laid it on the table beside the first.

He knew it. He had seen it once, in July, in his own room, at a page with only a few names on it and the newest in fresh ink, and he had asked her what the page was, and she had said that it was hers, and that he would see it when there was a table to see it at.

"That was half," said Ferra, to the table and not to him. "A no, by itself, leaves the question where it was. He's right about the question. If one of us is taken, how much goes with them? Nobody's ever asked it in this room." She opened the small book. "So I have."

It was a page for each of them. That was all it was, she said. A page with a name at the top, and under the name, in her square capitals, every box that person held: every room they could walk into that somebody else could not, every figure in their head that was nowhere else, every key on a string. Not what they did. What a man would have, who had them.

She passed it down the table, open, and let them read their own.

Saban's was half a page, and he read it with his eyebrows up and handed it on without a word, which from Saban was very nearly praise. Bram read his for a long time with his lips moving, and then took the book's own pencil out of its spine and added a line at the bottom in his square smith's hand, and gave it on. Armand's ran over the leaf. Imani's was longer than Imani had expected, by her face. Ward came to the table for his, and stood reading it with the book held out at arm's length, and gave it back to Ferra without looking at anybody, and went back to the door.

Ferra turned to the last name, and turned the book round, and put it in front of Emeric.

His page was not a page. It went on for nine leaves in her hand and was not finished; she had left the rest of the book after it blank, and ruled.

"That's my point," said Ferra.

He did not say anything.

"Three kinds of box," said Ferra, to the table. She did not need to look at the book for this; she had evidently said it to herself a great many times. "Open. A thing we show and sell and let them copy, and they copy it badly, and that's the end of it. The lamps, the rail, the paper, the water, soap. The release ladder lives here; the primers go out by it and nothing changes." She held up a second finger. "Shut. People may know it exists. They've seen it, or heard of it, or Saban's told them a story about it. But nobody outside the six holds more than one shut box, and the part that matters goes in last, by the few, at night. The ships. The suits. The wireless. The rubber." A third finger. "Under. Nobody knows it exists at all, and there's nothing to see from outside that says it does. The Forge. Delving. The stones that decide. The liquid. The register. The seed." She put her hand down. "Every name in this book has its boxes by kind. The more under a man holds, the more he walks with Ward, or with somebody like him, and the less he walks down lanes."

"Old Edrin held one shut box," said Saban. "His band. And a few hours of pumps."

"So Old Edrin's taken, and we lose a band and a few hours of pumps. That's what Old Edrin's page says, and it's the truth." Ferra looked down the table at Emeric for the first time since the dust. "Yours says, you're taken and we lose everything. That's not a reason to burn the town. That's a reason for your page to be shorter."

"And the stories?" said Saban. "For the shut ones."

"You keep them. Written down, every one, whatever we tell the coast about a ship or a crate. And I check every one against the money." Ferra turned the small book back to herself. "A story that the money doesn't bear out is a story somebody will one day read in a book, the way I read the Staple's."

"Salt fish," said Saban, and almost smiled. "I'll want a bigger ledger."

There was one more page she showed them, and it was not one of the six. She turned to it near the back. B. RENNER, at the head, and under it the two lines from July with the date and the one word against them; and under those, newer, one box. The crate in Bryda's strongroom at Grayrock, sealed under Ferra's seal, with STANDARDS ROOM. SPARES. stencilled on its lid in the works' black paint. Bryda had the key and did not know what was inside. She knew it was Kaldo's, and that it mattered, and that she would answer for it.

"She's held it since June," said Ferra. "She's never asked what's in it. Now it's written." She closed the small book. "She's the first steward with a page. She'll not be the last."

Bram had been looking at Emeric across the table all through this, steadily, the way he looked at a seam to see if it would hold. He had not said a word since the stone came out of the coat. He did not say one now. He got up when the table rose, and pushed his chair in, and went out without his cup.

Emeric sat where he was until they had all gone but Ferra, who was blotting the morning's last line; and then he got up as well, and did not look at her, and went out, and down the stair, and across the ward, and out of the castle's gate by the lake path toward the shed, because the shed was the farthest place from the long room that he could still call work.



He was angry for three days, and Kaldo went on round him as if he were a stone in a stream.

He did not shout at anybody; he never had. He was short with the yard and polite to everyone else, and he went down to the shed before it was light and came up after it was dark and slept on the saloon's bench in the K Discovery with a coat over him, and he did not go to the east stair, and he did not go down the hill. Nobody asked him to. That was the thing he noticed first, by the Wednesday noon, and it made him angrier still: that nobody needed to.

The stewards' letters went to Ferra direct; Saban carried them across the counting room himself, so that nobody would have to come down to the shed with them. Ward did not follow him down the lake path. He sent Osric to stand at the shed doors instead, which Osric did, eating, with his back to the shed, all day. Ferra sent the day's book down with a clerk at six with the pages he would want marked with paper slips, and the slips were in her hand and had nothing on them but the page numbers.

And Bram ate at his bench.

For nine years Bram had come up the hill at noon and eaten his dinner at the long table, whoever was at it, with his cap on the bench beside him; it was so much the shape of the day that Maren laid his place without thinking. On the Wednesday Emeric went past the works on the way back from the station at a quarter past twelve, and saw through the open doors of the shop the big figure at the far bench with a loaf and a lump of cheese and his knife, eating standing up, with his back to the door and a drawing pinned to the wall in front of him.

On the Thursday it was the same. Emeric stopped in the door. Bram turned round, chewing, and looked at him for as long as it took to finish the mouthful; and then turned back to the drawing, and cut another piece of cheese.

He did not say anything. He did not need to say anything, and they both knew it, and Emeric went on down to the shed.



The set went into the K Discovery in those three days, because it had been going to go in that week anyway and because there was nothing else in Kaldo he could put his hands on that would not want him to talk.

It was the bench engine, the one that had walked Bram's tea off the bench at the start of the month.

On the drawing it was a diesel; the works called it the oil engine. Rebuilt for the ship, with a dynamo on the end of its shaft and a clutch on the other, in a frame of welded angle aft of the battery room, it was a diesel-electric set, and by the Thursday afternoon Fletcher's men were calling it the set and Fletcher was calling it worse.

"It's loud," said Fletcher.

"It's loud."

"I built that ship so she'd go past a man's head at night and he'd think it was an owl." Fletcher stood in the battery room's after door with his arms folded and looked at the set in its frame as if it had been sick on his deck. "And now she sounds like a mill."

"Only when it runs. It never runs in silent running." Emeric was on his back under the frame with a spanner, and did not come out. "It doesn't lift her. It can't. Nothing that weighs that much lifts anything; the stones lift her, and if the stones die, the latch holds her. This is for after." He put the spanner on the last bolt. "She comes down in the sea with her stones asleep. Or in a field. Or on the far side of the Isle with nobody to tow her home. The battery motor gives her a few knots and a night. This charges the battery every day for as long as there's oil in the tank, so the lamps stay lit and the wireless keeps shouting where she is. And it clutches to the propeller shaft. On the water, she goes home on it. In the air, with her thrust stones dead and the latch holding her up, it walks her out of the hole at a crawl."

Fletcher thought about that.

"Ned," he said at last. "In his hayfield. A ship that can't shout."

"Ned."

"Hm." Fletcher went away down the passage, and came back with a tin of the oil from the store and a saucer and a box of matches, and knelt on the battery room's floor and poured an inch of the oil into the saucer, and struck a match and dropped it in.

It went out. It hissed, and lay on the oil's skin, and smoked a little, and went out.

He struck another and held it to the oil's edge until it burned his fingers, and the oil did nothing at all. Then he sat back on his heels.

"Nothing in my ship that lights off a match," he said. "That's the rule." He looked at the saucer. "It doesn't light off a match."

"It wants to be squeezed hot first. A match isn't enough."

"Then it can come aboard." Fletcher got up, and took the saucer with him, and stopped in the door. "The light stuff off the top of that still, the stuff that goes up if you look at it. That never comes in this shed."

"Never."

"I want it in writing."

"Ask the Chancellor," said Emeric, under the frame, and heard how it sounded, and Fletcher heard it too, and went away without saying anything.



Vantic's letter came on the Thursday, by the afternoon post from the capital, in the House's grey wax, and the clerk brought it down to the shed with the day book.

He opened it on the battery room's floor with oil on his fingers.

A single sheet, in her small level hand, addressed to him. It did not begin with a greeting. The House, she wrote, thanked His Grace for the figure, which it had received in July and entered under the question it had asked, three hundred and sixty to one; and she had told the House, as His Grace permitted, the night the figure came. The House noted that the Chancellor declined to price the four men, and concurred. The House had no column for them either. The vessels were struck from the House's list of liabilities as of the date of the figure and entered instead on its list of things His Grace had built that had paid, which was not a short list, and which the House did not show to anybody. She was his servant in all things that could be priced.

And under the signature, after a space, one line in the same hand.

That one was for the House. This one is not. I am glad of the four men. L.

He read it twice, sitting on the floor with his back against the set's frame.

Ferra did not read it over his shoulder. She read most of his letters over his shoulder now, with her chin very nearly on it, and had since the spring, and he had stopped noticing it the way he had stopped noticing the pen in her hair. He noticed now. He sat on the floor of the battery room with Vantic's letter on his knee and the shed quiet round him and nobody's chin anywhere near his shoulder, and read the last line a third time.

Wes. Garrick. Cade. Ned. The four men over Lisle's wood that the Chancellor would not price. And six names in the Hod's book, three of them children he had watched learn to read on Sela's back bench, which Ferra had written out in full in her stay, one after another, so that he would have to read every one.

He folded the letter and put it in his coat, in the inside pocket on the left, where the stone had been on Monday.

At seven Wren came down the lake path with a basket and Maren's compliments, delivered word for word: if His Grace did not eat it in front of the child, Maren would come down and stand over him herself. Wren sat on the threshold beside Osric and watched him eat all of it, with the frank attention of a girl of fourteen who would be asked afterward.



On the Friday afternoon they ran the set in the ship for the first time, with the K Discovery afloat at the shed's end and her propeller arm tilted down into the lake.

It started on the second try. The noise went round the shed's roof like a cart full of iron going over cobbles, and the herons got up off the reeds all along the bank in long grey strings, and Fletcher's men put their hands over their ears and grinned at each other. Then Emeric threw the clutch, and the propeller bit, and she went out into the lake on it, slowly, wallowing a little, under a column of grey smoke from the pipe at her stern, with nothing on earth holding her up but the water, and every stone she had asleep.

He took her round the lake twice. On the way in he cut the clutch and let the dynamo run on its own and watched the battery's needle climb, and in the shed he shut the set down, and the silence came back into his ears like water into a pail.

Ferra was standing on the slip.

She had her book under her arm and the pen in her hair. He did not know how long she had been there. She came aboard over the brow when the ship was made fast, without asking, and down the passage to the battery room, past him at the after door, and stood in front of the set with the book open, and began reading it.

She read the tank first. She had Fletcher's man tell her what it held, and wrote it down, and then had him take the cap off and showed him with her pencil where she wanted the tin's mark scratched on the gauge glass, so that a man could see in the dark how many tins were left. She asked what a tin of the middle cut cost at the still, and what it would fetch at the Wayford depot if they ever sold it, and wrote both, and drew a line between them. She asked what the set weighed, frame and tank and oil and all, and when Fletcher told her she turned three pages back to the ship's lift and read what the stones carried now and what they would carry with the set aboard, and did a sum in the margin, and put her finger on the answer: how much more the racks would have to hold, every run, for the rest of the ship's life, to carry a thing that would never run in the air unless something had already gone wrong.

She looked at that figure for some time.

Then she held the book out to Fletcher, and Fletcher, who had been told an hour before to ask the Chancellor and had decided to keep out of it, looked at Emeric over her head.

"It wants signing," said Ferra. "Somebody's got to put their name to it going on the ship's book. Who's signing?"

"It's the yard's," said Fletcher. "Or his."

"No." Ferra took the book back, and wrote under the figure, and blotted it on her sleeve, and turned it round so they could see: the set entered, the tank and the tin and the weight, and under it her initial. "This one I'll sign."

She closed the book, and put it under her arm, and went back up the passage and over the brow and up the slip, and did not look round.

Fletcher looked at the set for a while.

"She means the other," he said.

Emeric did not answer. He knew what she meant. He had known it since Fletcher's match went out in the saucer, and had not let himself know it, and he stood in the after door of the battery room with his hands black and the set ticking as it cooled and knew it now all the way down: that the thing he had made on Monday failed the wrong way. Tess had written it on her board in white paint, under the name of her box, in a word he had given her and she had not liked. A thing that did not know did nothing, and nothing was danger, and the train stopped. The ships were built that way. The latch, the watchdog, the two rails; a dead stone dropped the ship onto its latch and not into the sea. Every rule he had ever made in Kaldo said that when a thing was not sure, it failed toward the people. And he had built a stone that, when it was not sure, killed them, and taken it up the hill, and been angry for three days at the woman who had written down why.

On his other world they had a word for that as well. He did not need it.

"You'll want to wash," said Fletcher. "Before you go up."



He went up at nine.

She was in the counting room, at her own table, with the lamp lit and the shutter open on the warm dark and the ward's lamps below. She had the duchy's book open and was writing, and she did not look up when he came in, and did not stop. He stood inside the door. She finished the line. She blotted it.

Then she looked up.

He had thought about what he would say all the way up the hill, and he had thrown all of it away at the gate. Every version had been about him. He stood in the door of her counting room and found that what was left was very short.

"You were right," he said.

"Yes," said Ferra.

She did not say anything else, and nor did he. She put the pen down on the book, along the gutter.

"Imani told you on Monday," she said.

"She did."

"And Ward on Tuesday, and Armand, and Saban in his way. And my father's been eating his dinner at his bench for three days with his back to the door." She did not take her eyes off him. "He was right first, after Imani. He's just slower saying it."

"He didn't say anything."

"That's how he says it."

He came across the room, and stopped at the corner of her table, where he had stood a thousand times with a figure for her to check. He did not know what to do with his hands. He put one of them flat on the table beside her book.

"I'm still afraid," he said.

"I know. So am I." She looked at his hand on the table. "That's what the book's for. You'll have a shorter page by midwinter. It'll take years to make it short." She closed the duchy's book, a quarter of an inch, which at that table had always meant the business was done; and then all the way. "I was angry as well. You didn't notice."

He had not.

"Three days," said Ferra. She got up. She stood close, and put her hand flat on his chest over the coat, over the pocket where Vantic's letter was; and he saw her feel the paper there, and not ask. "And a half. You came in at nine." She took her hand back. "Not here. The window's on the ward."



Her room was at the top of the east stair, and dark, because the lamp was not lit and the shutters were closed; there was only the line of the moon under the shutter's foot, grey on the floorboards, and the grey square of the bed.

She did not light the lamp. She stood in the middle of the floor, and reached up behind her head, and drew the pen out of the knot of her hair; and her hair came down with it, all at once, over her shoulders, the copper gone dark in the dark. She looked at the pen in her hand. Then she went to the table under the shutter and laid it down there, carefully, square to the table's edge, the way she laid a pen down on a page she was coming back to.

"Not that one," she said. "Not in the air."

She came back to him. She put her two hands up and took his face between them, and looked at him, close, in the moon's grey.

"Up," said Ferra.

He lifted them.

The glyph came up between them, slow, faint, violet, and lit her face from underneath, and that was the only light in the room. He took his own weight and hers together, and their feet left the boards, and they rose through the dark, slowly, past the table and the shuttered window, to the height of the bed's posts, and past it, until her hair, hanging loose, touched the ceiling's plaster and spread on it a little; and there he held them. It cost him what it always cost, a steady draw under the breastbone like the last mile of a long climb. He did not notice it. He was watching her face.

She kissed him.

She did it the way she had done it in the exchange years before, in the dark under the beams, properly, with both arms round his neck, as if she had finished a long sum; and he waited for the hold to go, for the floor to come up and the ink to go over, the way it always had. It did not go. He felt it stay where it was, steady, under all of it, under her mouth and her hands in his hair and her whole length against him, the way a floor stays under a man who has forgotten it is there.

She felt it too. She drew back a hand's width and looked down past his shoulder at the dark floor a long way under her heels, and back up at him.

"It didn't drop," said Ferra.

"No."

"It always drops." She kissed him again, harder, testing it, the way she would have put her weight on a new stair; and it held. "When did it stop?"

"Somewhere in the spring. I didn't notice either."

She laughed. It was not loud. It came out of her against his mouth, short, as if it had surprised her; and then she put her forehead against his and stayed there, and he felt her breathing, and felt it catch once and steady, and did not ask.

He let the air take them. Not much: a draught, the slowest he could make, so that they turned in the dark under the ceiling the way a leaf turns on still water. His hair had come loose from its tie when she pulled him in, and without any weight to it, it did not fall. It lifted and spread round both their heads, all the long length of it, down toward the middle of his back, slow, like weed in a stream, so that her face was inside it, lit violet from below; and she put her hand up into it and watched it drift through her fingers and not fall.

"That," she said, "I didn't know it would do."

They turned once all the way round, and halfway again. Her feet were bare; she had stepped out of her shoes at the door. He felt her toes find his shin and stay there. She undid his collar while they turned, one button, then the next, and he let her, and did not help, because she did not like being helped with buttons and never had. Her dress buttoned up the back. He found the top one by feel and undid it, and the next, slowly, and she said, against his jaw, that he was slower at it than Imani, and he said that he was taking his time, and she said she could tell, and that she was counting.

"Counting what?"

"Buttons. Eleven." She was quiet a breath. "Turns. Four."

"And?"

"And how long you've held it without it moving." Her mouth was at his ear. "I've got to two hundred and something. I lost it. That's never happened." Then, quite clearly, in the voice she used for a figure: "Down. The bed. I want something under me that isn't you holding it up."

He brought them down.

He did it slowly, all the way, the hold steady to the last inch, so that the quilt came up under her back as if the bed had been lifted to meet her; and only then let the glyph go, and the dark came back into the room entire, and his hair came down round them both with its own weight again, over her face and her shoulders, and she laughed at that too, and pushed it back off her face with both hands, and kept hold of it.

The rest of the buttons went quicker. The dress went somewhere off the end of the bed, and the shift after it, and his shirt, which she pulled off over his head without any patience at all, so that for a breath he was blind in it and she was laughing at him again. Then there was nothing between them, and the room was warm, and the moon's line on the floor had moved a hand's width since they came in, and nobody in Kaldo knew where either of them was except, he supposed, by now, Imani.

He had been away from her three days and a half, in the same castle, past the foot of her stair four times, and had not known until her skin was under his hands how far away that was. She held on to him as though she had been counting the hours as well, and had not liked the total. There was nothing slow in her at first. There was the fight in it, three days of it, and she did not pretend there was not: her hands hard on his shoulders, and her teeth once, not gently, at the side of his neck, and her heel behind his knee pulling him down to her as if she meant to win something.

"Ferra."

"Yes," she said. "Yes. Don't you dare stop to ask me again tonight."

So he did not. He went where she pulled him. She set the pace, as she always had, by deciding it and then saying so: once, "Wait," and he waited, with his arms braced either side of her and his locs hanging round her face like a curtain, and felt her breathe under him and decide; and then, "Now," and he moved, and she let her breath go out of her in a long uneven run that was not a word. After that it was not a fight any more. It went slower, and then slower still, until it was only the two of them moving together in the dark of her room at the pace of somebody walking home, her hands gone from his shoulders into his hair and holding on there, her mouth at his jaw, the bed creaking in a rhythm that he would have been embarrassed by on any other night and could not now have cared about less.

She said his name once, close to his ear, the whole of it, the way she wrote it under a judgment. She said something else after it that he did not catch. And when it came to her she did not say anything; her hands closed in his hair, hard enough to hurt, and her whole body drew up under him and held, as she held a breath before a figure she was not sure of yet; and then let go, all of it, shaking, and laughed into his shoulder, helplessly, the way she had laughed on her back on her trestle in the ink, until he went over after her and could not have said afterward which of them was holding which.

They lay as they were for a long time.

The moon's line had gone off the floor and up the leg of the table, and found the pen lying square to the table's edge, and lit the gold band on it, a small bright line in the dark. Ferra lay with her head on his shoulder and one leg over his and her hand flat on his chest, over his heart. She was not counting. He could feel that she was not.

"Three days and a half," she said, after a while. "I'll not do that again."

"Nor will I."

"You will. Something else. Some year." Her fingers moved on his chest, a little, as if checking a figure she already knew. "And I'll write it down, and you'll be angry, and my father will eat at his bench. And then you'll come in at nine."

He did not argue with her. After a while she was asleep, all at once, as she always went, like a book shut; and he lay with her weight on him in the dark, and her hair across his face, and was asleep himself before the moon reached the top of the table's leg.



Imani came up at seven with bread from Maren's oven and the ward's night on her face, and sat down at the little table in Ferra's room, and looked at the two of them, and took a piece of bread.

They told her. Both of them, as the rule was: Ferra first, in order, flat, the counting room and the stair and the pen on the table and the lift and the bed, and Emeric after, the part Ferra had left out, which was that he had been wrong, and from when. Imani heard it all and ate her bread. She did not laugh at any of it. At the part about the hold not dropping she looked at Emeric across the table for a long breath with her doctor's face, the one for a patient who has got better in a way she had not expected and finds interesting; and said nothing about it, which meant she would say something later.

"I told him on Monday," she said, when they had finished.

"You did," said Ferra.

"At my bench. With his hand in his pocket on it the whole time, like a boy with a frog." She buttered the bread. "I told him it was wrong and why, and he went and put it on your table anyway." She looked at Ferra. "So I was first."

"You were first."

"Write it down."

Ferra looked at her. Then she opened her book at the inside of the back cover, where she kept the column with no heading that she believed he did not know about, and wrote the date, and after it, in square small capitals, two words; and turned the book round so that Imani could read it.

I. FIRST.

"Good," said Imani, and finished her bread, and went to bed.

Bram came up the hill at noon that day and ate his dinner at the long table, with his cap on the bench beside him, and said nothing about that either.



The mill began the week after. Paxton bought the tithe barn and its two ponds on Ferra's figure and not his own; the first of the flax went into the water at Milford to ret on the Wednesday, in bundles weighted with river stones, and the whole of the town's west end smelled of it within the week and complained to Paxton about it, at length, and Paxton sent the complaints to Ferra, who filed them. Bram cleared the end of his floor by the yard doors for the ring frames and put two of his best men on the first loom's crank, and on the wall above them, in the works' black paint, at Imani's asking and in her words, he put up the rule. Nobody under the school-leaving age on a frame. Not to sweep. Not to piece an end. Imani came to look at it, and read it, and said it would do.



Dov Kettle came down from Grayrock on the Monday, the thirtieth, on the morning train, with his tools in a box on his shoulder and a letter from the mill's master in his apron pocket.

He gave the letter to Bram at the works door without a word. It said, in a master millwright's careful hand, that Kettle had fitted the new wheel's shaft truer than any shaft the mill had ever turned, and had been impossible every day of the month, and that the mill's master released him to Kaldo with his blessing and some relief. Bram read it twice and put it in his own apron.

"Bay three," said Bram. "End of the shop. Past the second pillar." He did not point. "Hers."

Dov nodded, as if he had expected it, and went down the shop with the box on his shoulder between the benches. Emeric was at the end of bay three with Tess and Merrin when he came in, and Isa at her spool, and Dov put the box down on the floor at the end of Tess's bench without asking where, and straightened his back.

"Your gate tower's relays," he said.

Tess looked up.

"On your telegraph. I looked at them coming up through the town. Your clerk let me." Dov took a pin out of his apron pocket, a short steel pin no longer than a fingernail, and put it on the bench in front of her. "That's out of one. Steel, in a brass hinge. Brass wears to steel faster than you'd think, and oval, and it's worn already. By the spring that tongue'll sit a hair to one side and stick on a wet morning, and your clerk'll send a word that isn't the word the line sent him." He looked at the pin. "Your box at the junction's good. You'd not have that in your box."

"No," said Tess. "I wouldn't." She picked the pin up and turned it to the light from the river doors and looked at the wear on it, a long time. "How many did you take out?"

"One. I put another in. Mine." Dov looked at her steadily. "Steel in steel. Hardened. I'll do the rest when you say."

Tess put the pin down.

"Say," she said.

Dov took his apron off and hung it on the nail at the bench's end that had been empty since the spring, as if it had been left there for him; and Bram, in the bay's door with his arms folded, watched his best fitter in a year go to work at the wrong end of his own shop, and turned round and went back to his floor, and was short with everybody on it until Thursday.



The table sat on the Tuesday, the last day of the month, at ten, in the long room, with the crane going outside.

The business was ordinary. When Ferra turned the page and looked down the table and asked if there was anything else, he said there was.

"I'm going north on Thursday," he said. "On the lift hull, with Old Edrin at the seat. Not to the capital. To Varek's fort on the strait. He has a man there who says there are Randillian soldiers alive on the Isle of Shards." He looked down the table at each of them. "I want to ask the man if I can see what he remembers. Ask him. In words he can say no to."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"I'll come," said Saban.

Ward, at the door, said nothing; he would be on the lift hull on Thursday at dawn whatever anybody said, and they all knew it.

Imani looked at him across the table, and then at Ferra.

Ferra had the pen out of her hair. She looked at him a breath longer, and then she wrote it down: the date, and the place, and the names of who would go, and the reason, in the words he had used. Then she blotted it, and looked up.

"That's all I'm writing," she said.

That night he went to the chest in his room and took out the plain wood box, and opened it, and looked at the old flannel lying in it with nothing on it, and the faint grey stain at one corner where the stones had lain. He did not throw it away. He put it on the shelf beside Bram's silver star in its own box, with its four claws open and nothing in them; two empty boxes side by side. Then he sat down at his table and opened his notebook to the page with the drawing on it, and the small name under the drawing, and looked at it a long time.

He did not tear it out. Ferra would have wanted it kept. He drew a single line through it, corner to corner, the way she did when a thing was struck and not hidden, and wrote the date under the line, and closed the book.
