Chapter 62 - Crack

He had the third fire in his hand when he heard it.

It was a small sound, from across the water, from somewhere at the far end of the bridge among the burning houses: a crack, short and dry, like a knuckle, or a stone dropped onto another stone. Nobody behind the carts turned their head. In all that noise there was no reason to.

Then the wind came.

It came from where the sound had come from, across the river, and through the carts, and through him, and it was the strongest wind he had ever stood in, and it did not move anything. The flames on the roofs across the river stood up straight in it and did not lean. The smoke went up in its columns and did not bend. Rylan's hair, under his pushed-back helmet, did not stir. Emeric felt it go through him from front to back, a great soundless rush, the way a wave goes through a man standing in the sea, and he felt it take something with it as it went.

The fire in his hand went out.

He had been holding it over the river, a long sheet of it, halfway between the far bank and the sky, and his hold on it went the way a rope goes when somebody cuts it at the other end, all at once, with nothing on the end of the pull, and the flame he had been holding had nothing to burn in the empty air and went out over the water like a blown candle. The glyph over his fingers went with it, between one breath and the next.

Beside him the woman with the grey plait made a sound. Her ring had gone out too. Below the bridge the water she had been lifting out of the river fell back into it with a slap, short of the roof it was meant for, and she sat down on the ground behind the cart as if somebody had pulled her stool away, and looked at her hands.

Further along, at the river tower, the other ring was gone.



He put his hand up again.

He did it without thinking, because his hand knew how, and he reached for the fire across the river as he had reached for every fire for two years, and nothing came. Not a glyph. Not the start of one. He could feel his own mana inside him, all of it, where it always was, drawn well down by the two roofs, warm, ready, and he pushed it outward, and it did not go anywhere. It was like pushing on a door that had turned into wall.

He tried the other thing. He had not meant to. He reached for the nearest loose thing he could see, a stone on the bridge's parapet, toward his hand, the pull he had practiced alone at night with the shutters closed for two years, and there was nothing in that either. The stone sat where it was.

He was a man with his hands in his pockets.

"What was that?" Rylan said.

The woman with the grey plait answered him. She had not got up. "Somebody broke a stone," she said. "Over there. A big one." She looked at her hands again, and then at Emeric, and there was no professional interest left in her face at all. "We've nothing. None of us. Not till it fills back in."

"How long?"

"Nobody knows. A day."



Overhead the lamp hissed.

It had not stopped. Not one of them had stopped. Along the wall, as far as he could see, the lamps burned on in their hoods, white and steady, exactly as before, and the river lay under them as white as it had been, and the far bank and the dock quarter's roofs and the wharf and the men on the wharf were lit to the last button.

The men on the wharf were looking up.

He could see it from the carts: the whole far bank, all at once, stopping and looking up at the wall. They had been told something. They had been told that when the stone broke the lights would go out. A fire trick and a great many lamps, and every soldier on the river knew what a dead zone did to a lamp stone. They had been waiting for the dark. They had been going to come across the bridge in it.

The lamps burned. The light lay on them. On the road at the top of the ore yard a man on a horse, in plate, with no helmet on and a short grey beard, turned the horse round in a circle under the light, looking up at the wall, and Emeric could see his mouth open, shouting something at the men around him that did not carry over the fire.

Armand had said he would give a good deal to see his face. Emeric saw it. It did not help.

He looked along the wall at the lamps, burning in their hoods over the heads of the bowmen, and thought of the wire going down the bank to the powerhouse, and the belt off Harl's wheel, and Tess at her lever, and the stack of Bram's plate gone into breastplates, and the bows. None of it had ever needed him. That had been the whole idea of it. He thought of the dyehouse wall, and the list in chalk, and the last line at the bottom of it in Tess's careful hand, which he had looked at and not crossed out.



It was Ward who understood first what it meant for the carts.

He came down the wall stair at a run, which Ward did not do, and he was shouting before he reached the bottom, which he also did not do. "Bows to the carts. All of them. Spears behind." He took Emeric by the arm, hard, and put him behind the second cart, and did not let go until he had looked at him. "You have nothing?"

"Nothing."

"Then you are a man," Ward said. "Stay behind the cart." And he let go and went to the front of the barricade, where Rylan was already standing up and pulling his helmet back on.

Because the knights still had everything.

Emeric had known it. He had known it since September, and said it to himself again at Armand's chair a week ago: a knight's mana is inside him, and it goes on working. He had known it the way he knew a sum. He had not known it like this. They came over the far end of the bridge in the white light, five of them this time, every knight Grayrock had left, and they came across that bridge faster than he had ever seen men move. There were no rings of water now to knock them off their feet. There was no fire over the river. There was the bridge, and the carts, and the bows.

The bows were enough for two of them. At that range, down the length of a stone bridge with no room on it to turn, a bolt that found a joint or the thin iron of an arm went in, and two of them went down on the bridge and did not get up. The rest reached the carts.

It was very close and very fast after that, and he saw it in pieces.

He saw a Grayrock knight come over the top of the first cart in one jump and land among the spearmen, and two of the spearmen go down in the time it took him to land. He saw Osric take that man from the side, low, and both of them go down together in a heap of iron. He saw Declan with blood running down the inside of his leg from somewhere high up, not looking at it, holding the gap between the second cart and the wall alone with his back to the stone. He saw Ward, everywhere, never fast, never once where the man in front of him had thought he would be. He saw Rylan shout something, and laugh.

And he saw the gate-crew man who had sat in the church the night before working a word out on a slate in chalk before he would let it near the paper, kneeling at the end of the first cart with his crossbow spanned and his eye along it, and a Grayrock knight come round the end of the cart, and the gate-crew man shoot him through the body from a yard away, and the knight not stop.

The gate-crew man fell back against the wheel with his bow across his knees and did not move again.

Emeric had his bow off him, and the belt with the hook on it, before he knew he was going to do it. He put his foot in the stirrup and the hook to the string and stood up straight, the way Ward had made every man in the garrison do it, every morning since April, and the string came back and caught on the nut. He laid a bolt in the groove. The knight who had killed the gate-crew man was at the second cart, with his back half turned, fighting Declan. Emeric put the bow to his shoulder and shot him in the back, at five paces, where the plate was jointed, and the knight went down on his face across the cart.

He bent to the stirrup and spanned the bow again.

He did not cast another spell that night. He shot that crossbow until his hands were raw, and he did not know whether he hit anything after the first, and he did not think about any of it at all until much later, when he was sitting on the ground behind the cart with the bow across his own knees, and the bridge in front of him was clear, and the men on it were not moving, and somebody was crying, and he could not tell who.

Three men from Kaldo were dead at the carts. The gate-crew man, and two of the townsmen Ward had given spears to, whose names Emeric did not know and would have to ask Ferra for. Osric was alive, under the Grayrock knight he had taken off the cart, and swearing at the weight of him, and had to be dug out. Declan had sat down at last, against the wall, with both hands pressed high up on the inside of his leg, and was very white, and two of Imani's women came down the lane with a door between them and took him away on it, arguing with them the whole way. Ward walked along the carts and looked at every man there, one at a time, and said nothing to any of them, and every one of them sat up a little straighter when he had gone by.

Rylan came and sat down beside Emeric with his back to the cart. He looked at the bow across Emeric's knees, and then at Emeric's hands on it.

"You'll want Imani for those," he said.

Emeric looked down. The skin was off both his palms and the insides of his fingers, from the stock and the hook and the bolts, and he had not felt it, and he did not feel it now.



The dock quarter burned.

There was nothing between it and the fire now. The roofs he had pulled the flames off were burning again, from the roofs beside them, and the roofs beside those, and the fire went up the lanes away from the river faster than a man could walk. It went from thatch to thatch and from shed to tarred shed, and where it found the net lofts, with their winters of tarred twine stacked to the rafters, it went up with a roar he could hear over everything, and the flames stood twice and three times the height of the houses, and the heat came across the river onto his face like an open oven door.

He could do nothing. He stood at the carts and watched, with the crossbow hanging from his hand.

The buckets came. Ferra had the line by then. He did not know when she had come down from the exchange, or how long she had been there, but she was at the water gate at the bridge foot with her book shut under her arm and a line of people going out through the gate to the river and back, a long line, the dock quarter's own people in it, passing buckets hand to hand up from the water and along the foot of the wall to the houses at the bridge end, where the sparks were coming down. The sparks came across the river on the heat, big ones, whole burning wisps of thatch, and landed on the roofs of the town side, and the bucket line put them out, one after another, roof after roof, all night, and not one house on the town side of the river caught. Ferra counted the buckets. He heard her doing it, under her breath, in the gaps in the noise, and did not think she knew she was doing it.

Across the river nobody could get to anything.

He saw an old man come out of a burning net loft at the river end of a lane, slowly, with a coil of net over his shoulder, and walk down across the moorings into the river until the water was over his knees, and stand there, in the white light of the lamps, with the fire behind him and the net on his shoulder, looking at the town. Emeric did not know which of the nine he was. He watched him until the smoke came down across the moorings and he could not see him any more.

Somewhere back in the burning lanes a cow was bellowing. Then it stopped.

Below the dock quarter, round the bend of the bank, bay three stood dark at the edge of the light, with its door shut, and the fire was coming down the lanes toward it.

The man with the grey beard was still at the top of the ore yard, on his horse, riding up and down behind his men in the light of his own fire. He was well out of bowshot, and knew it. Twice the bowmen on the wall tried him anyway, and twice the bolts fell short among the ore heaps, and he did not look round either time.

Ward watched the horse, and then the men who turned when it turned. "Renner," he said, from the wall above, and nothing else.

Emeric watched the man on the horse for as long as the smoke would let him. The grey beard. The bare head. The straight back, and the arm that pointed and the men that went where it pointed. He wanted to be able to find that man again, anywhere, in any light.



A runner came down from the keep a little before the sky began to go grey. One of the boys from the school, sent from the line room, white to the lips, with the message in his mouth so that he would not forget it.

"The South Gate says horsemen," he said. "On the road. From the west. Lots." He swallowed. "The man said to say lots."

Ward heard it. He was sitting on the ground by the first cart with his back to the wheel, beside the gate-crew man, and he had his eyes shut, and he opened them.

"Milford," he said.

The levy. Paxton's men, on the road from the west with the carter's wagon behind them, coming to the South Gate at first light because that was what Renner had asked them for. Emeric thought of the last line of the letter, in its square heavy hand. He thought of his own answer, at the bottom of a cask of spirit: stay at the back, and nobody comes looking.

He did not know whether Paxton had read it. He did not know whether a man who had written that he was aware of what it made him would stand at the back of anything.

"The South Gate has its crew," Ward said. "And its lamp." He shut his eyes again. "It will be light soon."

Then the shouting came up from below the bridge.

It came from downstream, from under the near bank beyond the bridge's last arch, where the wall could not see: the reeds below the mill and the powerhouse, which Ward had looked at in May and not liked. Men shouting. Metal on metal. And a voice he knew, high and furious: Tess's, a single word that he could not make out.

Overhead, for the first time that night, the lamp flickered.
