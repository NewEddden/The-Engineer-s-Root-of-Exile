Chapter 56 - A Law of Nature

They found Jory four days up the road, off it by about sixty yards, in the scrub below a bank where the track bent east around a stand of alders.

He had been nineteen. He had ridden the northern run eleven times in two years and had been given it in the first place because he was light, which was the only qualification anybody had ever needed for it. He had gone out of the Western Gate on the fourth of the month with the quarter's satchel and a fresh horse and had not arrived anywhere.

The horse was gone. The satchel was gone. His boots were gone and his coat was gone and the purse he carried for the waypoints was gone, and whoever had done it had taken the time to take the boots, which Ward said afterward was the part that told you what kind of men they were and not much else, because it was the kind of thing every kind of men did.

Ward went up himself with eight and brought the body home in seven days. Imani laid him out. Doran buried him on the first dry morning, in the new ground, with his mother and his two sisters standing at the front and half the courier roll behind them.

Emeric priced it the way it presented itself, which was as banditry.

He had done this before. Three years earlier, going down the long water, he had come through a stretch of ground that nobody held and nobody rode and nobody was responsible for, and he had written it down that night as what it was, which was a policing failure with a name attached to nobody. He had put it on a list.

He did it again. Escorts doubled on the northern run, two riders minimum and never the same pair twice in a row. A standing arrangement with a Wayford stabler for remounts so that nobody had to sleep rough between the fork and the crossing. A letter to the crown's road office, which produced nothing, which surprised nobody. And a line at the bottom of a list in the notebook: the stretch between the fork and the alders, unheld, unpatrolled, costing Kaldo one boy.

He was angry about the list. Not about the answer, which he was satisfied with. About the fact that the answer was a list, and that a list was where things went when there was no money and no writ and no appetite to fix them, and that he had now put the same category of thing on the same list twice in three years and solved neither.

It did not occur to him at any point that winter that he had the wrong category.

The barony paid the household at the rate for a man killed in service, which Ferra had set two years earlier and which Emeric had not looked at since. He signed the authority in a stack of eleven other things on a Thursday.

The winter went the way winters went now.

Armand's baseline programme took its first four hundred readings and Armand complained about the cold in the measuring room until Colm built him a stove and then complained about the stove. The upper workings froze again and the lower seam came down. Vantic went away for a month in the middle of it, having given Ferra the date she would return, and returned on it, and nobody in the keep learned where she had been and nobody thought to ask.

A letter came from Grayrock, in Bryda's own hand, correct and impersonal, proposing that the grain carts move to the eleventh and the twenty-fifth for the spring because the new road had changed what the horses could do in a day. Emeric read it twice and found nothing in it that was not in it.

The thought arrived in April, in the foundry yard, while he was doing something else entirely.

He was arguing with Ferra about whether the second weighing of the ore should happen at the pithead or at the gate, which was a real question with money in it, and she was saying that a weight taken at one end of a road and disputed at the other end is a weight that has to be taken twice or not at all, and she said the word arrived.

And Marcus, who had not surfaced in a long time and who never came when he was wanted, put his hand up.

Marcus had sent, in twenty-two years at a desk, something on the order of ten thousand messages. He had no idea what the true figure was and it did not matter; the point was the order of magnitude and the other point was that not one of them had ever failed to arrive. Not one. Sending was arriving. They were the same act with two names, and if you had asked him at any moment in those twenty-two years whether a message he had sent had reached the person he had sent it to, he would not have understood the question well enough to answer it. He would have asked whether the man had replied.

It had felt, for an entire adult life, like a law of nature.

It had been a habit. A habit that had never once been contradicted, across ten thousand instances, in a world engineered at enormous expense to make sure it never would be. And a habit that is never contradicted is not distinguishable from a law from the inside. There is no test available to the man standing in it. He does not know he is holding an assumption, because an assumption that has never been violated does not feel like anything at all.

It cost him, the way they always cost him. He stood in the foundry yard with a live argument in front of him and lost about a minute and a half to an office he had not seen in seven years, and the smell of it, and the particular grey of the light through the window nearest his desk, and Doyle. It was the most expensive thirty seconds he had spent in a year and by an enormous margin the most useful.

"Ferra."

"I'm not finished."

"What has the capital ever sent back to us?"

She stopped. It was not the question she had been braced for and she took a moment with it.

"Sent back," she said.

"In two years. Anything. A refusal. An acknowledgment. A clerk's line saying a matter was received and would be considered. A demand for more detail. A demand for less. Anything at all that refers to a thing we sent them."

Ferra did not answer immediately, which was how he knew the answer.

"No," she said.

They went through it that evening, and what they had sent was this.

Eight quarter accounts. The census correction, with both figures and both dates, which she had been proud of. A full report of the war, written within the month, in Emeric's own hand, with Ferra's figures attached and the coalition's numbers stated honestly in both the wrong and the corrected version. The ore contract, lodged for the record as a holder is obliged to lodge an exclusive arrangement with a neighbouring barony. Three petitions: one for a writ over the lower water hamlets, one for relief on the crown tithe in the year after the burning, one about the school. A query about whether a toll levied at the crossing was lawful. The report on Sethe's hiring. Two letters about the road office. A request for a physician's licence for Imani that Emeric had sent without telling her.

Twenty-one items across two years. Not one of them had ever been answered, refused, acknowledged, queried or referred to.

And he had had an explanation for it the entire time.

The explanation was that a third son exiled to a plague barony in the south is not somebody the capital writes to. It was not a stupid explanation. It was the correct explanation for nineteen of the twenty-one items taken individually, and it fitted every single thing he had ever observed, and he had been given it at the beginning by his own father in a courtyard, and it had been true then.

He had never tested it. Not once. He had not tested it because there had never been an afternoon when testing it was the most useful thing he could do with the afternoon, and because it was not an assumption, it was just how things were, and you do not test how things are.

Nobody in the room said anything about the coalition letters. Nobody in the room remembered them.

Ferra found the shape in two days, once she had been asked the question, and she found it because she had been logging every outgoing dispatch by date and carrier and destination for four years without anybody asking her to.

She brought it up on two sheets and she did not editorialise.

"A road that loses letters loses them at random," she said. "That is what loss is. Random loss takes everybody's post, in proportion to how much post everybody sends, and it takes the interesting and the dull at the same rate, because the road cannot read."

"And?"

"This road has not lost Saban's correspondence. Not one item in four years, and he moves more paper than we do. It has not lost the factor's letters. Hers leave every week on the same day whether anything has happened or not and every one of them arrives, because the answers come back." She turned the sheet round. "It has not lost the Duvrenne letters, or Milford's, or the Grayrock schedules. It has lost twenty-one items out of Kaldo in two years and it has lost them into silence."

"Only ours."

"Not only ours. Only the crown's." Her finger came down. "Everything in that column has one thing in common that nothing outside it has. It was addressed to the crown or to an office of the crown. Everything else this barony sends north gets where it is going."

Emeric looked at the two columns for a long time.

"That isn't loss," he said.

"No," Ferra said. "Loss doesn't choose."

He built the test himself over the following week and he built it to be boring, which took some discipline, because his first four designs had been clever.

Three dispatches. Ordinary ones, with nothing in any of them that anybody would want: a return on the crossing's traffic, a note on the parish rolls, a restatement of a figure in the last quarter's account that had been rounded wrong in Kaldo's favour by four crowns. Nothing worth reading. Nothing worth stopping. Sent at intervals across the spring and the early summer, months apart, because a test with three points taken in one fortnight is not a test of anything except one fortnight's weather.

Each of the three went up the crown post in the ordinary way, sealed, entered in the ordinary book at the gate.

And each of the three was paired with something the road did not know about.

A word-for-word duplicate, unsealed and folded inside a bale of Saban's, going north by a different route entirely and put into the hands of a factor in the capital with instructions to open it and write back confirming the date and the contents.

A sealed note of Saban's own, entirely unrelated, sent to a correspondent in the capital on the same day by the same crown post, so that if the road was simply eating everything out of Kaldo on those days it would show.

And a receipt, taken and lodged at a waypoint the crown post did not control, attesting that a satchel of such a weight, with such a seal, had been in the rider's hands and moving north on that date.

Ferra queried the fourth line of the design, which was that nobody outside five people was to be told what any of it was for, including the riders, including the factor in the capital, who was told only to confirm receipt. Emeric held it. It was not the sort of secrecy he liked, and he said so, and he held it anyway.

Then they waited, and the barony went on being a barony, and it took four months.

All three crown copies died.

Every one of the pairs survived. The factor in the capital confirmed all three duplicates, with dates, in a bored hand, and asked whether Kaldo required anything further. Saban's unrelated notes all arrived and were all answered. All three waypoint receipts came back.

So: three items sealed for the crown, entered in a book, carried by three different riders, on three dates across four months, in three kinds of weather, none of which arrived. And beside each one, on the same day, out of the same gate, a thing that did arrive.

Ferra laid the geometry out on the table with the waypoint receipts in date order and a finger on each.

A letter out of Kaldo going north crossed at the Left Bridge, went up the east bank, took the fork, and ran six days to the crossing, where the crown post consolidated the southern bags, sorted them, and sent them on north in a different rider's hands. Every receipt they had was from south of that point. Every confirmation they had of a thing arriving came from a route that did not pass through it.

There was one place on six hundred kilometres of road where every single thing Kaldo sent to its king was physically in somebody's hands, out of a bag, on a table, being sorted.

"Wayford," Emeric said.

He said it in the small room with four people in it, and then he gave the only order he gave about it for a year.

Ferra. Ward. Saban. Armand, because Armand had come in behind Saban and Emeric had made a decision in the half second available to him and had not regretted it since.

"Nobody says that name outside this room," he said. "Not to a clerk. Not to a rider. Not to Imani, not to Doran, not to Vesk. Not in a letter, in any form, in any code, because I am not clever enough to write a code that a man who reads letters for a living cannot see is a code."

Saban asked how long.

"Until I have something a court will take. It could be years."

"You understand what you are asking," Armand said. "You have found the thing and you are proposing to do nothing about it."

"I'm proposing to let it continue." Emeric put both hands on the table. "The moment he knows we can see it he stops, or he changes how he does it, and either way what I have is a year of dead letters and a theory. He is not committing a crime that leaves a body. The method is the evidence. If he changes the method there is nothing left to find, and the last two years become a thing that happened and cannot be proved."

Nobody argued with it. Ward said, after a while, that it was the first time he had heard Emeric decide to be robbed on purpose, and Emeric said that was about right.

It was Saban who asked the question, a week later, at the end of an entirely different conversation, with his hand already on the door.

"What was the boy carrying?"

Emeric said he did not know offhand.

He went and looked it up. It took him about two minutes, because Ferra logged every dispatch by date and carrier, and the fourth of the month was there in her hand with the rider's name beside it.

The quarter's reports. The crown's copy.

He sat with that for most of an hour in a room with nobody in it.

A rider four days out, off the road by sixty yards, with the horse gone and the boots gone and the purse gone and the satchel gone. Taken by men who had made it look like exactly what it looked like, and who had done it well enough that a man who had been suspicious of everything in his life for six years had not been suspicious of it for one afternoon. And a policing failure on a list, in his own hand, underneath another one.

He did not say any of it out loud, then or later. There was nothing to say that would not have been about him.

He found Ferra in the counting room and asked her to find the boy's household and double what the barony had paid them.

"It's been done," she said.

"When?"

"The week it happened. I did it out of the contingency and entered it. You signed the sheet it was on." She did not look up. "And it was done again in the autumn, and that one wasn't the barony. The courier roll put a purse together. Eleven of them, out of their own wages, and they asked me not to write down who gave what, so I wrote down the total and left the rest out."

Emeric stood in the doorway for a moment.

"Do it a third time," he said.

"That's charity now, not compensation. It'll read as guilt."

"Do it anyway."

"All right," Ferra said, and wrote it down.

He left for the capital in the first week of the following spring, which was the earliest the road was fit and the latest his patience would bear.

He took Ward and nobody else. Not Ferra, because Ferra in a carriage was Ferra not in the counting room for six weeks, and because what he was carrying was a pattern and not yet a case. Not an escort, because an escort going north out of Kaldo was a thing somebody would write down.

The satchel held the dispatch log, the three waypoint receipts, the factor's three confirmations, the two columns Ferra had drawn, and the list of twenty-one items with their dates.

He did not hand it to a courier. He did not hand it to the crown post. He did not put it in a bale of Saban's. It went across the Left Bridge under his own coat, on his own horse, and it stayed there for eleven days, through Wayford, where he changed horses and paid a stabler and said nothing to anybody and did not look at the crossing house any longer than a man looks at a building.

What he had, he knew perfectly well, was not a crime. It was an absence with a shape to it, and an argument about what shapes mean, and no name on any document anywhere.

He rode north with it anyway.
