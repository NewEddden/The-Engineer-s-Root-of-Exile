Chapter 88 - The Sick-Day Column

Imani came to the exchange on a Tuesday in the second week of June, without being sent for, with her register under her arm.

She did not come to the exchange. In nearly three years Emeric had seen her in the back room twice, once to complain about the price of lye and once to complain about the price of lye again, and both times she had stood in the door and said what she had to say and gone. This time she came in and put the register down on the end of Ferra's trestle, on top of whatever Ferra had been working on, and sat down on the bench as if she meant to stay until something was settled.

"I have something that doesn't behave," she said.

The register was the ward's. Every name that had come through the door since the ward opened, and the day, and what was wrong, and what was done, and when the person went home, or did not. Before that, in the front of the same book, in a younger and worse hand, every name she had seen since she was nineteen, when she took the post from her mother. The plague pages were the worst written and the most complete. Emeric had seen the book on the shelf by the ward's side door a hundred times. He had never seen it off the shelf.

Ferra looked at it on top of her work, and at Imani, and moved her work out from underneath it without saying anything, which was a kind of welcome.

"You keep the work rolls," Imani said to her.

"I keep the work rolls."

"Every day a man doesn't come in, you write it, and the overseer's word for why."

"Since the first spring."

"And I write what the overseer doesn't know." Imani put her hand flat on the register. "Nobody's ever laid them side by side."

So they laid them side by side.

It took until well past dark. Ferra's rolls were in her own neat columns, one sheet for each crew, a stroke for each day lost and a word beside it: sick, hurt, burying, drunk, gone. Imani's register was in names. Ferra read a name off a roll and Imani found it in the register, and read what was wrong with that man on that day, and Emeric wrote the two of them down together on a clean sheet, crew by crew. Ferra was quicker than either of them. She was quicker than both of them put together, and did not wait for them, and by the fourth crew she had begun to read the next name before Emeric had finished writing the last, and he had to ask her twice to go back, and she went back twice with the face of a woman being patient on purpose.

The farm crews lost days at the rate farm crews lose days. The garrison lost a little fewer, being fed better and made to wash. The dyehouse lost more, and the lime kiln more again, and nobody was surprised by either.

The still crew lost a third fewer than the farms.

So did the four women on the drying floor at the soap yard, where the bars cured on racks over a bed of the barony's charged fire stones in the damp months.

"Since October," Imani said, before he could ask. "Since the two mages started filling the stones and the stones went back to the still and the racks. Before October those nine were losing days the same as anybody. After October, they aren't." She turned the register round to show him. "And cuts. A man on the farms cuts his hand on a sickle, it closes in nine days. Ten. A man on the still burns his hand on the worm, it closes in six. Seven. One at a time you'd never see it. I didn't. I saw it at the ninth one."

"Age," Emeric said.

"Two of the still crew are the oldest men on any crew in the barony. I looked."

"Food."

"Same kitchen. They eat the barony's noon bowl with everybody else. Ferra's issue book says so and so does Maren."

"Hours. Where they sleep."

"Same hours as the soap yard, which loses days like the farms. Six of the nine live in the lower ward, in the same lanes as the dyehouse men, who go down at the usual rate." She shut the register. "I've been sitting with it for five weeks. I've taken out everything I can think of. There's one thing left that the nine have and nobody else has. They have their hands on charged stone, bare, all day."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"I'm telling you a column is wrong," Imani said. "I'm not telling you a cause."



He walked home that night by the long way, and did not sleep much when he got there.

He had thought it before. Not this. Something near it. In the first autumn, at the front of the long room, a boy had asked whether they could be mages, and Emeric had said if they studied hard, and heard it go out of his mouth half a lie, because one in five hundred was born with the faculty open and nothing anybody knew of opened it in the rest. Except that Armand's old notebook said there were made mages. A handful of old knights, in all the tower's records, who had lived among stones for fifteen or twenty years until the sense came awake in them. Fifteen years. A handful of men. Nobody, so far as he knew, had ever tried it on purpose.

Nobody had tried it on purpose here either. He had put charged stones in the hands of nine people every working day since October because a still wanted heat and a drying rack wanted heat and a fire stone was cheaper than wood. That was all. Nobody had asked them anything. He had not known there was anything to ask.

And in April, Linnet Shaw had sat on the edge of the well in the garrison yard and read every man who carried for the still crew with a drained stone held for a count of a hundred, and they had read as low as anybody. A breath. Whatever was in Imani's column, it was not in Linnet's slate. The register said something was happening. The slate said nothing was.

He lay on his back and looked at the ceiling and thought that he would very much like to know which of them was right before he said anything to anyone.



Imani did not want to know which of them was right before he said anything to anyone.

"Today," she said.

It was the next morning, at the side door of the ward, in the sun, with a basin of something steaming on the step between them that she had been about to throw out when he came up the street. She did not throw it out. She stood over it with her arms folded.

"I'd like to know what it is first."

"So would I. So would they."

"If I tell nine people that the stones in their hands are doing something to them, and I don't know what, I've frightened nine people for nothing. If it's good, every man in Kaldo will want a stone in his hand by Friday. If it's bad, they'll leave the still, and the still pays half the garrison." He heard himself and went on anyway. "And it goes round the town by dark, and the next thing it's at Doran's door, and somebody asks Doran whether the Light approves of a barony that changes people with stones."

"All of that's true," Imani said.

"Then wait. Until Armand can look at them. Until there's something to tell."

"There is something to tell." She did not raise her voice. Imani never raised her voice; she lowered it, and the lower it got, the more it carried. "There's a column. It's about their bodies. They've had their hands on a thing all day for eight months, and something is happening to them, and you know it and they don't." She looked at him. "They're not your column. They're people."

"I'm not saying never."

"You're saying later. You said later to me." She did not look away. "You had my letters in your hand in August, and you knew what had been done with them, and you said later. You said it again on my step in October. You thought it was kinder. You told me in the laundry yard the next year, and do you know what I minded, when you told me?"

He did. He did not say it.

"Not the letters," Imani said. "The months. That a man had decided what I could stand to know about my own life, and decided it for me, and been kind about it." She bent and picked up the basin and threw the water out into the gutter, where it ran away down the hill steaming. "They can stand being frightened. People stand being frightened every day. What they can't stand is finding out a year from now that you knew, and kept it, and called it waiting."

She went in with the basin and left the door open behind her.

He stood on the step. He had been arguing with her for some time, he realized, after he had stopped believing what he was saying, because he had started saying it and did not like to stop.



He told them that afternoon, at the still.

The still crew were five men and the worm and the pot and the long bench where the jars stood, in the corner of Bram's forge yard where the still had stood since its first autumn, and the four women from the drying floor came over from the soap yard because Abloh sent them, and stood together at the end of the bench with their arms folded, the way women stand who have been told to go somewhere and not why. Imani stood by the yard gate. She did not come any nearer and she did not leave.

He told it plainly. That there was a difference in the records, Imani's and Ferra's. That the nine of them lost fewer days than anybody else in the barony, and healed faster, and had since October. That nobody knew why. That it might be the stones, because the stones were the only thing they had that nobody else did, and it might be something else that nobody had thought of yet.

"Is it bad?" one of the women said.

"I don't know."

"You don't know if it's bad or good?"

"I don't know if it's anything. If it's the stones, I don't know what they're doing, or what it means in a year, or in ten." He made himself say the next part, because it was true, and because the easy thing would have been to leave it out. "A thing that makes a cut close faster isn't necessarily a thing you want in you for twenty years. Or it may be. I don't know."

The men at the still looked at each other. One of them looked at his hands.

"Anybody who wants to can go to other work from tomorrow," Emeric said. "Same wage. The barony will find it. Your place here kept, if you want to come back. Nobody will ask you why, and nobody will remember that you went."

He did not say that they were free to stay. He thought about saying it, and did not, because it would have been his thumb on the scale, and the scale was theirs.

Two went.

One of the still crew, a man with three children under seven, asked whether the drains wanted hands, and was told they did, and was on the drainage crew at the bottom of the lower ward by the end of the week and seemed, when Emeric saw him there, reasonably content. One of the women from the drying floor put her tray down on the end of the bench while Emeric was still speaking, and looked at nobody, and walked out of the forge yard, and did not come back. Abloh found her other work at the soap kettles. She never said why, and nobody asked her, because they had been told not to.

The rest stayed. Most of them said it was the wage, when he asked, and he believed them, and understood, walking back up the hill, that it had never really been a question with two ends to it. A lord standing in a yard asking whether you would like to be poorer is not asking anything.

The last of them to answer was a still-crew man named Mott. He was in his thirties, from a hamlet on the lower water that had emptied in the plague, and he had had four jobs since, none of them for long, and his hands were stained grey to the second knuckle from the stone dust that came off the trays. He had not said a word all afternoon. When Emeric got to him he looked at his own hands for a while, front and back, as if they belonged to somebody else and he were being asked to give an opinion on them.

"Money, mostly," he said. "I won't say it isn't."

"Mostly."

"If there's an answer," Mott said, "it's about me. Isn't it?" He looked up. "I'd rather be standing where it happens than hear it secondhand down the lower water."

He went back to the worm. Emeric stood in the yard a while longer, and then went to find Bram, who had heard every word of it from the forge door and wanted to know whether he ought to be washing his own hands more.



She was on the step of the ward at dusk, sitting, which he had never seen her do, with her elbows on her knees and her hair coming down at the back from the day.

He stood in the street below the step. She did not tell him to go away. She did not tell him anything.

"You were right," he said.

"I know."

"I'd have waited until I was sure. And I'd never have been sure, and I'd have gone on waiting, and called it care." He looked at the step, not at her. "Two went. The rest stayed. One of them said he'd rather be standing where it happens."

"Mott."

"You know him?"

"I've had his hands in my basin four times." She looked at the street. "He's never once asked me what I was putting on them."

He should have gone then. He had said what he came to say. He found that he had not, quite, and that the rest of it was in his mouth before he had decided whether to let it out.

"I've thought about it before," he said. "Not your column. The thing under it. Since the first autumn."

Imani turned her head.

He told her. The boy at the front of the long room, and the half a lie. Armand's old notebook, which he had read on the road south and every winter since. The made mages: a handful of old knights in all the tower's records who had lived beside stones for fifteen or twenty years until something in them woke that had not been awake before. Nobody knew how. Nobody had ever tried to find out. Nobody had ever had nine people with their hands on charged stone all day for eight months and a healer who wrote down every cut.

"It may be nothing to do with it," he said. "It's fifteen years, in the notebook. This is eight months. It's a broken leg's worth of difference, not a mage." He stopped. "But I thought of it the night you brought the register. I should have said so at the trestle. I didn't."

She did not say anything for a while. The street was going blue. Down the hill somebody was calling a child in, the same name over and over, patient and then less patient.

"That's one," Imani said.

He did not understand. Then he did.

A sheet half folded in the laundry yard, and the basin, and her father's chair, and her father's razor, and her voice saying that's mine, now you, and waiting, and getting nothing. Over a year ago. He had thought she had let it go. He had thought, if he was honest, that he had been let off.

"It's a small one," he said.

"It's one." She stood up and dusted her skirt, and looked at him from the step, which put her eyes a little above his. Her face did not say anything at all. "The kettle's on."

She went in. She left the door open.

In three years she had never once said it without a reason after it. A sheet to fold, a drain to empty, a lamp to hold. There was no reason after it. He stood in the street long enough to be sure of that, and then he went up the step and in.
