Chapter 88 - Held Breath

Ward uncoiled the rope himself.

He did it without a word, on the grass by the chimney in the first light, paying the new hemp out through his hands a fathom at a time and looking at every fathom as it went, the way he looked at a harness strap before a ride. Then he tied one end round Emeric's chest under the arms, in a knot Emeric did not know, and tied himself on six yards further up the same line, in the same knot, and pulled on both until they bit.

"Terms," Ward said.

Emeric waited.

"You say it out loud. What the lamp's doing, what the other thing's doing, where you're going, what you've found. All of it. I don't have to understand it. I have to hear it." Ward checked his own knot again. "I do exactly what you say, when you say it. I don't argue. I don't think. And if you go quiet, I haul. Both of us. Whatever you're in the middle of. I don't wait to find out why."

"I can't talk under the line. I'll be holding my breath."

Ward looked at him.

"Then do the other thing," he said. "The one you did to her. At the table, over the lathe, before she wrote it down."

Emeric did not say anything for a moment.

He had not known Ward had seen it. It had been a pause at a table, a hair's width of the reach held short, a sentence shaped behind his teeth and heard in one head, with Vantic at the end of the table. He had been sure nobody else in the room had noticed. He had been sure of it for a year and a half.

"You saw that."

"I watch your face," Ward said. "Your throat moved. Hers answered." He finished the knot. "You've got a stone on me. Do that."

It would work. Ward carried his stone against the skin, as he had since the morning in the yard, and the crack in the reach ran at the speed of the room, a short exchange in a pause, and down there a pause was all there would be. It cost attention. It cost the kind of attention a man with his breath held and his hands in the dark did not have much of. It would also mean that for the whole of every dive there would be somebody else in his head, six yards back, above the line, listening.

He found that he wanted that more than he wanted any argument against it, and he did not examine why.

"All right," he said.

It was not tactical. He knew that, standing on the grass with the rope round his chest and the sun coming up over the spoil. There were tactical reasons, and they were real, and they were not the reason. The reason was that he did not want to be alone down there, in the black, below the line, where the only sound would be his own blood. He did not say it. Ward did not ask. Ward, who had not said yes last night and did not say yes now, picked up the coil and went to the ladder.

They went down the old shaft on the carpenters' ladders to the foot, where the good air went past their faces toward the chimney, and along to the gallery mouth. Hask's men had been told to stay at the foot of the Low Drift, at the far end of the crosscut, and had not. There were a dozen of them in the crosscut itself, as far in as the draft reached, lamps held low, faces orange in the dark, silent.

At the gallery mouth Emeric lowered the lamp on its stick and watched the flame sag a pace in, and die at three.

Ward was looking at the dead flame.

"There's a stone for this," he said. "There's a stone for everything else."

It was not an argument. Ward did not make arguments. It was a man putting a thing down where it could be heard, the way he had put the terms, and behind them in the crosscut a dozen lamps had gone still to listen.

"There's a stone that moves air," Emeric said. "Moves. That's the whole of the word. It pushes what's already there from one place to another, and it doesn't make one breath of anything. Set it up in the gallery and all it does is stir what's down there, and stirred dead air is dead air."

"Set it up here, then. Where the good air is. Push this down."

"That's the one worth asking." He nodded at the black slope. "It's a hole with one mouth. Blow into it and the air goes in as far as the push carries and then comes straight back out past your ear, because that's still the easiest road out. You'd get a great deal of noise in the first ten paces and nothing at all at forty. You can't rinse a dead end by shouting into it."

"Carry it in with you, then."

"It blows what's round it. What's round it in there is the thing that kills me. I'd go down that slope with a stone fanning dead air into my mouth at a good steady rate, and I'd die on time and tidy, and it would still be running when you hauled me up."

Ward took that, and then did not take all of it. "The water plant takes the bad out of the water," he said. "You built that."

"I did." It was two pages behind the second column in the notebook and had been for two years: a stone tuned to scrape the good part of the air out of a foul mixture, the same trick as the water, one element over. He had thought about it on the road down and again at the chimney and put it back both times. "It would take a season to get wrong the first four times. Then it would want trying somewhere a man can walk away from." He looked at the gallery mouth. "This is not somewhere a man can walk away from. Two of them tried."

"And every stone I have ever set stops," he said. "They run down. The ambient goes thin. A rune scrapes and loses a hair of its shape, and the whole of it goes quiet and polite and sits there looking exactly the way it looked when it worked. It doesn't tell you. That is also how the air down there kills people, and I am not going seventy paces under a hill with two different things on me that both fail without saying anything."

Ward looked at the rope in his hands.

"This one tells me," he said.

"That's why it's the rope."

"The line's two paces in," he said aloud. "The floor drops. The gallery goes down about one in six, the survey says. I'll go in to the line, breathe, and go under. I'm going to walk in until I've counted forty, and turn, and walk back. Count for me."

"Counting."

He walked in the two paces. The air at his face went from moving to still. He reached down with the other sense, the faint one, and felt the line lying across the gallery just ahead of him and below, a thickening in the medium like the top of cold water, level, patient.

That was where he made the mistake.

He knew he was going to need all the breath there was, and he did what he half remembered divers doing, from somewhere, from nowhere, from a place he could not have named. He breathed hard at the line. Fast and deep, again and again, a dozen times, until his fingers tingled and his head went light and bright and he felt as though he could hold his breath for an hour. Then he held it, and went under.

It was black. It was blacker than anything he had ever stood in. The lamp was behind him, dead, and the dead air lay on his face like a cloth, and he could feel it on his skin, cool and still and weighing nothing. He put one hand on the gallery wall and walked.

Going down, he said, behind his teeth, into the reach held short, and felt it arrive six yards back. Wall on the left, timbered. Floor steady. Rock ahead, I can feel it. Ten paces.

Ward's voice came back, not through the air, through the other way. Ten.

He walked. He had counted forty before he went in and he counted now, and the sense gave him the gallery ahead in its faint dark way, the walls and the rotten props leaning in, the floor falling away. Twenty. It was easy. That was what he noticed, and it pleased him. He was not uncomfortable at all. He had breathed so well at the line that there was no pull in his chest to breathe again, no ache, nothing, and he thought he might go on past forty, to fifty, and see further.

Thirty, he said, and heard it come out slowly.

Thirty, said Ward.

He went on. The gallery went on. He was saying something to Ward about the props, and then he was not quite sure what he had been saying, and then he noticed that the edges of the black had gone grey, which did not make sense, because it was black, and he thought about that for what seemed a long and interesting time.

Somewhere, very far off, somebody was saying his name.

Then the rope bit into his chest like a hand and he was going backward up the slope, his heels dragging, and he did not remember turning round. The air changed on his face. It was moving. Somebody's arm was across him, hard, and somebody was saying breathe in a voice that was not through the other way at all but out loud, in the gallery, a great flat shout that echoed, and he breathed, and the grey went out of the edges of things, and it was Ward.

He sat against the wall two paces above the line with his head between his knees and breathed for some time.

"You went quiet," Ward said. "Then you said a word I don't know. Then you went quiet."

"How long?"

"Sixty-one."

He had thought it was perhaps thirty-five. He sat and looked at that for a while.

He knew exactly what he had done. He knew it the way he knew the thing about glass and wood fires, completely and from nowhere: that the need to breathe comes from the body's own foul air building up in the blood, not from the good air running out, and that a man who breathes hard before he goes under blows the foul air out of himself, and so feels no need to breathe at all, and goes on feeling none, while the good air runs down to nothing, and he falls asleep in the water without ever once wanting to come up. It was what had nearly happened. It was what happened to the two men who had gone in against Renner's order, in a different way, with the same ending: no warning, no pain, the edges of things going grey, and then nothing.

He did not say any of it to Ward. He could not have said where he had learned it, and a man who could not say where he had learned a thing he had just nearly died of was a man who had better not start explaining it in a mine to his bodyguard.

"I did it wrong," he said. "I won't do it that way again."

"What did you do?"

"Breathed wrong at the line."

Ward looked at him for a long time in the lamplight, and did not ask anything else.

From the second dive on he took three slow full breaths at the line, the way a man breathes before he lifts something heavy, and no more, and went under, and his chest began to ache at about twenty and hurt at about thirty-five, and he turned round at forty every time, and came back up the rope into the moving air with his lungs burning and his head clear. It was much worse. It was the right way. He had not needed anybody to tell him which one would kill him.

The second dive gave him the gallery.

He went in the forty paces with the sense out ahead of him as far as it would reach, and talked the whole way, and Ward counted, and the dozen men in the crosscut behind them heard nothing but the count. Props on the left. Props on the right, fallen, step over. Floor wet here, not water, just damp. Something on the floor. Iron, a pick head. Leave it. He felt the gallery go on past the reach of forty paces, and felt, at about the limit of what the sense would give him, the right-hand wall fall away into an opening.

Fork, he said. Thirty-five, forty paces. Branch to the right. Going back.

Forty, said Ward, and hauled a little on the rope, not to pull him, just to let him know which way was up.

He sat above the line after the second dive and breathed and drew it on the gallery floor with his finger in the grit, by lamplight, for Hask, who had come in along the crosscut to the gallery mouth against orders and was crouching there with his cap in his hands. The gallery going down. The fork at forty paces, on the right. Hask looked at it and nodded, and looked at the prince breathing, and did not say anything.

On the third dive he went past the fork.

He turned at the fork the first time, as he had said he would, and went back and breathed and went in again, and the third time he did not stop at forty, because at forty he could feel something ahead of him that he had been feeling faintly since the first dive and had not let himself look at.

It was not like the dead air. The dead air was a thickening in the medium, a weight, a level. This was the opposite. It was a place where the medium ran hot and dense and strange, the way it had run round the four carriage stones on the bank below the foundry while Armand held the boat, only there it had been four stones and here it was not stones. Ahead of him, below him, in the foot of the gallery and past it, the sense found something that was not a seam, not a vein, not a line of stone running through other stone, but a body. It went down. He reached for the bottom of it and did not find one. It went on down past the reach of the sense, past everything, into the foot of the hill, and he could feel it the way a man in a boat on a still night can feel that the water under him is deep. Beyond it, at the very edge of what the sense would give him, the gallery stopped in a blind face of rock, perhaps five and twenty paces on.

Found, he said, behind his teeth. Found it. Forty-six. Going back.

Forty-six, said Ward, and then, a moment later, still through the other way and nothing like his usual voice: Come back.

He had his hand on the gallery floor. There was something under it, loose, the size of his fist, heavy for its size, lying in the grit where a pick had knocked it off the face before the closing and nobody had come back for it. He closed his hand on it. Then he turned round, and it was the hardest thing he had done in either of his lives, because it was right there and his hand was on it and there was a whole body of it an arm's length further down, and his chest was on fire and every part of him that had spent ten years wanting to know what was at the bottom of things wanted to go one more pace.

He turned round, and walked up the slope with the rope pulling him, and came up into the moving air with the thing in his fist, and sat against the wall and breathed.

Ward sat down beside him. He did it slowly and heavily, like Armand on the bank, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the dozen men in the crosscut behind them did not say anything either.

"That's a long breath," Ward said at last. It was flat, a fact about the weather. "Forty-six paces down a hill in the dark and forty-six back. You never learned that in the yard."

"No."

"And not in the room, either. Nothing in the room puts anything on a man." Ward looked at the gallery mouth. "So you've just got it."

"I suppose so."

Ward nodded as though that settled it, and was wrong, and Emeric did not correct him. It was not the breath. Any man could hold his breath for forty-six paces who was frightened enough, and most of the men in that crosscut could have done it. What they could not have done was the other part. It was the turning round. It was standing in the dark with your hand on the thing you had come for and the whole of the rest of it a yard further down, and your chest on fire and everything in you saying one more pace, and walking back up the slope anyway because you had said forty and meant it. He had nearly not done it. He was not going to say so to Ward, who had been six yards back in his head for all forty-six of them and had said come back.

He opened his hand in the lamplight.

It was black, and heavy, and flecked all through with points of light that caught the lamp and held it, the way the stones under the carriage had caught the dawn. It was the size of his fist and felt, in his palm, not quite as heavy as it should have, as though some small part of its weight had already decided to be somewhere else.

The men in the crosscut had come up to the gallery mouth to see. Nobody reached for it.

He did not start designing anything. He had half expected to, and was relieved to find that he did not, sitting there with the thing in his hand and the whole of the notebook's second column in his head. The deposit was a stock, not a flow. Every ounce of it that was ever used was an ounce gone, and there would never be any more. And none of it, not an ounce, not this piece in his hand, was his to use for anything until the Crown had been told it existed and had been paid its share. That was the next thing and it was not a thing for a gallery.

What he came up the ladders with was the stone, and a list, and the list was longer than the stone was heavy. The largest thing on it was simple, and he looked at it squarely on the ladder with the light coming down from the chimney hatch above him. He now held the largest body of the rarest stone in the kingdom, in a barony whose last baron had brought his men up Kaldo's road and died there with most of them, on a hill held by a widow on a charter he could revoke, in a south where every man with reach already had a reason to want him gone.

He left the fix behind him the way he left everything he wanted to outlast him, which was in daylight, in front of people.

He drew it outside the tally house in the afternoon, on the bare ground by the door, with forty men and Hask and Ward standing round. The gallery, going down, with its fork at forty paces on the right. A wall of canvas soaked in oil and tar, stretched on light props down the middle of the gallery from the mouth to the far end, with a second, shorter wall across the mouth of the fork to close it off. And across the gallery mouth at the top, a door, a canvas door on a frame that swung shut on its own weight, so that the draft coming down the shaft could not go past the gallery any more. It had to go in. It had to go down one side of the long wall to the very end of the gallery and round the end of the canvas and back up the other side, and out, and it took the dead air with it every step of the way.

"Air goes where it's easiest," he said. "That's all it ever does. Same as water. Same as the fire. You don't make it go where you want. You make where you want the easiest place."

One of the young ones at the back asked it, half to the man beside him, in the voice of somebody who does not expect to be heard.

"Could he not just do it, though. With the stones."

Forty men went quiet at once, which is louder than anything else forty men do. Hask began to turn round with his face already set for it.

"I could do some of it," Emeric said.

He let that sit, because it was true and because not one of them had expected it. "With that wall up and that door hung, a stone at the mouth would push harder than the fire pulls. It would clear the gallery faster than the fire will. I could set one tomorrow."

"Then why the canvas," said Hask, flat, giving him the room.

"Because a stone has to be fed." He crouched by the drawing. "Somebody has to know when it's running down. Somebody has to know what he's looking at when it is. And somebody has to care enough, on a wet morning in the fourth year, to walk up the east path and put his hand on it. That somebody has to still be here, and still be able, and still want to." He put a finger on the long line in the dust. "This is a wall and a door. It doesn't know anything. It doesn't want anything. It doesn't stop when I stop. A man who can't read can hang it. A man who hates me can hang it, and it will work for him exactly the same as it works for anybody, and it will still be pulling when every stone I ever set has gone dead and the lot of us are under the grass."

The young one at the back had gone red to the ears. Emeric found him in the ring and held him a moment.

"It was the right question," he said. "Ask it about everything I build."

He had been building that way for years and had never once said it out loud, and standing in the dust at Grayrock he heard what it sounded like from the outside and did not much care for it. Anything of his that only worked while he was alive was not a thing he had given anybody. It was a thing he had lent them, at interest, against a day he would not be there to be asked about.

He handed the stick to Hask, and scuffed the drawing out.

Hask drew it back. He did it without being asked, and without looking up at anybody, crouching on his bad knee in the dust with forty of his own men watching: the gallery, the fork, the long wall, the short wall, the door, and the arrows, down one side and round and up the other. When he had finished he looked at it for a while, and then added a second door, further down, at the fork, which Emeric had not drawn and which was a better idea, and stood up.

Nobody had expected him to come out.

He understood that only when he came up the last ladder into the afternoon light at the sealed head and found the crowd. They had been gathering since the morning, Ward told him later. Men off the shifts, their wives, children, the old men who sat on the tally house wall, the cook from the hall. They had not come close. They had stood in a loose ring on the spoil bank and on the east path and on the slope above the chimney, well back, a hundred yards and more, the distance a person keeps from a thing that has killed people, and they had waited to see him not come back, because that was the only ending the east working had ever given anybody.

When he came up the ladder into the light with a stone in his hand, the distance closed.

It did not close all at once. A few of the children came first, and then the men from the crosscut, who were already telling it, and then everybody, until he was standing at the chimney's foot in a crowd of people who had been told for a dozen years and more that the hole behind him was cursed, looking at him as though he were something that had come up out of it.

He knew what he had bought.

He had bought it without meaning to and it was worth more than anything in his fist. Every one of those men would tell it. They would tell it tonight in the hall and tomorrow in the tally house and next month in Milford, and it would go down the river road and up the Wayford road and along every road in the south, and it would get better every time. The prince went down into the cursed working and walked into the dead air and came out holding the curse. No decree could have bought that. No charter.

And it would reach, in time, the people who were already telling themselves the other story. That Emeric of Kaldo could not be met on any field, because the last three barons who had tried were dead or ruined. That he could not be caught by any law, because the last man who had tried that had been put to death in his own town square by the Crown. That he could not, now, even be killed by a hill. That he must therefore be dealt with by other means, which were the only means left.

Every time he did a thing well, he made himself more dangerous to the people who wanted him gone, and every time he made himself more dangerous, they wanted him gone more. He had built the whole barony to hold that off. He had no answer to it at all, and he stood in the crowd at the chimney's foot with the stone in his hand and looked for one, and there was not one there either.

That night, in the tally house, he wrote the shortest line he had sent up the relay since the work began.

Gallery seventy-one paces. One fork, right, at forty. Found. Came out.

Then he wrapped the stone in oilcloth, and put it in the leather bag that already held three bundles of letters tied with tape and a dead man's account book with a false heading, and buckled the bag, and sat with his hand on it.

Six years of patient letters. A man taught what to look for, from a long way off, by someone who knew what the colour of some spoil meant before anybody on this hill did. Paid to sit on it. Paid to let his men believe in a curse. And tonight, in a leather bag on a table in the tally house, the curse and the letters that had made it, in the same bag, under his hand.

The hand that had reached into these hills had reached from a very long way up. He had known that since the box. Tonight he had held what it had reached for, and it was heavy, and not quite as heavy as it should have been.
