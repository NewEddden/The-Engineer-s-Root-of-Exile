Chapter 267 - A Week

By dark it was in his shoulders.

It did not hurt yet. That was the strange part, afterward, when he tried to tell Imani the order of it for her book: that for the first six hours nothing hurt at all, and he lay on Bram's boards under the tree with the blanket to his chin and his cuffed hands on his chest and felt it come up his arms like warm water rising in a bath, and was interested. He had been afraid of it for a year. He had read Vey's figures lying under their tree a hundred times and pictured every kind of pain a body could have, and here it was, and it was warm, and it went up his arms and into his shoulders and across the top of his chest and up the back of his neck, and he thought, quite clearly, well, that's not so bad.

It was a pull. That was the nearest he could come to it. Something in him was being drawn wider, the way a pipe is drawn on Bram's bench, a little at a time, through a die; and as it was drawn, it drew. The dome's air came into him. He did not breathe it in. He felt it come in through his skin, thick and slow, into the places that were being widened, and fill them, and the places went on widening, and it came in again.

The cuffs drank at his wrists and ankles. He could feel them filling. Whatever was being pushed out of him by the widening went into the flat stones against his skin and stopped there, and the stones grew heavy and cold, and he was glad of them.

At some point the light in the canopy went down for the night, as it did, to a faint violet like the last of a sunset under a lid; and at some point after that he could not remember lying down.

At some point after that, it started to hurt.



Bram counted the forty-first cartridge at a quarter to midnight, and the forty-second at midnight, and at three minutes past midnight every leaf on the tree shut.

He was looking at it when it happened. He had been looking at it on and off for eight hours, between cartridges, sitting on the upturned crate by the collar with the rack at his elbow and his coat buttoned to his chin and a scrubber mask round his neck in case. He had looked at it the way he looked at a furnace when he did not trust the man feeding it: often, and without looking as though he was looking. And the tree had stood there in the dome under its two tiers of violet leaves with the faint blue light going along under the branches, the way it had stood every night for a year, and the boards by its trunk with the shape on them under the blanket, which had moved a good deal since ten and was not moving now; and then, between one breath and the next, every leaf on both tiers folded up along its middle and turned its edge to the sky.

All of them. At once. It made a sound. Bram heard it through the glass, very faint, like a crowd of people all closing their books together in a hall.

He stood up.

Under the tiers the blue light along the branches had gone out. The tree stood in the dark dome like a closed umbrella.

"Armand," said Bram.

The old man was in his chair under the screen with the rug up to his eyes and his head on one side. He did not stir. Bram went and put his hand on his shoulder and Armand woke the way old men wake, all at once and looking about him as if somebody had moved the furniture.

"The leaves," said Bram.

Armand looked. He looked for a long time, through the gap in the screens, at the folded tree; and then he looked at the boards; and then he put both hands on the arms of his chair and got up out of it, which he had not done without help since February, and went to the glass and put his face nearly against it.

"He's taking it," he said.

"Taking what?"

"The room. Its air. He's pulling it into himself faster than your collar's putting it back." Armand did not turn round. "The room's gone under the tree's line, and he's taking it lower. It's shutting itself up to keep what it's got. A plant does that in a drought." He put one hand flat on the glass. "Double it."

"The feed?"

"Double it. One at the quarter and one at the three quarters, and the one on the hour. Keep the room up. If the room goes down under him he'll take the tree's next, and I don't know what that does, and nor does anybody living."

Bram looked at the rack. There were nineteen full cartridges in it, and Ivo's boy had taken the empties up to the rig at nine and come back with a note that said there would be another twenty-four by six if nobody needed him for anything else in the night.

"Go and wake Ivo," said Bram to the screen, where Imani's nurse Bettany was sitting on a stool at the pass-box with her mask on and the ward's night sheet on her knee. "Tell him I want the rig running till it's light. Tell him all of it. Tell him I said please."

Bettany went.

Bram took the forty-third cartridge out of the rack. He screwed it into the collar's mouth, and turned the valve, and felt the rush go through the iron into the room; and he looked at Ottoline's watch on its nail on the screen's post, and said, out loud, to the dark ward, to Armand at the glass and the folded tree and the shape on the boards, "Forty-three. Quarter past."

At the half hour he said nothing, because there was nothing on the half hour. At a quarter to one he said "Forty-four." On the hour, "Forty-five."

He counted all night. Armand stood at the glass until Bram made him sit, and then sat in his chair with his eyes open, and when Bram said a number Armand said it after him, under his breath. At five the leaves on the lower tier opened a crack, a finger's width, all together, and stayed that way. At first light Bram's voice had gone to a croak and he went on with it.

The shape on the boards did not move again until morning. When it did, it was the hands. They came up off the chest, both of them, cuffed, and closed on the edges of the boards on either side of the body and held on there, the knuckles standing up white through the dark dome, the way a man holds the sides of a boat in a sea.

They did not let go again all that day.



On the third day Imani looked inside him.

She did it on the Monday afternoon, on her two o'clock hour, sitting on her stool in the open pass-box with the inner door folded back against the glass and the outer one shut behind her, and her mask on, and the dome's air on her face round its edges like the air off an open oven door. She had decided to do it at six that morning, at the end of her night hour, and then sat on the decision for eight hours to see whether it would still be there; and it was. So at two she sent Bettany to her dinner and shut the outer door and sat down and folded her hands on her knee.

He had taught her how in March, in the delve.

That had been her note, not his. She had sent it to him across the ward in February, on a scrap of the ward's sheet folded in four and given to Finn: You looked into the mason. Show me. And in March, in the slow time under the long table, he had. It took four nights. You did not look with the eyes. You let your sense go out of you thin, thinner than you ever let it for anything else, so thin that a living body's own mana would not push it back; and then you let it go in. It went where a body's mana was thickest: the blood, the nerves, the soft wet places. It went more faintly through bone, the way it went faintly through wood. What you got back was not a picture. It was a feeling of shapes, warm and cool, quick and slow, that you had to learn to read, as you had once had to learn to read the black marks in a book.

Her sense had opened in the winter. She had told him in January, at the table, as if it were a cold she had caught: that since the first snow she had been able to feel where people were in the ward with her eyes shut. Which bed. Which way they were lying. Whether they were asleep. She had not liked it. She had liked it less when he told her it would not go away.

She had never used it on purpose on a living body. She had decided, in March, under the table in the dark, that the first body would be his. He had said that was a bad idea; that the first body ought to be someone ordinary, someone thin to the sense, someone whose inside would not be roaring. She had said that was why.

She shut her eyes.

The dome came at her first. She had known it would; she had been sitting at its door for two days with her sense opened a crack, and it had sat on her the whole time like the weight of a hot day. With it opened wide the weight became a shape: the whole room, round and close, thick from the ring to the top hub; and the tree in the middle of it, a column of something so dense and so slow that her sense slid off its edges like a hand off ice. And at the tree's foot, a long shape lying on its back on the boards.

She let herself go thin, and went into him.

It was loud. He had warned her. It was so loud she nearly came straight out again, the way you step back from a door into a room where a hundred people are shouting. All of him was moving. Every channel she had been taught in March was there, the long ones down the arms and legs and the short ones in the hands and the fine ones round the eyes, and every one of them was full and running, the mana in them rushing outward toward the wrists and ankles where the cuffs drank it, and back, and outward again. That was not new. She had felt his mana from across a room for a year; it was always moving. What was new was the channels. She knew the size of a channel in a man's arm. She had felt Bram's and Ward's and her own in the delve, and a hundred in the ward with her hand on a wrist, without knowing that was what she was feeling. His were wrong. They were wider than any she had ever felt, and as she watched, if watching was the word, they were getting wider: the walls of them going slowly outward under the rush, the way a river's banks go in a flood. A little at a time. In every channel she could reach. All at once.

And it hurt him. She could feel that too. It was in the walls, where the widening was. Not a sharp hurt. A wide one, everywhere, a burn under the skin from the crown of his head to his heels.

She went looking for the stone.

It was where it ought to have been, in the stomach, low on the left. She knew its shape at once, because it was the only quiet thing in him: a long small egg of silence in all that noise. But it was not the size it had been. It was smaller. She went close to it, very thin, and felt its skin; and its skin was going. It was going the way a lump of sugar goes in hot tea, from the outside in, slowly, the edges softening into the stomach's wet; and out of it, as it went, came threads. Fine as hair. Dozens of them, running out from the stone into the mana round it, and along the mana, outward, into every channel she could follow; so that the stone was not sitting in him, as a swallowed pebble sits. It was in all of him. It was unrolling itself through him like a map being opened.

She followed one of them out. It went down the left arm with the rush, fine as a hair, to a place above the wrist where a channel's wall had been pushed outward faster than any wall could go, and had split. She knew that split. She had felt what it left a hundred times, with her hand on a knight's forearm in the ward: the hard pale seam the rite laid down, in a hurry, crooked. This one was not laid down. It was open, and the room was pouring through it into the flesh round the channel and cutting as it went; and the thread from the stone was already in it. It ran along the tear the way a needle runs along a hem, and behind it the wall was whole again, and wider, and smooth. No seam.

She found another, higher up the same arm. Then a dozen. Then she stopped counting, because they were everywhere she went, in every channel, opening and closing faster than she could follow one to its end: torn, and mended, and torn again a hand's width on.

She stayed there a long time.

Then she did the thing she had told herself she would not do, and went to his knee.

Rowena had looked at that knee on the Rowena trip and told him it clicked on stairs and would be worse by forty; and Imani had heard it click on stairs every day since, and said nothing, because a click in a knee was not anything a healer could mend. It was there in the hollow view, faint, the joint a ring of cooler shapes round the warm rush of the blood and the nerves. She could feel the old wear on the cartilage's face. She could feel the threads from the stone, here as everywhere, running fine through the warm places round the joint. And she could feel, very faint, so faint she was not sure afterward and wrote that she was not sure, that the warm places round the wear were busier than they ought to have been. As though something had noticed it.

She came out.

She came out of him slowly, the way he had taught her, and out of the dome's weight, and opened her eyes, and the pass-box was there with its iron walls and its little window and the tree beyond, folded half shut, and his hands on the board's edges, white. Above the cuff on the nearer forearm she could see them now with her own eyes, because she knew where to look: fine silver lines coming up under the skin like frost coming up a pane, and dulling, and going, and others coming up where they had been. Her own hands were cold. She had been in for forty minutes by the watch on the pass-box's wall and it had felt like five.

She took off her mask, because there was nobody to see, and wiped her face with her sleeve. Then she put the mask back on, and took her pencil and the ward's book off the shelf, and wrote it down: in the hospital's hand, which was a plain upright hand anybody could read, with every word spelled out and nothing in shorthand, because the hospital's book was for whoever came after her, and whoever came after her would not know what she had meant by a mark. Channels, all, widening, and how she knew. Pain in the walls, everywhere, constant. The stone in the stomach, low left, smaller than swallowed, the edges dissolving; threads from it in every channel followed. The walls tearing, everywhere, as at the rite; every tear followed closed again behind by a thread, and no seam left. Silver on the skin of both forearms, coming and going. A line that stays means the mending has stopped. If one stays an hour, he comes out. The knee. Not sure. Writing it so that if it is so, there is a record of when it was first seen, and by whom, and that she was not sure.

She signed it. She dated it. Under the date she wrote the hour, and the length of the look, forty minutes; and under that, after a while, in the same plain hand, that she had not been able to tell from inside whether he knew she was there.



He knew.

He did not know it was her, at first. There was nothing in him on the third day that could have known a person; there was the burn, which was everywhere and did not stop, and the dark, and the boards under his hands, which were the only thing he was sure of, and Bram's voice at the edge of the world saying numbers. But in the middle of the afternoon something came into the burn that was cool and thin and careful, and moved through him slowly, like a hand feeling for something in a drawer in the dark, and stayed a long time near the place in his stomach where all the pain began, and then, oddly, went down to his knee.

He thought: oh. It's her.

He did not think anything else for a day and a half.



On the fifth morning the cuffs were full, and a pane frosted.

Nobody saw it start. Ward saw it first, from the pass-box, at half past four in the morning, on his own shift at the door, which Imani had given him on the second night because he had stood outside the screens all through the first one and she said that if he was going to stand there anyway he could stand there usefully. He was looking at the cuffs. He had been looking at the cuffs on and off for an hour, because the one on the left wrist had gone the dull grey of a slate roof in rain, the colour a stone went when it had all it could hold, and Ward had held enough full stones in his life to know the look.

Then he looked past the boards at the glass, because something had moved there, and it was the frost.

It was coming up the inside of a pane on the far side of the trunk, the one nearest the boards, low, at the height of the mattress. White. It grew the way frost grows on a window on a January morning, in feathers and ferns, from one point outward, but fast: as fast as ink goes through blotting paper. In the middle of it, where it had started, there was a shape. A ring, half closed. A stroke inside it, and the start of another.

It was the beginning of a glyph. It had come out of the man on the boards because the stone at his left wrist could not take any more of what was pushing out of him, and it had gone where his mana went first when he was not watching it, which was cold. It had not had a hand to point it, or a will to finish it. It had got as far as the inside of the nearest pane and stopped there, half made, and was spending itself on the glass.

Ward did not shout. He said "Bram," at a reasonable level, and Bram came off his crate at the collar as if he had been fired from it.

The frost covered the pane to its edges. It went no further; it did not cross the iron strip into the next triangle. It lay on the inside of the glass, white and thick and fine, with the half-made ring in the middle of it, and the glass behind it was so cold that Bram, who had his hand flat on the outside of the same pane in the time it took Ward to say his name, took his hand away again with a hiss and looked at his palm.

Then the frost stopped growing.

They stood there, Ward in the pass-box and Bram outside the glass, and listened to the pane.

Ivo's glass had been poured on the levels of the big room and annealed for three days. Ivo had said, when the dome went up, that there was not a pane in it that would crack under anything short of a man's hammer. Ivo had also said that he had never had a pane of it so cold on one face and so warm on the other, and that he did not know, and that nobody had ever asked him.

The pane made a sound. It was very small. It was the sound a lake makes in the deep of winter at night, when the ice settles.

It did not crack.

"Cuffs," said Ward. "Now."

The second set was on the shelf in the pass-box, empty, cold, the colour of a dry slate. He took them down and put his mask up and went in.

He changed them one at a time, so that there was never a moment the man on the boards had less than three on him. The left wrist first, the full one: he opened its band and lifted it away, grey and heavy as a cobblestone and so cold that his fingers ached through his gloves; and in the half breath before the new one closed, he felt what came off that wrist. It was like standing in the door of a forge with the fire raked. Then the new band closed and the stone in it began to drink, and the push went into it like water into a dry field, and was gone.

The skin of the forearm above it was wet with sweat, and there were lines on it. Silver, fine, branching up toward the elbow, the same pattern Ward had carried on his own arms for most of his life; coming up under the skin as he watched, and going. He had never seen tracery go. He looked at it a breath longer than he had to.

Emeric did not wake. His hands stayed where they had been for four days, closed on the board's edges. Ward had to work the new cuff on round the wrist without moving the hand, and did. He did the right wrist. He did the ankles. He was a long time about the ankles, because the skin under the old bands was raw where Imani had said it would be, and he wrapped each one first in the lint from his pocket that Imani had given him at the door for that purpose and told him to use, and that he had pretended not to want.

He was inside for six minutes. Imani had given him five.

When he came out through the pass-box he had the four full cuffs in his arms and the frost had already started to go off the pane, from its edges inward, in long wet runs; and Imani was there at the outer door in her ward coat over her nightgown, again, and her feet bare on the iron, again, with her watch in her hand.

"Six," she said.

"The ankles."

"I know it was the ankles." She took his wrist, there in the door, and counted his pulse, which nobody had asked her to; and Ward let her, which nobody had ever seen him do. When she had done she let go. "Sit down. You've been in a charged room six minutes, and you're not a tree."

He sat down on Bram's crate, which was the nearest thing.

The pane was nearly clear. In the middle of it, where the half-made ring had been, the last of the frost held a moment longer than the rest, so that for the length of a breath there was a pale ring standing on the glass, with its stroke and its half a stroke, like a word written on a window with a wet finger; and then that went too.

Bram stood looking at the place where it had been.

"I'll pour a set for every pane by the boards," he said at last. "Inside shutters. Lead-lined. On hinges."

"After," said Ward.

"After," said Bram. "But I'll pour them."

He did. The shutters stayed on the glass room's inside face until the great dome replaced it, years on; and anybody who had been in the ward that morning, which was four people, could have shown you which pane.



The terrace went up on the Thursday, in a day, while Emeric lay in the dome and did not know it.

Nobody had planned for that. That was the thing Tess kept coming back to afterward, sitting in the shed at the head of the rails at sundown with her hand on the tracer's stylus and the shell standing out there in the last light: that it had not been in anybody's plan. The plan had been Hob's kitchen on the Monday, which they had done, and then a fortnight of test walls and courses and corners, every kind of joint on the drawings, to find out what the long printer did badly before it did anything that mattered; and then, in May, when Emeric was up and about and could stand at the rail and frown at it, the first house. Bram had said so. Bram had said so on the Monday afternoon at the gate with Hob's kitchen standing behind him, in front of everybody, and gone up the lane at a quarter past four to the castle to sit with a rack of cartridges all night.

He had not come back.

He came back for an hour at noon on the Tuesday, grey in the face, and stood at the rail, and looked at the corner test they had done that morning without him, and said it was all right, and went up the lane again to sleep in the hall. On the Wednesday he did not come at all. Ferra sent a note at nine on the Wednesday morning that said, in her square capitals, BRAM IS AT THE DOME. THE WORKS ARE TESS'S. INITIALLED, F., and it was pinned to the shed door, and the crew read it, and so did Gale.

So on the Thursday morning, Tess printed the terrace.

She did not decide to. She thought about it on the Wednesday night, in her room over the works, with the drawings on the bed and her chair pulled up to it; and she thought about what Bram would say, and what Emeric would say, and what Ferra would write in her book, and decided she would not; and in the morning she came down to the rails at six and found Hob already there, on the cart track, in his Sunday coat, with a can of tea for the crew and his plan rolled under his arm, looking at the empty ground where his house was going to be. He had been looking at it since five, he said. He could not sleep for it. He had never in his life had a house with a stair in it, and he had thought he would like to come and look at where the stair would go.

Tess looked at Hob, and at the empty strip of rolled clay with the pegs in it, and at the long printer with its stones in and its blades down and the crew coming in at the gate with their caps in their hands; and she looked at the drawings in her lap, which were not Hob's plan, but the whole terrace, four houses, eight rooms down and eight up and four stairs, that Emeric had drawn in January and Bram had redrawn in February and she had redrawn again in March, at a twentieth of the size, for the tracer; and she thought, oh, for the Light's sake.

"Crew," she said. "Mixer on. All four plots. We're doing the shell."

Osric, who had come in at the gate behind the crew and was halfway through taking his coat off, stopped with one arm out of it.

"All four?"

"Walls to the first-floor line. Openings left for Gale's lintels. No roof, we've no roof drawings for the tracer yet." She turned the chair to the table. "If it goes wrong, it's my name on it, and I'll tell Bram myself. If it goes right he can be cross about it in a house."

It went right.

It did not go right all at once. It went right the way things in Bram's shop went right, which was by going wrong in small ways all day long and being caught. The mix stiffened in the hose at ten, because the day came up warmer than Monday's and nobody had thought of that; Osric worked out why and had the water cart's boy wet the hose's canvas every quarter of an hour with a can, and it stopped. The arm jammed on its rail at the second house's back corner, because the rail had a burr on it nobody had dressed; one of the pump men dressed it with a file in a quarter of an hour, with the crew sitting on the clay drinking Hob's tea, and they went on. Gale and two of his men set the first lintel over the first door of the first house at noon, while the walls were still going up round them, in the gap the nozzle had left for it, and the nozzle came round on its next course and went over the lintel and on, and the wall closed over it as if the stone had always been there; and Gale stood back and looked at that for a long time.

The stair walls came up. The parlour walls. The party walls between the four houses, double, with the gap between them that Emeric had drawn for sound and Bram had grumbled about. Tess sat in the shed with her hand on the stylus and ran it round the drawing, course after course, a twentieth of the size; round the kitchen and the parlour and up the stair's line and round the back room and out, and on to the next house, and back to the first for the next course, and the gantry went over the ground out there at full size with its wheels grinding on the rails, and did what her hand did.

At four o'clock she noticed her hand had not stopped moving for six hours and she could not open it.

Osric took the stylus. He did not ask. He put his hand over hers on it, and kept the gantry going with the lever with his other hand, and when she had got her fingers out from under his he put the stylus in the next groove of the drawing and ran it round the course, more slowly than she did, his tongue between his teeth; and she sat beside him in her chair and rubbed her hand and watched the drawing and said "left" and "slower" and "the stair, the stair, mind the stair," and he minded it.

At half past six the walls of four houses stood up out of the ground at the bottom of ward 3 to the first-floor line, with their doors and windows waiting, and Gale's lintels in, and the stair walls going up inside them with the steps still to come; and the crew stood along the rails in the evening light and looked at them, and nobody said anything, and then everybody did at once.

Hob walked into his house through the front door. He walked through the kitchen into the parlour and out of the parlour to the foot of where the stair would be, and stood there, and put his hand flat on the wall.

Ferra came down the lane at seven, with her book, because she came down to the rails at seven every evening that week, at the end of the day's sums, on her way to the castle to sit an hour at the dome before she went to bed. She stopped at the top of the lane.

Then she came down, and walked once along the four shells with her book under her arm, and came to the shed door.

"Bram said a fortnight of test walls," she said.

"Bram's at the dome," said Tess.

Ferra looked at her for a moment. Then she opened her book against the shed's door frame and wrote, and Tess, who could see the page from her chair, read it upside down as it went: Ward 3, terrace, four houses, walls to the first floor, one day. Tess, bay three, master. Osric. Then she closed the book.

"I'll read it to him tomorrow," said Ferra. "In the quarter's sums."

"He's not awake."

"No," said Ferra. "I'll read it to him anyway."



She read it to him on the Friday night, in the delve.

She had not done it before. None of them had; it was the kind of thing Emeric might have thought of, and might have done, and they had thought of it for him. There had been a meeting about it on the Wednesday, at noon, round her table: Armand, and Ward, who had slept two hours, and Imani in her ward coat with her book, and Ferra. Nobody could go into the dome for longer than a visit. A man in a fever as deep as his did not hear the voices at the pass-box; Imani had tested it, on the Tuesday, by reading the ward's sheet aloud at the inner door for half an hour, and his hands had not moved. But the delve was not a room in the ward. The delve was where they went when they went under at the long table, the six, in the slow time; it was a place that a delver went to from wherever his body happened to be; and a delver who was asleep went there, Armand said, whether he meant to or not, if somebody else was there to go to. He had seen it in the tower. A master who lay dying in the infirmary at the tower's foot had been met in the delve by his students every night for a month, and had known them there when he knew them nowhere else.

"How long can a man stay in?" Ferra had asked.

"In that air?" Armand had looked at the window. "In the dome he'd be there all the time if he could find it. He's soaking in the stuff. The delve's cheap from there." He had folded his hands. "It's from here that's dear. A night under at the table from here, for one of you, is a night. And you'll come up with a head on you."

"Then it's me," Ferra had said. "On the last night. Before Imani says it's over." She had looked round the table. "I've the quarter to do anyway. I may as well do it to somebody."

So on the Friday at ten she went down the stair to the old wine store under the hall, past the paper on the wall by the door, and sat down in her chair at the long table in the dark, alone, with her book in her lap, and put her hands flat on the wood, and went under.

The delve, the way she had learned to come into it, was a long room with no walls that she could ever see, and a table, and chairs. She did not know what it looked like to the others. She had asked, the first year, and every one of them had described something different, and she had stopped asking. For Ferra it was the counting room at the old hall, the year she was fifteen, before Emeric came: the long ledger table under the window with its green baize and its ink stains and its brass rule, and the window full of a grey light that never changed. She had never told anybody that. She had never told anybody that she had chosen it, and could have chosen anything, and had chosen a room where she had been cold and happy and good at something and nobody had known.

He was at the table.

She had not expected that. She had expected to have to look. He was sitting at the far end of the long baize table in his shirt, with his elbows on it and his head down in his hands, and he was not in the dome; or the dome was not in him; or this part of him was not anywhere that hurt. He did not look up when she came in. She sat down across from him and opened her book.

"It's Friday," she said. "Half past ten. I've the quarter."

He looked up.

He looked terrible. In the delve you looked how you thought of yourself, Armand said; she had never quite believed it until now, because Emeric in the delve had always looked like Emeric. He looked grey. He looked as if somebody had wrung him out and hung him up wet. But his eyes came onto her across the table and stayed, and he did not say anything; and she saw his shoulders come down a little, very slowly, the way they did when he came into her room at night after a bad day and found her still up.

She read him the quarter.

She read it in order, from the first line of January to the last line of March, every column, because that was how a quarter was read; and because she had thought on the stair down that if she began to choose which lines to read she would choose the ones that mattered, and if she read him the ones that mattered she would have to stop and say something about them, and she did not want to. So she read all of it. The soap works, up eleven in the hundred on the quarter before, Grayrock's line carrying most of it. The spirits, down, because Wayford had had a dry January and somebody in Wayford's lodge had decided to make a point of it. The Almanac: four thousand two hundred and six copies sold through the station's window by the end of March, and nine hundred more ordered by the three baronies' shops, and a letter from a printer in the capital asking, very politely, whose press it was. The glass works. The pot room: three hundred and twelve pounds of the white metal in the quarter, four times the quarter before, and not a pound of it sold yet, because Vantic had not decided at what price. The scrubbers. The rails. The coal. The wages, which she read in full, all four baronies, every gang and every grade, because she knew he liked to hear them and pretended he did not. The school, where Sela had asked for two more rooms by September and had been told yes before the quarter ended, and Ferra read the line that said so and the line under it that said where the money for it would come from.

Somewhere about the wages he put his head down on his arms on the baize, and she thought he had gone, and went on reading. Somewhere about the school he lifted it again.

At the end of March there was a line in pencil, added that afternoon, below the line under the quarter, where nothing ought to have been. She read it the same as the others.

"Ward 3. Terrace. Four houses, walls to the first floor. One day. Tess, bay three, master. Osric."

He was looking at her.

"In a day?" he said.

His voice in the delve was his own and nobody else's, and it was the first thing he had said to anybody in six days, and it was that.

"Thursday," said Ferra. "Six till half past six. Hob was there at five. Gale set the lintels in the gaps as the walls came round. Tess's hand locked at four and Osric took the stylus." She turned the page back to show him, as if he could have read it. "Bram doesn't know yet. He's been asleep in the hall since he came off the collar at six this morning. When he finds out, I've told Tess she's to be standing in the parlour. I'll be in it too. Somebody has to bring the figures."

He laughed. It came out of him the way it came out of a man who has been ill a long time and has forgotten how; it caught in the middle and went on anyway. He put his face in his hands again and laughed into them, and she sat across the baize from him and watched him do it, and did not laugh, because she wanted to watch.

When he took his hands down his eyes were wet.

"Four houses," he said.

"Sixteen rooms. Four stairs. Lintels in, roof to come. I priced it." She did not look at the book. "It came out under Gale's old rate for the cellars alone. Before the lintels. I'll do the lintels when there's a roof on."

He looked at her a long time.

"Ferra."

"Mm."

"There's a thing I've been thinking about." He looked at the baize. "In there. In the, the dark, there's not much else. I've been thinking about it for three days. I can't stop."

She waited.

"The stone in my band," he said. "The one on my wrist. Ward took it off me before the cuffs. It's bound to me. I did that myself, years ago, before I knew what I was doing." His hands were flat on the baize. "And my mother's, in the star, in my room. And Corbin's nine. Every one of them's got my mark on it, from the inside. I put it there." He looked up. "What if somebody ate one?"

Ferra did not say anything.

"Not one of the five in your box. Those are new. Nobody's ever touched them but me, and only to put them in it. But one of mine." He was very quiet. "I don't know what happens. Vey doesn't say. Vey's people only ate their own. If somebody ate a stone with me written all through it, I don't know if they'd get what I've got, or get me, or get nothing, or die. I don't know if I'd feel it." His voice went flat. "I don't know if it would come out of me."

"Then nobody eats one," said Ferra.

"You can't promise that."

"I can promise I have them." She closed the book. "Your band's in my desk with the five and the sixth, under my key, since Saturday at four. Ward gave it to me himself and watched me lock the drawer. The star in your room I moved to my room on Saturday night, because your room had nobody in it. Corbin's nine are below the hall behind the door with the gravity stone in it that only you can open, and nobody's been through that door this week, because nobody can." She put her hands on the book. "That's all of them. I counted on Sunday. I can tell you where every stone with your mark on it in this barony is, and who has the key to it, and when it was last seen. If one goes missing, I'll know by the next quarter, and I'll know to the day."

"That's not the same as nobody eating one."

"No," said Ferra. "It's the thing you can do about it." She looked at him. "You've been lying in a glass room for six days with your hands in iron thinking about a thing you can't do anything about. I know you have. I've watched you do it in daylight for ten years." She opened the book again. "So I'll write it down. Stones with your mark. Where. Who holds the key. Last seen. A line a quarter, under the quarter. Then it's a sum, and you can stop thinking about it, because I'll be thinking about it for you."

He was looking at her across the baize as if she had done something he had not expected.

"Is that a yes?" said Ferra.

"It's a yes."

"Good." She wrote. "I'll start the column on Monday. I'll call it something dull so nobody reads it."

"What?"

"Inventory, sundry." She did not look up. "Nobody in the history of the world has read a column called inventory, sundry."

He laughed again; better, this time. Then he put his arms on the baize and his head on his arms, and she thought he would sleep; and instead, after a while, without lifting his head, he put out one hand along the table toward her, palm up, across the green cloth and the ink stains of her old counting room, where nobody had ever been but her.

She looked at it.

She closed her book. She put her hand in his.

They stayed like that, in the delve, at the long table, for what Ferra thought afterward was perhaps an hour, and was by Ottoline's watch on the delve room's wall three and a half; and she did not read him anything else, and he did not say anything else, and when she came up at last in the dark under the hall with a head on her like a hangover and her hand cold on the table, her book was open in her lap on a blank page, and on the blank page, in her own square hand, a line she did not remember writing.

It said: Inventory, sundry. One.



On the Saturday at sundown, Imani said it was over.

She said it at the pass-box, with her hand through the inner door on his wrist and her book on her knee, and she said it to Ward, without turning round, the way she would have said it on the ward about a man whose fever had broken. "It's done." His fever had gone down in the afternoon, slowly, from four, a little each hour on her sheet, and the hands had let go of the boards at five; and at a quarter to seven his eyes had opened and found the canopy, which was open again, every leaf, glowing; and then found her, at the door, and stayed.

Ward went in with the key and took the cuffs off.

He did it without his mask, which Imani let him do, because it was over. He took them off one at a time and laid them on the boards, all four, and they were grey and full again, all of them, and he picked them up together and carried them out through the pass-box in his arms the way a man carries something dead out of a house, and put them down on the snow-melt in the ward by the screens, and did not look at them again.

Armand measured him in the hall at nine.

He had his sheets. He had had them since he came south: the long ruled sheets in his small square hand on which he had taken every delver's measure, and his own, and Ward's, and Emeric's every quarter, with the drain stone and the scale and the watch. He did it the same way he always did it, at the end of the long table under the window, with Emeric sitting across from him in a clean shirt with his wet hair down his back and a bowl of Maren's broth half eaten at his elbow, and the drain stone in his hand; and Emeric pushed into the stone until Armand said stop, and Armand read the stone on the scale and wrote, and looked at what he had written.

Then he gave Emeric a fresh stone and made him do it again.

Then a third.

He did not say anything. He laid the three stones on the table side by side and looked at them, and then at his sheet, and then back through the sheets to the first page, and ran his finger down the column from that page to this one, all of Emeric's measures, quarter by quarter; the slow climb of it, a little every season, with the steeper places where the Band trips came and the long flat stretches where nothing had moved and Emeric had stopped asking. Then he came back to tonight's line, and put his finger under it, and left it there.

"Well?" said Imani, from the end of the table.

"I'm going to want to do it again in the morning," said Armand, "when he's eaten."

That was all he said that night. It was enough. Emeric had known him most of his life.

He went up the keep stair at ten with Imani's hand under his elbow because she would not take it away, and halfway up he stopped on the landing.

"What?" said Imani.

He went up two more steps, and came down one, and went up again.

"My knee," he said.

She looked at it, and then at him.

"It doesn't click," he said.

Imani said nothing for a moment. Then she took her hand from under his elbow, and went up the stair past him, and stood three steps above with her book open against her chest and her pencil out; and said, "Again. Slowly. Up, and down, and up," and wrote while he did it, standing on the stair in the dark between two lamps; and when he had done it four times and the knee had not clicked once she wrote one more line, and shut the book, and went on up without saying anything else at all.

He found out, in the morning, what the line said. She showed him at breakfast without being asked. It said: Seen on the third day. Not sure. Sure now.



He gave up on sleep a little after the second bell.

The week was over. Imani had said so at sundown, with her fingers in his wrist and her book open on her knee, and Ward had taken the cuffs off and carried them out of the dome as if they were something dead. He had eaten. He had bathed, and sat in the bath a long time looking at his forearms, and there was nothing on them: not a line, not the pale seam of one. He had lain down in his own bed for the first time in eight nights, and his body would not have it. It hummed. There was no other word for it. It felt like standing next to the powerhouse dynamo with his hand flat on the casing, except the dynamo was him, and it would not wind down.

He went up the keep stair in his shirt and breeches and let himself out onto the leads.

The town was dark below him except for the arc lamps on the canal ring and a few late windows in the lower ward. Cold. April cold, the kind that got under a shirt. He stood at the south parapet and did not do anything for a while.

Then he lightened himself, the old way, the cast he had done a thousand times, and the leads went almost weightless under his feet.

He did not push off the air. He had never liked that, even in the suit: the shove of the air stones at his back, the sense of being thrown. Old Edrin had told him on the lift hull last autumn how an air mage flew a ship, and he had been thinking about it ever since. You didn't push. You took the air out of the place ahead of you and let the air behind fill it, and you went along with it, the way a room's air goes through a door someone has opened.

He cast it a few yards past the parapet, a small hole in the night. The air at his back went to fill it and took him a hand's width off the lead.

He came down with his knees bending on their own. That was new. In the suit the frame did the bending for him.

He tried it again, a little higher. Then once more, and this time he felt everything: the grit on the lead under his bare soles, a cold seam where the sheets overlapped, the wind coming up the keep's face and parting round his shoulders. The suit had always lifted him like a man carried in a chair. This was his own weight, his own skin, every thread of it.

The third time, he went.

He went out over the parapet and kept going. Gravity held him where he wanted to be. The hole ahead of him drew him on, and he moved the hole and the town turned under him. Slate roofs, the dark square of the market, a dog barking at nothing in a yard in the lower ward. He took the curve over the north wall so tight he could have touched the merlons, and he didn't touch them, because he knew exactly where his hand was. He had flown for years with his body inside a machine. He had not known how much the machine had been guessing.

It cost. He could feel it cost: the air was dear to him, it always had been, three times dearer than a fire or a fall, and every yard of the hole ahead of him came out of what he had. A week ago it would have emptied him before the north wall. He went over the north wall and out past the station and over the dark printed shells of ward 3, which he saw for the first time from above, four of them, square and pale in the starlight with their doors waiting; and he was not empty. He was not near it.

Out to the Mere, low. Over it to the falls, where the noise came up at him white and the spray beaded on his arms, and back.

Halfway home he let himself drop.

He dived at the black water headfirst, and the old reflexes took over: he drove mana into his shoulders and neck and back, braced the way a knight braces before he hits stone. At the last moment he didn't take it. He rolled out of the dive and pulled level, and glided with his chest a forearm's length above the lake, so close the air between him and the water felt thick.

He put his hand down and touched it. A ring went out from his fingers and caught the moon.

He pulled the heat out of the water under his palm, threw it away past his fingertips the way he'd learned the Lantern Night of the cones, and the lake went hard under his hand: a plate of ice the size of a door, grey and thick at its middle. He had stood on one like it at the Mere last autumn in the rehearsals, for a bet with Bren. It had tipped and taken him under, because a plate on water has nothing beneath it to push against.

He landed on this one, crouched, and as he jumped he took hold of the plate with gravity and pulled it up, as hard as his legs drove down. The two met and cancelled. The ice didn't move a finger's width. His legs had a floor under them, and he went off it like a swimmer off the wall, up and out over the water, leaving a pale disc turning slowly behind him.

He laughed out loud. There was nobody to hear it.

Then he folded and went in.

The cold shut over him. It didn't hurt. It was so sharp it felt like news. His eyes were no use, the lake was black, so he opened his sense instead and let it go down.

There was more there than he would have guessed. The weed on the bottom held a faint haze of mana, like breath on a window. Perch moved through it, small sparks, a whole school of them turning together and then scattering when he came near. And under the weed, dark, lay the long shape of something that held almost nothing at all: an old hull, dead wood, a boat from the years when Kaldo's port emptied, lying there with its ribs up.

He stayed until his lungs complained, and came up laughing, water running out of his hair and his nose and his shirt, and lay on his back on the Mere with the stars over him and felt very young.

The flight home was cold. He took it slow.

He came in on the keep's east face, to his own window, because the leads door let onto the stair and the stair went past rooms where people slept lightly. He hung beside the window with his weight cancelled and reached for the latch, and his wet fingers slid off it. Without thinking he put a foot out against the wall to steady himself and set his weight into it, the Plant, the way he'd set it into a yard a hundred times.

The wall became the floor.

He felt it happen. His foot was down, and down was the wall, and the rest of him swung round to agree with it. He was standing on the keep. Too hard: the brick under his heel cracked and a flake of its face went spinning away into the dark. He eased off and slid a body's length toward the ground before he caught it again, and then he had it, light and steady, and he was standing sideways on the east face of his own house with his shirt hanging toward the courtyard and water dripping off his chin onto the stones below.

He stood there a while. Then he took a step. And another. He walked the four yards to the window like a man crossing a room.

The window opened before he reached it.

Ferra was sitting on the end of his bed in her nightdress with a shawl round her and a candle on the chest beside her. Imani was in the chair by the cold hearth with her arms folded and her book shut on her knee, which was worse than open.

Neither of them said anything. They looked at him, at the angle he was standing at, at the water.

He stepped over the sill, and the floor became the floor again, and his stomach noticed.

"Seven days," said Imani. "I said when it was over. I said it at sundown. That's six hours ago."

"I know."

"Where have you been?"

"The falls." He thought about it. "And the bottom of the Mere. Briefly."

"You were on the wall," said Ferra.

"I was."

"Standing on it."

"I found out I could. About a minute ago."

Ferra looked at the window and then at him for a long moment, and he couldn't tell whether she was going to be angry. She pulled the shawl closer instead. "There's brick in your hair."

He was grinning. He couldn't help it, and he didn't try. He came across the room to her with his hands out, meaning to pull her up off the bed, meaning a great many things.

Ferra leaned back. "You're drenched."

"You're dripping on my book," said Imani, and moved it.

"Back up a bit," he said. "Both of you. Please."

Imani raised an eyebrow and didn't move. Ferra drew her feet up onto the bed.

He held his arms a little out from his sides, and put the fire into the cloth. Not into himself; into the wet shirt, the breeches, his hair, slow and spread thin. The fire's mark showed for an instant at his chest, there and gone. Then the steam came. It came off his shoulders in a rush, rolled up past the candle and spread under the ceiling, and the shirt went from cold to warm to dry against his skin, the water in his hair hissing out of it. He kept it slow so nothing scorched. It took longer than he'd hoped and cost more than it should have, and when it was done the room smelled of lake and hot linen and he was standing in a warm cloud with his hair standing up.

There was a short silence.

"That," said Ferra, "is not fair."

Imani got up. She came across the steam to him, took his wrist, and counted. She counted all the way to sixty with her eyes on the candle, and he let her. Then she let go.

"Fine," she said. "You're fine." She sounded annoyed about it. "You'll tell me everything you did tonight, in order, before breakfast, and I'll write it down."

"Before breakfast."

"Every jump." She put her hand flat on his chest, where the shirt was warmest, and left it there a moment longer than counting needed. "And then you can try that again."
