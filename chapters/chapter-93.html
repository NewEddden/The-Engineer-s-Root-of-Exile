Chapter 93 - The Line Under It

Ilsa's sheet came down the river road at the end of the first month of the autumn, with the ore tallies, the way it came every month, and it had a column on it that had not been there before.

Ferra saw it first, because Ferra saw every sheet first. She brought it up to the long room in the evening and laid it in front of him without a word, and put one finger at the head of the new column, and took the finger away.

At the pithead, not carried.

Under the heading, day by day through the last weeks of the summer, a figure in the small upright hand. Some days it was nothing. Most days it was not. The deep ground had been giving iron since Hask's men went down below the old galleries in the spring, and the tally house had weighed every basket of it up out of the hill, and on most days of the late summer there had been more of it than the carts could take away. So it had stayed where it was, on the ground by the pithead, and Ilsa had weighed that too. At the foot of the column she had ruled a line and put the sum of it: a heap of ore the size of a hundred and forty cartloads, which nobody had asked her to measure, and which was growing.

"She asked nobody," Ferra said. "She saw the difference between two columns and gave it one of its own."

"Her mother's daughter."

"Her own," said Ferra.

He looked at the figure at the foot for a while. He had seen the heap. He had ridden past it with the assessor a month ago, a long grey mound beside the tally house with a boy sitting on it, and had known exactly what it was, and it was a different thing to see it with a line under it in somebody else's hand. Then he took the notebook out of his coat and opened it at the second column and laid it on the table beside the sheet, turned so that she could read it. Grayrock, where he had written it the winter Bryda's first letter about the veins came down. Two words under it, and a line under those.

Ferra read the two words for some time.

"When did you draw that?"

"At the ford. On the way up."

"Before the price."

"Before the price."

She took up her pen and wrote something in the day book, short, and he did not have to read it upside down to know that it was the date, and the words the ford, and nothing else.

"Then you'd better bring it to the table," she said.

He brought it to the table on the next quiet morning, with the shutters open on a sky that had finally rained, and the six in their places, and Vantic at the end with her small book, because anything that was going to cost what this was going to cost was above her line.

He did not begin with the iron. He began with Ilsa's column, and passed it round, and let every one of them look at the figure at the foot before he said anything about it. Then he told them about the twenty-six carts, and the fords, and the two flat boats lying empty on the gravel. He told them Pask's words from the early summer, because Pask had said it better than he could have. And he reminded them of Saban's two silver on every four at Tarrant's pit, which every one of them had heard in the winter and not thought about since.

"The road carried everything the hill gave for about a year," he said. "It doesn't any more. The river carries it for half the year and then it doesn't carry anything. And it's going to get worse, because Hask isn't at the bottom of the deep ground and nobody knows where the bottom is."

"Widen the road," said Ward.

"I've widened it once. Two carts can pass on it now in most places. It doesn't help. A cart carries what a horse can pull over stone, and a horse can pull what it can pull. You can put more carts on it, and more horses, and more men, and every one of them costs what the last one cost." He put his hand flat on the table. "So I want to change what the horse pulls on."

He had drawn it the night before, very plainly, on a single sheet, because it was a very plain thing. Two lines of rolled iron, laid on blocks of stone, the width of a cart apart, running beside the road and not on it. A wagon with iron wheels, and a lip on the inside of every wheel that kept it on the rail. A horse walking between the rails, on a path of its own.

"On stone, a horse pulls one cart," he said. "On iron, on the level, the same horse pulls six. Not because it's stronger. Because there's nothing for the wheel to sink into and nothing for it to climb out of."

Bram had taken the sheet and was looking at the wheel. He did not look up.

"There's more," Emeric said. "Not yet. The first wagons will go behind horses, and that's all anybody outside this room is to know. After that there'll be an engine on it." He looked round the table. "Not a word of the engine goes out of this room. The rails are a road. People have seen roads. They'll look at it and see a road with iron in it, and they'll think I'm a fool with money, which they've thought before."

"That's the second time you've asked me for the same thing," Vantic said, from the end of the table. "The answer has not changed."

Saban said, "The carters."

He said it without any weight on it, the way he said the first word of a price.

"There are thirty men between here and the hill who live off that road," Saban said. "Thirty carts, near enough, and sixty horses, and a man at Grayrock called Garrow who keeps four horses more than he needs because you paid him to, once, and then the road got good and he put them to work. You put a horse on iron that does what six do on stone, and five of every six of those horses are standing in a field. So are five of every six men."

"They'll drive the wagons."

"Some of them will." Saban turned his cup round on the table. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying there's a price, and it isn't in Ferra's book, and it'll be paid anyway, and I'd rather you heard it from me than from Garrow."

"I'd rather hear it from you too," Emeric said. "Put it in writing. Who they are, what they carry, what they're paid. I want to know the number before I lay the first rail."

"You'll have it."

Imani had been reading the sheet over Bram's shoulder. "Men get hurt laying iron," she said. "I've never seen it done, and I know that. Every man on the gang goes in my book from the first day, with his name and his work, and I want to be told the same day when one of them is hurt, not the next."

"Yes."

"I'd like it written down that I asked."

"It's written down," said Ferra.

Ward had been looking out of the window, which was his way of listening. He turned round.

"It runs both ways," he said.

Everybody looked at him.

"Iron from the hill to here," Ward said. "Men from the hill to here. Men from anywhere past the hill to here. You're building a thing that brings whatever's on it to the foot of the Left Bridge faster than anything has ever come to it." He said it flatly, the way he said the number of men on a road. "I'm not against it. I want to know where it can be cut. And I want to know that before anybody else works it out, because somebody will."

"The river does the same," Emeric said.

"The river does it for half the year and everybody can see what's on it."

"Yes." He thought about that for a moment, and did not have anything better. "Walk it with Bram when he surveys it. Mark every place you'd cut it. I'd rather know."

Ward nodded, and went back to the window.

It was Saban who said the other thing, a little later, after Ferra had started asking about money and before she had finished.

"The tower sells stone to make a carriage lighter," he said. "You've four carriage stones in a box. Put a stone under every wagon and one horse pulls twenty."

"I spent two ounces of stone in the yard a month ago," Emeric said, "on a blank the size of my thumb. It lasted a little over five hours. It bought a number and a rule and nothing else." He did not look at Armand. "Stone spends while it works. It doesn't care what it's carrying. Every hour a stone held up a wagon of ore, it would be burning something the Crown sent eleven men to weigh, to save the price of a horse."

"It would carry the first wagon very well," Armand said. He had said nothing until then, and he said it mildly, to the table. "It would be spent before the hundredth."

Saban put up his hands, and smiled, and let it go. He had not expected to win it. He had wanted to hear the reason said aloud, so that the next man who asked him could be given it, and Emeric knew that, and had given it.

Ferra had her sheet out by then.

"The stone money's spent," she said. "All of it. Fletcher's hull and the section and the hospital roof, to the coin, and I won't move a line of it. This comes out of the ore and the tolls, after the quarter's service to the house, and not before." She did not look at Vantic, and Vantic did not look up. "So tell me what a mile costs."

"The iron is ours," Emeric said. "It comes off the hill as ore and goes through our furnace and our forge and our mill. The blocks are Hask's spoil, cut. The chairs that hold the rail to the block are cast here. The ballast is what the hill's glad to be rid of. What we pay out in coin is men, and horses, and wagons, and the price of a summer."

"And the iron's time," said Ferra.

"And the iron's time."

"Then I'll give you a number when I've seen Bram's." She wrote something. "Not before."

Bram put the sheet down. He had been doing a sum on it with his thumbnail, in the margin, and he had got to the end of it and not said what it was.

"Fletcher's plate first," he said.

"Fletcher's plate first."

"Then the mill's not yours till the thaw." Bram folded his arms. "You can have Jorrin in the evenings for the rolls. You can't have the rolls."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair," Bram said. "It's the order."

Vantic had written for some time while the others talked. When they had finished she put the pen down and spoke to the table, in the voice she used for a clause.

"This is not maintenance," she said.

"No."

"I did not suppose that it was. I would rather it were not called so, in any book of yours or any letter." She turned a page. "The covenant is lifted. The house has no view on what you build, only on what you pay, and on the day you pay it. I will enter this as new plant, above the line, from the ore and the tolls after service, and I will attend the spending of it." She paused, and added, in exactly the same voice, "Every carrying line I have seen priced was worth more to the man who tolled it than to the man who built it. You will be asked to toll it. You will be asked by people you would prefer not to refuse."

Saban looked at her. It was the look he had given her in the winter over Tarrant's lease, a moment longer than a man looks at a factor.

"Where have you seen one priced?" he said.

"In the books of houses that lend," Vantic said, "which is where I have seen everything," and went back to her own.

Ferra, who was writing, did not look up. She wrote something small in the back of her book, below the other small things she kept there, and closed it.

They surveyed it in the last weeks of the autumn, before the ground went hard.

Bram carried the level, and a boy carried the staff, and Emeric and Ward walked the chain between them off Bram's master foot, a hundred links at a time, out of the foundry yard and north along the river road. Ward stopped every mile or so and looked at the ground on both sides for a long time without saying anything, and wrote in a book of his own, and did not show anybody what he had written.

The first thing Bram wanted was the width. He did not trust a width that had come out of a drawing. On the second morning he went out onto the old road with his rule, where it ran through a cutting and the ruts were worn down into the rock by more carts than anybody could count, and laid the rule across them, from the middle of one rut to the middle of the other. He did it in four places, a mile apart. It came out the same in all four, to within the width of his thumbnail.

"There," he said. "That's the width of every cart in the south. Somebody set it before your grandfather's grandfather, and every wheelwright since has built to it because the ruts were already there." He put the rule away. "Build to it, and every cart we own is half a wagon already. You only change the wheels."

The second thing was the rise. The river road climbed with the river, the whole way, and Bram read it off the staff every few chains and wrote the figures in a column, and in the evening at the guest house he ran his thumb down the column and said that a horse would not notice it, and neither would an engine. It rose all the way to the hill and it never rose hard.

Until the hill. On the fourth day they came to the foot of Grayrock, where the road left the river and climbed to the tally house door, and Bram set the level on the first shoulder and sighted up the slope, and read the staff, and read it again, and looked at the figure he had written for a long moment.

"That'll want a rope," he said.

"It'll want a rope."

"Loaded down, empty up. The full ones pull the empties." Bram looked up the slope at the head-frames on the shoulder. "Like a well bucket."

"Like a well bucket."

Bram nodded, and wrote it in his column, and did not ask where Emeric had got it from. He had stopped asking that years ago.

They came back down the road in the first frost. Bram drove the first stake himself, in the corner of the foundry yard, where the ore carts came in, and cut a notch in the head of it with his knife, and wrote beside it in his column: here, and nothing else.

Then he went back into the mill, where Neta was bringing a bar out of the forge at a yellow heat for Fletcher's plate, and the iron road went into the book as a figure and a stake, and waited for the hull, because the hull was first, and the hull was not going to wait for anything.
