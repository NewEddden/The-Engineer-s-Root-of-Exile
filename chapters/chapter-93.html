Chapter 93 - The Board

The three standpipes went in during the first week of September, at the bottom of the lower ward, where the clay line came down from the second cistern and stopped.

They were Bram's, and they were the first things Kaldo had ever stood up in a street that a machine had made round. A cast iron pipe as high as a man's chest, poured in the cupola from the last of the tithe-house grates, bored true on the lathe, with a brass tap at the top that turned on a brass thread, cut on the lathe too, so that a child could turn it with one hand and it would shut off clean and not drip. Bram turned the first tap himself, on the first morning, in front of the whole of Cooper's Row, and the water came out clear and cold from the Mere and ran into a bucket a woman was holding, and the woman looked at it and then at Bram as if he had done something improper.

Doran came down from the church without being asked and stood by the first standpipe in his old cassock and blessed it, briefly and plainly, the way he blessed a grave, and went home again. Nobody had sent for him. Emeric suspected that nobody had needed to.

The well at the bottom of the lane, the one below where the plague rows had been, the one the map in his first summer had shown worst of all, Imani capped herself.

She did it on the third day, when the standpipes had been running long enough that nobody could say they did not work. She came down with a bucket of mortar and a trowel and two of the gate crew carrying a flat stone, and had them lay the stone over the mouth of the well, and then knelt in the lane in her apron with her sleeves rolled up and mortared round the edge of it herself, all the way round, slowly and carefully, as if she were closing a wound and meant it to stay closed. Half the lane watched her do it. She did not look at any of them. When she had finished she stood up and looked at it, and wiped the trowel on the stone, and went back up the hill to the ward with the empty bucket, and did not say a word to anybody, and nobody said a word to her.



He cut the stones in the room, over four afternoons, with Ward in the chair by the door.

Twelve of them, out of the chest. Seven earth, for the stretches of the wall, because they would be living in stone and were happiest there. Five water, for the water gate and the drains and the head of the river stair, where they would sit in the wet and like it. He took them into the room one at a time, and sat at the bench with each one in front of him in the light, and went into it.

That was the only way he had ever been able to describe it to himself. Into it. As if the stone were a house with rooms of its own, and he could walk through them, and write on the walls.

He thought about Armand while he did it. Armand in the chair with his eyes shut, breathing out into the house and listening for his breath to come back off whatever it touched. That was what these would do, nearly. Each one would feel what came into its field, the way Armand felt what his breath touched, and know roughly how much of it there was. Only the stone would not breathe out. It would not spend anything. It would sit in the wall and listen, and do nothing at all, for weeks or months, until something came close enough to push against it, and then it would answer.

Or not answer. That was the whole of the work. Deciding what it should answer to.

Everything alive carried some mana in it. A man, a dog, a rat, a sparrow on the parapet, a heron at the mouth of a drain. Less the smaller it was. If he cut a stone to wake for anything at all, it would wake all night for the rats in the drains, and drop a shutter in the line room every time a cat went along the wall, and in a week the man sitting the board would stop getting up for it, as Ward had said he would. It had to wake for a person and sleep through everything smaller. And he did not know, in the room, how much a person was. He knew what one felt like at a few paces. He did not know it as a number written inside a stone.

So he guessed. He cut each one to wake for something a little less than a grown man and to sleep for anything less than that, and to press harder the more there was, and to stop pressing when it had gone, and to spend nothing in between. He wrote it into each stone from inside, as much as it would hold and no more, and came out of the room each afternoon with his head aching and his nose running, and Ward looking at him from the chair by the door as if he had been somewhere a long way off and come back thinner.



Vesk set them.

He did it the way he had trued the wheels, without a word, with his hands flat on the masonry, and the stone of the wall moved under his hands like wet clay and took each one in, and closed over it, so that when he stepped back there was nothing to see on the face of the wall at all. Seven along the dark stretches. One at the water gate. One in the mouth of each of the three drains under the narrow walk above the lower ward. One at the head of the river stair, under the top step.

Beside each stone, in a little clay box mortared into the wall behind it, was a stack of carbon discs the size of coins, and a copper wire running from each end of the stack. When the stone woke it would lean on the stack. The harder it leaned, the more tightly the discs pressed together, and the more freely the current ran through them. A man would push the stack a little. Two men would push it more.

The gate crew strung the wire along the inside of the parapet and up the wall and in at the line room window, the same way they had strung the line, and had opinions about it at every pole.

The board in the line room was Bram's, and Tess's. A frame of oak as wide as a door, with twelve little windows in it in two rows, one for each stone, with the name of the stretch painted under each window in Tess's careful hand. Behind each window, a small iron shutter, held up by a magnet she had wound herself. When a stone woke and the current came, the magnet let go, and the shutter dropped and showed white, and a bell on the top of the frame rang until somebody put the shutter back up. And beside each window a needle, like the line's needle, that swung as far as the current told it to, so that the man sitting the board could see not only which stretch, but how much.

Emeric called it an annunciator. Tess said that was a ridiculous word. The garrison called it the board, and in a week so did everybody, including Tess.



They tried it on the second Sunday of the month, the whole garrison and half the gate crew and Arthur, who had not been asked and could not be got rid of.

Emeric sat the board in the line room with Rylan beside him. Ward took the testers out onto the narrow walk above the lower ward and the water gate and the head of the river stair. Arthur ran between them up and down the keep stair, with messages, because somebody had to and he was the only one who wanted to, and by the fourth trip he was red in the face and still running.

Maren's kitchen cat went first. One of the gate crew carried it up onto the walk in a basket and let it out at the far end of the river stretch, and it walked the whole length of the stretch along the top of the parapet with its tail up, as if it owned the wall, past two stones, and sat down at the end and washed. In the line room the board did not move. Not a needle. Rylan looked at the board, and at Emeric, and said that the cat would be very disappointed.

Then the dog.

It was the guardhouse dog, a big brindled animal of no particular kind that the garrison fed scraps and called by four different names. A gate crew man walked it along the river stretch on a rope, and at the head of the river stair, when it put its nose down to smell the top step, the bell on the board went off like a fire bell, and the river stair's shutter dropped white, and its needle swung a little way over and stayed there.

Rylan put his hand over the bell to stop it.

"Well," he said. "That's a dog."

Emeric looked at the needle for a while. It had not swung far. Not very far at all. But it had swung, and the shutter had dropped, and if it had been three in the morning the walking men would have gone to the river stair at a run and found a dog.

He went back into the room the next afternoon with the river stair's stone, which Vesk took out of the wall for him without being asked and put back after without being asked, and cut it again, a little higher. When they walked the dog past it on the Tuesday the board stayed dark. The dog put its nose on the top step and smelled it thoroughly and moved on, and nothing happened in the line room at all, and the gate crew man on the other end of the rope looked up at the keep as if he expected applause.

They did the rest of it that afternoon. A heron that came down to the mouth of the second drain on its own, as herons did every evening, and stood on one leg in the outflow fishing for whatever came out of the lower ward: nothing. A hutch of Maren's rabbits, set on the walk at the water gate and left there for an hour: nothing, except that the rabbits got out and ate the gate crew's cabbages, and the gate crew wanted it written down that this was the board's fault.

Then the people.

Ward walked the river stretch on his own, at a walk, past the stones. Each shutter dropped as he passed it and each needle swung well over, and stood, and came back as he went on. Declan walked it. The same. One of the gate crew, a small man, walked it, and the needles swung less far, but the shutters dropped, every one.

Osric walked it, and the shutters dropped, and the bell rang, and Osric heard the bell from the wall and came back and walked past the first stone again to see if it would ring again, and it did, and he did it again, and Rylan in the line room began to laugh, and Osric did it four more times before Ward went out onto the walk and stood in front of him until he stopped.

Osric and Declan together, side by side, swung the needles nearly twice as far as either of them alone.

And Arthur, who had been sent along the walk with a message for Ward and had forgotten to stop at the first stone, dropped the shutter at the water gate, and swung its needle over as far as the small man from the gate crew had swung it, and a little further.

Rylan looked at the needle. Then at Emeric.

"That's the boy," he said.

"That's the boy."

Rylan looked at the needle again. A boy not yet fifteen, all elbows, swinging it as far as a grown man. Emeric did not say anything. He knew why. Armand knew why, and Sela, and the boy himself, and that was all, and Sela had said that was three too many.

"He eats a lot," said Rylan, after a while, and reached up and put the shutter back.

That night Emeric sat in the line room with the board in front of him and Tess's numbers for every needle beside him on a slate, and worked out the line he had been trying to find since the room. A grown man. A boy. A big dog, which had swung the needle about half as far as the smallest man on the gate crew, though it outweighed him. Rabbits and cats and herons, nothing at all. Whatever it was that a person carried, a person carried a good deal more of it than a beast of twice the size. Enough more that a line could be drawn between them. He drew it, on the slate, and looked at it, and then cut every stone to it, one afternoon at a time, over the next week.



Tess taught her first class on the Thursday of that week, in the long room, from her chair at the front, on a morning Sela gave her.

Sela had not wanted to give it to her. Sela had said that the oldest children had their figures to do and their letters, and that electricity was not a thing a child needed to know to keep a shop or mend a net or bring in a harvest. Tess had said that it would be, and Sela had looked at her across the long room without answering, and then said, one morning, the oldest only, and you'll be gone by noon.

So she had the oldest. Twenty of them, the eldest of the school, thirteen and fourteen and one or two fifteen, on the benches in front of her, and Arthur at the end of the front bench because he had not been asked to sit anywhere else. She had a crock cell on the table beside her chair, and a coil of copper wire, and one of the board's spare shutters with its magnet, and a bell, and her bench book, and nothing else.

She did not tell them what electricity was. Emeric, standing at the back by the door where Sela had put him and told him to stay, had expected her to, and she did not. She showed them the bell. She put the wires to the cell and the bell rang, and twenty children jumped, and she took the wires off and it stopped, and she put them on and it rang, and she did it until they stopped jumping. Then she asked them what was in the wire.

They told her. Fire, one of them said. A spirit, said another. Nothing, said a third. Tess wrote all three on the slate behind her and said that none of them was right and none of them was stupid, and then she showed them the shutter, and the magnet, and the drop, and talked for half an hour about the board in the line room without once saying the word stone, and by the end of it they were arguing with her, all twenty, and she was answering every one of them, and Emeric, at the back, found that he had stopped listening to what she said and was only watching her say it.

Then she let them taste it.

She had two short ends of bright copper wire on the cell, a finger's width apart, and she held them out, and told the first child on the front bench to put the tip of his tongue across both at once. He looked at her. She looked back. He did it, and his eyes went wide, and he sat back hard on the bench with his hand over his mouth, and the bench behind him went up like a flock of birds.

All twenty of them did it. They queued. Arthur did it three times. The long room was full of children with their hands over their mouths and their eyes streaming, shouting that it tasted like a spoon and like a spoon left in vinegar and like lightning, though none of them had ever tasted lightning, and Tess sat in the middle of it in her chair, holding out the wires, with an expression of deep and serious satisfaction.

Sela came in from the back at the noise.

She stood in the doorway and looked at it. Twenty children with their hands over their mouths. Her own son at the front of the queue for the fourth time. A girl in a wheeled chair holding out two wires.

"You've had them licking wire," Sela said.

"Copper," said Tess. "Clean copper. I boiled it."

"You did not ask me."

Tess considered that. She considered it honestly, the way she considered a column that had come out wrong.

"No," she said. "Next time I'll ask you."

Sela looked at her a while longer. Then at Arthur, who had his tongue out and was halfway to the wires again, and who took it back in. Then she said that it was noon, and that anybody who was not out of her long room in the time it took her to walk to the front of it would be doing figures until dark, and the long room emptied like a bucket with the bottom out, and Tess wheeled herself out last, past Emeric at the door, without looking at him, and with the faintest possible smile.



The engine went onto the bench in bay three the following week, on paper, for Bram and Tess.

He drew it the way he had drawn the wheelchair, from three sides, and then the parts on their own, bigger. A boiler of Grayrock plate. A cylinder, cast in the cupola and bored true on the lathe, with steam let in at both ends in turn so that it pushed both ways, out and back. A beam across the top, rocking on a pillar, to turn the push into a turn. A flywheel to keep the turn steady. A second vessel, beside the cylinder, standing in a trough of cold water from the race, into which the spent steam would go and be cooled and turn back to water and leave nothing behind it, a hole where the steam had been, which would pull the piston as hard as the steam had pushed it. And on top, two iron balls on arms, spinning on a spindle, that would fly out as the engine ran faster and close the steam off, and fall in as it ran slower and open it again, so that the engine held its own speed without a man standing over it.

He named each part as he drew it. The cylinder, the beam, the flywheel. The condenser. The governor. He did not tell them where the names came from, and neither of them asked.

Bram looked at the whole of it with his hands behind his back until Tess had finished reading it upside down. Then he put one thick finger on the governor's spinning balls.

"That," he said, "is a toy."

"It's the cleverest thing on the sheet."

"It's a toy that's the cleverest thing on the sheet." Bram took his finger away. "I'll make it last." He looked at the boiler. "It's a kettle with ideas," he said, and Tess, beside him in her chair with the condenser already half drawn again in her bench book in her own way, did not look up, and said it was a very good kettle, and Bram said nobody had asked her.



He found the other thing on the wall, on the last night of the trials, standing in the dark at the corner over the bridge with his eyes shut.

Ward was walking toward him along the walk from the north. Rylan was walking toward him along the walk from the south, from the river tower. They were the same distance away. He knew it without opening his eyes, because he could feel them both coming at the edge of his own sense, a hand's width and then more than a hand's width, a warmth and then a presence. He had done it since the first autumn, a little, the way Armand said.

What he had not done before was notice that he could tell them apart.

Not by how much. They carried about the same, Ward and Rylan, both of them rite-made, both of them a long way above any man on the gate crew; on the board their needles swung to the same mark. It was something else. Ward came on slow and even, like a man walking a measured distance, each step the same. Rylan came on in a quicker, lighter beat, with something irregular in it, like a man who keeps half stopping to listen. It was not in the amount at all. It was in the rhythm.

He opened his eyes. Ward and Rylan arrived at the corner from either side within a breath of each other and looked at him standing there with his lantern down.

He told Tess the next day at the powerhouse, because she would want to know, and because he could not tell anybody else.

She listened with her pen still. Then she looked at the drawing of the board pinned on the powerhouse wall.

"Could a stone learn it?" she said. "The rhythm. Not how much. Who."

He thought about the inside of a stone, and how much it would hold, and how simple the thing was that he had written in each of those twelve. Wake for this much. Press this hard. Sleep.

"Not one we can cut," he said.

Tess wrote that down. She wrote it, he saw, exactly as he had said it, word for word, with the date beside it, on a page by itself, as she had written down the wire that moved toward his hand two summers before.



Behind the shutters that night, with the cloak over them and the candle on the hearth stone and the bucket beside it, he did the thing he had been working toward since Linnet stood in bay three and told him the air was a crowd.

He took his own weight off his feet, which was easy now, so easy that it was nearly nothing, and hung a hand's width above the floor in the middle of the room. And then, holding that, he reached back past himself into the still air at the far wall of the room, all the way to the plaster, and leaned on it, gently, the way she had said, and let it lean on the rest.

The air moved. It moved like a door opening at the far end of a corridor, and it took him with it, hanging, across his own floor, a yard, the length of a long stride, and set him down by the hearth with the candle flame lying flat beside him and his head ringing like the board's bell.

He sat on the floor by the bucket and breathed. A yard. Two holds at once, his own weight and somebody else's element, and a yard of floor for it.

He would take it.



The board's first real catch came on the second Thursday after it went live, at a little before two in the morning.

A garrison man was sitting it, one of the steady ones, with a lamp and a cup of something cold and his boots off under the bench. He told it afterward exactly as it had happened, because Ward made him, three times. The bell went. Not long. A short hard ring, and the river stair's shutter dropped white, and the needle beside it swung over to a grown man's mark and hung there, and then, while he was still getting his boots on, came back, and lay still, and the bell stopped. Something the size of a man had come up to the top of the river stair and stood there, and then gone back down.

He sent the walking men. Ward went himself, from the gate tower, at a run along the narrow walk with a lantern, and Emeric came down from the keep behind him because the bell in the line room could be heard through his door, and they reached the water gate within a few breaths of each other.

There was nobody on the river stair.

Ward held his lantern out over it. The stair went down in its worn stone steps to the river, which was low and black and very quiet. The top three steps were wet. Not with dew. With water that had come up out of the river on somebody's legs and run off onto the stone, a broad dark patch on each step going up, and on the top step, smaller, the shape of a pair of feet where somebody had stood, and turned round. Below the stair, at the edge of the mud where the river had gone down, there was one clear print in the soft ground, a boot, narrower than a Kaldo boot and nailed in a pattern none of the garrison knew, pointing down into the water.

Nothing on the river. No boat that anybody could see. No sound. Whoever it had been had come up out of the water, and stood at the top of the stair, and gone back into the water, and the water had closed over him.

Ward stood at the bottom of the stair with the lantern held out over the river, looking at the black water, until the garrison men behind him began to shift their feet.

"He came to look," he said.
