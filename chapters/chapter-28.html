Chapter 28 - The Tradesman

The caravan came through on the second week of the month, the way it had come through on the second week of the month for as long as anyone in Kaldo could remember.

Not because Kaldo was worth anything. Because Kaldo was on the way, and a man who moves goods for a living will stop anywhere that might have a coin in it, and even in the plague years there had been coins in Kaldo, held by progressively fewer people.

Emeric had watched them arrive and leave eighteen times without once going down to the market, because until that summer he had nothing to sell and no reason at all to be seen wanting anything.

That summer he had four thousand bars of soap a month and a payroll that was going to eat him in September.

He did not go himself, and the reason was not modesty.

A prince at a table sets the price before anybody opens their mouth. Every man in that market would have quoted him a courtier's number, and would have been correct to, because a courtier's number is what a courtier pays and everyone involved understands the arrangement.

So he sent Ferra down with the barony's paper and full authority to bind it, and stayed out of the market entirely for a fortnight, and the traders spent most of that fortnight not knowing who they were actually dealing with.

Two of them worked it out. Neither of them was Saban.

The test was Emeric's and Ferra ran it, and it was not a riddle and there was no trick in it anywhere.

It was three ordinary commercial questions, arranged so that greed and honesty would answer them differently, and put to every man with more than two carts.

First, a shipment on terms that were quietly too generous. Not absurdly. Enough that a man who knew his business would notice and would want to know why it was on the table, and a man who did not care would simply take it.

Second, a quantity discount with an arithmetic error in the barony's favor, buried three lines down in a column of perfectly correct figures.

Third, a plain question, asked flatly at the end and not dressed up as anything: what would he charge to carry something he had not been told the contents of.

Most of them failed the first.

Several failed the second, and two of those found the error afterward and came back, which Ferra recorded separately, because a man who checks his own work overnight is not the same as a man who does not check it at all.

Two failed the third badly enough that she ended the conversation in the middle of it, which she reported to Emeric in one sentence with no adjectives in it, and he did not ask for more.

One man took none of it.

He turned down the generous terms in under a minute, on the grounds that anybody offering them either did not know his own business or was going to change his mind about it in three months, and he had been on the wrong end of both.

He found the arithmetic error, corrected it out loud, and was mildly offended that it had been there, in the manner of a man who does not enjoy being made to look for things.

And on the third question he did not haggle and did not moralize. He said that he did not carry sealed goods. Not for a high price and not for a low one and not as a favor, because a man who does it once carries whatever he is given afterward, and the second time it is not a favor, it is a record.

His name was Saban. Early forties, weathered, not large, with the particular economy of movement of a man who has loaded his own carts for twenty years. He had his own small string, four carts and a spare, and a network he had built one town at a time by being the man who turned up when he said he would.

And he was from Kaldo.

He did not mention it. Ferra found it in the tithe rolls that night, because she had by then a register of every name this barony had ever had and could put her hand on any of them in under an hour. Left during the war years. Did not come back after the plague, for the ordinary reason, which was that there was nothing to come back to and a man has to eat somewhere.

She told Emeric before the second meeting.

He sat with it for most of an evening and decided not to use it.

It would have worked. That was the difficulty. Three streets from the north well, a name in a roll, a mother probably in the parish book, and a sentence delivered warmly at the right moment in a negotiation would have moved that man further than any margin on the table. Emeric could see the shape of the sentence and could hear himself saying it.

And if Saban ever found out where it had come from, which he would, because men in trade find out everything eventually, then every warm thing Emeric ever said to him afterward would be filed under the same heading.

So he went into the second meeting with nothing but the offer, and it cost him a good deal more than the offer was worth, and he knew it while he was doing it.

He came to the table himself for that part.

An exclusive contract. Saban sold Kaldo's soap, at his own margin, anywhere he could reach, and Kaldo sold it to nobody else.

And the proceeds did not come back as coin. They came back as goods the barony named. Ore, lard, timber, tin, salt, anything on a list Ferra maintained and revised monthly. Saban stopped being a merchant with a route through here and became the barony's hands outside its own border.

Saban said that was a great deal of trust to put in a man he had met twice.

Emeric said that was true, and then Ferra put the sheets on the table.

Not the ledger. The ledger would have told him what he already assumed.

Production by week, against ash intake, for eleven months, in a column he could run his thumb down. Cost per bar, broken out into tallow, lime, fuel and labor, each one carried separately so a change in any of them could be traced without guessing. A line marking the week the operation crossed out of loss and into profit, with the date on it. Sales by buyer, sorted largest to smallest, which nobody in the kingdom did because nobody in the kingdom had thought to.

And a curve, drawn by hand, of the whole eleven months, rising.

Saban had been in trade for twenty years. He had seen good books and he had seen books kept by men who were hiding things and books kept by men who were not hiding anything because they did not know what was in them.

He had never seen a business look at itself.

He went quiet for a long time. Then he started asking questions, and the questions were good.

He wanted to know what happened to the curve if tallow went up a third, which it did every second winter. He wanted to know whether the labor line included the shop or only the boiling. He wanted to know what the eleventh month looked like without the two biggest buyers in it, and when Ferra covered them with her hand and showed him, he grunted, which appeared to be approval.

The two of them ended up forty minutes into a conversation about seasonality that Emeric largely could not follow, and he sat back and let it happen and enjoyed himself considerably.

Then Saban said, half joking and entirely serious, that he would like to hire her.

"You can't have her."

It came out faster than any sentence Emeric had said since he arrived in this barony.

It landed in the room like a dropped plate.

He heard it about half a second after it left him. She heard it at the same moment he did. Both of them went red, which was the worst available outcome and the one with the least possible deniability in it.

Saban, who had been reading rooms professionally for two decades and could have made a very great deal of that, looked between them exactly once and then found something extremely interesting on the sheet in front of him and studied it with total absorption for as long as was required.

He said nothing at all.

That mercy was the only reason the meeting continued, and everyone in the room knew it, and nobody mentioned it afterward for a very long time.

He said he would think about it, and he was right to.

Exclusivity meant his whole living rode on one town's product. The town was a byword for dying in every market between here and the capital. And the man offering it was a disgraced prince who had held the place for a year and three months and whose predecessor had been hanged.

So he stayed a week, on his own account, at his own expense, and walked around.

He saw paid work everywhere, which he had not seen in a town this size in years, and he saw it paid weekly, which he had not seen anywhere at all.

He saw a board at the yard gate with rates written on it against jobs, in numerals a man could read from six feet, with the same rate against the same job regardless of who was doing it. He stood in front of that one for a while. He counted the women on the kiln haul.

He saw children in a parish hall on a Tuesday afternoon, learning numerals from the blacksmith's daughter, who was the same woman who had put the sheets in front of him on the Friday.

And he heard the story about the staging three times in four days, from three people who did not know each other, and it had grown slightly each time, and the core of it was true.

A man sent home off a bad staging, over a thing that had not happened, and paid for the day. By the third telling the staging had been about to come down and the man had a family of six. By any telling it was a thing nobody in this kingdom had ever heard of.

He drank the water. He asked the healer, directly, whether anybody had died of it in the last year, and she said no, and said it in the tone of a woman who had been asked that question by a lot of people and had enjoyed giving the answer every time.

There was a foundry going up across the eastern branch with two storeys and three chimneys on it.

And a woman at the north well told him, unprompted, that the prince was not to be trusted, and then told him where to buy the soap.

He signed on the Thursday and he was honest about why, which Emeric had not expected.

Two thirds of it was the numbers. A man who shows you his true margin is either a fool or has decided to work with you, and this one was not a fool, and Saban had made his living for twenty years on that distinction.

The other third he did not say to Emeric. He said it to Ferra, once, in the yard, on the morning he left, because she was from here too and he had worked that out on his own without anybody telling him.

He had grown up three streets from the north well. He had spent twenty years telling himself Kaldo was gone, and he had been right for nineteen of them, and he had come back this spring expecting to be right again.

He moved his main operation inside a month. Carts, stock, two men, and a lease on a warehouse by the dead port that cost him almost nothing, because nothing had been in it for eleven years and the man who owned it had stopped believing anybody would ever ask.

Across the way from it stood the old exchange. Stone, good stone, with the roof open over one bay and grass in the gutters and a stepped front that had been built by somebody who expected a great deal of traffic.

Saban mentioned in passing, unlocking the warehouse, that his father used to take tallies in there.

Emeric said nothing and looked at it for slightly too long.

Ferra noticed him doing it and did not say anything either.

The reach was the most valuable thing Saban brought and Emeric did not find out the size of it until he asked directly.

People in every barony within reasonable travel. Names, not contacts. Men who would take a message and men who would hold stock and men who would tell him what a price had done last month. Milford. Wayford, which mattered more than the rest of them put together because everything went through Wayford. Grayrock. Half a dozen smaller holdings that did not appear on a crown map at all.

Friendly terms with most sellers, including the greedy ones, which he said without any particular disapproval. A greedy seller is the easiest kind to stay friendly with, provided you never cheat him and never expect him to be anything else.

The first turn came back faster than anybody in Kaldo had expected.

The soap went out in the last week of August and the money was back before the end of September, and the payroll crisis resolved itself quietly in a small room with three people in it, and nobody on the Works ever learned how close it had come or that it had come close at all.

Emeric took the barony's cut and put none of it in a strongbox.

It went into the shop expansion, the kiln, and the first real orders Kaldo had placed anywhere in eleven years.

Lard from Milford, because soap at four thousand bars a month had outrun what Kaldo's own butchers and fishing boats could render, and Dunmore was pleasant about it and charged correctly again.

And ore from Grayrock, against the day the barony's own diggings thinned.

Saban brought back the quote in October. Ferra checked it against what Wexler had paid for eleven years, which she had, because she had everything, and it was higher.

Not much. Inside the range that a bad season at the workings would explain without anybody having to invent a reason.

Emeric paid it without haggling. He could afford it, and he said so out loud, and he said the rest of it out loud too: that he wanted the relationship a great deal more than he wanted the difference, and that the first order was not the place to save nine silver.

It was a perfectly good reason. Ferra recorded the variance in a column and did not raise it.

The report came at the end of the month and it was the thing Emeric had not asked for.

People were asking about the orders. Not aggressively. Nobody had turned up anywhere and demanded anything. They were asking in the ordinary way men in trade ask about anything unusual, which Saban said was the dangerous way, because nobody remembers being asked.

Large convoys going into a barony on the southern edge of the map. Full.

And coming out full.

That was the part that made no sense to anybody watching. A place buys or it sells. A poor place buys a little and sells a little and the carts are half empty in both directions. Kaldo was doing both, at volume, in the same month, and a year ago it had been doing neither.

Two of the questions had been about who was buying.

One had been about who was paying.

Saban gave his professional opinion flatly, because he was not a man who dressed things up, and he gave it standing in the doorway with his hat already on.

Somebody would come and look. He did not know who and he did not think it was soon. But a town that goes from nothing to this inside a year gets looked at, by somebody, eventually, and the looking had started.

Emeric thanked him, and filed it, and did not sleep especially well.
