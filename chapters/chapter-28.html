Chapter 28 - The Medical Ward

The granary had been gone for two hours before anybody on the wall knew it was burning.

Four men did it. They came in over the western stretch, which Ward had said twice was a stretch he could not hold and which had been held all day by three men and two unfinished harnesses, and they went to a building forty paces from the mill that they did not need to look for, because two of them had carted grain into it for Wexler for six years before Emeric turned them out.

They did not fight anyone. They put fire into a thatch roof over four hundred tons of dry grain in the last week of August and then they went back over the western stretch, and one of them was taken on the mill road that night and three of them were not.

The two men who had been on the granary since the rise were on it when it went up.

They got the doors open and they got eleven sacks out, and then the thatch came in and they went out through the back of it and were pulled out of the yard by a miller and his son, and both of them were alive at nightfall and both of them were burned from the shoulder down on one side.

"They'll keep their arms," Imani said, at eleven that night, in the church, without being asked. "Both of them. It's the wrong depth to be the bad kind and I've had my hands in it and I'd tell you if I thought otherwise. They'll be six weeks of the worst pain either of them has had and then they'll be men with a bad arm that works."

"Say the number of things you'd need that you haven't got."

"Clean cloth. Then clean cloth. Then clean cloth." She did not look up. "It's not clever. Burns want covering and want the covering changed and every change is a chance to put something in there that finishes them, and I will get through more boiled linen in a fortnight than this town has owned since the plague."

The bin on the hill was untouched.

Emeric walked up to it at dawn on the second day because he could not make himself go and look at the granary yet, and stood in front of six feet by four by five of stone floor and timber walls and slate roof, forty paces up the hill and nowhere near anything that burned, and put his hand on the lid frame.

Inside it was the seed for the whole barony, at the edge of dry, on his decision, out loud, in daylight, with Dunnel's two corrected words on the page.

He had put it there because the granary was one thatch forty paces from a mill with two men on it who were not on the western wall. He had said that sentence to Dunnel at the shed door in front of Halloway and Ferra, and he had written it in his own list on the seventh night, and he had been describing the thing that then happened exactly as it happened, and it had not once occurred to him to move anything else.

Ferra found him there an hour later with the count.

"The field's harvest," she said. "The venture's grain. The barony's four parts. All of it, less eleven sacks and whatever the rain does to what's under the fall, which I will not know for three days." She had the book against her chest and did not open it. "I am not going to give you the pounds this morning. You will want them and you will not use them well today."

"Give me the households."

"Thirty-seven for their winter. Nineteen more who put grain into a page for a share of a foundry and have neither. Four hundred people who came here in the spring on Imani's letters and on yours and who have nothing behind them but a barony that has just spent everything twice." She said it evenly. "And no dead. Not one, on that wall, all day. I want those two facts said in the same breath by somebody, and it is going to have to be me, because nobody else in this town will put them next to each other for a year."

What was on the approach was worse than the granary and took him until the middle of the second day to understand properly.

A hundred and forty-nine dead on the graded slope, most of them off a mining hill, none of them buried. Two hundred and twelve wounded taken up into the town in the night and the morning, carried by their own uninjured, who had been left behind when Grayrock broke and had spent the dark hours on the slope not knowing whether the wall was going to come out.

It was the last week of August and it did not rain.

Imani said it on the graded approach at noon on the second day, standing in the middle of it with her sleeves rolled, and she said it without any pleasure at all.

"Look where they are."

"I know where they are."

"Say it out loud, because I have been standing here for an hour telling myself I have it wrong." She pointed down the slope, past the whitewashed stones at a hundred and twenty paces, past the fence line. "It falls away from your joint. It falls all the way to the low ground and the low ground goes to the north well, and there are three hundred and sixty men lying on it in this heat, and about a third of them are opened up, and every one of them that dies stays where he is until somebody moves him."

"Uphill," Emeric said. "And upriver."

"Uphill and upriver of the water you spent your first season fixing." She turned round. "That is the plague. That is not like the plague. That is the same thing, in the same place, from the same direction, and you built the ground it is lying on."

He had written it in his own list on the last night before the assault. He had written it because he had noticed it while siting the stones and had had nowhere else to put a killing ground, and he had written it so that nobody including himself got to be surprised, and he had been surprised anyway, because writing a thing down is not the same as believing it will arrive.

"Then the burying isn't a funeral," he said.

"The burying is the same job as the wells, and I want it run like the wells and not like a kindness." Imani was already walking. "Below the mill. Downstream of everything. Deep, and quick, and today, and I want the men doing it washing before they eat and I want somebody standing there to make them."

Corliss ran it. Nobody appointed him to it and nobody needed to.

He had bought boxes and ground and digging out of a provisioning column for sixteen years, and he had a line in the book with his name against it now, and he took thirty men and two carts down to the new ground below the mill and put a hundred and forty-nine men from Grayrock into it in two days with his arm still not right, and he wrote down every one that had anything on him to write down.

Doran had the pews stacked against the south wall.

He had done it the day the levy was called, without telling anybody, and Emeric had asked for the floor on the tenth day and been given it before he finished the sentence, and by the time the first wounded came up from the slope there was a stone floor two hundred feet long with nothing on it, water heating in the yard, and a table of clean linen at one end and a bin for the used at the other with four paces between them, which Imani had insisted on and which Emeric had thought was fussy for about half a day.

Two hundred and twelve men on that floor, and nine beds in the whole barony, and the rest of them on straw.

Imani asked for the delve on the second night.

She did it in the vestry with the door shut and Ferra present, because she had asked for Ferra by name, which was how Emeric knew what was coming before she said it.

"There's a man on the north side of that floor with a thigh bone in two pieces and the ends of it a hand apart," she said. "I can set it. I have set nine of them in my life and four of them came right. The four that came right were the four where the man was drunk enough or far enough gone not to fight me, because setting a femur means pulling a leg straight against every muscle a frightened man has, and a man who is awake will not let you, and he cannot help it, and it is not cowardice."

"And the other five."

"Walk badly. Two of them can't work." She kept going. "There are eleven more like him on that floor and there are about forty that want closing properly and cannot be closed properly by a woman working fast on a man who is moving. So. In July you opened Corliss's arm to the bone in a place I cannot see and cannot follow you into, and I made you tell me the mechanism and you told me, and then Ferra wrote a line down and dated it."

"Nothing alive goes in there."

"In her hand," Imani said. "Which I understood at the time and understand now. And I am asking you tonight to take it back, and I would like to know what it costs before I ask again, because I am not going to ask you to break your own rule without knowing what I am asking."

Emeric was quiet for long enough that Ferra opened the book.

"It doesn't numb the leg," he said. "That's the first thing and it's the thing everybody gets wrong, including me for about a month. It doesn't switch a thing off out here. Where I go, a man feels what he believes he feels, and that runs in both directions, which is why Bram burned his hand on a furnace that wasn't hot and why Corliss's arm opened on a blade that doesn't exist." He put his hands flat on the vestry table. "The other direction works the same way. He can stop feeling a thing he knows about. It isn't me doing something to his leg. It's his own head withholding a thing it already knows how to make, and it works on a man who lets it and does nothing at all against a man who braces."

"Then it's consent."

"It is not consent and that's the part I want in the book tonight." He looked at Ferra until she was writing. "It's the absence of a braced line. A man who agrees is unbraced. A man who is screaming with a broken femur is unbraced. A man raving is unbraced, and a child is unbraced, and a prisoner tied to a post who has given up is unbraced, and there is nothing in what I can do that tells the difference between the first of those and the last of them. Write that. Write that I said it in a vestry with two witnesses on the second night, before I did any of it, so that nobody in ten years gets to find it out about me."

"And what stops you," Ferra said.

"Nothing stops me. That's what I've just said." He heard how it sounded and did not soften it. "So write the line the other way round. Not what I won't do. What I'll be asked for and by whom. It goes on a patient who has asked, or on a patient Imani has decided for, and Imani signs it and I don't, and if it is ever done on a man who has not asked and is not being treated then it was done by me on my own authority and the book says so in advance."

Ferra wrote it. She read it back twice, once flat and once slowly, and then turned the book round.

Imani signed it.

Emeric did not, and had to be asked twice not to, and afterward wrote in his own list that he had wanted his name on it very badly and that he had wanted it for reasons that would not survive being written down.

The femur took nine minutes.

The man was a Grayrock carter, thirty-odd, off the sorting floor, who had been on the slope for eleven hours. Imani told him what she was going to do in words with nothing hidden in them, and told him what Emeric was going to do in words that were as close to true as she could get without a mechanism she did not have, and asked him, and he said yes because he had been listening to himself for eleven hours.

Emeric went in and got hold of the thing the man knew about his own leg, and the man stopped knowing about it.

Then Imani pulled the leg straight, with two men on the shoulders, against a body that did not fight her, and the ends of the bone came past each other and seated, and she splinted it, and it took nine minutes and the man lay there through all of it with his eyes open.

He asked, twice, in the middle, whether she had started.

Emeric came out of it and had to sit down on the vestry step, and Imani let him sit for four minutes and then said, "How many of those have you got in you."

"Tonight? Nine. Ten."

"Out of two hundred and twelve."

"Out of two hundred and twelve." He had his elbows on his knees. "It's not the skill. The skill costs nothing, it's a thing I can do with my hands full. It's that I have to hold it the whole time and there is a bucket and the bucket has a bottom, and I have about a tenth of what my brother has and everybody in the kingdom has known that about me since I was nine, and it turns out that the number they were laughing at is how many men I can hold still in a night."

"Then you're the shortage."

"I'm the shortage."

"Which is the shed," Imani said.

He looked up at her.

"Your shed with the warm floor that couldn't stop, that wanted a man standing in it with his fist in the grain," she said. "You drew a stone and a floor and a roof and a man and you didn't notice putting the man in, and you told me that in June and you were pleased with yourself about having noticed. You have just built the same thing out of yourself."

So he taught her to sew.

That was the second night and the whole of the third day and most of the fourth, and it was not what he had expected it to be, because he had expected it to be difficult.

She could already sew. She had been closing wounds for nineteen years with what she had, which was a needle, a thread boiled because he had spent a season making her boil things, and no way at all to hold a man still. What she did not have was any of the rest of it, and the rest of it was not magic and did not require him and could be written down.

Cut away what is dead before you close anything, because dead flesh left under a stitch is a wound that shuts on top of its own rot. Do not close a wound that has been open on a slope for eleven hours in August; pack it, and leave it, and close it in four days if it is clean. Bring the edges together without dragging them, because a stitch under tension cuts its own way out and takes the closure with it. Close in layers, deep first. Leave a way out at the low corner for anything that wants to come out, because something always does.

Imani made him say the reason for every one of those and would not write down any of them until he had.

"Cut away what's dead," she said, on the third morning, with a needle in her hand and a Wayford man's forearm open in front of her. "Why."

"Because whatever kills people in a wound lives better in dead flesh than in live, and a closed wound is a warm dark room, and you've just given it one with the door shut."

"And you know that how."

"The same way I know about the wells." He did not look away from it. "And you'll want to ask me the rest of that question one day and I'd rather it wasn't this week."

"I've a hundred and eighty men on that floor," Imani said. "It isn't this week."

By the fifth day she was better at it than he was, which he had expected on about the second, and which he wrote down anyway.

He lost one.

A Grayrock miner, twenty-two, with a bolt low in the belly that had gone in through quilt and come to rest somewhere it should not have been possible to reach, and he had been on the floor for three days and had not died and had not got better.

Emeric opened him.

He did it because pain had stopped being the barrier, and because he had a room full of men who had stopped screaming when he held them, and because for four days everything he had tried had worked. He knew the whole of that at the time. He wrote it down that night in exactly those words, so that he could not later have it as a thing he had discovered afterward.

The boy did not feel it. He was awake for the first while and talked about a sister in a village Emeric had never heard of, and Emeric held him and Imani opened the abdominal wall in layers the way she had been taught four days earlier, and there was nothing wrong with any of it as work.

He bled inside, and going in did not stop it, and it turned out that the bolt had been the only thing standing in the way of it for three days.

His pressure went out from under him over about twenty minutes and he was calm the whole time and asked Imani twice whether it was going well, and she told him the truth the second time, and he said all right, and died about a quarter of an hour later without any pain at all.

Nobody in that church knew it was going wrong until it was most of the way through. That was the thing Emeric could not put down afterward. A man being cut into tells you where you are with him, and the only reason anybody had ever known when to stop was that the patient screamed, and he had taken that away in order to be able to start.

"He'd have died in the straw," Imani said, afterward, in the yard.

"He'd have died in the straw in four days in a great deal of pain and I'd have written nothing down about it."

"Yes." She was washing her hands, and kept washing them. "And I want it noted that I would not have opened him, and that I said so, and that I could not tell you why beyond nineteen years of watching bellies, and that I did not push it because you were sure."

"Then push it next time."

"I'm going to."

What to do with three hundred and forty living Grayrock and Wayford men was the problem with no clean answer, and it took him until the sixth day to accept that there was only one thing on the list.

He could not feed them. There was no granary. There was eleven sacks and a seed bin he would not open and a barony that had just lost thirty-seven households' winter, and three hundred and forty extra men eat as much in a fortnight as four hundred acres yields.

He could not hold them, because holding them meant feeding them and guarding them with forty men who had not slept.

He could not march them anywhere, because marching two hundred wounded men three days up the Wayford road in August kills a third of them and does it in front of witnesses.

He could not turn them loose armed, and he could not turn the sound ones loose and keep the hurt ones, because the sound ones were the only means of carrying the hurt ones.

So he sent them home.

All of them, on the sixth and seventh and eighth days, in parties of forty, walking, unarmed, with their own uninjured carrying the ones who could not walk, with bread out of the eleven sacks for two days on the road, and every one of them closed and splinted and dressed by Imani or by one of the four women she had spent the week teaching.

Ward argued about the armour for an hour and about the men for about ninety seconds.

"The plate stays," he said. "Every harness, every mail shirt, every helm, and the arms, and I'll not hear about it."

"The plate stays and goes in the furnace as feedstock. I've never once considered otherwise."

"Then we're agreed and I've nothing else." Ward looked out at the road, where forty men were forming up in the dust with two of their own on a hurdle. "You'll want somebody to tell you this is generous."

"It isn't generous. I've run out of options in a specific order and this is the last one on the sheet."

"Aye. And in about a fortnight there'll be two hundred houses on that hill with a son in them who walked home stitched," Ward said. "You didn't buy that. Buying it wasn't available to you. But you've got it, and I'd not throw it away by explaining to anybody how it happened."

Emeric got the list out on the eighth night, in a castle where people were finally sleeping, and left the space empty at the top, and wrote underneath it:

No dead on the wall and everything under the thatch gone. Ferra says nobody in this town will put those two facts next to each other for a year and she is right, and I am going to have to be the one who does it out loud in November.

I described the granary exactly as it burned. I said it at the shed door on the seventh day and wrote it that night, two men on one thatch off a stretch Ward cannot hold, and I moved the seed and I moved nothing else. I did not fail to see it. I saw it, wrote it, and did not act, and I have no name for that yet and would like one.

Three hundred and sixty men lying uphill and upriver of the north well, on ground I cleared and graded to a hundred and twenty paces. I put that in the list on the last night so that nobody including me would be surprised. I was surprised. Writing a thing down is not the same as believing it.

Ferra has the line in the book and Imani signed it and I did not. I wanted my name on it and the reasons I wanted it would not survive this page. What I can do does not know the difference between a man who agrees and a man who has stopped being able to refuse, and there is nothing on my side of it that will ever tell me. There is only who is allowed to ask.

I am the shortage. Nine men a night out of two hundred and twelve, on the number the court has been laughing at since I was nine. Imani called it the shed and she is exactly right and I did not see it and I had seen it before, in June, about a floor and a stone and a man with his fist in the grain.

I opened a belly on the fourth day because pain had stopped being the barrier and four days of everything working had made me stupid. He felt nothing and he died anyway, and nobody in the room knew it was going wrong until it was nearly done, because the only warning anybody has ever had is the one I had just taken away. Imani said she would not have opened him and could not say why and did not push it because I was sure. She will push it next time. I have asked her to and I do not know whether I will let her.
