Chapter 28 - Three Farmers

There was no iron for plows. There was iron on Grayrock's barges, at Grayrock's price, and there was the iron in Kaldo that nobody was using, and Ferra had already told him which of those two he could afford.

So Bram went through the town's dead things.

The cooperage on the lane below the dyehouse had been shut since the plague took the cooper and both his sons, and in its back shed, under a fall of rotten staves, there were bundles of hoop iron that had been bought for barrels nobody had made: long flat strips, rusted at the edges and sound in the middle, tied in dozens with wire. From the houses the barony held for tithe there were fire grates, heavy and black, a few of them burnt through, which Bram set aside with a look of personal disappointment, and the rest, which he did not.

Ferra counted every piece in and every piece out. She stood in the forge doorway with her book, because Bram would not have her nearer the fire with it, and wrote down the weight of each bundle as Tess called it off the scale.

Out of it, over a week of his evenings, Bram made four plowshares, nine scythe blades and a dozen hoe heads, and said that was what there was. Emeric asked about the grates in the empty houses on the north side. Bram said they had been counted, and were in the scythes.

The plow itself came off a page. Emeric had seen it once in the agronomy text, a photograph of an old one in a museum, with a curved board behind the share that took the slice of earth the share cut and turned it over. He drew the curve as well as he could remember it. The carpenter looked at the drawing, took it away, and came back two days later with a board of seasoned ash cut to a slightly different curve, which he said would turn the soil instead of shoving it. Bram faced it with strips of hoop iron, riveted flat, so it would scour clean instead of wearing through.

"Where's this from?" the carpenter asked, running his thumb down the iron.

"A book."

"Book's wrong about the curve."

"Yes," said Emeric, who had already seen that it was.



They tried it first on the baron's farm, because the baron's farm was his now and was allowed to fail.

It was forty-odd acres below the west road, worked for Wexler by a reeve who had worked it for Wexler's father and regarded every change to it since as a personal remark. He had brought Ferra his books in October. He brought the oxen now, with the same expression, and stood at the headland with his arms folded while Farris, who had come up the road to see, took the handles because the reeve would not.

The share went in. The oxen leaned. The board took the slice of earth the share lifted and rolled it over, all of a piece, and laid it down upside down beside the last, and the ground behind the plow turned over in one long dark ribbon, like a page being turned.

Farris stopped the team at the far end and looked back down the furrow, and then at the sky, and then at the furrow again.

"It doesn't scratch," he said.

The old plows in Kaldo were frames with a point, and they scratched: they opened a line in the ground and left the soil on both sides of it, and the weeds with it, and you crossed the field again the other way to break what you had missed. This buried the weeds under the slice it turned, and left a clean bed behind it.

Farris ran it the length of the field and back twice more before he would give the handles up. On the way back to the road he said, without looking at anybody, that if it worked on a strip it would work on the rest of the field, and he would rather find out this year than in three.

"You said one strip," Emeric said. "To start."

"I've started." Farris looked at the sky. "That's me started."

Clover seed was another matter. Nobody on the west side had any, because nobody on the west side had ever sown clover on purpose, and the rotation wanted it under the barley in spring. Farris knew a man in Milford who grew it, a day west, and sent to him by a carter that same week without asking anybody's leave, which Emeric heard about from Ferra when the carter's bill came in.

Hollis took the plow to his strip and no further. He ran it down the strip with Farris's oxen, borrowed for the morning, and then took his old plow down the strip beside it, so that anybody stopping at his hedge could see the two of them lying side by side, and judge.

Geddes did not take it anywhere. He came up the lane most evenings that week and stood at his own hedge, a little back from it, and watched the other two fields being turned. Once Emeric saw him take something small out of his pocket and turn it over in his fingers while he watched, and put it back.



Farris had his first school morning in the middle of that week, because Sela had put him on the wall in chalk for it and he could not think of a way to argue with chalk.

He did not come to the exchange. He made the children come to him, a dozen of them, out along the west road to the edge of his field, and when they were all there he picked up a fistful of the turned earth and squeezed it, and opened his hand. It had held together in a lump. He broke it with his thumb, and it crumbled.

"That's ready," he said. Then he went to the wet corner by the ditch, scooped another fistful, squeezed it, and opened his hand on a lump that stayed a lump, shiny where his fingers had been. "That's not. You put seed in that, you're feeding it to the ground."

That was the whole lesson. He made every one of them squeeze a fist of each until they could tell the two apart with their eyes shut, and then he sent them home.

Emeric, who had stood at the back of it with Rylan and had squeezed his own fistful when Farris looked at him, found that he could tell them apart as well. The book had said soil should be friable. It had not said what friable felt like.



The seed drill was for spring, and it nearly did not get there.

Emeric had drawn it in the evenings: a frame on two wheels, a box for seed across the top, and under the box a row of spouts, each with a small wooden coulter in front of it to open a slot in the ground, so that seed went in at a depth and in a line instead of being flung by the handful and eaten half by the birds. Bram built the frame from the drawing, true to it, and they wheeled it out to the yard behind the forge to try it empty.

It bound. It ran three yards over the cobbles and locked, and ran again when Bram rocked it, and locked again a yard further on.

Bram took the wheels off and put them back. Emeric checked his drawing, which agreed with itself in every particular. They wheeled it again. It bound.

Tess had been watching from the forge door. She came over, got down on her back on the cobbles, and slid underneath the frame, and lay there while Bram pushed it a yard and pulled it back.

"It twists," she said, from underneath. "Every time a wheel goes over something. The whole frame goes out of square, and the axle rubs the cross-piece. There." She put her finger on a bright patch on the underside, where the wood had been polished by something it was not supposed to touch. "It wants a brace. Corner to corner."

Emeric looked at his drawing.

He had drawn it flat. Every line on that sheet had been true, on the sheet, and the sheet had never been made to go over a cobble. A frame on four corners with nothing across the diagonals would rack on the first bump in the road and go on racking until something rubbed through, and he had known that for longer than he had known Tess's name, and had not drawn it.

Bram fitted the brace that afternoon. The drill ran the length of the yard and back and did not bind.

Tess got up off the cobbles and dusted her back off and went back to the bellows, as though she had been sent to fetch something and had fetched it.



The field tap came last, and the mason made it.

The aqueduct ran over Farris's field on its arches thirty feet up, as it ran over the whole of the west side, and since the trough had gone across the gap there was water in the channel overhead again, going past on its way to the town. The mason's plan was simple, and he explained it in one breath, which for him was a speech: a hole in the channel floor above a pier, a plug in it, a spout down the pier to a trough at the bottom, and from the trough a channel along the head of the field.

Emeric agreed to all of it. Then, before a single stone was cut, he asked where it went.

Nobody had asked him to ask. He heard himself do it and was faintly surprised, and went on anyway. Water let onto a field had to go somewhere when the field had drunk what it wanted, and the somewhere was below the field, and below this field was a ditch, and the ditch went somewhere after that.

So he walked it. He walked Farris's drain from the bottom of the field, along its ditch and the next ditch it ran into, across a lane and through a hedge and down past the last farm on the west side, all the way to the river, below every well and every intake in the barony, with Rylan behind him asking at every hedge whether this was strictly necessary.

It was clean all the way. He walked back up and told the mason to cut the hole.

Hollis watched the first water come down the spout into the trough, and along the head channel, and out into the furrows of Farris's field, and said, "Kaldo grew grain for four hundred years without that."

"It did," Emeric said. "Most years the rain does it. This is for the years it doesn't."

Hollis considered that and seemed to find it more honest than he had expected.

It had been a dry autumn. The man at the next hedge began carrying a bucket from the trough every evening the week the water came down the pier. Farris did not. He let the plug out once, at first light, long and slow, until the ground at the bottom of his field was dark, then shut it, and did not open it again. When Emeric asked him why not a little every evening, he looked at the sky and said that a crop watered a little every day grew its roots in the top of the ground, where the water was, and fell over in the first wind. Emeric, who had read the same thing on a page, said nothing.



The winter wheat went in during the second week of November, behind the new plow on Farris's whole field and on Hollis's one strip, from seed Hollis had picked himself, three paces in from the edge and never from a wet corner. The old way went in beside it on Hollis's other strip, and on every other strip on the west side, as it always had.

Ward stood at the field edge on the last afternoon of the sowing, watching the sowers go down the field with their bags.

"The farm is in," he said.

"Most of it."

"The garrison will not keep forever."

"After winter."

"You said that in October."

"It's still true."

Ward looked at him, and then away up the road toward the town, where forty-one men who answered to nobody were spending another afternoon doing it.

When the light began to go, Emeric walked down the lane to Geddes's hedge and stood a little along it from him, and the two of them watched it go off Farris's field without saying anything. Rooks came down onto the new ground in a crowd, and a boy went out with a rattle and put them up again.

"It'll come up or it won't," Geddes said eventually. "I'll see which."

Then he went in.

Later that night Ferra turned her forge book round on the table so he could read it.

The hoops were on it, bundle by bundle, and the grates by the piece, and against them the shares and the scythes and the hoe heads, and the weight that had gone into each. Below that, in a short column of its own, was what was left: enough scrap for the drill's irons and the spring's repairs, and not much more. There were no more cooperages in Kaldo.

At the foot of the page she had written what the next lot of iron would cost, at the price on the last bill of lading, if it came down the river on a Grayrock barge.

It was a Grayrock number. She had underlined it once, so that he would know she knew it too.
