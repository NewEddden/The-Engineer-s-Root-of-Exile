Chapter 350 - Gold Roses

By the morning of the fourth of October the wedding had cost four hundred and six shillings, and Ferra could account for every one of them except the cake.

She went through it in the dark, on her back in her old bed under the slope of the forge loft roof, because it was a quarter to five and there was nothing else to do with her hands. Abloh's bill, which he had tried to tear up in front of her and which she had made him write out again, fair, with the hours. The gold for the rings, a little under half an ounce, bought at the House's rate through Vantic's clerk and entered on the day with his receipt pinned to the page. The chapel, nothing, because Doran had said that if she tried to pay him he would marry somebody else that morning instead. Tamsy's cups, on loan, breakages at cost. The cake had been ordered by Maren, and Maren had said don't you worry about the cake, and Ferra had been worrying about the cake for nine days.

Imani was asleep across the loft in Tess's old bed, on her front, with one arm hanging down to the boards and her locs spread over the pillow and off it. When Ferra had told her that a Kaldo bride slept her last night under her father's roof, Imani had said she wasn't sleeping hers under the castle's with him either, and that if one of them was going to lie awake looking at a ceiling it might as well be the same ceiling. She had then slept nine hours without moving. Tess was on a pallet between the beds, because it had been her loft too, and she had said she wasn't missing it for a husband she wasn't even having.

The column was closed. Ferra had closed it on the twenty-third, the morning after the Stone Board, by walking down to the chapel at eight before she could find a reason not to. Doran had been in the bicycle shed again, on his knees with a tyre lever, and she had stood in the shed door and said the whole of it at once, the date and the three names and the canon, quickly, the way you take out a splinter. He had said yes before she got to the canon.

"The canon says one," she had said anyway, because she had prepared it.

"The canon says a great many things." He had sat back on his heels with the lever in his hand. "A man who doesn't believe one word of it once told me to keep my faith. I kept it. It's mine now, not Ostrel's. I'll read the words the long way round, that's all." He had looked at the tyre. "If Ostrel wants to ask me about it they'll have to say it out loud. They won't."

Rowena had been a letter on the evening packet, and her answer had come back by air two days later in her own hand, one line on a sheet of Emberfell paper: Of course. Both of you. I'll fly in the night before.

And they had told his mother on the twenty-fourth, the three of them, by the tree, with Harrold on the edge of the mattress. Ferra had got as far as We wanted you to hear it before anybody when Amara said, "October. The fourth, I should think, from the way you've been holding that book against your chest," and Harrold had laughed so suddenly that he had to get up and walk round the east bed.

His mother had asked for two things. She wanted to see them afterwards, in the clothes, before anybody took anything off. And she wanted a chair.

"They'll put a chair for me anyway," she'd said. "That's what's done for the dead. Put one of my roses on it, and let the town look at it and be sorry for Harry. He's earned a little of that." She had looked at her husband, who was trying to look as though he hadn't. "Not much."

Ferra got up at five and dressed in the dark in her work skirt, because the dress was Abloh's and lived on a hook in its own bag until the last possible moment, by order. Downstairs, through the boards, she could hear that her father was already up, and that he wasn't doing anything, which was unusual enough that she went down to see.



The forge was cold. There was no coal on the hearth and nothing in the bellows, and the anvil had been swept, and Bram stood at it in his shirt sleeves with two small buttons of gold on the face of it, side by side, each about the size of a pea.

"You did them last week," Ferra said.

"Did them last week. Didn't like them." He didn't look round. "Melted them back."

"That's the gold I paid for."

"Still is. It's all there. Weigh it after if you want."

She would. He knew she would. She sat down on the bottom stair with her arms round her knees, and above her the stair creaked, and Tess came down three steps in her nightgown with a shawl over it and her hair flat on one side and sat on the step above Ferra's, with her bare feet by Ferra's hip, and didn't say good morning.

Bram picked up the first button and closed his hand on it.

Ferra felt the heat start before she saw it. Since the sleep she could feel what a metal was doing if she was near enough and quiet enough, the way you can hear a pot about to boil from the next room, and the gold in her father's fist went from cold to warm to something that wasn't solid any longer while his knuckles stayed exactly the colour they always were. The air above his hand began to shake. He opened it.

The gold lay in his palm like a drop of honey, bright, rolling a little as his hand moved, and it didn't burn him.

Then he did the other thing, the thing she had felt him practising for a month through the floor of the loft at night and had never asked about. He put the tip of his thumb to the drop and drew it up, and the gold came up after his thumb in a thread, and the thread bent round on itself in the air above his palm and closed into a ring, not touching his skin anywhere, turning slowly. He looked at it with his head on one side as he looked at any piece of work, and moved his thumb a little, and on the ring's face, where it would sit on the back of a finger, the gold gathered and folded and opened into a rose the size of a lentil, with its leaves.

Ferra felt every fold. It was like reading a sum in somebody else's hand and finding it right all the way down.

"Show-off," said Tess, from the stair.

"Hm," said Bram, which in the forge meant yes.

He let the ring down into his palm. It sat there, cooling, the colour coming up in it from red to gold as the heat went, and he set it on the anvil and picked up the second button and did it again, the drop and the thread and the rose, a little faster, because the hands remembered. The second rose faced the other way. Ferra saw it and didn't say anything, and he saw her see it.

"Mirrors," he said. "One for each side of him."

Tess put her chin on her knees.

"My da made my mam's ring out of a horseshoe nail," she said. "Here. At that door. He bent it round her finger with her hand on the anvil, and she said afterwards she nearly ran."

"She did run," said Bram. "He caught her at the gate."

"That's not true."

"It's mostly true." He turned the second ring over with one finger, to look at the inside. "She came back for her shawl. He was stood there with the nail. She said if she was coming back she might as well have the ring."

Tess thought about that. Her mouth went sideways.

"I want it written somewhere," she said, "that mine's not a nail."

"Nobody's said you're having one," said Bram.

"Nobody's said I'm not."

Ferra did not look round at her sister, because if she did she would ask about Osric, and it was half past five in the morning and her own wedding day, and Tess would never forgive her for either of those things. She looked at the rings on the anvil instead. They were still warm. She could feel them from the stair.

Bram picked them up and came across the forge floor and stood over her, and put them both into her hand and folded her fingers over them with his, which were warm too, all the way through, as if he'd been holding a mug.

"They'll stay warm all day," he said. "I put a bit in."

"A bit of what?"

"Fire." He took his hand away. He stood there in his shirt sleeves in the cold forge and looked down at her with the face he had worn at every important moment of her life, the one that looked as though he was working out what something weighed, and she waited for him to say whatever it was.

"Right," said Bram, and went upstairs to put on the suit Abloh had made him, which he had told Abloh was very good and told her he would sooner be buried in.



Imani was at the loft's one glass with a twist of paper open on the sill.

The earrings were small, two plain gold studs, and Ferra had seen them exactly once in all the years she had known her, at the bottom of the satchel, when Imani turned it out on a ward table looking for a probe. A healer who washed to the elbow in lye and spirit twelve times a day did not wear gold in her ears. Imani had said so then, and put them back.

The left one went in. The right one wouldn't, because the hole had closed over somewhere in the last few years of the ward, and Imani looked at it in the glass with her healer's face, the flat one, and put it in anyway, with a small sound through her nose.

"That's bleeding," said Ferra.

"It isn't."

"I can see it from here."

"It was a dot. It's stopped." Imani turned her head to look at it sideways. Since the sleep she could see under her own skin as easily as anyone else's, and she was looking, Ferra could tell, at something an ordinary person couldn't have seen in a glass. "It's mended. I mended it."

"With the earring in."

"Round the earring. It'll come out fine. Probably." She let go of her ear. "Don't tell him."

"He'll know. He'll look at it all day."

"He'll look at it and think it's pretty," said Imani, "and not know why it's red."

Abloh came at seven with his two bags and his pins in his mouth and one of his girls behind him carrying a lamp, and threw Tess out of her own loft. Tess went as far as the stair.

The dresses were black. That had been decided in August, when Emeric had told the two of them what brides wore where he came from, in the dark, in bed, where the three of them said that kind of thing, and Imani had said that white was for the ward and she was not being married in her scrubs, and Ferra had said white showed everything, which was true. So: black, as they had worn on the cradle's deck at the capital five years ago, when the court had looked at them and not known how to finish its sentence. Abloh had kept the drawings.

Ferra's had the lattice over the bodice, gold diamonds close as a net, and the gold straps crossing it, and her shoulders bare, and the sleeves beginning below them and running tight to the wrist with a gold band at the elbow and another at the cuff. Down the skirt, scattered, gold roses. Abloh had cut it again from new, because she was not the shape she had been five years ago, and he had said so to her face at the first fitting and she had said thank you, Abloh in a voice that made his girl drop the pins.

Imani's was plain. Close in the body, high at the throat, no lace anywhere, sleeves to the wrist and cut to push up past the elbow without tearing, as before, because Imani had said that if somebody fainted in the chapel she wasn't going to stand there in a dress she couldn't kneel in. One gold rose high on the left breast, worked so close that the cloth didn't show under it. Abloh put it a thumb's width to the left of where he wanted it, without being told, and Imani looked at it in the glass and said, "Yes," and he looked prouder of that than of anything else in either bag.

When he had done, Ferra stood in the middle of the loft with her arms a little out from her sides and said, "The bands."

"They're level," said Abloh.

"They were level five years ago."

"They're level now."

Tess came up off the stair with Bram's steel rule from the shop and measured them, the elbow and the cuff, left and right, from the point of the shoulder, holding the rule along Ferra's arm with her tongue between her teeth. Abloh watched her do it with the expression of a man watching his house searched.

"Level," said Tess.

"I said."

"Now it's written." Tess put the rule in the pocket of her own dress, which she had been sent upstairs to change into and hadn't, and looked at her sister properly for the first time that morning, and went pink, and looked at the floor instead. "You look all right," she said. "Both of you. It's fine. Go on."

Bram was at the bottom of the stair in the suit. It was dark grey and fitted him, which was the problem; he was used to clothes that stood a little off him in the heat, and this one didn't, and he stood inside it like a man inside a sentence he hadn't written. He looked up at the two of them coming down.

He offered Ferra his right arm, and she took it.

Then he offered Imani his left, without a word, and Imani looked at it, and at him.

"Only for the stairs," she said, and took it, and kept it all the way down the hill.



Emeric had slept alone for the first time in fifteen months and done it badly.

The castle bed was too wide. He had lain in the exact middle of it out of habit, with nobody's knees in the backs of his and nobody's cold foot anywhere, and listened to the castle settle, and got up at three and read Tess's drive figures for the humanoid's left knee, and gone back to bed and lain in the exact middle of it again. Ward came at six with Abloh's second bag and his own face.

"I look like a waiter," said Ward.

He didn't. He looked like a big man in a very good dark suit who had been told he couldn't wear his sword in a chapel and had brought his belt anyway, under the coat, with the dead-zone stone in it, and was daring anybody to notice. The suit was the same grey as the knights' and the Crestless's and Bram's; Abloh had cut fourteen of them in a month and had told Ferra the number with no pleasure at all, because it meant his girls had done eleven.

Emeric's coat was black, long, with the gold worked down the skirts, vine and leaf and rose, the way it had been at the capital. In the waistcoat, over the heart, in the pocket Abloh had sewn shut five years before and sewn shut again on the new one, was Bram's steel. He put two fingers on it while Ward did his collar, and Ward saw him do it and said nothing at all, which from Ward was a long speech.

They walked down. It was a cold bright morning with the smell of the moat on it, and the town was out, all of it, the way the town came out for the tram's first day and the first Lantern Night and not much else. People stood on the walls of the lane. A ropewalk family had brought a bench from home and was sitting on it in a row by the chapel gate, eating. Somebody had tied gold ribbon round the lamp post at the corner, and somebody else, by the look of it, had tried to take it down again on the grounds that it wasn't the post's wedding.

Doran's chapel held a hundred and twenty if they were friends. Doran had opened both doors on the lane and propped them, and the town stood outside them ten deep, and the hundred and twenty inside were very good friends indeed.

Emeric stood at the front with Ward and looked at them because there was nothing else to do.

His father was in the front pew on the right, in plain dark blue, with an empty chair beside him on the aisle and a gold rose lying on the seat of it. The town looked at the chair as the town had been meant to, and was sorry for him, and Harrold sat beside it with his hands on his knees and his face perfectly still and the very corner of his mouth not still at all, because he had told Emeric at breakfast that he'd been practising being sorry for himself in the glass and wasn't good at it.

Behind him, Waylon, in the same dark blue, with Alina beside him in Duvrenne's colours and Duvrenne's party in a block behind her. Maeve on Waylon's other side, who had come back from the capital on the people's train again and paid for it herself, and had turned round in the pew twice already to wave at someone in the lane. The Empire's envoy two rows back, among the envoys, with his hands folded on a small book.

On the left, the six who weren't getting married, which was four: Armand in a cardigan under his coat, because he had asked Abloh whether a cardigan was permitted and Abloh had said nothing was permitted and he could do as he liked; Saban beside him, who was watching the envoy; and an empty place for Bram. The three knights in a row behind them in the grey, Rylan with Wren and Finn beside him, Declan running a finger round the inside of his collar, Osric at the end, looking at the door Tess would come in by. Behind the knights the Crestless, Arthur and Mira and Bren, in the grey without a crest, sitting up very straight because Ward was looking at them. Mira's scar ran white down her cheek into the collar. Bren had already started counting the roof beams.

Keller further back, with a leather case on his knee. Prudence Fairley at the end of a pew with her good brown on and her hands in her lap, as she sat on the board. Vantic in her House's colours, with the House's book. Tamsy, Maren, Dov, Ottoline in a hat, Sorrel, Fletcher from the deep yard, Abloh coming in late at the side with pins still in his lapel.

Sabina sat four rows back on the right, in grey, with Mabry beside her. She had looked once at the empty chair and the rose on it, when she came in, and then at Emeric, and her face had done nothing whatever, which was the most anyone had ever had from it.

Corbin was at the very back, by the side door, standing, because he had asked to stand. His watcher was beside him. Emeric could see the line of the drain anklet under his trouser cuff, and Corbin saw him see it, and lifted his chin a fraction toward the front pew, toward the empty chair, with an expression that said he knew exactly whose chair it wasn't, and thought it was well done, and was not going to say so in a church.

Rowena stood at the front on the other side of the aisle from Ward, in the green of Emberfell, with her hands folded in front of her. She had flown in at ten the night before and come up to the castle and told him his mother would have hated the coat, which he'd known she would say. Zale sat in the pew behind her, too big for it, with his knees against the back of the bench in front.

"You're doing the thing," said Ward, very low, beside him.

"What thing?"

"Counting people."

"I'm not counting."

"Hundred and eighteen," said Ward. "And a dog. Stop it."

Doran came out of the vestry.

He was in the red. Ostrel had sent it in July with the letter that had accepted him through its teeth, a cardinal's red, cut for a much taller and much younger man, and Doran had taken the sleeves up himself with a needle and thread and done it, by the look of the cuffs, at night. He came to the step below the altar and stood on it and looked at his chapel and at the crowd in his lane and at the empty chair, and Emeric saw his face change at the chair and change back.

"This is the first thing I've done in it," he said to Emeric, not loudly, plucking at the red on his own arm. "I wanted it to be a good one."

Then the lane went quiet from the gate inwards, and Emeric looked up.



Bram brought them in on both his arms.

That was the first thing, and it nearly undid Emeric before anything else could, because nobody had told him. Bram in the grey suit he hated, walking slowly, with his face set as it was set for a pour, and Ferra on his right and Imani on his left, and the lane turning to follow them in through the doors like a field turning after the wind.

Ferra in the black and the gold, with her hair down past her shoulders, which it hadn't been at the capital, and the fringe in her eyes, and the bands on her sleeves exactly level. She was looking straight at him and her mouth was very firm, the way it was when she was holding a figure in her head she didn't want to lose.

Imani with the one rose, and her locs up, wound high with a gold cord, and something in her ears.

He looked at her ears, because they were new. Small gold studs, plain. The right ear was a little pink at the lobe. She saw him see it and narrowed her eyes at him all the way down the aisle, and he had no idea what he'd done.

Bram stopped at the step. He took his left arm out of Imani's hand first, carefully, as if he were setting down something hot, and then his right out of Ferra's, and stood back, and went and sat in his place on the left beside Armand with his hands on his knees. Tess had come in behind them and slid into the pew next to Osric. She had changed her dress after all. She still had the steel rule in the pocket of it; Emeric could see the end.

Rowena came and stood behind the two women, one hand not quite touching each of their backs.

"Well," said Doran, and opened his book.

He didn't read from it straight away. He held it open on his palm and looked down at the page.

"The book has words in it for a man and a woman," he said, to the chapel. "I've read it a good many times this week, to see if there was a page I'd missed. There isn't." He looked up. "So we'll go round twice. And then once more, all together, and God can count. He's better at it than I am."

Somebody in the lane laughed. Doran let them.

He turned Emeric by the elbow, gently, the way he'd once turned the dying in his cot to the window, until he faced Ferra.

"Emeric," he said. "Will you have Ferra to be your wife, to keep her and be kept by her, in plenty and in want, in health and in sickness, and to keep faith with her until one of you is dead?"

"Yes," said Emeric.

"The book says I will."

"I will."

Ferra's mouth moved. It wasn't a smile. It was the place a smile would go when she let it, held shut.

Doran turned him again, a quarter turn, until he faced Imani. Imani held out her hand, not for his, and he knew what she wanted, and gave her his wrist, and she put two cool fingers on the inside of it in front of a hundred and eighteen people and a dog.

"Emeric," said Doran, who had sat at enough of Imani's bedsides to know her habits and didn't so much as blink, "will you have Imani to be your wife..."

He went through it all again. In plenty and in want. Until one of you is dead. Emeric listened to every word of it the second time, as if he hadn't just heard it.

"I will," he said.

Imani looked at her fingers, and then at Doran.

"True," she said, quite loudly.

The front pews went. Maeve made a noise into her glove that Emeric had last heard at the capital's high table, and Harrold put his hand over his eyes, and in the lane the people who hadn't heard asked the people who had, and it went back through the crowd and out to the gate in a long ripple of laughter, and Doran waited for all of it to come back with the patience of a man who had once waited out a plague.

Then he asked Ferra. Ferra said "I will" at once, with no gap at all, as if she'd been keeping it ready, and then, because she was Ferra, "on the terms in the charter," and in the fifth row Keller made a sound like a man stepping on a stair that wasn't there.

Then Imani. Imani said "I will" more slowly, looking at him, and her hand was still on his wrist, and she didn't take it off.

Doran closed the book on his finger.

"This part isn't in it," he said. "I wrote it on Tuesday." He looked at the two women. "There'll be one house. Two of you in it, and him, and his work, which is a fourth person and not a quiet one. Will you keep the peace between you in it, and help each other carry him when he's heavy?"

"Mostly," said Imani.

"I'll keep a record," said Ferra.

"That'll do," said Doran, "God knows it'll have to," and somewhere under the front pew there was a scuffle and a small boy came out from beneath it on his hands and knees, at Emeric's feet, with a gold rose in his fist.

He was perhaps four. He belonged to somebody in the lane and had come in under fourteen pews to get there. He looked up at all of them from the floor with the open satisfaction of a child who had found the most important thing in the room and taken it.

Emeric looked at the empty chair. The rose was gone from it.

Harrold was already out of the pew. He went down on one knee on the chapel floor in his good blue, at the boy's level, which no man who had been king of Randillia had done in a chapel in anyone's memory, and held his hand out flat, palm up.

"That's my wife's," he said.

The boy considered him. Then he put the rose in Harrold's hand, stem first, very carefully, because it was gold and he'd been told about gold, and Harrold gave him in return a copper out of his own pocket, and Mira came along the end of the pew from behind and picked the boy up under one arm like a sack of meal and carried him out of the side door past Corbin, who stood aside for her and said something to the boy on the way past that made him stop crying before he'd properly started.

Harrold laid the rose back on the chair. He straightened it. He sat down.

"Rings," said Doran.

Bram got up again and brought them. He put them into Emeric's palm, and they were warm, both of them, as if they'd been kept in somebody's pocket against the skin. Emeric looked at the roses on them, the two of them turned opposite ways, and understood it, and looked across at Bram, and Bram looked at the roof.

He put the first on Ferra's hand. It fitted. Bram had never measured either of them; he had only looked at their hands, at a thousand meals. He put the second on Imani's, which was the hand with the kettle scar on the back, pale and shiny, and the rose sat just above it, and she turned her hand over and looked at it with no expression at all and then looked at him with a great deal.

"There," said Doran. "In the sight of God and this parish and the lane, which I can't stop, you're married. All three. Whatever Ostrel thinks."

And then he stopped, because the book stopped there, and he had nothing after it.

Emeric stood between them with his hands empty. Where he came from, the man in Doran's place would have said one more line, and everyone in the chapel would have known it was coming and waited for it, and he had known that his whole life and had known since August that Doran would never say it.

He leaned in, so that only two people heard.

"Where I come from," he said, "he tells me now that I can kiss you."

"He's not going to," said Imani, under her breath.

"No."

"Then don't wait for him."

She went up on her toes and kissed him, in front of everyone, with her hand still on his wrist, and then stepped back and nodded at Ferra as though passing her the ledger, and Ferra, who had gone red to the fringe and did not stop for it, took his face in her two hands and did it properly.

The lane made a noise that he heard afterwards had been heard at the dam.



Doran's register lay open on the vestry table, the same register in which, three years earlier, Ferra had refused to put her name, because a page in it with three names and a date would have been one more paper in the Church's case. She looked at the page now. Emeric watched her look at it. Then she took up Doran's pen and signed, and the pen broke in her fingers.

It went with a small sharp crack, in half, about an inch above the nib, and ink went across the blotter. Ferra looked at the two pieces in her hand.

Since the sleep the six were stronger than their hands had learned yet, and Ferra had broken fewer things than any of them and minded every one.

"That's the diocese's pen," said Doran mildly.

"Put it on my account."

"You haven't got an account."

"I have now," said Ferra, and finished her name with the broken half, which still had a nib, pressing very lightly, and the signature came out thinner than her usual hand and perfectly legible. She wrote pen, one, the chapel's in the margin of her own book when they got back to the hall. He saw her do it.

Imani signed with Doran's other pen, holding it as if it were a scalpel. Ward signed for him. Rowena signed for both brides, in a big plain hand, and blotted it, and looked at the three names above hers and nodded at them, once, as if they'd been added correctly.

Vantic signed for the House.

She wrote Vantic, for the House Ducatis, in the hand she used for loan papers, upright and small. Then she sat with the pen above the page and looked at the line above hers, at his name, for the space of a breath, longer than a witness needs to look at what she has witnessed. She blotted it.

"The House notes," she said, "that the parties were of sound mind." She handed the pen back to Doran. "I'd query one of them."

Emeric laughed. Ward laughed. Ferra, beside him, did not; she looked at Vantic with her steady grey-green look for a little longer than the joke needed, and Vantic met it, and something passed between them that he didn't catch, and then Ferra said, "Thank you for coming," in a perfectly ordinary voice, and Vantic said it was the House's pleasure, which it presumably was.

Keller had his case open at the end of the table, with the crown's roll in it.

"The register's the Church's," he said. "This is the law's. It goes in both." He turned the roll to Ferra. "The law doesn't say how many. I read it. Mistress Ferra made sure I did." He looked at her over his spectacles. "You wrote down that it wasn't there."

"I didn't put it in," said Ferra.

"No. So I'm putting this in instead." He wrote the three names in the crown's roll, and the date, and the parish, and under them, in his verdict hand, Lawful, and turned the roll back toward himself. "There. Now if anybody wants to argue it they can come and argue it with me."

Nobody had ever wanted to argue anything with Keller twice.



The feast was in the castle hall with the great doors open on the inner ward, and the inner ward full of trestles, and the trestles full of the town. Ferra had done the seating. She had done it on four sheets with a key in two colours, and it held for almost an hour.

The coffee came out with the first course, which was not when coffee came, and that was Tamsy.

She came along the high table herself with a tray, past the envoys and the knights and the Stewards, and set a cup down in front of Emeric, and stood back with her hands under her apron.

"Milford," she said. "The glasshouse. First picking." She looked at the cup, not at him. "Two pounds of beans off eleven bushes in four years, and they had to be told twice to flower. Maren roasted them. I ground them. You're not to have touched it."

"I didn't touch it."

"I know. I watched."

He drank it. Ferra and Imani, on either side of him, had both stopped eating to watch him do it, and on the other side of Ferra, his father had stopped too.

It was good. It was a little thin, and bright at the back, and it was the first cup of coffee he had ever drunk that had grown in this ground, and it was better than any he had made in fourteen years of trying, on the ward's kettle or anywhere else. He put the cup down.

"Say it," said Imani.

"It's better than mine."

"Everything's better than yours," said Ferra. "That's not the sentence. Say the sentence."

"It's very good, Tamsy."

Tamsy nodded once, the nod she gave a customer who'd paid, and went back down the table with the tray, pouring for the envoys, who didn't know what they were drinking and were too polite to ask.

Prudence Fairley, three places down, had a cup and a slice of something and a question.

"Who's paying for all this?" she said to Ferra, across the knights.

"I am," said Ferra. "Out of my own column. Not the crown's."

Prudence looked down the length of the hall at the trestles in the ward, and the cake coming in through the far doors on a board between four of Maren's girls, which was the size of a cart wheel and had, Emeric saw, a crown on it made of sugar and two smaller crowns on either side, which nobody had asked for.

"Good," said Prudence, and took a second slice of the thing on her plate. "Then I'll not feel bad."

Ferra was looking at the cake.

"Maren," she said, not loudly.

"It's a gift," said Imani. "She told me. From the kitchen. Don't you dare cost it."

"I wasn't going to cost it."

"You've costed it. You costed it while it came through the door. I watched your lips."

"Nine shillings," said Ferra, "at the least," and put her face down into her hand, and Emeric felt her shaking beside him and didn't know until she lifted her face that she was laughing.

Armand gave the toast, because Saban had refused and Ward had said he'd sooner fight the envoy. Armand stood up in his cardigan and looked down the hall at the town with the pleased, alarmed face of a man who had lectured for fifty years and never once to a room that was eating.

"I was at a great many weddings in the capital," he said. "In the tower. Hundreds. I was asked to most of them because I was old and somebody's tutor, and I sat at the back and was given the wine nobody else would drink." He lifted his cup, which was coffee. "I've never been at one where I knew the bride's sums would come out right. Either bride's. I don't think the groom's ever have." He thought about it. "That's all. I'm told that's enough. To the three of them."

The hall drank. In the ward somebody started the toast again, late, and the trestles took it up, and it went round the walls twice.

Bren, at the end of the knights' table, had finished counting the roof beams in the chapel and started on the hall's. Ward reached along the table without looking and knocked on the wood by Bren's plate with one knuckle, twice, and Bren stopped, and then, after a decent interval, began again with the windows.



Waylon stood up after the cake, as he had said he would, and as Alina had told him he would.

He was not a man for standing up. Emeric had watched him stand up to speak in front of people perhaps four times since he'd come south, and every time he looked as though he were dismounting from a horse he didn't trust. He stood now with one hand flat on the table, in his duke's dark blue, and the hall saw him and went quiet more slowly than it had for Armand, because it didn't know yet that it ought to.

"Alina and I," said Waylon, "are going to be married."

He sat down.

The hall waited for the rest, and there was no rest, and Alina, beside him, looked at the side of his face with an expression that was half despair and half something else entirely, and stood up herself.

"Not soon," she said. "When it can be done properly. In Kesrath, and then again here, so that nobody on either side can say they weren't asked. My father knows. He's very pleased." She looked down the high table at the Duvrenne party, who were not surprised, and then further, at the envoys. "I thought everyone should hear it on the same day. So nobody has to hear it from anybody else."

She sat. The hall stood up for her, which it had not done for Waylon, and Waylon didn't seem to mind; he gave her the short sideways look he gave a horse that had cleared a gate, and drank his coffee.

The Empire's envoy had been sitting four places down from Duvrenne's, with his small book on the cloth beside his plate. He did not write in it. He sat and looked at Waylon, and at Alina, and at Harrold, and then, longest, at Emeric, as though he were adding a column he'd thought was finished and finding a figure in it he hadn't been given. Then he rose, and came up the table, and bowed to Emeric, gracefully, exactly as low as he had at the crowning.

"Your Majesty will forgive me," he said. "A long road, and an early start."

"Of course."

"My felicitations to the queens. Both." He bowed to Ferra and to Imani. "And to His Grace of Aldane." He bowed to Waylon, who looked up, surprised to be bowed to by anybody. "A happy day for Randillia. And for Duvrenne."

He went out through the side door with his secretary behind him, before the dancing, before the second pot of coffee, and the hall barely noticed.

Saban noticed. He had moved, at some point during the cake, to the end of the high table nearest the door, and he leaned back now in his chair and watched the door close.

"He'll write tonight," he said.

"He's writing now," said Ferra, not looking round. "Did you see his hand on that book? He was writing in his head the whole time she was talking." She took a sip of coffee. "A treaty they could live with. They signed it themselves. They can read a treaty, and buy it, and in ten years tear it up. A marriage they can't do anything with. Two of the crowns they hold on paper are a family now, and the king of one of them already said no to their gold." She put the cup down. "He's not angry. He's frightened. That's why he was so polite."

Saban looked at her with respect and some irritation, because it had been his sentence.



The dancing was in the ward, under lamps on poles, to a fiddle and a drum and Noll Finch on something that he said was a pipe.

Emeric danced with Ferra, badly, and with Imani, worse, and with Maeve, who led, and then he was taken away from all of them by the town, one after another, women from the ropewalk and the dye house and the ward, Prudence Fairley for exactly one turn and no more, a girl of about seven who stood on his boots for the whole of it. Rylan danced with Wren, who was too old to want to and told him so for the whole of it ("Everyone's looking." "They're looking at me, love, I'm the one who can't dance."), and Finn refused his father outright, so Rylan danced with Declan, which made the whole ward stop and clap. Tess danced with Osric. Osric stepped on her foot in the first turn and apologised, and stepped on it again in the third and apologised again, and on the fifth she said something to him that Emeric was too far off to hear and Osric didn't step on her foot again all night. Tess looked, in the lamplight, more pleased than he had ever seen her look about anything that didn't have a drive in it.

Mira had come back from returning the boy to his mother and now stood at the edge of the ward with Arthur, watching the dancers with the critical eye of two people who had been taught to watch feet.

"Declan drops his shoulder," said Arthur.

"He drops it in the yard too," said Mira. "Nobody's ever got him for it."

"Somebody will."

"Not tonight," said Mira, and pulled him onto the gravel by his sleeve before he could argue.

Sabina did not dance. She sat at the end of a trestle with Mabry and a cup of Tamsy's coffee, untouched, and when Imani came past her with a plate Sabina put out one gloved hand, not quite touching Imani's arm, and said, "The right one's inflamed."

Imani stopped.

"Your ear," said Sabina. "It's been closed some years, I'd think. You put it in anyway." She looked up at Imani with her colourless face. "I did the same, at mine. It went bad and I wore it the whole night and said nothing. Don't do that. Take it out at the door."

Imani looked at her. Then, to Emeric's great surprise, she sat down on the end of the trestle beside Sabina and talked to her for ten minutes about ears.

Corbin came up the side of the ward once, with his watcher three paces behind him, and stopped by Emeric at the edge of the lamplight.

"The chair," he said.

"Her idea."

"I thought so. It's too good for you." Corbin looked out at the dancers. He looked, in the lamplight, his age and not quite his age, the way all of them did now. "A year ago I'd not have come."

"Why did you?"

Corbin thought about it, plainly, the way he thought about everything now that he said anything at all.

"My watcher likes cake," he said, and went back down the side of the ward to his place by the gate before Emeric could answer, and the watcher went after him, not denying it.

Maeve found the two women near the end, by the trestles, when Emeric had been given back to them.

She had been dancing for three hours and her hair had come down and she didn't care. She looked at the two of them, the way she had looked at them on the capital's high table five years ago, without pretending she wasn't doing it, and then at him.

"You two wrote," she said. "Every month. Five years. I've got a drawer of you." She turned on him. "He wrote four times. Two of them were about drains."

"They were good drains," said Emeric.

"They were very good drains, I went and saw them." She took Ferra's hand and Imani's hand, one in each of hers, and held them. "It's one house now. I want one letter. All three of you in it. And I want his part in it, at least a page, and if it's about drains I'm sending it back." She let go, and touched his arm, once, going past, the way she always did. "So write to me," said Maeve. "All of you."



They walked to the dome at dusk.

The town followed them as far as the moat bridge, singing, and at the bridge Ward stood in the middle of it in his good grey suit with his feet apart and said that was far enough, and the town, which had known Ward for fourteen years, agreed that it was. They went across the bridge alone, the three of them, in the black and the gold, with the noise of the ward behind them going on without them, and the gravel loud under their feet.

Harrold had gone ahead. He was inside, on the edge of the mattress, with the gold rose from the chair in his hand.

His mother stood at the foot of her own bed.

She had put herself in a good dress. She could; projected, she wore what she remembered, and she must have remembered this one carefully, a long blue thing with a high waist, twenty-eight years out of fashion. She stood very straight. Her hands were folded in front of her.

They came in under the glass and stopped, because she was looking at them, and they let her.

She looked a long time. At Ferra's sleeves. At the lattice. At Imani's one rose, and Imani's ears, and his coat, which Rowena had said she'd hate. At their hands. She came forward across the dome floor, not walking exactly, and stood very close to Ferra and bent and looked at the ring.

"He put the leaves on the right side," she said. "Nobody ever does. They put them both under, like a cabbage." She looked at Imani's. "And that one's turned. So they face each other when you stand on either side of him." She straightened. "Did you tell him to do that?"

"No," said Ferra.

"Then your father is a better man than I knew, and I thought he was quite good." She turned to her son. "And that coat is too much."

"Rowena said you'd hate it."

"Rowena was right. I'm glad you wore it." His mother looked at the three of them together, standing in her dome in the green and gold air with the tree going up over them into the dark of the glass. "You took my roses," she said.

"You said we could."

"I did say. I didn't know it would look like this." Her voice went, a little, and came back. "I was married in roses. Harry dropped the ring."

"It went down a step," said Harrold, from the mattress. He had heard every word; since the summer he heard her as plainly as any of them. "One step. It didn't go anywhere."

"It rolled under the bishop."

"It was a small bishop."

Amara laughed. It was the laugh, the real one, the one that Harrold looked up at every time like a man hearing his name across a street; and he looked up now, and Emeric watched his father's face, young again and not used to it, and looked away to let him have it.

"Did anybody drop anything today?" said his mother.

"Ferra broke the chapel's pen," said Imani.

"Good. Something should break. It's lucky." She turned to Imani. "And you put your earrings in a closed ear on purpose, because you'd decided to and you weren't going to be told by an ear."

Imani's hand went to it. "Does everyone know?"

"I'm a mother. Take it out before you go to bed." She looked at all three of them, one after another, slowly, the way he had seen her look at the east bed when she was counting what had come up. "I wanted to see you. Now I have. Go away."

"Mother..."

"It's your wedding night," said Amara, "and you're standing in a glasshouse with your parents. Go away. Come back in the morning and tell me nothing at all."

At the door Ferra turned round. Amara was standing at the foot of the bed, by her own sleeping body, with her hand out flat a little above Harrold's shoulder, not touching it, because she couldn't, and Harrold had put his own hand up beside it in the air, an inch off, not touching either.

Ferra took the ledger out of the pocket Abloh had sewn into her skirt for it, against his principles, and opened it at the page in October that had been ringed twice since the spring, and wrote under the date, by the light of the dome, very small: She saw. Then she shut it.



Ward was at the castle door when they got there, sitting on the step in his suit with his coat off and his belt on, eating a slice of the cake out of his hand.

"Everybody's still in the ward," he said. "You've got about an hour before they remember you." He stood up and brushed off his trousers. "I'm going to bed. My room's at the far end. I'm going to put a pillow over my head anyway, out of respect."

"Goodnight, Ward," said Imani.

"I mean it about the pillow."

"Goodnight, Ward," said Ferra.

He went. Imani took the earring out of her right ear on the step, as she had been told to twice, and looked at it in her palm, and put it in Emeric's waistcoat pocket, the one that wasn't sewn shut. Then she looked up at him.

"Work first," she said. "That's the rule. Fun later."

"It's nine o'clock at night."

"Then it's later," said Imani, and Ferra opened the door.



Ward came into the castle kitchen at seven the next morning and found Emeric already there, at the table, in his shirt, with both hands round a cup of coffee he had made himself, which was bad.

Ward looked at him for some time from the doorway with the bread knife in his hand, the way he looked at a man who'd come off the yard's long run.

"You look," said Ward, "like the Hod after the storm."

"Thank you."

"Did you sleep?"

Emeric thought about it honestly. "Some."

Ward cut bread. He said nothing else at all, for long enough that Emeric began to be grateful, and then said to the bread, "I had the pillow," and that was all.

They came down at half past.

They came down together, dressed for work, Ferra in her grey with the ledger under her arm and her hair twisted up with a pen through it, and Imani in her blue scrubs with her satchel across her and the right ear, he noticed, perfectly healed and bare. They both looked as though they'd slept nine hours in a field of clover. Ferra kissed him on the top of the head on her way past to the bread. Imani took his wrist on her way past to the kettle, two fingers, counting, and let it go.

"Hm," said Imani.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's high." She poured. "For a man sitting down."

Ferra sat opposite him with her bread and opened the ledger at the October page and wrote something on it. He leaned over to see. It was a single small tick, beside yesterday's date, in the margin.

"What's that for?"

"It's a tick."

"I can see it's a tick."

"Then you know what it's for," said Ferra, and turned the page, to the first of November, where the next column was headed South Sentinel and had nine lines under it already, the first of which was No work, which she had crossed out, and written underneath Less work, and crossed that out as well.

"I'm at the ward at eight," said Imani. "Danner first. Then the board's minutes. Then the rat." She drank her tea standing up. "You're at the Motion Shop. Tess wants the knee."

"I'm at the records hall till four," said Ferra. "Then the dome. She said tell her nothing, and she'll want everything."

"And then?"

Ferra looked at him over the ledger. Imani looked at him over her cup. Neither said anything at all, which was worse.

"Later," said Imani, and patted his cheek, and took her satchel and went.



Zale had his ship on the fourteenth.

She lay on the second slip at the yard in the grey light, a hull of the Endeavor's class with the Endeavor's lines, welded in blocks, with her stones set and cold and her deck still smelling of Zale's own teak. He had come home in September to find her finished and walked round her three times without going aboard. He had not named her. A sailor didn't name a ship on land, he said, and Fletcher, who had named eleven, said that was a story sailors told to get out of paying for the paint.

What made her his was down her sides.

The Endeavor and her sisters went forward on their engines. This one had veins. Tess and Fletcher had drawn them over the winter: channels let into the hull plating along both flanks from the bow to the quarter, each a hand wide and shut with louvres, with air stones set in their throats. Charged, the stones drew air in at the forward ends and drove it out astern down the veins, and the ship went forward on it, in still air, with nothing turning. She flew without him; every layer of her was written down, and Fletcher had taken her across the bay twice in August with a stone-keeper and a boy from the yard.

But she was Zale's because of what happened when Zale was at the helm.

They took her up at nine, in the slack before the land breeze, Zale at the wheel and Fletcher at his elbow and Emeric at the gauges. Rowena had come down to the yard to watch her son and stood on the slip's end with her hands in her sleeves, as still as a post. At five hundred feet Fletcher put the vein stones on at half, and the ship went forward, steady, at the speed the gauge said she would, and Fletcher wrote it down.

Then Zale whistled.

It wasn't much of a whistle. Two notes, low, rising, the kind a man whistles walking home. Emeric felt it go out of him, as he'd felt it on the coaster years ago in a dead calm and said nothing, and felt it reach the veins, and the air in the veins came to it the way a dog comes to the right voice. The needle on the vein gauge went up a third. Nobody had touched a stone. The deck leaned forward under their feet, gently, and Fletcher grabbed for the rail.

"Again," said Fletcher, when he'd got his breath.

Zale did it again. The needle went up again.

"It's the same air," said Zale. He sounded faintly apologetic. "It's only asked nicer."

Emeric wrote the figures himself, in his own book, under Fletcher's. It was the reason he'd promised Zale a ship at all: not because the ship needed him, but because the air did what Zale asked better than it did for anybody in Kaldo, and a ship that went where Zale went would go further than any other.

When they were down again and the slip's crew had her chocked, he took Zale aside along the yard wall, where the noise of the riveting was loudest.

"Two things," he said.

"Only two?"

"A charge log. Every stone, every watch, wherever you go. What it held at the start and what it held at the end. Fletcher will give you the book."

Zale nodded. That was ordinary; every Kaldo ship kept one.

"And a jar of water," said Emeric. "Sealed. From past the last islands. As far out as you can go and still come back easily. Not further."

Zale looked at him.

"Water," he said.

"In a jar."

"What's in it?"

"That's what I want to know."

Zale thought about that for a while, with his big hands on the wall, looking past the yard to the sea. He had sailed past the last islands farther than any man in Kaldo. He had never gone into the open water beyond them, where the old charts went blank, because no one who went there came back with anything but the wish not to go again.

"I'll get you your water," he said. "Two jars. One for you and one for me, so I can look at it." He turned back toward the slip, toward the ship with no name. "My mother's been standing on that slip for an hour and she won't say what she thinks. I'm going to go and make her."



Sorrel asked her question on the sixteenth, on the turbine floor.

The Works ran three turbines now, in a long brick hall below the dam, and the floor between them was the warmest place in Kaldo in October, so anybody with a reason to be near it found one. Sorrel had a reason, which was that her second glider's wing root was on the bench by the door being looked at by Dov, and Dov would not be hurried. She stood on the iron walkway over the second turbine with Tess and watched the casing shiver as the steam went into it.

Emeric was at the far end with the gauge board and Bram's figures, and heard every word, because the hall carried sound and Sorrel had never once in her life asked a question quietly.

"It's a wheel of blades," said Sorrel. "In a pipe."

"It's a lot of wheels of blades," said Tess. "In a pipe."

"And the steam goes in one end and pushes them round and comes out the other." Sorrel leaned on the rail. "What if you didn't feed it steam? What if you opened the front of the pipe, and the blades pulled the air in themselves, and pushed it out the back?"

Tess considered the turbine. She had the look she wore when a question was better than the person asking it knew.

"Then what turns the blades?" she said.

"The air."

"The air's what you're pushing. Something has to push it. Here it's the boiler over there." Tess nodded up the hall. "You want it with no boiler. So it would have to make its own push, inside the pipe, as the air went through. It would have to burn its own fire in there. In the middle of the wheels." She pulled a face. "In a pipe that's spinning. In the air. On a glider."

Sorrel thought about it.

"But could it?"

"I don't know," said Tess honestly. "Nobody's tried. It would want metal that doesn't melt, for a start, and we haven't got any." She looked down the hall, and Emeric found something very interesting on Bram's gauge board. "Ask me in ten years."

"I'll ask you in two," said Sorrel, and went down the steps to see what Dov had done to her wing root.

Emeric wrote down the steam pressure on the second turbine, which he had already written down, and then the date, and then nothing. He knew what she had described, and its name, and that the first ones on the world he'd come from had eaten their own blades in an afternoon. Sorrel had walked up to it from the other side, on her own, with a glider and a question. He put his pen away and said nothing, which was the hardest thing he did that week.



Four miles up in the Band, on the night of the seventeenth, the Well was filling.

It had hung on its pin for eleven months without moving and taken the sky's mana into its shell a little at a time, as the plan said it would, slowly, and the station's night hand had nothing to do but watch it happen and write it down. He sat at the log table in the lamp's small light with his coat on, under the sense stones set into the wall in their row, eight of them, each a pale bead in a brass ring, each warm with whatever the Band was doing outside, steady as a sleeping breath. At the turn of the watch he read them, one to eight, and wrote eight figures, and they were the same eight figures he had written at the turn of the last.

At twenty minutes past two the fourth stone flickered.

It went bright and dim and bright again, once, quickly, the way a candle does when a door opens somewhere else in a house, and then was steady.

He looked at the other seven, which had done nothing, and at the gauges for the pin and the shell, which hadn't moved, and out of the port at the Band, dark and still and full of stars. He sat and watched the fourth stone for a quarter of an hour. It did nothing either.

Then he wrote in the log, in his ordinary hand, under the turn of the watch: 02.20. Sense stone 4, one flicker. Cause not found. Others steady. And went on with the watch.
