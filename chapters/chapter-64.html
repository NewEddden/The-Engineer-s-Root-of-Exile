Chapter 58 - The Boring Answer

He gave the interesting answer two days and they were the two happiest days he had spent in a year.

The idea was a stone that kept its own account of a journey. Not a lock, which can be cut, and not a seal, which can be lifted by anybody patient with a warm knife. A sense rune, etched shallow into a blank the size of a thumbnail, set into the body of the satchel under the lining, which registered the one event that mattered: the wax line across the flap parting.

He worked it out on the second evening with Vesk leaning over the bench saying almost nothing, which was Vesk's version of encouragement. It was elegant. It was cheap. It could go into forty satchels for the price of a horse. Nevin cut a test blank in an afternoon and Emeric sat in the shed with it and opened the flap and watched the little thing do exactly what he had designed it to do, and for about an hour he was twenty-two again and in a hole in the ground with a light in his hand.

Then he took it up to the long room and wrote down, in order, what it would actually be worth in front of Keller, and it took him about forty minutes to kill it.

It failed on people.

Not on physics. The rune was fine. The rune would register a wax line parting for as long as the charge held and it would never once lie about it. What it could not do was tell anybody why the line had parted. A satchel that had gone eleven days through hard rain with the leather swelling and a lame horse putting the whole thing against a saddle bow four thousand times produced a reading, and a satchel opened with a warm knife at a sorting table produced a reading, and the two readings were not different enough to matter. There was no baseline. Nobody had ever measured what an ordinary journey looked like, because nobody had ever had a reason to, and building that baseline would take a year of satchels in weather.

And at the end of that year he would have a stone, and a man would have to stand in front of a magistrate and say what the stone meant.

He wrote the sentence down because he wanted to see it in ink. A judge who will not convict on persuasive is not going to convict on a rock's opinion of the weather.

There was a second reason, and it was the one that actually finished it. The men at the crossing went through those bags with their hands. A hard thing the size of a thumbnail sewn under a lining is not a thing that survives being handled by people who handle bags for a living. They would find it inside two months, and then they would know, and then the method would change, and the method was the only evidence he had.

He put the blank in a drawer and sat for a while with his hands flat on the table.

He was not being modest about it. He wanted that clear in his own head, because he could feel the more comfortable version of the thought trying to arrive, the one where a wise man sets aside a clever toy. It was not that. He had been given a standard by a man who knew what a standard was for, and the standard ruled out every single thing he was good at. No instrument. No rune. No insight. Nothing he could build in a shed in a week and be pleased with. What the standard wanted was paper, in volume, over years, boring enough that nobody would think to interfere with it.

He had spent seven years in this barony proving that the dull answer beats the brilliant one. It turned out to be considerably easier to prove when the dull answer was somebody else's.

What he built instead took eleven days to design and had nothing in it that would have interested anybody.

Every report Kaldo sent to the crown went out twice. The same day. The same words, copied exactly, with Ferra checking the second against the first line by line and initialling the check, because a duplicate that differs from its twin in any particular is a duplicate a barrister can argue about for an afternoon.

The first copy went up the crown post in the ordinary way, through the ordinary gate, in the ordinary book, sealed, and consolidated at the crossing at Wayford with everything else out of the south. Nothing about it changed at all. That was the point of it.

The second went by a road Emeric controlled, and it carried its twin's paperwork with it.

That was the whole trick and it belonged to Keller. A lodgment receipt, taken at the gate from the crown post's own clerk at the moment the first copy was handed over, stating the weight of the item, the addressee's office, the seal device and the date, in the clerk's own hand with the crown post's stamp on it. The clerk issued four hundred of those a year and thought nothing about any of them. He had no idea what he was participating in and would not have been able to describe it if asked, which was the specification exactly.

The receipt travelled attached to the second copy. So every item on the second road arrived carrying proof, in a stranger's handwriting, that its twin had gone official on that date.

Bram cut the die.

Emeric went down to him with the requirement and Bram listened to it with his arms folded and then asked one question, which was whether the flaw was supposed to look like a flaw. Emeric said yes, and Bram said that was the interesting part and went away for three days.

What came back was a wax die with a nick in the shoulder of the device, off the vertical by a degree or so, exactly where a die-cutter's hand slips when he is finishing a shoulder and tired. Any competent forger cutting a copy from an impression would smooth it. Not out of carelessness. Out of craft. A man copying a seal is trying to produce a good seal, and a good seal does not have a slipped shoulder in it, and the nick would come out under his hand without him ever deciding to remove it.

"He'll do it on the first one," Bram said. "He won't even see himself do it. That's a lovely nasty piece of thinking, that."

"Can you cut it consistently? Every impression, the same?"

"It's one die. It's the same nick every time until the die wears, and I'll cut you a new one off the same pattern when it does, and I'll keep the old one in a box with the date on it." Bram turned it over in his fingers. "You'll want the dates on the box."

"I will."

The folds were Ferra's. Each copy went into a particular sequence of creases, the same sequence every time, with the wax laid across the third crease rather than flat on a face, so that the sheet could not be opened and put back without the wax parting along the fold line or the fold line showing through the wax. It did not stop anybody opening anything. It made it impossible to open something and have it look unopened, which was a different and more useful property.

And there was no magic in any of it, anywhere, on purpose. Not a rune, not a charged blank, not one thing that a man going through a bag with his hands would find. Everything about the twin mail was designed to be entirely uninteresting to the people it was aimed at, so that the people it was aimed at would go on doing to it exactly what they had been doing for three years.

Ferra said the thing out loud on the evening they settled the last of it, which was late, with the design finished and the first eleven copies stacked and ready for the morning.

"I want to say what this is," she said, "once, and then I'll run it and I won't raise it again."

"Go on."

"It doesn't stop anything. Not one letter that would have been taken will arrive. We are not protecting the post. We are building a record of a crime while the crime goes on happening, and the men doing it are the ones manufacturing the record, and it only works for as long as they keep doing it."

"Yes."

"So every report you send from now on is bait, and you're paying for it, and the cost of the bait is that the crown goes on hearing nothing out of this barony for as long as it takes." She was not arguing. She had that flat-surfaced way of laying a thing on the table when she wanted it acknowledged rather than debated. "You are choosing to stay invisible to your own king in order to prove that you are being made invisible to him."

Emeric sat with that for a moment, because it was true and because she had put it better than he had.

"How long," she said.

"A year. Keller said a year and he said he'd refuse eleven months."

"Then it's a year," Ferra said, and picked up the stack.

Vantic worked it out in seven weeks and she worked it out off the postage line.

There was nothing else for her to work it out from. Nobody told her. Emeric had not decided to keep it from her so much as failed to think of her as somebody it concerned, which was its own kind of answer and one he was not proud of when he examined it later.

What she saw was an expenditure heading that had run at a predictable figure for four years and that doubled in one quarter and then held at the doubled figure, with no change in the volume of the barony's business, no new counterparty, and no explanation entered against it. She did not need to know what the twin mail was. She needed to know why a line moved, and that is the entire content of her profession.

She came to him with the sheet on a Tuesday and asked, in eleven words, what the barony was buying.

Emeric had about two seconds to decide and he did not really use them. He told her the truth. All of it, in order, including Wayford by name, which he had forbidden five people to say out loud and now said himself to a creditor's agent with a lawful right to report anything she liked to anybody she liked.

He did it because he had worked out a long time ago that the one thing that would end the arrangement between them was being caught in a lie, and because the arrangement between them was the most valuable thing in the barony after the ore contract, and because she had asked a direct question and she had never once asked one she did not need the answer to.

She listened to the whole of it without interrupting.

Then she asked whether any part of the expenditure was recoverable from anybody, and he said no, and she asked whether there was security behind it, and he said there was not, and she wrote it up as an operating cost against no asset, which is what it was, and which is the worst category a lending house has.

She did not obstruct it. She did not have the power to obstruct it, and she did not attempt to use the powers she did have to make it difficult, and Emeric noticed that she could have.

What she did do, which neither of them remarked on, was enter the figure at the bottom of a page in the back of her own book, under the earlier line about the tolls at the crossing, in a short column with dates beside it that had been running for three years and that had nothing to do with the loan at all.

The second road was not one road. That was the other half of it, and it was the half that killed a man.

A fixed carrier is a target. Anybody watching Kaldo for a month would see a second rider leaving the same gate on the same days as the crown post and would not need to be clever about the rest. So Emeric ran a rotation, seven carriers deep, with the order set by Ferra out of a schedule nobody else saw, and no carrier used twice running, and no carrier told that the item he was holding had a twin.

Four of them were Saban's. Two were merchants Saban vouched for, one a boatman of Fletcher's who went up the coast and inland.

The seventh was Dunnel, and Emeric chose him personally.

He was a wool factor. He ran the western circuit, Milford out to the coast and back, five times a year, and he had run it for nineteen years, and he sold Kaldo lard for the soap works at a rate that had been renegotiated exactly once.

He came up in the spring with a cart and a boy and a great deal to say.

He was fifty and pleased with himself in a way that was entirely harmless. He knew every innkeeper between Milford and the sea by their first name and the names of most of their wives, and he told Emeric so, and then told him three stories about them, and Emeric let him, because he was watching the man and because the stories were quite good.

"I'll want you to carry something," Emeric said, when there was a gap.

"Letters?"

"Letters. Mine. North, by your own road, in your own bales, to a man in the capital who'll write back confirming he had them. Nothing about it changes anything you do. You go where you were going anyway, on the days you were going anyway."

Dunnel thought about it for slightly less time than Emeric had expected.

"Is it dangerous?"

"I don't think so." He said it carefully, because he had asked himself the question and had answered it. "Somebody is interfering with my post on the northern road. That's what this is about and I'd rather you knew it than didn't. But you're not on the northern road. You're on a road you've run for nineteen years with wool on it, and the men interfering with me are interfering with a crown post satchel, which is a different thing entirely from a wool factor's bale."

"Then I'll take it," Dunnel said. "What's it pay?"

They settled on a figure in about a minute. It was fair and slightly over. And then Dunnel said the other thing, cheerfully, while he was folding the note away.

"I'd have done it for less, mind. I like this place. I've sold into forty holdings and there's four of them settle a bill the month it's raised and yours is one, and the woman who pays it can do the arithmetic in her head faster than I can do it on paper, which I find frankly offensive." He put his hat on. "A man likes to be useful to the people who buy his lard."

Emeric was pleased with the choice for a year and his reasons were sound, and he went over them afterward a great many times looking for the place where they were not, and never found it.

Dunnel had no stake in any of it. He had never carried anything for anybody in nineteen years. There was no pattern to break because there had never been a pattern; he went the same way at the same times with the same cart, and had done since before Emeric was born, and the thing he was invisible behind was not secrecy but ubiquity. Everybody on that road knew Dunnel. Nobody on that road would ever look twice at Dunnel. A courier can be identified as a courier. A man who has been driving wool west for nineteen years cannot be identified as anything except a man driving wool west.

It was, on every ground Emeric could construct at the time or afterward, the best of the seven choices.

Then the year happened.

Two piles of paper accumulated in the long room, in two boxes, on the same table. The left box held the lodgment receipts and the second-road confirmations and the returned attestations, dated, stamped, initialled, cross-referenced by Ferra into a running index that got longer every month. The right box held what should have matched it and never did: the crown's acknowledgments, refusals, queries, referrals, clerk's lines.

The right box stayed empty. Fifty-one items across the year and the right box stayed empty, and the emptiness was the product, and there were weeks when Emeric could not make himself look at it.

Nothing else in the barony noticed. That was the strange part of that year, and the good part. Kaldo worked.

The foundry ran two shifts through the summer and could have run three if it had been permitted a second furnace. The soap went down the long water and out to the ports. The debt came down on the schedule Ferra had drawn and the curve she had shown him on the shortest day two years earlier turned out to have been conservative by a season, which annoyed her.

Armand's baseline programme went through its second full year and its second complete round of readings. Nine hundred and sixty people measured, three times each, twice over. Armand would not discuss any of it and posted a note on the measuring room door saying so.

Orrin finished the last of the corrections. The school's curriculum had been wrong in nine places and was now wrong in none that anybody could find, and the strange consequence of two years of being corrected by a stranger from the tower was that Orrin had become a considerably better teacher than he had ever been, which Emeric noticed one afternoon standing at the back of a room and did not tell him about until much later.

Hollis's arm was no better. It had been six years. Imani had stopped saying anything about it and Hollis had stopped expecting her to.

There was one cost that year that Emeric learned about sideways and never had confirmed.

A Grayrock carter mentioned it at the Kaldo end of the new road, to another carter, in a yard, in the ordinary way men complain about their employers, and one of Ferra's clerks heard it and wrote it down because writing things down was what that barony did to people. Grayrock's cut stone, which went east and had nothing to do with Kaldo's ore, had lost two contracts inside a season. Both to the same buyer. The buyer had been apologetic and had cited a change in his requirements.

Bryda's letter that quarter was four paragraphs about weights, schedules, the condition of the lower seam, and a proposal to shift the carts to the eleventh and the twenty-fifth permanently rather than seasonally. It was correct. It was impersonal. It did not mention the stone contracts, or the buyer, or anything else.

Emeric read it twice, the second time for what was not in it, and found nothing that was not in it, and put it in the box with the others.

Late in the year, in the winter, there was an evening.

They were arguing about the carriage allocation between the foundry and the soap works, which was a column that did not matter, on figures that were four crowns apart. It was past midnight. Neither of them had eaten. Neither of them wanted to stop, and both of them had noticed that, and the argument had been going for an hour longer than the argument was worth.

Ferra stood up, put the sheet down squared, and left without saying anything at all.

Emeric sat in the room for some time afterward and did not do any more work.

Dunnel was killed on the western road in the last week of autumn, eleven miles short of the coast, with the cart burned and the boy face down in a ditch twenty yards off and the horses gone.

They took the wool. They took the money. They took his coat and his boots and the boy's boots, and a crate of finished tin that was worth about nine crowns, and they went through the bales and scattered what they did not want across forty yards of road in the rain.

It read as robbery. It read as robbery to the Milford reeve who came out to it, and to the coastal magistrate's man who came three days later, and to Paxton, and to Saban, and to Ward, who went out and walked it himself and came back and said there was nothing in it he could point at. Men had robbed a cart on a road where carts get robbed.

That is what it was. Emeric was never in any doubt about that. Somebody had paid men to do a robbery and the men had done a robbery, thoroughly and without any refinement whatsoever, and there was not one thing about the scene that distinguished it from the forty other carts that had been taken on that road over nineteen years while Dunnel drove past them.

The crown's copy of that report died at the crossing in the ordinary way, in silence, the way all fifty-one of them died.

The second copy died on a western road with a fifty-year-old wool factor and a boy whose name Emeric had to ask for.

He went back over the decision for most of the winter.

Dunnel had been the right choice. That was what he kept arriving at, and it was not comfort. Every reason he had had was still a good reason. A nineteen-year route. No history of carrying. Invisible through ubiquity. He had reasoned it carefully at the time and he could not now find the sentence that was wrong, because the sentence that was wrong was not about Dunnel.

He had judged the man correctly and had judged, entirely wrongly and without ever noticing that he was judging it, what would be done to that man. He had assumed that a thing aimed at a crown satchel stayed aimed at crown satchels. He had assumed there was a ceiling, somewhere, on what men would do about one letter. He had not put a number on that ceiling or written it down or examined it, because it had not presented itself as an assumption at all.

Nobody said any of this to him. Ward did not say it. Ferra did not say it. Saban, who had vouched for four of the other six and had nothing to do with the seventh, did not say it, and went out and got very drunk once, alone, which was not a thing he did.

The barony paid Dunnel's widow, and the boy's mother, at rates Ferra invented because there was no rate for a man who was not on the payroll, and Emeric signed both without reading the figures.

And then he read the pile and drew the wrong conclusion out of it, and he drew it carefully, and it took him three days.

The standard was four things and all four were required. Dated: he had that. Independently attested: he had that. Produced over long enough: he had a year and eleven days, and he had made sure of the eleven days.

Paired, with the twin's fate known and demonstrable.

He had fifty items where one copy died and the other arrived and could be shown to have arrived by a stranger with no interest in any of it.

And he had one where both copies died. Same report. Same day. One into the silence at the crossing and one into a ditch on a western road, and neither of them attested at the far end by anybody, because neither of them got to a far end.

A set with a hole in it. One item in fifty-one that did not do what the other fifty did, sitting in the middle of the year, available to any barrister alive for an afternoon's work on why a pattern with an exception is not a pattern. He could hear the argument being made. He could make it himself, and did, twice, out loud, in an empty room.

He did not write to Keller. There was nothing to write that did not amount to asking a man who had told him twice that he was not an ally to be one.

He went down to the counting room in the last week of the year and told Ferra to keep both piles going.

She asked whether he wanted the index continued in the same form.

"Same form."

"And the rotation? Six carriers now, not seven."

"Six. Don't replace him."

She wrote it down and did not ask him anything else, because she had read his face on the way in and had a fairly good idea what he thought he had, and did not agree with him, and had not yet done the work that would have let her say so.

The winter closed in. The two boxes sat on the table in the long room, the left one full and the right one empty, and for the first time since he had come south Emeric stopped believing in a thing he had built.
