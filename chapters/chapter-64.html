Chapter 64 - The Break

The horn blew again as he came out of the side door of the ward, long and flat, from the bottom of the town.

One of Imani's women had caught him at the door and wrapped his hands, both of them, palm and fingers, in strips of boiled linen, without asking him and without letting go of his wrists until she had done. He looked at them now in the grey light. They were somebody else's hands, white and fat with cloth. He could feel nothing in them at all.

Rylan was on the step behind him. He had blood on him that was not his and some that might have been, and the dent in the back of his helmet, and he had not sat down since the powerhouse.

"South Gate," Rylan said. "Or the bridge?"

"South Gate," Emeric said. "It's closer."



The South Gate was at the bottom of the lower ward, and they went down to it at a run through lanes that were full of people who had not slept, standing in their doors, looking up at the sky over the river where the smoke went up in a great brown column with the sun coming up behind it. Nobody asked him anything. A woman held a child up to see him go by, and he did not know why.

He went up the gate stair onto the wall above the gate, where the arc lamp still burned in its hood, pale now against the morning, and looked out.

The road ran away from the gate across the fields, white in the lamp's light for a long bowshot, and grey beyond it, out to the first bend. A long bowshot out, short of the bend, where the light stopped, Milford was standing.

There were more of them than he had pictured. A hundred and fifty, perhaps, and hardly a man of them in iron. Spears and billhooks and drovers' sticks, a few shields, leather coats, and men on horses along the front of them, and behind them, along the verge, a line of wagons with their teams still in the shafts. He knew one of the wagons. He knew its driver, too, sitting on the box with his elbows on his knees, looking at the town, not cheerful at all. In front of the whole of it, on a broad brown horse, sat a broad man in a plain coat and a plain steel cap, with his hands crossed on the pommel, looking at the gate.

They had stopped exactly where the light ended, and they came no closer.

The gate crew were in the dark of the gate passage below him, where Ward had put them, with their bows across their knees. The crew's lead came up the stair to stand beside Emeric and look out over the parapet with him.

"They came up in the last of the dark," the crew lead said. "Blew the horn twice. Formed up there. Haven't moved since." He scratched his jaw. "Not one foot."

The broad man on the brown horse lifted his head, as if he had felt himself looked at. He could not have seen Emeric's face at that distance, against the lamp. He looked at the wall. Then he looked away, east, toward the smoke over the river, and kept looking at it.

"Leave them," Emeric said.

The crew lead looked at him.

"If they come into the light, shoot. If they stay where they are, leave them." Emeric turned for the stair. "Nobody goes out to them. Nobody."



They went along the wall walk to the east, above the roofs of the lower ward, toward the corner over the bridge. It was quicker than the lanes. The light came up as they went, and the lamps along the walk went from white to pale to almost nothing against the day, and the smoke came across the walk in waves, and below them the town was waking up into the smell of it.

At the corner, Ward was on the wall again, at the parapet, looking down at the bridge.

Grayrock was forming at the far end of it.

They had pulled back off the bridge in the night, after the knights, and the bodies were still on it, the knights' and the others', where they had fallen. Now, in the first of the sun, the levies were coming back out of the burned lanes onto the road at the head of the bridge, a great many of them, in a block, with shields at the front and spears behind, and men with axes. They were forming slowly, in no particular order. Behind them the dock quarter smoked in long black rows, with the chimneys standing up out of it alone, and here and there a beam still burning, low and red.

And behind the block, at the top of the ore yard, by the yard gate, the man with the grey beard sat his horse.

He had not been off it all night. Emeric could see that from the wall, from how he sat. He was pointing at the bridge, and talking to the men round him, and men were going from him down to the block and coming back. He had no knights left to send.

"He has seen Milford stand," Ward said. "So he will give them a reason." He did not look round. "This is the last of it. If it fails, he has nothing."

"Can the carts hold it?"

Ward was quiet. "Three times they held," he said at last. "There are fewer men at the carts than there were the first time."



Emeric went down the wall stair to the bridge foot.

The men at the carts were sitting where they had sat all night, with their bows across their knees, and they looked up at him and did not get up. The woman with the grey plait was sitting against the bridge parapet with her eyes shut. The long thin one was asleep beside her with his head on her shoulder, and she had let it stay there.

She opened her eyes when he crouched down beside her.

"It's coming back," she said.

He had felt it on the stair, and not believed it: a prickle in his chest, faint. He held his wrapped hand out over the cart, and closed his eyes, and reached, and there was something there. Thin. Like a draft under a door. The empty place the wind had left behind it was filling in again, from somewhere, slowly.

"Early," she said. She was looking at the river, running past brown and quick below the parapet. "The river, maybe. Moving water. I don't know." She shut her eyes again. "I've nothing in me to try it with, anyway."

He looked over the cart at the man on the horse at the top of the ore yard.

It was a long way. Across the bridge and past its far end, and the length of the wharf, and the whole width of the ore yard with its black heaps, uphill a little, to the gate at the top of it. Two hundred and fifty paces, perhaps. Twice what a crossbow would carry with any force left at the end of it, and Ward's bows had tried it twice in the night and put their bolts among the ore heaps.

Fire might reach that far, thinned to almost nothing. He had never sent it half so far. And fire at that range, on a man in plate, a man who was very likely rite-made, with tracery under his skin pushing back at any spell that touched it, would be a light, and a heat, and a man ducking his head and riding out of it, and going on pointing.

He looked down. There was a sheaf of bolts in a bucket against the cart wheel, where the gate-crew man had kept them. Square-headed, short, Bram's iron points on Kaldo ash.

He took one out.



He had never done the two at once on anything that mattered.

He had done it at night, behind the shutters, the winter before last: hanging a hand's width off his own floor with a candle flame bent toward him, a ring for each, counting. Forty, he had got to. He had done forty counts of it, on his own weight and a candle, alone, with nothing at stake but a headache.

There was tarred twine on the cart, the net twine the gate crew had lashed the barricade with. He cut a length of it with his teeth and wound it round the bolt behind the head, tight, turn on turn, so that whatever he set in it would burn without him. Then he knelt up behind the cart and laid the bolt flat across the palm of his wrapped right hand, point out, toward the top of the ore yard.

Fire first. That was the one everybody could see, and it had to be the one everybody saw. He reached past the bridge to the nearest thing still burning in the dock quarter, a roof beam at the river end of a lane, low and red, and took hold of it, and the hold came, thin and weak in the thin air, but it came. He drew a thread of fire off the beam and across the river, and set it in the tar, and wound the rest of it round the bolt in his hand, round and round, until the bolt was a rod of flame as long as his forearm, and the glyph stood up in the air over his hand, the ring and the mark for fire, bright as a coal, so that every man at the carts turned his head.

Then the other one.

He made it as small as he had ever made anything. He made it in the middle of the fire's ring, behind the fire's mark, where the light was thickest: a ring the size of a fingernail, with its mark and its one stroke, away from the hand, so small that he could not have seen it himself if he had not known it was there. The fire's glyph burned round it and over it. Nobody looking at his hand would see anything but fire.

He held both.

It was like standing on two horses. The fire wanted all of him, and the push wanted all of him, and he had half of himself for each, and his head began to pound at once, behind the eyes, and his nose filled. The man at the top of the ore yard turned his horse, pointing at the bridge, and the block of levies at the head of it began to move.

Emeric pushed.



The bolt went off his hand without a sound.

It went too fast to follow, and the fire went with it, streaming back off it, a line of flame drawn across the morning from the carts to the top of the ore yard, straight and flat over the river and the black heaps and the heads of the men at the end of the bridge, who ducked under it with their arms over their heads. It was there for no time at all. Then it was gone, and there was only the line of it left on his eyes, and at the far end of the line, by the yard gate, a burst of fire where the man on the horse had been.

The horse screamed. It came out of the fire on its own, and went down the road toward the river at a dead run, with its stirrups flying, and the men round the yard gate scattered out of its way.

The man was on the ground. He was on fire, the coat over his plate burning, and he did not roll, and he did not beat at it, and he did not get up.

Emeric had let go of both holds the moment the bolt left his hand. He was on his knees behind the cart, holding onto the wheel with his wrapped hands, and the blood was coming out of his nose onto the linen, and the world was swinging slowly round him and not settling. He could hear the men at the carts. They were shouting. He did not understand what they were shouting, and then he did. They were shouting at the far end of the bridge, at the block of levies, which had stopped moving. They were shouting the name of the man on the ground.

Somebody's hand was on his shoulder. It was Rylan's.

"I didn't know you could do that," Rylan said. He was looking across the river at the yard gate, where the burning shape lay on the road with the men standing back from it in a ring.

"Neither did I," Emeric said, into the wheel.



The block at the head of the bridge broke from the back.

He watched it happen through the spokes of the wheel. The men at the rear, nearest the yard gate, nearest the thing on the road, turned first, and went, down through the black lanes toward the moorings where the barges were, and then the men in front of them, who had felt the men behind them go. It did not take long. A block of men is held together by every man in it believing the man behind him will stay. Once enough of them stopped believing it, there was no block, only a great many men running for the river.

Ward was down off the wall. Emeric did not see him come. He was at the carts, and he looked at the far end of the bridge, and then at Emeric on his knees with his hands round a cart wheel and his face red with blood from the nose down, and he did not ask anything.

"Now," he said, to the men at the carts.

They went. They dragged the first cart aside and went across the bridge in a body, Declan's place empty, Osric limping, the bowmen spanning as they went, and on the far side they took the far end of the bridge, and the head of the road, and the ore yard, and stopped there, as Ward had told them to, and chased nobody into the burned lanes, and let the river take the rest. The barges went off the moorings one after another as the men reached them, overloaded, pushing off with poles and spears and hands, out into the current, and the current took them down past the town toward the sea, under the bridge and past the burned mill and the powerhouse, where a few of the bowmen on the wall shot at them and most did not bother. Some of the men did not reach the barges. They sat down in the mud of the moorings with their hands on their heads, and waited for whoever came.

Two of them did not put their hands on their heads. They held them up instead, high and open, with the fingers spread, and kept them there while the air filled back in round them. Neither of them made a ring.

A few of the running men went down the bank past the old works, past a big figure standing in the door of bay three, and not one of them turned in.

The coast woman had her eyes open again.

"Fire," she said, to nobody, looking at the far end of the ore yard. "From here." She shook her head slowly, and leaned it back against the parapet, and did not say anything else about it.



A boy came down from the keep at a run a little after the sun had cleared the smoke, one of the school's, sent from the line room, red in the face this time.

"The South Gate says all well," he said. He had the flat hand held up in front of him, to show it, as the board showed it. "All well. The man said to say they've gone."

"Milford?"

"Back up the road. All of them. The man said they never came into the light."

Ward was beside him. "Do we follow?" Ward said.

It was the first question Ward had asked him since the bridge. Emeric thought of the broad man on the brown horse, looking at the smoke. He thought of a line at the end of a letter: I am aware of what this makes me.

"No," he said.

Ward nodded, once, and went back across the bridge to his men.



The holds were gone out of him, both of them, and there was nothing left in him to put them back with. He could feel the mana in the air round him filling in, thicker every minute, as the wind's emptiness closed over. It did not matter. What he had in him was what he had, and it was very little, and he would need all of it for one more thing.

He stood up. The wheel helped. Rylan helped more.

"Where?" said Rylan.

Emeric looked up the hill, over the roofs of the lower ward, to where the old market hall stood above Cooper's Row with its side door, and a woman inside it with both her hands on a girl's back.

"The ward," he said. "It's clear."
