Chapter 283 - Black Keel

The K Endeavor came home at a little after six on the ninth of December, in the dark, with the month's oil in her tanks and two stones in a bucket.

Emeric was on the quay at the secret dock when she came down. He had not meant to be. He had meant to be asleep; but the line room's boy had knocked at his door at a quarter past two with a slip from the wireless, in Ferra's cipher, from the K Endeavor off the Isle, and he had read it by his candle and not gone back to sleep. It said: THIRD TANK. ONE LOST. TWO TAKEN. HOME BY SIX. And under it, which was not cipher and was not in the book and could only have been Fennick leaning over the operator's shoulder: IT WORKED.

So he had got up at five and dressed and walked down to the dock in the frost to see what had worked.

She came down out of the dark over the water, long and grey, with her running lights out and her oil hose coiled on her deck, and settled on her cradle by the dock's end the way she always did, so gently that the quay boards did not move; and the first man down her ladder was Fennick, in a canvas coat three sizes too big for him with his eyebrows white with salt, carrying a wooden bucket in both arms, with a lid on it, as carefully as a man carrying a bowl of soup up a stair.

Behind him came her diver, Teague, a long quiet lighterman's son from the port who had been diving the Isle's tanks for her all year in his brass helmet and his canvas dress; and Teague was grinning. Emeric had never seen Teague grin. Teague came down the ladder and stood on the quay in the cold and grinned at nothing, steadily, with his hands opening and closing at his sides, and his note in Emeric's sense was all wrong: loud, bright, buzzing, like a knight's on a morning after a heavy night in the yard.

"Highness," said Fennick, and set the bucket down on the quay boards between them, and took off the lid.

There was seawater in it. And at the bottom, under the water, two stones.

They were grey, and rough, and small: one the size of a fist and one a little smaller, broken on one face where they had come away from the rock. They did not glow. Shard stones did not glow. But Emeric felt them from where he stood, through the water and the bucket's oak, as he had felt the chip in July on the K Endeavor's deck: alive, small, very dense, like a breath held. Watching. He put his hand down toward the water, slowly, and an inch above the surface his hand slowed of its own accord, as if the air had thickened round it, and would not go nearer.

"Two," said Fennick. "There were three. The first one went."

"I had your slip," said Emeric. "At two."

"I wrote that last bit myself." Fennick looked pleased. "Your boy on the set said it wasn't in the book. I said put it in the book."

He sat down on the quay's bollard, because his legs were done with standing, and told it.

He told it the way he told everything, too fast and out of order and with a great many hands; and Teague stood by him, grinning, and put the order back in when Fennick lost it. They had gone down to the third tank in the dark at midnight, the two of them, Fennick in the boat and Teague on the bottom in his helmet with the light of a battery lamp in a sealed tin on his chest. Teague had found the place at the cliff's foot where the pick would not go in. Fennick had sent down one small charge, one stick, in a tin with a long waxed fuse, and Teague had wedged it against the biggest lump he could see in the seam, the one that stopped his pick a full hand short, and come up the ladder; and Fennick had lit it.

"Nothing," said Fennick. "Nothing happened. You'd think a stick of that would shift a cliff. I'd seen it shift a hill. It went off, I felt it come up through the boat's bottom, and the water went white, and the stone sat there. Took it. All of it. Didn't move." He held up his hands. "And then it went."

"It burst."

"It burst. After. A breath after. Like it had swallowed the bang and couldn't hold it." Fennick shook his head. "And there wasn't any noise. That's the thing. There wasn't a sound. There was a wind." He looked at Emeric, and for once his voice came down. "A wind, out of the water, that went past my face and didn't move my hair. Didn't move the water. The water was flat as a plate after the white went off it, and this wind went across it, and it never made a ripple."

"A dead zone."

"I know what it was. I've read the book." Fennick waved at the town. "Armand's book. I know what a dead zone is. I've never been in one with a stone that cost a barony going off at the bottom of it." He looked at the bucket. "And then Teague said, go on, go back down, and I thought he was mad."

"I went down," said Teague. He was still grinning. "I was cold. I thought, it's gone, the big one, and the ones round it, they'll be standing there doing the cushion again and I'll be swinging at them all night. And I put the pick to the next one along." He made the motion, small, a short swing from the elbow. "And it went in."

Emeric said nothing.

"Like into chalk," said Teague. "Straight in. No cushion. Nothing. It was just rock. And I cut round it, quick, as quick as I've ever cut anything, and it came away in my hand, and I put it in the bag, and the next one, the same, and I was on the third and it started to come back." He put his hand out flat in the air, and pushed, gently. "Like the tide. The pick started slowing again. I could feel it come back on me through the water. Like somebody leaning on a door. And I let the third one be and came up."

"How long?"

"Three minutes," said Fennick. "I had the glass on it. Three minutes and a bit from the wind to Teague's head coming up. The rock's that close round them." He tapped the bollard. "Fills from the rock. Like your gallery."

"And then," said Teague, and stopped grinning for the first time, and looked at his hands. "And then I was sitting on the boat's thwart with the bag in my lap and my helmet off, and they were in the bag, and they were, I don't know, they were pushing at me. Through the canvas. Like they wanted something." He turned his hands over. "So I put my hands on them. I don't know why. Fennick said don't. And I felt it go out of them into me." He looked at Emeric. "Some of it. Not all. It came up my arms like hot water. And I let go when it got to my shoulders, because it was too much, and they stopped pushing." He grinned again, helplessly. "I've been like this since. I could lift the boat."

"Don't," said Fennick.

"I'm not going to lift the boat."

Emeric looked at the two of them, the old blaster with the salt in his eyebrows and the diver humming like a knight; and then down at the two stones in the bucket, alive and watching.

It was the old method. He knew it was. The tower's books had it, Armand's had it, in one line, a hundred years old, from the days when the seams above the waterline were still being worked: blow one, and cut in the quiet, and drain what you cut. Nobody had done it in living memory because nobody had found a seam in living memory. He had read it in the summer and meant to tell Fennick, and had not yet. He had meant to work it out on paper first.

They had worked it out in a boat at the bottom of the night, by blowing one up and going back down to see what happened.

"You found it yourselves," he said.

"Found what?"

"The way it's done." He looked at Fennick. "The old quarrymen's way. The way the seams on the cliffs were cut, a hundred years ago. You blow one. In the dead zone it leaves, the ones round it can't guard themselves, and you cut them. Quickly. Before the rock breathes back. And you drain them a little, by hand, before they wake, so they're quieter when they do." He looked at Teague. "Not to nothing. Never to nothing. You did it right."

Fennick looked at him for a long moment.

"You knew," he said.

"I'd read it."

"You'd read it." Fennick looked at the bucket. "You'd read it and you let me go down there with a stick of dynamite and find out."

"I hadn't told you yet," said Emeric. "I'd meant to. I'm sorry."

Fennick thought about that, and about the bucket, and about the wind across the water that had not moved his hair.

"No you're not," he said, after a while, and laughed, and got up off the bollard. "And nor am I."



He took them up to the study in the bucket and put them in a lead-lined box with the lid off, on the blotter, in seawater, and looked at them.

Two. A fist and a little less. Kaldo's first two stasis stones in its own hands; the first anybody had cut from a live seam in a hundred years.

They were no use yet. They would not be any use for a long time. A raw shard stone guarded itself: it had nothing written in it but its own nature, which was to feel anything move fast through the space round it and stop it, softly, like a net. You could not cut it, or shape it, or set it in a frame, while it did that; and you could not tell it what to let through and what not, because there was nothing in it to tell. That had to be written. From inside. The guard lowered, so that Bram could cut and mount it; the dial put in, soft to hard to hold; the register, the long list of every note it was to pass and every note it was to stop, written into a control stone and the shard stone's own inside tied to it.

Weeks of delving. For each. The six could help with the cutting and the register; he could not do it alone in the time there was, and he did not want to. He wrote it on the slate on the study wall, under the K Amara's other stones, the lift stones and the trim stones and the steadies, all of which had to be written too, and stood back, and looked at the slate, and thought that he had been a fool to think the slow part of building a ship was building the ship.

Ferra came in at eight with his breakfast on a tray, and put it down, and looked at the stones in their box, and then at the slate.

"Two," she said.

"Two."

"For the K Amara."

"For the K Amara and the K Discovery. Their shells." He looked at the slate. "When they're written. Which won't be this winter."

Ferra wrote in her book. Then she looked at the box again, and frowned a little.

"Somebody felt that," she said. "Last night. Teague says it went past them like a wind." She looked at him. "Anybody with a sense, on the Isle. Anybody on Kesrath's tower, at the far end. Any mage awake in a boat on that sea."

"Yes."

"Every one you cut, they'll feel."

"Every one," said Emeric. "Every cut's a dead zone. Every dead zone's a bang somebody could feel, if they were listening, and awake, and knew what it was." He looked at the stones. "The oil runs are silent. The stone runs never will be. So we don't do many."

Ferra wrote that down too, word for word.



The K Amara's keel went down on the eleventh, in the airship yard by the port, black.

It did not go down the way the K Discovery's had, eight years before, in the covered shed by the lake, one long oak timber laid on its blocks by Fletcher's men with half the town crowded in at the doors. It went down in pieces. The welders had built it in the shop at the yard's head over the autumn, in blocks: twenty-two lengths of steel plate and angle, each one the height of a man and thirty feet long, welded along every seam with the arc, the blue-white light of it coming out of the shop's windows night after night until the port's children called it the lightning shed; and on the eleventh of December the yard's cranes brought them out one at a time on the rails and laid them end to end on the slip's blocks, and the welders closed the joints between them in the open, under canvas screens, in the cold. Eleven days. When it was done, a single black spine lay on the slip, four hundred and twenty feet of it, and the welders stood about on it in their leather aprons with their masks pushed up and looked down its length toward the sea, and somebody started to clap, and stopped, embarrassed, and then they all did.

Black. Not painted; that would come. The steel itself, the colour the arc left it, a dull blue-black like a gun's barrel. Emeric had meant to have it painted grey, like the K Endeavor, like the yard's other hulls. He stood on the slip at the end of the eleventh day and looked down the spine in the low winter light and decided not to.

The town, which came out to look at it in its hundreds over the next week from behind the yard's fence, thought it was a market.

That was what the yard had said she was. The biggest ship Kaldo had ever laid down, with a great open deck along her back from end to end under a glass roof: a flying market, to carry Milford's grain and Grayrock's copper and the soap and the spirits and the paper to every fair in the kingdom, and set down in a field outside a town and open her sides, and sell. A shop that flew. It was a good story and it had the great merit of being half true; she would have a deck along her back, and it would be roofed, and she would carry a great deal. The town liked it. The town came and stood at the fence and argued about what it would sell first.

Beside her, on the two slips either side, the welders were laying two more.

Smaller. A good deal smaller: the hull Kaldo already knew, the K Endeavor's, seventy-five yards from end to end, her lines drawn off the K Endeavor's own on the drawing floor plate by plate; because the K Endeavor flew, and had flown all year, and a hull that flew was worth more than a better hull that might. Escorts one and two. The yard called them the twins. They would lift in the summer, the slips' gangs said, if the stones were written by then, and the slips' gangs did not know what the stones were or who wrote them or how, which was as it should be.

Their frames were not steel. That was the other new thing in the yard that winter. Above the steel of their hulls, the ribs and the stringers and the decks' beams went up pale grey, almost silver, light enough that two of the riggers could carry a frame between them that would have taken six in steel: Tess's metal, the metal of the pot room, with a little copper in it and cooked twice in the forge's ovens so that it came out hard. Duralumin. Emeric had said the word at the table in the spring, and the riggers had shortened it in a week to dural, and swore at it, because it could not be welded, it had to be riveted, every joint of it, by hand, two men to a rivet, one with the hot rivet in tongs and one with the hammer; and the yard rang with it all winter from first light to the dark, a sound like a thousand clocks.

"Every ton," Emeric said to Bram's foreman at the yard, who had asked him why, on the second day, standing under the first frame with his hands over his ears. "Every ton we save in her bones is a ton the stones don't have to lift. All her life. Every day she flies."

"It's a lot of rivets," said the foreman.

"It's a lot of tons," said Emeric.



They talked about the guns at the drawing table on the fourteenth.

Ward had asked. He had stood at the yard's fence on the twelfth and looked at the twins' hulls, at the two great round holes cut in each of them in the deck's line, fore and aft, ringed with steel, with nothing in them; and come up to the drawing office in the yard's head that evening and stood at the table with his arms folded and asked what they were for.

"Guns," said Emeric.

"There's nothing in them."

"There's going to be nothing in them for a long time." He pulled the drawing across the table between them. Two rings in each hull, fore and aft, each the width of a cart; and above each, drawn in faint pencil, a squat steel house that turned on the ring, and out of its face two long barrels side by side. "Twin guns. A foot across the bore. The biggest Kaldo can make." He tapped one. "They'll throw a shell five miles. From the water."

"From the water."

"From the water." Emeric turned the drawing round so that the escort lay side-on to them. "Fire that, on the sea, and the ship goes backward. A long way. The kick's as big as the shell, the other way. But the water holds her. She sits in the water like a cart on a road, and the water takes the kick and she rocks and comes back." He put his finger on the hull, in the air, above the table. "Fire it in the air, and nothing holds her. There's nothing to push against. The kick goes into the ship and the ship goes, sideways, backward, wherever the gun was pointing away from. Fire a broadside off one side of a ship in the sky, and you knock her round in a circle." He looked up at Ward. "Until something holds her."

"The stones," said Ward. "In the bucket."

"A stone like those. Written the right way. Not to catch things coming at her. To catch hold of the air itself, the mana in it, and hold on, so she can't slide." He shook his head. "I don't know yet if it can be done. I think it can. I don't know how long it takes to write. And those two go into shells first, for the K Amara and the K Discovery, because a ship that can't be hit matters more than a ship that can hit." He tapped the rings. "So the twins get their rings now, while they're being built, empty. Because you can't cut a hole that size in a finished hull without unmaking it. And the guns go in when there's a stone to hold them still."

"And if there's no stone?"

"Then they fire from the water, or not at all. And the keel guns fire straight down." He drew his finger down through the hull's bottom, where two long wells went down through the keel. "Straight down, the kick goes straight up, into the lift. The lift takes it. That one we can do now."

Ward looked at the drawing a long time.

"Then why draw them," he said. "The big ones. If you don't know."

Emeric thought about it.

"Because the stone might come before the war," he said. "Or the war before the stone. I don't know which. And if the stone comes first, and we haven't cut the rings, we'll have a stone and nowhere to put it." He rolled the drawing up. "It's a promise. To ourselves. That we'll have something to put in them."

The welders heard about it, somehow, the way welders hear about everything. By the end of the week they were calling the empty rings the promises; and by the end of the month the whole yard was, and nobody but Ward and Emeric knew where the word had come from.



By midnight on the fourteenth he had stopped thinking about the guns, and was thinking about walls instead.

He sat on at the drawing table after Ward had gone, with the stove burning down and the rolled drawing under his elbow. Every mage in the tower knew that stones bolted into a wall could throw a man back off it, any way you liked. The Forge's lattice, in its anchors, pushed a melt in from every side at once. And every gravity stone in every hull Kaldo had ever flown pushed up, or down, and no other way, because that was what a free stone did; and he had built every ship Kaldo had on it without once asking why.

So why could a bolted stone push sideways, and a loose one not?

Not the bolt. A bolt was a piece of iron and gave a stone nothing. It only kept the stone pointing tomorrow the way it had pointed today, so that when it was told to push along a line, the line was still there. The stone did not want ground. It wanted something still to push from, so that it knew which way was which. Bolt it to a wall, and the wall was still. Let it loose in a hull, and the only still thing it had was the world's own down; so down was the only way it knew.

And there was a thing aboard the K Discovery that did not move, whatever the hull did.

He cut the stone between one and four in the morning, with his hand flat on it and his eyes shut, gone in: a gravity stone, a blank no bigger than a walnut. The quiet clause first, at the bottom, under everything, as on every stone since the oven shed. Then the push. Then he put his head down on the drawing, and at five he sent for Tess.

She came at seven in her chair, with the K Discovery's spare steady in its box on her knees and Fletcher's terms for the loan of it in writing, which came to this: that it came back the way it went. She looked at the walnut on the blotter, and at him, and at the dead stove, and said nothing about any of them. She made him say the shelf aloud, and wrote it. If the wheel loses its line, stop pushing sideways, go back to up and down like any stone, and do nothing else. Then take your still from the wheel, and push along your cradle's line, as hard as you are told and no harder. She drew her small box round the first line.

They built it on the bench under the window with the shutters closed, because a working stone glowed. The steady went on a sled, a foot of planed oak on two waxed runners. The stone went into a little brass gimbal pinned to the steady's inner ring, so that whatever held the wheel held the stone. Tess had it lift first, the right way up, against her spring balance, and the needle went round to twenty pounds. Then Emeric turned the cradle a quarter round on its pins and locked it, so that the stone's down pointed along the bench at the far wall, and Tess hooked the balance to the sled's tail and held it.

She spun the wheel up until it hummed. He told the stone to push.

The violet came up in it, faint in the shuttered room, and the needle went over, and over, and stopped.

"Nine," said Tess. "And a bit."

"About half."

"About half." She let go.

The sled went along the bench, slowly and then less slowly, the runners whispering on the oak and the wheel humming in its rings; and it made no other sound at all. No hiss, as an air stone hissed; no wind. The page of Tess's open book did not lift as it went by. At the bench's end Emeric caught it, and it leaned into his palms and went on leaning, like a dog at a door.

He sent it again, and halfway down the bench rapped the steady's case with the handle of a screwdriver, hard, so that the rings banged against their stops and the wheel went over in them with a sound like a dropped spoon.

The sled stopped.

It did not lurch or slew. It sat on its runners with the violet still lit in its cradle; and Tess put one finger under its nose and lifted, and it came up a good deal lighter than a foot of oak and a brass wheel had any right to be, because the stone had lost its still and gone back to the only way a free stone knew.

"That's the box," said Tess.

"That's the box."

She wrote it down.

"Will it hold a gun?"

"No." He had known that since about three. "Half a lift, sideways, and the kick's as big as the shell. Fill her keel with these and she'd still go backward." He switched the wheel's motor off and let it run down. "The guns wait for the bucket."

"Then what's it for?"

"Standing still. A ship in a wind drifts. Fletcher holds her over a spot by hand, and she still walks off it a yard at a time." He tapped the cradle. "This holds her where she is, level, as long as you like, and nobody hears it."

"What do I call it?"

"A held stone. The wheel holds it, and it pushes off what's held."

She wrote it at the head of the page and underlined it twice.

"Her steadies go in on the eighteenth," she said.

"Then these go in with them. In the keel, beside the steadies, before the plating closes."

"How many?"

"As many as I can cut by then."

"So not many," said Tess, and closed her book. "Bram'll be sick he missed it. He'll ask which way up you put the cradle, and I'll say the right way, and he won't believe me."

Outside, the riveters started, one and then all of them, the yard's thousand clocks. Tess took Fletcher's wheel away on her knees, to go back the way it went, apart from the knock, which she said she would not mention; and Emeric opened the shutters on a grey morning and sat down again by the sled, and thought about a wheel a good deal smaller than that one, and where on a man you might put it, and did not write it down.



Her cabins were Imani's.

That had been settled years before, after the Hod: no ship of Kaldo's went into the Band again with a cabin that Imani had not set the mark for. Iron in the walls, a skin of sheet iron between the oak and the plating, to dull the Band to a seep; a scrubber in the pump line, a long box of spent stones that drank the mana out of the air coming in; and the bypass round the scrubber that Imani set by hand, to the mark, so that the people inside breathed exactly as much of the Band as the weakest of them could take and no more. Every ship had it from the keel up now. The K Amara would have it at every sealed door, a head tank to each compartment, like her diesel.

Imani came to the drawing office on the fifteenth to set the marks for the K Amara's compartments, with her own book, and her shade card, and two years of numbers.

She laid them out on the table. Every charge run the Hod had flown in two years; every man and woman aboard her, every run; who had bled at the Band's foot and when, and how much, and at what mark, and for how long after. She had it all in her own small square hand in columns, and she had drawn a line through it in red ink, and she put her finger on the line.

"Look," she said.

He looked.

The veterans did not bleed.

Not at the mark. Not with the scrubber shut. Not at all, now; not Ned, not Cade, not Old Edrin, not the hands who had flown the racks every week for two years. Through the first year they had bled like everybody, at the foot, after an hour, the nose and then the gums; and then later, after an hour and a half, after two; and then, some time in the spring, she could not say when, because it had crept, they had stopped. She had shut the scrubber on purpose on three runs in October, with their leave, at the Band's foot, for an hour, with her watch in her hand, and they had sat in the Hod's house with the Band coming in unfiltered and complained about the cold and played cards, and nobody had bled. Not a drop.

"New hands still do," she said. "Every one. The ones who came on in the summer bled the first week like the veterans did two years ago." She ran her finger down a column. "Arthur doesn't. He's been aboard nearly as long as Ned. He stopped in the summer." Another column. "Mira and Bren bleed. Later than a new hand. A good deal later. Mira at two hours and a bit, Bren at nearly three." She looked up. "A year on the racks. Two years for Arthur. And the ones who've been up there longest don't bleed at all."

"They've been brought up to it."

"They've been brought up to it." She sat back. "Slowly. Without anybody trying to. Just by going up, every week, for two years, and sitting in it, and coming down." She looked at him. "You did it with the town. With the water. Slowly, so nobody tears. It's the same thing. A body can be brought up to dense mana if you're slow enough." She tapped the red line. "That's what this is."

Emeric looked at the columns a long time.

He was thinking about the edge of the world. About Zale at the corner table under the inn's stair, telling him about water that got thick past the last islands, and then hungry; about a run-down lamp stone that had come back from out there full on its own. About what it would be, to take a ship out there, past the islands, into a sea that dense, with a crew of new hands who bled in the Band after an hour.

And about a crew who did not.

"Fit them anyway," he said. "The scrubbers. Every compartment. For the new hands."

"I was going to," said Imani.

"And write that down." He tapped the red line. "Not in the ship's book. In yours. That it can be done. Slowly. And how long it took."

Imani looked at him with her head on one side.

"You're thinking about something," she said. "That isn't this ship."

"Yes."

"Is it something I'm going to have to set a mark for?"

"Some day," said Emeric. "A long way off."

She looked at him a while longer. Then she wrote it down in her own book, under the red line, in her square hand, with the date; and closed the book, and put her hand flat on his on the table for a moment, not counting anything, and took it away, and went back to the ward.



Ferra did the K Amara's tanks on the sixteenth, and saw the figure.

She did all the ships' tanks. She had since the K Discovery's first voyage: how much diesel, how much water, how much food, how much of the Band in canisters behind iron, how many days aloft and how far, all of it in her book in columns before any of it went into a hull. The K Amara's diesel set was the biggest Kaldo had built, two great engines on a common bed in her after hull driving the dynamos that drove her screws and her pumps and her lights, their crankshafts built up in sections because nothing in Kaldo could forge one that long in a piece. And her tanks were sized for getting home, not for going anywhere, because Kaldo had only one oil well that it could not admit to having; and Ferra had the figure for how far getting home was, because Emeric had given it to her.

She looked at it. She looked at the map on the drawing office's wall, where the four baronies were and the capital was and the Isle, and Duvrenne across the strait, and the Emberfell coast in the west. She looked at the figure again.

It was more than any of that. A great deal more. Twice the farthest thing on the map, there and back, and a margin on top.

She looked at him across the table. He was drawing a valve and did not look up.

She did not ask. She wrote the figure in her book exactly as he had given it, and drew a small box round it, and went on to the water.



The first glass tower in Kaldo was topped out the same week, on the civic ring, and Vantic asked him up to see it.

It was hers. House Ducatis's, rather; the House's bank, which had moved its seat to Kaldo two years ago and its money with it, and had been building the tower since the spring on the plot at the ring's south-east corner that Ferra's office had sold it, in sight of the dome. Twelve storeys. A steel frame, the welders' again, riveted and bolted and welded where welding would serve; and hung on the frame, from the second storey to the top, glass. Plate glass, from the glassworks by the river, the big panes that Ivo's men had learned to roll and grind flat that year, each one taller than a man; so that from the avenue the tower was a column of windows, grey in the day and gold at dusk, with the steel showing through it like the veins in a leaf.

They went up in the lift. It was the second electric lift in Kaldo after the new hospital's, a cage on a cable in a shaft with its motor at the top, and it had brakes on it that would bite the shaft's rails if the cable went, which Vantic had had demonstrated to her personally, twice, with sandbags. She stood in the cage with her gloved hands folded in front of her and watched the floors go by through its grille, one after another, and said nothing at all until they stopped at the top.

The top floor was empty. Bare concrete, raw steel, a great square room with glass on all four sides from the floor to the ceiling, and the light coming in through it from the west, low and gold, across the whole of Kaldo.

He had not seen the town from this high except from a ship. It was different from a ship. A ship moved. This stood still and let you look.

There it was. The dome, close, a great round of glass on its colonnade over the inner ward, catching the sun; and the six water towers round it with their blue cones; and the avenues going out from it, four straight lines of lamps just coming on; and the red tile roofs of the wards packed between them; and the walls; and beyond the walls, the brickfield and the station and the station's chimneys, and the long black line of the new road going out toward the cutting; and away to the north-east, at the river's fork, the port, with its cranes, and the yard, and on the yard's middle slip, small and black from here as a line drawn with a pen, the K Amara's keel.

Vantic stood at the glass and looked at it.

"What is she for?" she said.

She did not turn round. She did not need to say which.

Emeric came and stood beside her at the glass.

He thought about the story. The flying market, the shop with wings. The town liked it. Vantic's own clerks had probably told her it, in good faith, at breakfast. He thought about what Vantic had done for him in nine years, and not done, and written down, and not written down; and about the box Ferra had drawn round a figure that afternoon without asking.

"She's for the day my father dies," he said.

Vantic did not move.

"Or the day before it. If somebody can't wait." He looked at the keel. "She's for the day somebody comes south with the crown behind him, or the Church does, or both of them do, and wants what's down there." He nodded at the town. "She's a warship. The biggest anybody has ever built. She'll carry the other ships' orders, and their guns' orders, and enough knights to hold a city, and she'll fly over anything that stands up and look down at it, and I hope to Doran's Light and anybody else who's listening that nobody ever makes me use her for what she's for." He was quiet. "And after that."

"After that."

"After that, if there is one, she's for going a long way." He looked south-east, past the port, past the fork, to where the sea was, grey now in the dusk. "Past the last islands. Further than anybody's gone and come back."

Vantic stood at the glass for a long time.

"Thank you," she said, at last.

"For what?"

"For not telling me it was a market." She turned her head and looked at him, for the first time since the lift; and her face was what it always was, still, careful, giving nothing away that she had not decided to give. "My clerks believe it. That's as it should be. I'd have known you'd lied, and I'd have had to decide whether to write it down." She looked back at the town. "Now I don't."

The lamps came on along the ring below them, one after another, round the dome.

"It's a beautiful tower," he said.

"It's a bank," said Vantic. "It has to be beautiful. Nobody puts their money in an ugly one." She paused. "Livia wanted a garden on the roof. The House said no. Glass is cheaper." She did not explain that, and he did not ask her to. "Perhaps on the next one."



They put the stones in at night.

That had been the rule since the K Discovery. The yard built the hull; the yard built every frame and plate and rivet of her, all winter, in the open, and any man who worked there could walk home and draw her lines on a tavern table for anybody who bought him a drink. Let him. A hull's lines did not fly. What flew was the matrix in her belly, the lift stones in their racks and the trim stones and the steadies and the latch and the inducer and the stones that told them all what to do; and those went in last, at night, after the yard was shut and the gangs were home, by the few who had sworn. Bram's best. The six, when they could. Emeric. None of the welders who built her would ever see a stone go into her. They called it the fitting gang, the ones who came at night; they knew it existed and did not know who was in it, and Saban had seen to it that the few who had tried to find out had found out wrong.

The first stones went into the K Amara's keel on the night of the eighteenth: the deep ones, the steadies, set in their cradles in the spine itself before the hull's bottom plating closed over them for good.

And the yard, that night, was full of steam.

It came up off the water of the slip's channel at a little before midnight, white, thick, rising off the black water in the cold like breath off a horse; and spread over the slip, and the keel, and the welders' sheds, and the cranes' feet, and lay there, a long low bank of it, the height of a two-storey house, so that from the yard's fence, where a man might stand in the dark with a spyglass, there was nothing to see but white. Under it, at the keel, the lamps of the fitting gang went to and fro, small and gold and blurred, and did not show.

Emeric stood at the slip's head in the white, with his hands out, and held it.

Fire, to make the steam: heat poured into the channel's water along its edge, a long strip of it, not enough to boil but enough to smoke in that cold. Water, to give it body, lifted off the channel's surface in a fine breath and thrown into the heat. And air, a very little air, to lay it down: the lightest touch, not a push, a lean, so that the white went where he wanted and stayed there, low, over the slip, and did not go up into the night and blow away as it had in September on the river, like a sheet off a line in a gale.

Less wind. Ward had said so. Give it somewhere it would rather be. He gave it the slip, and the cold night air over the slip that was denser than the warm, and let the steam settle into it the way water settles into a hollow, and only leaned.

It held.

It held for four hours. It cost him, steadily, in the slow way that a long holding cast costs, so that by three in the morning his arms ached to the shoulders and there was a dull ache behind his eyes; but it held, and lay over the slip in the dark, and nobody at the fence saw anything at all. At four, when the last steady was in and the plating's first plate was tacked over it, Ward came up out of the white to the slip's head where Emeric stood and looked at him, and then up at the bank of steam lying flat over everything like a laid sheet.

"Better," said Ward.

From Ward that was a great deal.



Bram came to the plant on the nineteenth.

He was not supposed to be out of bed. Imani had said a month. It had been three weeks and three days; and on the morning of the nineteenth Ferra went in to take him his breakfast and found the bed empty and his boots gone and his left arm's sling hung neatly over the bedpost, and came down the stair at a run, which nobody had seen Ferra do in years.

He was at the condensing plant at the secret dock, where Emeric was, with his sling back on, because Tobe had made him put it on at the gate.

He had come to see the column go.

The column had been on the plant's floor for a month, under canvas: the second one. The first, the plant's own, stood where it had stood for two years in its iron frame at the floor's end, tall and lagged and humming, drinking the Band's mana out of the air that the ships brought down and dripping it, pale blue and cold, into the reservoir that fed the forge and the dome and the racks; and it had broken, in its first year, eleven times. The valve seat. The pump packing. The glass. Bram's men had mended it every time and learned something every time, and in the second year it had broken twice, and since the summer, not at all.

And at the floor's far end, beside it, stood a pine box, chest-high, with a lid; and on the lid, in Tobe's chalk, two years old, the letters a little rubbed: NOT YET.

Emeric had told the six why, two years ago, when Bram had asked why the column could not go up in a ship and make its own liquid in the Band instead of the ships carrying the air down to it. Three things. It broke; it broke all the time, and a thing that broke all the time could not be mended at four miles up by men who could not breathe. There was only one Band ship to spare, and she was the K Discovery, and she was needed for everything else. And the liquid could not come down. Not safely. Not in any amount. A tank of it in a hull that went down through the air into thin mana was a tank that wanted to boil, and if it ever went wrong, it went wrong into a room full of people. Tobe had chalked NOT YET on the box that day, for the column that would go up one day, and the box had sat there since, under the chalk, with the drawings in it.

The new column was the box's drawings, built.

It was smaller than the first, and lighter, and better: two years of the first one's breakages built out of it, a new valve seat that Bram's lapping room had made, packing that did not weep, the glass thicker where the glass had cracked. Its pump ran on an electric motor, off the ship's own dynamos and her batteries, so that there would be no fire anywhere near it. And it fed not a reservoir on the floor but a tank: a great round tank of welded steel plate, double-walled, with nothing between its two walls but empty space, pumped empty, so that no heat could come in through it from outside; the same trick as Ivo's silvered flask, two years before, the size of a teapot, made the size of a brewer's vat because the yard could now weld plate. It would sit in the K Amara between her hulls, outside the sealed rooms where the people were, so that if it ever warmed and boiled, it would boil out to the sky and not into anybody's lungs.

And its liquid would never come down. She would drink the Band at the Band's foot, and make her own liquid there, and keep it in her own tank, and use it there. The thing that could not safely come down would not have to.

It broke no longer. There was a hull to spare. And the tank never came down.

"Three," said Bram, when Emeric had walked him round it. He had stood by it with his good hand on its lagging and said nothing for a long while. "You said three things."

"Three things."

"That's three." He looked at the column. "That's all three."

"That's all three."

Bram took his hand off the column, and walked across the plant's floor, slowly, because of his ribs, to the pine box at the end; and stood looking down at the chalk on its lid. NOT YET. Then he lifted his good arm, and with the sleeve of his coat, in three long slow strokes, rubbed it off.

He did not say anything. He looked at the clean lid a moment. Then he turned round, and saw Ferra in the plant's door with her breath coming short and her face white, and Imani behind her with her bag and her face not white at all but a colour much more dangerous; and said, "Ah."

"Bed," said Imani.

"I've come to see the..."

"Bed. Now. Or I'll have Ward carry you, and he'll enjoy it."

Bram looked at Emeric for help. Emeric found something very interesting in the column's pressure gauge.

Bram went to bed.



The Hod went out on the twenty-second, at first light, south-east.

It had been Ned's idea, and then Imani's numbers, and then Emeric's. If the Hod's veterans no longer bled at the Band's foot, they could stay up longer; and if they could stay up longer, the Hod could go further on a run than she ever had, with the scrubber shut and her racks charging the whole way, and nobody would come down sick. How far, nobody knew. Ned wanted to know. Ned had flown her for two years and had never once been allowed to find out what she could do.

So she would go out over the sea, south-east, where there was nothing: no coast, no fishing grounds, no lanes, nothing that anybody in Kaldo had ever had a reason to sail to, so that nobody would see her; and where there was water under her the whole way, in case her stones failed and she had to come down. That was Ned's rule. Fall room. A ship that might fall flew over water. And she would go out for a day, and as far as she could, and turn, and come home, and Imani would have her numbers.

Ned at the helm under the cupola. Emeric. Arthur, Mira and Bren, the three of them who had grown up on her racks and in her house, Arthur for two winters and the other two for one, and who would be the test: two who still bled, late, and one who did not. Declan and Osric in their flight suits, because Ward would not let the Hod go anywhere without two knights who could fly, and Ward was right. Cade, for the racks; and one of the new hands, a port boy called Sully, because Imani wanted one new hand aboard to compare. Nine. Ned's rule was four aboard, never three, and nine was a great deal more than four, and Ned was content.

In the Hod's hold, in their canvas bags, eight of Abloh's tents. She carried them now on every run, by Ward's order, since the Proving class had gone north under them in the snow; a ship that came down somewhere strange should have a roof to put over her people when she got there. Abloh had come down to the dock the week before to see them stowed, and had looked at the bags in the hold with an expression of deep personal satisfaction, and then at Ned, and said that if any of them came back with a hole in it he would know whose fault it was.

Ferra was on the dock at dawn with her book, in her grey coat, with the frost on the boards. She had done the Hod's figures the night before. She read them to Ned at the ladder's foot, as she always did: the charge in her racks, the canisters behind iron, the water and the food and the hours.

"Out today and back tonight," she said. "Late. You'll be on the dock by midnight tonight if you turn when you said. If you don't turn when you said, you've got three days of water and food and four of charge, and then you're a boat." She looked up at Ned. "Turn when you said."

"I'll turn when I said," said Ned.

"You've never once turned when you said." Ferra closed her book. "The Long Table's on the twenty-fifth. If you're not back for it, Maren will kill you. I'll help."

She looked at Emeric, at the ladder's foot, with the frost on his collar.

She did not say anything else. She did not kiss him, on the dock, in front of Ned and the hands; she reached out and turned his collar down where it had folded under, as she had on the night before the war, years ago, and let her fingers rest a moment on the cord at his neck, and took them away.

"The twenty-fifth," she said.

"Before."

"Before," said Ferra; and wrote it down.

Imani was not on the dock. Imani was at the ward, because a child had come in at four with a fever. But on the Hod's chart table, in the house under the cupola, when Emeric went up the ladder, there was a folded paper under the chart weight with an I on it; and inside it, in her square hand, the Hod's crew's names in a column, with a space beside each for a number, and at the bottom: Write them down every hour. Bring them home. All of them.

And under that, smaller: Bring yourself.

He put it in his coat.

Ned lifted her off the dock at twenty past six, into the grey, with her two hulls dripping and her fins catching the first light; and turned her south-east over the fork and the marsh and the long dun line of the coast; and opened her out over the sea.

The coast went behind them. The sea went on ahead, grey and flat and empty to the edge of the world, with the low sun coming up over it on their left hand, and nothing on it, nothing at all, not a sail, not a rock, not a bird; the sea that nobody in Kaldo had ever had a reason to cross.

"Nothing out here," said Ned, at the helm, pleased, as if he had made it himself.

Emeric stood under the cupola with Imani's paper in his coat and looked south-east at the nothing, and his sense, which he kept open as he always did, went out through the house's iron, on purpose, with the pull behind the eyes it always cost him in a hull, ahead of the ship over the water as far as it would go, and found nothing either.

Water. Cold water, and deep, and thin in mana, the way the sea round Kaldo was always thin; and under it, nothing he could feel; and ahead of it, for as far as he could reach, more of the same.

He took out Imani's paper, and wrote the hour beside the first column, and went down to take Sully's pulse.
