Chapter 44 - The Long Water

Saban came back with the sand, and with a piece of news he thought was small.

The sand was everything Emeric had asked for. It came up the water in forty sacks, pale as flour beside the river sand, and it ran through the fingers the way river sand never did. Saban unloaded it into the foundry yard himself, because he did not trust a boatman's back with a load he had been paid to care about, and he mentioned the news in the middle of the unloading, as an aside, in the voice a man keeps for weather.

"There's a boat maker down at the lower water," he said. "Building on the beach. Fletcher, he said his name was. Says he used to build up here, before. Asked after your soap man by name."

Emeric had a handful of the sand. He put it back in the sack.

"Say that again."

Saban said it again, with the air of a man who had not expected to be asked, and then, because Emeric was looking at him in a way that was new, he added what else he had seen. Hulls on the beach. Boats working the channel through the bar. A salt house. Smoke from more chimneys than he had troubled to count. People.

Emeric went for the tithe map.

It was the old one out of the parish, decades old, drawn by a long-dead clerk to record which household owed the church what. He had had it since his second week in Kaldo and had used it for three years for everything his own drawings did not cover. His pencil was on it in a dozen places. He had marked wells on it. He had marked the divisions on it with Ferra, arguing over the ninth. He had spread it on this table more times than he could count, and he spread it again now and looked, properly, at the bottom half.

The barony did not end where the town ended.

He had known that. He had known it the way a man knows the far side of his own house is there. The two branches ran down past the island and joined below it, and the joined water ran on south a long way, through ground the clerk had drawn as reed, down to a lagoon and a long pale bar with a single channel cut through it, and then the sea. And along that water, all the way down, there were marks. Small ones. A square for a house, a cross for a chapel, a name written small beside a cluster of squares. He had seen those marks every week for three years. He had never once asked what they were.

It got worse the longer he looked, which is the way that kind of thing generally goes.

In his first summer he had bought salt off that coast. He had paid a boatman to fetch it and paid him badly, and the boatman had gone down and come back and gone again the next month, and Emeric had written salt, coast, by boat on a list and filed the coast under suppliers. Somebody had boiled that salt. Somebody had loaded it. In the same summer he had gone partway down the same water himself, to a river village in the reeds where a sailmaker lived three streets from a shipwright, and had brought the sailmaker home and left the shipwright where he was, because he had nothing to offer a man who builds hulls. He had written the shipwright down as unresolved. He had never been further down the water than that village in his life.

He sent for Abloh, who came up from the soap house with lye on his sleeves, heard the question, and seemed to find it a good deal less surprising than Emeric did.

"Fletcher went down to the beach the spring after I came up to you," Abloh said. "Where else would he go? There's nothing to build in a river village. Anything big gets built at the bottom now and sold where it sits. It doesn't come back up that current."

"You knew where he was."

"I knew where he went." Abloh shrugged. "You never asked. I thought you'd decided."

Emeric had not decided anything. He had stopped asking, which is a different thing, and which feels from the inside exactly like having decided.

It was nobody's fault, and that made it worse.

Kaldo meant the island. To the people who lived on it, it had always meant the walls and the port and the farms across the Left Bridge, and nothing past them. Downriver was another country. Nessa had told him so on his fourth day in the town, and he had heard it as an answer to a question about two men, not as a piece of information about a barony. The tithe map was older than most of the people in Kaldo. Wexler had never gone down there. No collector had made the trip since before the plague, so those places paid nothing, were asked for nothing, and appeared on no roll anybody had kept since. They were Kaldo in law and nobody's in practice, and they had been getting on with it.

Ferra came when he sent for her. She read the map. Then she went to her own shelf and read her own ledger for his first year, and found the salt line, and went very quiet.

Her count was one thousand eight hundred and forty-seven. She had built it by hand, household by household, against the tax roll and the tithe book and the parish register and her own feet, and it was the best piece of work anybody had done in that barony in a generation. It counted the island and the near farms, because that was where the records were.

"There are people down there," she said.

"Yes."

"How many."

"I don't know."

"Neither do I." She put her finger on the lowest of the marks, the cluster by the bar. "I have been paying them. There is a line in my own book for their salt, in my own hand. I wrote down what it cost and who carried it and I never once asked who made it. They are not in my count. They are not inside it anywhere. They are on top of it."

She said it flatly, and the flatness was the fury. Emeric had seen her angry about Wexler's roll, about the invented men and the invented pay, and that had been a hot, clean anger, because it had been somebody else's lie. This was her own number. She had built it and signed it and let a barony be run on it for years, and it was wrong, and it was wrong upward, and there was nobody to take the paper to.

"I'll need to go down," she said.

"Not yet," he said. "I'm going first."

She looked at him for a moment and did not argue, and he could see her deciding to argue later.

He sat up with the map that night after she had gone.

Three years of arithmetic on a barony. Wells, divisions, the main, the census, the garrison, the foundry, the war. He had priced the burning of his own crop to the bushel before it happened. He had never once walked the southern half of his own land, and it had not been hidden from him in any way. It had been drawn on the table in front of him the entire time, and he had been putting his cup down on it.

He could have sent for them. That would have been quick and cheap and correct on paper, and it would have been the same mistake made a third way.

Three years before, he had stood at a blacksmith's back for six hours in a hot shop while the man worked on past the point where the work ran out, and he had learned the one thing about Kaldo he had never since had to relearn, which was that people notice who comes to them. A man who is summoned tells you what the summons wants to hear. A man who is visited tells you what he thinks.

He took Ward. Nobody else.

They went down along the joined water in the second week of winter, on horses, with one pack animal, on what the tithe map called a road and the ground called something else.

It was the first time in more than a year that Emeric had been outside anything he had built, and he noticed it on the first morning and could not stop noticing it. No wall. No wheel. No pipe under the ground. The island fell away behind them and the farms thinned and stopped, and the land went flat and wide and wet, and then the reeds began. They ran on both sides for most of a day, taller than a man on a horse, and the path through them was a raised bank that somebody had built a long time ago and nobody had mended since. Late on the second day the air changed. He tasted salt before he could see anything that explained it.

The bandits were at the worst stretch of the bank, where it narrowed between two pools and a horse could not turn.

Four of them. They came out of the reeds on foot with a rope across the path, two short spears and a hatchet between them, and a great deal of confidence, and it was a perfectly reasonable decision on the information they had. Two riders, no escort, no colours, a good horse and a laden one, on a road that nobody guarded. Their information was that nobody from the town above ever came down this way, and that anything which did come down it was worth taking. Both halves of that had been true for years.

Ward wore his harness under a riding cloak, as he wore it everywhere, and they did not know what they were looking at. Nobody outside Kaldo did. Black Kaldo steel over the old capital crystals, and to a man with a hatchet it looked like a heavy coat.

It was over in well under a minute.

Emeric barely moved. He had his hand on his own sword and did not draw it, because by the time drawing it would have meant anything there was nobody left to draw it at. Ward did not hurry and did not shout. He did the work in the order it needed doing, and then stood in the path with the rope at his feet, breathing somewhat harder than usual, and watched the reeds for a while in case there was a fifth.

There was not.

Emeric got down and looked at them. Four men, none of them old, all of them thin in the way people get thin when a road is their only harvest. He did not feel what he had half expected to feel. There was no lift in it anywhere. What he felt was closer to the irritation of a column that does not add.

This road was his. Men had been working it for years, by the wear on the rope, and nobody had come, because nobody knew it was anybody's to come to. Whatever those four had been, the reason they had been standing there was a policing failure, and the policing was his.

They did not leave them in the reeds. Emeric would not, and Ward did not suggest it. They covered the four with their own cloaks and marked the place with the rope tied to a stake, and at the first hamlet they reached, late that afternoon, Emeric paid two men to go back with a cart and bring them in, and paid the chapel for the burying, and wrote the place down.

He wrote the rest down that night, under the heading he kept for things that were his fault. Southern road. Unpoliced. Four dead. And beside it a note to send men and make the stretch safe, which he knew perfectly well he could not afford that winter, and wrote anyway.

In the hamlet he had noticed Ward watching him. Not the hamlet. Him. It was the look of a man checking whether another man had enjoyed something. He had not, and Ward seemed to see that, and went back to watching the hamlet.

Four men had seen Kaldo steel outside the barony, and not one of them was going to tell anybody. Emeric thought about that later and did not like how tidy it was.

It came back on the third morning, past the last of the reeds, with the lagoon a grey line ahead of them and nothing alive in sight but birds.

The same pull. No direction in it. A weight behind the eyes, as though something had leaned against the edge of a sense he had no name for. He reined in without deciding to. Ward reined in beside him, looked at him, and then looked at the empty flats in every direction, and found nothing, because there was nothing there to find.

That was the problem. Ward was at his stirrup.

In an alley in Kaldo, in his first week, he had felt exactly this, and a few days later Bram had mentioned that Emeric's big man had spent a quarter of an hour on the tannery roofline, and Emeric had crossed the alley off. Ward, watching him. Explained. He had been relieved to cross it off. He had not reopened it, and he had stopped counting.

He could not give this one to Ward. Ward had not been out of arm's reach in three days.

So he went back through it on horseback, in his head, the way Ferra would have gone back through a ledger, and found that it had never balanced. The banquet hall in the capital, the night before the road. The treeline the night of the tea, with the fire going and the horses picketed. The alley. And now a mudflat with nothing on it but birds. He had explained one of four and let the explanation stand for all of them. Crossing one off was what had stopped him counting, and he understood that now, sitting a horse in the cold, and it did him no good at all, because knowing what he had done wrong gave him nothing to do right. He had four points and no line through them. He had no mechanism.

That was worse than having no explanation. Before, he had not known there was anything to explain.

He said nothing to Ward. Ward did not ask. They rode on.

The lower water was a working port.

Not large. Not dead. That was the first thing, and it took him some time to get past it. Fishing boats pulled up in rows on the lagoon side. A salt house with its pans steaming in the cold. A long beach of pale sand, the same sand that was sitting in sacks in his foundry yard, with three hulls standing on it in frames, one of them nearly finished. Coasting boats, broad and shallow, moving in the channel through the bar. A quay of piled stone that somebody had built properly a long time ago and somebody else had kept up since. Smoke from sixty chimneys, or seventy. A few hundred people, at a guess, who had not seen a tax collector or a soldier or a lord since before the plague, and had been getting on with it.

They received a prince of the blood with mild interest.

Nobody bowed. Nobody ran for anybody. A woman gutting fish on the quay looked at Ward's harness a moment longer than she looked at Emeric, and went back to the fish. Two children followed the horses as far as the salt house and lost interest. An old man mending a net asked who they were, was told, and nodded as though he had been told the price of rope.

One man put down his mallet and came over, and he was the only person in the place who looked concerned.

"Does this mean we're going to be taxed?"

Emeric had spent three years learning what it cost to lie to people in this barony, and he answered honestly.

"Probably. Not this year. I don't know what it would be, and I won't know until I know what you have. I'll tell you before it happens."

The man thought about that. "That's not much comfort."

"No," said Emeric. "It isn't."

The man went back to his mallet, and that was the whole of the ceremony.

Fletcher was on the beach.

He was older than the stories, which was the first thing. Nessa had talked about a man who built half the hulls that ever left the Mere, and the man on the beach was grey and slower in the back than that man would have been, and he was working, and working well, on a hull a good deal smaller than the ones he had built when the city port was alive. Emeric watched him set a plank for some time before going over. The plank went down the way a thing goes down when the man putting it there stopped needing to check years ago.

Fletcher knew who he was inside a minute. It was not difficult, and it did not impress him.

"You came down as far as the reeds once," Fletcher said. "For Abloh. Three years back."

"Yes."

"You didn't come on to me."

"No. I had nothing to offer a man who builds hulls."

Fletcher weighed that and seemed to find it honest, which did not make it any better. "When he went up to you, I came down here. A river village is no place to build anything without him." He put the mallet down on the plank. "So. Why has the crown turned up now?"

Emeric told him. The sand, and a trader's aside, and a map he had owned for three years. He did not dress any of it.

Fletcher found the part about the sand funny. He found the part about Abloh funnier.

"Boiling fat for a prince," he said. "He always said cloth was wasted on this coast. I told him he'd end up in a kitchen." He looked up the beach toward the salt house. "Your first salt came out of those pans. I'll wager nobody ever told the woman who boils it what it was for."

"Nobody ever told me who boiled it," said Emeric.

Fletcher gave him a look that was not quite approval and was not far from it.

Emeric did not pitch.

He had come down the water with a shipyard in his head, and a port, and a future, and he left all three of them tied to the horse. He had stood in enough doorways by now to know what enthusiasm with no plan sounds like from inside the house. So he asked what Fletcher was building. He asked who it was for. He asked what Fletcher could not get.

Then he listened, for most of an afternoon, sitting on an upturned hull with the wind coming in off the bar, and answered questions when he was asked them and not otherwise.

What came out was simple and it was not good. Timber was the constraint. What grew near the coast was salt-stunted and twisted, and straight timber had to come down the water from the hills and was dear. Iron fastenings were worse. What came down the coast was expensive and soft and rusted through in two seasons, and a hull is only ever as good as what holds it together. And there was no reason to build anything larger than what stood on the frames, because there was nobody to sell a larger hull to. The coasting trade used what it had. The fishermen used what they had. The big yards up the coast built for big houses, and nobody from a big house had set foot on this beach in living memory.

Emeric said one thing, once, when Fletcher had finished.

"Kaldo has a foundry now. It casts to a pattern. Everything we make is cut to one measure, and the fittings come off the same mould every time."

Then he left it there.

Fletcher said nothing to it for a long while. He picked a fastening up off the sand, a dull brown nail as long as a hand, turned it over, and put it back down.

"I'll think about it," he said.

Bram had said that once, in a hot shop, after six hours. Emeric had learned since what it meant in this barony. It was a yes with a waiting period attached, and the worst thing a man could do with it was try to shorten the wait.

He thanked Fletcher and did not try.

Nobody had agreed to anything. They rode home the next morning.

The strategic fact did not arrive as a triumph. It arrived on the second day, in the reeds, as a cold thing, the way the arithmetic of a mistake usually arrives, all at once, with the whole sum showing.

The barony had a working coast. It had a shipwright who knew his trade, a beach to build on, and a sandbar that made glass. It had a salt house and a fishing fleet. And it had a coasting trade, boats that had been going out through that channel loaded and coming back light for years, and not one person in the town above had the least idea where they went.

He had spent the war and every month since telling himself, and Ferra, and anybody who asked, that Kaldo's trouble was that it had no market. That nobody would buy from a plague town. That every road north ran through men who wanted him gone.

He had been saying it with a trade route running out of the bottom of his own barony the whole time.

He found Saban that night in the yard, still counting sacks.

"The coasting boats at the lower water," Emeric said. "Where do they go?"

Saban looked up. It was a question he had never been asked, about a thing he had never thought mattered.

"Down the coast," he said. "Mostly to Duvrenne."
