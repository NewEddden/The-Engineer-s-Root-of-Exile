Chapter 44 - First Surplus

They cut the wheat in the last week of August, on the baron's farm first and then out along the west side, strip by strip, with every scythe Bram had made in November and some that were older than Bram.

Emeric cut too. Nobody asked him to and nobody stopped him, and by the middle of the first morning the men on either side of him had stopped watching to see whether he would last and started watching to see how long it would take him to learn to keep the blade's heel down. It took him until noon. By noon his hands had come up in blisters across the heel of the palm, and Farris, passing behind him with a bundle of bands over his shoulder, looked at them and said they would be calluses by Thursday, which they were not.

Behind the scythes came the binders, women and children mostly, gathering the cut wheat into sheaves and tying each one with a twist of its own straw. Behind the binders came the parish clerk with his hazel stick, and the reeve beside him with his own, because the tithe took every tenth sheaf and it was counted in the field, as it had always been counted, before a single sheaf left it.

That was how the number came, and it came from nobody who wanted it to come out one way or the other: from the parish clerk and the reeve, walking the rows with their sticks and counting under their breath, and cutting a notch for every tenth sheaf.

Farris's field, the whole of it under the rotation, turned with the iron plow and sown from Hollis's picked seed, with his own dung on it, came out at eleven sheaves for every seven off his neighbor's strip on the other side of the headland. Same soil. Same rain. The reeve counted Farris's twice, because he did not believe it the first time, and then he went and counted the neighbor's again to be sure the fault was not there, and it was not.

Farris did not say anything when the reeve told him. He looked at his field, stubble now, with the stooks standing on it in rows, and then at his neighbor's, and then he took his hat off and wiped the inside of the band with his thumb and put it back on, and went to help his neighbor carry.

The neighbor came to Emeric that evening at the edge of the field, a little stiffly, and asked what it would cost him to put his strip in the rotation next year.

"Nothing," Emeric said. "The same as it cost Farris."

The neighbor looked at Farris's stooks. "It cost Farris a year of the whole west side laughing at him."

"Then it'll cost you less," said Farris, from behind them, and the neighbor went home.

Hollis's one strip came out well too, short of eleven to seven but better than his other strips, by enough that the reeve noticed, and Hollis stood at the end of it with his arms folded while the reeve told him, and nodded once, and said it had been a good year for rain, which was true.

Geddes did not put a strip in. He came and stood at the edge of Farris's field on the last evening of the cutting, a little back from it, as he had stood at his own hedge in November, and watched the stooks, with one hand closed on something in his pocket.



Then it rained.

It rained for six days at the end of August, warm soft rain off the sea that did not stop and did not get heavy, and the stooks stood in the fields and drank it.

Emeric went out on the third day and pulled a sheaf out of the nearest stook on the baron's farm and felt the heart of it. It was wet through, the straw dark and limp and the ears cold to the touch. He knew what wet grain did in a heap. It heated. It went musty and then it went black, from the inside out, and the first anybody knew of it was the smell. He wanted it in, under a roof, now, and dried, and he said so to Hollis, who was walking the next row with his hands behind his back looking at the stooks one by one.

Hollis stopped. He looked at the sheaf in Emeric's hands and then at Emeric.

"Put it back," he said.

Emeric put it back.

"Not like that." Hollis took it out of his hands and set it back in the stook himself, upright, leaning in, the ears up and together at the top with the other sheaves around it so that their heads made a little roof and the rain ran down the outside of the straw instead of into the middle. He did it in one movement, without looking. "You stand them up. Butts out, heads in, so the wind goes through the bottom and the rain goes off the top. Then you leave them be."

"For how long?"

"Till they've heard the church bells three times."

Emeric looked at him.

"Three Sundays," Hollis said, with the patience of a man explaining something to a child who has been to the capital. "You leave a stook in the field till it's heard the bells ring three Sundays running. Then it's fit to carry. Not before. Carry it before and it heats in the barn, and you've lost it, and the barn too, like as not."

"Three weeks."

"Three Sundays. Might be two and a half weeks if you cut on a Friday." Hollis looked at the sky, which was low and grey and still raining. "It's a saying. It isn't a sum. The wind dries it. The sun dries it. You can't hurry the wind."

He showed Emeric how to set a fallen stook back up so that it stood, and made him do six on his own, and corrected four of them, and walked off down the row without saying whether he thought Emeric had learned anything.

He had been in this world a year and a half, and he had read a good deal about farming in the other one, and none of it had told him that a stook was a roof.



Not everybody waited for the bells.

Some of the west side carted early, in the first dry day after the rain, because they had been hungry for four years and could not stand to see the grain lie out in the field another fortnight. They carted it into their barns and their lofts and their back rooms, and within a week there was a sweet hot smell coming out of half the barns on the west side, and men were forking heaps over in the middle of the night to let the heat out of them, and losing.

Kaldo had no granary. It had not needed one in four years. What it grew it ate, and what it did not grow it went without.

What came in after its bells went onto the threshing floors and under the flails, two men to a floor facing each other over the spread sheaves, the swingles coming down in turn, thump and thump, from first light until the dust drove them out to breathe. Emeric took a flail for one morning, hit the man opposite on the shoulder with it twice, and was given a broom.

The worst of it was the seed. Hollis's seed wheat, the best of his strip and the best of the baron's farm, picked over head by head in the field by Hollis himself for the autumn sowing, had gone into the baron's barn on the threshing floor, not early, after its bells, but in the one week when the barn was full of other men's wet grain heating round it on every side. On the fourth day after it went in Hollis put his arm into the heap to the elbow and brought it out and smelled his hand, and came to find Emeric.

"It's warm," he said. "It wants drying. Now."

Emeric could have done it with his own hand, held a fire over the heap for hours as he had held the retort, and it would have cost him a night and a headache. He did not have a night. He had the barns of the whole west side heating, and the granary that did not exist, and a mason and a carpenter waiting on him at the span at first light.

Armand had a stone.

He had a case of them on the bench under his window, tower stones: two lamp stones, one of them spent, and a pair of plain fire stones loaded in the capital, of the kind the tower used to warm its reading rooms in winter, each about the size of a walnut and dull red in the light. He brought one down to the barn himself that evening, slowly, with his hand in his coat pocket and his face grey from the hill, and showed Hollis how to set it in the iron dish on the threshing floor under the slatted rack where the seed was spread, and how to wake it, which anybody could do, by closing a hand round it and wanting heat.

"It will give heat until it is told to stop," Armand said, "or until it is empty. It will not be empty for a month."

"And who tells it to stop?" Hollis said.

Armand looked at Emeric. "Either of us."

The stone glowed in its dish. The air above it shook. Within the hour the rack above was warm to the hand and the damp was coming off the seed in a faint visible breath, and Hollis stood looking at it with his arms folded, plainly not liking it and plainly not able to say why.

Emeric meant to come back at midnight and stop it.

At midnight he was out at the span with the mason and a lantern, looking at a heap of broken stone in the field below it, and he did not get back to the keep until two, and when he got back to the keep he lay down in his boots for an hour, which became four.

Armand, it came out afterward, had gone to bed at nine, because the prince had said he would see to it.

Hollis found it at first light.

The near end of the rack had caught. Not much: the slats over the dish had charred through and two had burned and fallen into the dish, and the fire had gone along the rack a little way and then gone out on its own in the damp seed. The barn was full of thin smoke. The stone sat in its dish under the burned slats, glowing, perfectly content, doing exactly what it had been told, and nobody in the barn could make it stop.

By the time Emeric got down from the keep, with Rylan running ahead of him, Hollis had pulled the seed off the rack with a rake, the whole of it, onto the floor, and was standing over the heap.

The near half of it was brown. Not burned. Toasted, as dark as bread crust, and dry as sand. The far half looked as it had looked the night before.

Emeric closed his hand round the stone in its dish and told it to stop, and it stopped, and went dull, and he took his hand away with the heat of it still in his palm.

Hollis did not look at him.

"The far half's sound," Emeric said.

"The far half's sound," Hollis agreed. He bent and picked up a handful of the brown and let it run through his fingers. "This was the best of it. The middle of the heap. It was on top, over the dish, where I put the best, because the best wants the most care." He let the last of it fall. "I'll sow the rest. I'll sow this, if I have to. Seed's seed."

"Not that. That's been cooked. It won't come up."

"You don't know that," Hollis said, "and nor do I," and began shoveling it into a sack.



The rule went up that afternoon, chalked on the door of the baron's barn in Emeric's own hand, and the next week painted on a board by Abloh and nailed up inside the doors of every barn on the barony's land, with a picture under it for those who could not read it, of a stone in a dish and an eye.

No stone runs unless somebody who can stop it is standing beside it. No stone runs without a glass.

The glass was a sand-glass, a big old one Hale found in the keep, which ran an hour and had to be turned, and somebody had to be there to turn it. Armand read the board on the barn door twice, standing in the yard, and then went back up to the east tower and did not come down for two days, and when he came down he did not mention the barn at all, and neither did Emeric.



The granary went up in nine days, because it had to.

The mason found the stones for it lying in the field below the span, where they had been lying since the old span came down: the spoiled ones, cracked through or split at the face, that he had picked over in April and thrown aside as no use for an arch. They were good enough for this. He cut sixteen of them into squat posts and sixteen into wide round caps, and set the posts in four rows of four on a level bed of gravel on the baron's farm, with a cap on every post. The carpenter laid a floor of heavy beams across the caps, and built the granary on the floor, a long wooden barn on stone legs with slatted vents along both sides under the eaves.

A rat could climb a post. A rat could not climb out round the underside of a cap. The air went under the whole floor and up through the slats. Nothing touched the ground.

Tess, when she came out to look at it, said it was a line on its knobs.

The town carried the grain in. Every barn on the west side that had heating grain in it was emptied onto carts, and the grain was spread on the granary floor a hand deep and turned with wooden shovels morning and evening by the men whose grain it was, and some of it came back, and some of it did not. What was black at the heart of the heaps went to the pigs, and then to the midden. What was sweet went into the granary, and stayed sweet.

The water watch closed its sluices the same week. Farris's students had run it all summer, fifteen of them off his school mornings, turning the boards on the channels below the field tap by a schedule chalked on the pier in big figures and little pictures: which field had water on which morning, for how long, and whose turn it was to lift the boards and whose to drop them. They had not missed a turn all summer. Now the wheat was in and the schedule said nothing, and three of them came out to the pier on the first morning anyway, out of habit, and stood looking at the chalk, and had to be told.



Ferra drew up the sheet at the end of September, at her table, when the last of the grain was in the granary and the reeve had brought his tallies down the hill.

She showed it to him without a word, the way she showed him anything she thought he ought to look at for himself.

Seed set aside for the autumn sowing, at the full measure, with Hollis's brown sack written against it in its own line and a ring round the line. Winter stores for every household on her count, at the real number, not the roll's. The burials, against the winter's deaths, at last year's rate. The school's bread. The tithe.

And under all of it, after all of it, a line that had not been on any sheet of Kaldo's since the plague.

Over.

It was a granary floor a hand deep and a little more, and nowhere in the world to sell it that was worth the cartage, and no promise at all about next year. But it was over and above every other line on the page, and it was there.

"It won't stay like this," Ferra said.

"No," Emeric said. "But it did happen."

She looked at him, and then down at the sheet, and did not argue.

The school had opened again the week before. Sela had a hundred and thirty-one names on her list by the end of the first week, and stood at the door counting them in, and sent Arthur up to the keep at noon with the number on a slate, because she said the prince would want it and she was not walking up that hill to tell him.
