Chapter 121 - The Passage

They lodged in the outer ring, in a stone house in a lane off the south road, because Ward chose it.

He chose it on the evening they came in through the city's south gate, the twenty-second of July, after riding past two inns on the south road that would have done and that Ferra had looked at with longing. He chose it for reasons he gave nobody until Emeric asked him on the stair, which were that it had one door to the lane and one to a yard with a wall round it, that the stair was stone and turned to the right going up so that a man coming up it had his sword arm against the wall, that the passage at the top ran the length of the house and was flagged and narrow and had four doors off it and a window at the end, and that the woman who kept it had buried a husband who was in the crown's foot and did not like questions. Ward liked all of that. He said so, in that order, in the voice he used for a road.

They had the four rooms off the passage. Emeric at the window end. Ferra next, with the three boxes. Ward next. Rylan and Holt in the last, by the head of the stair.

For three days nothing happened.

Emeric kept to the four places he had told Armand he would keep to, and did not go anywhere else. The chancery, on the twenty-third, to lodge the barony's arrival with the clerk of the court and have it receipted, which the clerk did with a little start of surprise at the name, and looked at Emeric a second time, and then a third. Keller's room, on the twenty-fourth, for a quarter of an hour, where Keller did not stand, and asked whether the barony's witnesses had arrived, and was told that Paxton of Milford was lodged at the posting house by the north gate at the barony's expense, and wrote it down, and dismissed them. The posting house, to see Paxton, who received him in the yard standing up and said that he had come to say it in court and would say it in court and saw no need to say it first in a yard, and Emeric agreed, and went away. And the lodging.

On the twenty-fourth, on the chancery steps, coming out, a man in white went past at the bottom of them.

A cleric. Not a bishop; plainer, with a black cord at his waist instead of gold, and a younger man walking a pace behind him with a satchel. They were going along the street toward the cathedral end of the inner ring, talking, and the cleric did not look up at the steps at all. Emeric had already turned. He had turned before he knew he was doing it, back up two steps and into the chancery's doorway, and stood in the shadow of it with his hand on the doorframe until the white had gone along the street and round the corner. Ward, beside him, did not ask why. Ward had heard Armand too.

That was all. Three days.

On the night of the twenty-fifth, the night before the hearing, the woman who kept the house went to bed at ten and put out the lamp at the foot of the stair, as she put it out every night. Ferra went to bed at half past ten, after she had read the witness column in the summons book through twice more and Emeric had heard her through the wall shut the book and put it under her pillow. Ward did not go to bed. Ward sat in his room with the door open a hand's width and a candle on the floor behind him where it would not show in the passage, and his sword across his knees, and the bracer on his arm with its black Kaldo iron and its rewritten stone.

Emeric lay on his bed in his clothes in the dark with his boots on and did not sleep either.

He heard the yard door at a little after two.



It was not loud. A latch lifted, and the creak of a door that wanted oil, and then nothing for a long while, so long that he began to think he had heard the woman of the house going out to the privy. Then, very faintly, on the stone of the stair, the sound a soft boot makes on a step when the man in it is trying to make no sound at all.

He got up. He did not light anything.

He did not need to. He let himself go out, thin, the way Armand had taught him by the fire in December, not far, only into the passage and down the stair: and the house came back to him in the dark the way a dark room comes back to a man who claps his hands. The passage, long and narrow and flagged. Ward in his doorway, a wall, utterly still. Ferra behind her wall, asleep, small and close. Rylan, awake, quick, with the half stop in his beat. Holt asleep.

And on the stair, coming up it, five.

Five notes he did not know. Ordinary men, a commoner's warmth each, nothing in them that a stone would flinch at. Three in front, close together, and two behind. And something cold and hard and narrow in each of their right hands, that gave back nothing at all, the way steel gives back nothing to a mage's sense: knives.

He came back into his body at the window end of the passage with the stone in his fist that he carried now in his left-hand pocket, always, with an edge on it, and the edge biting.

The first of them reached the top of the stair.

Ward was already in the passage.

Emeric did not see him go. One moment Ward's door was open a hand's width with the candle behind it, and the next the candle was out and Ward was standing in the middle of the dark passage between the stair head and Ferra's door, and the man at the top of the stair had stopped dead with his knife half up because a shape had come out of the dark in front of him where no shape had been.

Then the man went down.

Emeric heard it more than saw it: a sound like a sack of meal hitting a flagged floor, and the knife skittering away across the stones, and Ward already past where the man had been and into the second, who had come up the last steps behind the first and had time to see nothing at all. The bracer. Emeric felt Ward call it, felt the stone at his wrist go bright and hot in the dark like a coal blown on, and Ward moved the way Emeric had watched him cross the yard in March, in three steps, too fast to follow, and the second man went back down the stair the way he had come up it, all of it, without touching a step, and hit the turn at the bottom with a sound that Emeric did not let himself think about.

The bracer went bright again. And again. Ward did not stop to breathe.

Three left, on the stair. Two of them had turned to run.

The third had not. The third was the one at the back, and he was quick, and he had seen what was happening at the top of the stair and gone the other way from it: down three steps and over the stair rail into the gap between the stair and the passage wall, where the passage ran back past the stair head toward Rylan's door, and he came up out of that gap in the dark at a run, low, with his knife held under his forearm the way Rylan's book said a man held a knife who knew what he was doing, straight down the passage past Ward's back, toward the window end.

Toward Ferra's door. And past it, to Emeric's.

He did not think. He had told himself in Kaldo, and on the road, and in the yard at the posting house with Ward's blade at his head, that he would not, here, whatever happened. Not in this city. Not a day either side of its walls. And the man came at him down the dark passage with the knife under his arm, and Emeric's left hand closed on the stone in his pocket, and he felt it come up in him, all of it, the heat and the hardness going down into his arms like water finding its level, the thing Armand had heard in his note and told him to leave at home, and he could have taken the man's knife wrist in his right hand and closed it.

He did not.

He held it. He held it down, close in, the way he had held it in the yard at dawn, and let it go out instead.

Fire. His. The one thing he did that the whole kingdom knew he did.

It went out of his right hand as a flat bright sheet of flame across the width of the passage at the height of a man's face, not hot enough to kill, a flare, a door of light opening in the dark, and the man running into it threw his arm up over his eyes with a scream and came on blind. Emeric had sent it all outward. For the half breath it took to come back in, there was nothing in his arms but a man who had drilled at dawn for four years, and he stepped to the side the way Ward had drilled him a thousand mornings and the man went past him, and the knife went past him too, along his left forearm, and opened his sleeve and the skin under it from the wrist half to the elbow, and he felt it go and felt nothing.

And then Rylan had him.

Rylan had come out of his door at the stair end when the first man went down, and gone the long way, behind the stair, and come up the passage behind the man who had gone over the rail, and he came out of the dark behind him now as the man staggered past Emeric with his arm over his eyes and the flame still hanging in the air, and hooked his ankle. Not hard. A leg hold. Rylan's foot behind the man's foot and Rylan's weight on the man's hip and a twist, the way he had taught six men in the yard on the morning of the boat because Osric had laughed at him, the way it was drawn on the fourth page of Close Quarters with two arrows and the word Here. The man went down on his face on the flags. Rylan went down on top of him with one knee in the small of his back and his hand on the knife wrist, and turned the wrist, and the knife came out of it.

"Stay," said Rylan, to the man under him, as he would have said it to a dog.

The flame went out.



The two who had run were gone. They had gone down the stair over the man at the bottom of it and out of the yard door and over the yard wall, and Holt, who had woken at the first sound and come out of the last room with his crossbow and had nobody to shoot in the dark, had heard them go over and stood at the window at the stair head watching the lane, and saw nothing.

Ward came back up the stair with a candle.

He had lit it from somewhere below. He came up slowly. He was breathing hard, which Emeric had very rarely seen, and the bracer on his arm was dull and warm, and his sword was in its sheath; he had not drawn it. He held the candle up at the stair head and looked down the passage.

The first man lay at the top of the stair, on his back, with his eyes open and his head at an angle that was not one a head could be at. The second lay at the turn of the stair below, and Ward did not hold the candle that way.

The third lay on his face in the middle of the passage with Rylan's knee in his back. He was very young. When Ward brought the candle down the passage Emeric saw that he was not much older than Arthur, a thin boy with a bad haircut and a split lip, and that he was crying, without making any sound, with his face against the flags.

"Two," said Ward. He looked at the boy. "And this one."

"Five came up," said Emeric.

"Two ran." Ward looked at Holt at the window, who shook his head. Ward held the candle up and looked at Emeric's arm, at the sleeve hanging open from the wrist to halfway up and the dark wet length of the cut under it, which had begun to drip on the flags. He looked at it for some time without saying anything.

"It isn't deep," Emeric said.

"No," said Ward. He looked at Emeric's face. Then, very briefly, at Emeric's right hand, hanging at his side, which had not touched the man with the knife, and had not closed on anything, and was shaking. "You let it go the right way," Ward said, quietly enough that Rylan, on the floor, would not hear.

Emeric did not ask him what he meant. He did not need to.

Ferra's door opened.

She was in her shift with her coat over it and her hair down, and she had the summons book in her hand. Not a candle. The book. She stood in her doorway and looked at the passage: at Ward with the candle, and Rylan kneeling on the boy, and the dead man at the top of the stair, and the knife on the flags where Rylan had thrown it. And Emeric, against the wall at the window end, with his arm.

She looked at the arm.

She looked at it for the length of a breath. He saw her face go white, which it did not do easily, because it was white to begin with. Then she looked away from it, deliberately, the way she looked away from a column she would come back to, and opened the summons book.

"Who's in the house," she said.

It was not a question. It was the start of a list.

The woman who kept it, who was at that moment coming up the stair from her room off the kitchen in her nightgown with a poker, and stopped at the turn where the second man lay, and said something to God. Her boy, who slept in the scullery and had heard the yard door. Two other lodgers on the floor above, a cloth merchant from the west and his clerk, who had come down the upper stair in their nightshirts and were standing at the top of it holding each other's arms. The lamplighter, who had been in the lane under the window when the two went over the yard wall and had seen them, and had shouted, and was now at the lane door hammering on it. The night watch for the ward, two men with a lantern and a pike, who came round the corner of the lane at a run at the lamplighter's shout.

Ferra had every one of them inside a quarter of an hour.

She did not ask Ward or Emeric to help. She went down the stair in her coat with the summons book open on her arm, past the man at the turn without looking at him, and stood in the kitchen of the lodging house by the fire, and had them brought to her one at a time, and wrote. Their names. Where they had been. What they had seen and heard and at what hour by the cathedral bell, which had struck two not long before the yard door. Who had come in by the yard and who had gone out over its wall and which way they had run down the lane. She had the lamplighter tell it twice, and the second time he remembered that one of the two had a grey horse waiting at the end of the lane, tied to the rail of the tannery, and that he had heard it go.

Then she had the watchmen sign it. Both of them. Under their own account, which she had written down from their mouths, of what they had found when they came up the lane, and the time by the bell. One could write his name. The other made his mark, and Ferra wrote his name beside it, and he looked at it and nodded.

When she came back up the stair, the kit Imani had packed for the road was open on Emeric's bed and Ward was holding his arm under the candle, cleaning it with spirit from Rylan's flask, and it hurt the way spirit hurt.

Ferra stood in the doorway with the book.

"Nine," she said. "In the house, the lane and the watch. Every one written and signed, or marked. The watch captain's coming at dawn for the dead and the boy." She looked at the arm under Ward's hands. "Keller will want them all. He'll have them all."

"Good," said Emeric.

Ferra did not move from the door. She stood and watched Ward bind the arm, three turns, firm and flat, the way Imani bound a wrist, and tie it off.

"You've ruined that shirt," she said.

"Yes."

"It was the one you were going to wear in front of Keller." She looked at the shirt, lying on the floor where Ward had cut it off him. "I'll find you another. There's a man two lanes over who sells them to clerks." She paused. "It'll be a clerk's shirt."

"That's fine."

"I know it's fine," said Ferra. She did not go. "Your hand's shaking."

He looked at it. It was. His right hand, on the blanket, the one that had not closed.

"It'll stop," he said.

Ferra looked at it a little longer. Then she came into the room, and put the summons book down on the end of the bed, and sat down on the bed beside it, not close, and put her own hand flat on top of his on the blanket, and held it there. She did not look at him. She looked at the door.

They sat like that while Ward finished with the kit and packed it and went out to the passage, and while Rylan's voice came up the stair telling the boy, quietly, over and over, that nobody was going to hurt him if he lay still, and while the cathedral bell struck three.

When the shaking had stopped she took her hand away and picked up the book and went back to her room.



The boy talked before dawn.

He talked to Rylan, which surprised everybody but Rylan. He lay on the floor of the passage with his hands tied in front of him so he could wipe his face, and Rylan sat on the flags beside him with his back against the wall and his legs out and did not ask him anything at all, only talked, about Kaldo, and the river, and a boy of nine who had flown over it on his father's back in the dark. And after a while the boy, who was from Wayford, and whose name was Coll, began to talk too.

A man had come to the tavern at the bottom of the ferry slope at Wayford a fortnight before, and asked for five who could use a knife and keep their mouths shut. A man in a good coat, who had paid half in silver on the table and said the rest would come after. He had not said whose silver it was. He had not needed to. Everybody at the bottom of the ferry slope at Wayford knew whose silver was in every coat that came into that tavern, the boy said. It was the only silver in Wayford.

They were to come to the house in the outer ring on the night before the court. They were to go up the stair and find the room at the window end and the young man with the gold eyes in it, and see that he did not go to court in the morning. That was all. They were not to touch the woman with the boxes, because the boxes were to be taken, all three, and the woman would know where everything was in them, and would be needed to say so.

Rylan listened to all of it, sitting against the wall with his legs out.

"Two of you ran," he said, at the end.

"The two from the slope," said the boy. "Cousins." "They had horses. At the tannery." He wiped his face with his tied hands. "They've gone home."

"Not home," said Rylan. "Not if they've any sense." He got up off the flags. "There's no home for them at Wayford after this. Not with the man in the good coat." He looked down at the boy. "Nor you."

The boy looked up at him from the floor.

"What'll they do to me?" he said.

Rylan looked at Emeric, who was standing in the doorway of his room with his arm bound under a clerk's shirt that Ferra had bought from a man two lanes over at half past five in the morning.

"That's for the court," Emeric said. "You'll tell it to the court. All of it, the way you told Rylan. The tavern, and the man in the coat, and the silver on the table, and what you were told to do." He looked at the boy. "And then we'll see."

The watch captain came for him at dawn with four men and a cart. He went down the stair between two of them with his tied hands in front of him, and at the turn, where the second man had lain and did not lie any more, he looked back up at Rylan, once, and Rylan nodded to him, and he went on down.

The bell struck seven. The court sat at nine.

Ferra came out of her room in her good coat, with her hair pinned up hard, and the barony's book under one arm and the summons book under the other, and stood at the head of the stair.

"The boxes," she said.

Ward and Rylan went to fetch them.
