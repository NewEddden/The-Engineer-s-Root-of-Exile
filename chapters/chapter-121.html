Chapter 121 - Rowena

The herald's summons was eleven words long and said that the sovereign of Emberfell would receive the prince of Randillia and his party at dinner. He said it, and bowed, and went, and nobody in the long room of the harbour house said anything for a moment after the door closed.

"Sovereign," said Saban. "Not duchess. Not queen." He turned the word over. "She picked it before we were tied up."

Emeric went to dress.

He did it in the small room they had given him at the top of the harbour house, with a window on the mountain, and it took longer than it should have, because the coat had a great many fastenings and he had been out of the habit of anything that fastened at the throat for ten years. The suit stood in the corner under its cloak. Before he put the coat on he opened the small iron door in its breast and took the grey stone out, and opened the catch Bram had made at the back of the silver star, and set the stone back where Bram had set it. Then he pinned the half-cape at his left shoulder with it, and ran the silver chain across his chest to the epaulette, and looked at himself in the dark window.

He had not worn anything like it since the capital. He had forgotten how much of a court coat was built to make a man stand as though somebody were watching him.

Imani met him on the stair and looked at the collar.

"Did you eat?"

"I ate."

"Properly?"

He had eaten half a loaf and some of Maren's cold meat standing up in the galley of the ship an hour before, because Imani had sent him there, and he said so, and she looked at him as if she were deciding whether to believe a patient, and let him past.

The hall was hot.

It was a long room of dark stone near the top of the town, with tall windows open on one side to the night and the mountain, and even with the windows open it was hot, because the heat came up out of the floor. There were no fires in it. There were lamps along the walls, and a long table down the middle under them, and at the table, besides Kaldo's party, perhaps twenty people, men and women both, in dark good clothes, who had plainly been told who was coming and had plainly been told nothing else.

She sat at the head of it.

She was not in armour. She wore a long coat of deep blue, high at the neck, and no jewellery at all but a plain ring, and her hair was bound back as it had been on the quay with its rings of steel. The scar ran down her cheek in the lamplight. She sat very straight, with her hands on the arms of a chair that was not a throne but was plainly the chair everybody else's chair was measured from, and she watched him come the length of the hall toward her.

He had thought about what to call her all afternoon.

On the quay the watch captain had called her *Your Grace*. The herald had called her sovereign. She was Harrold's queen; she had never stopped being Harrold's queen, and a prince of Randillia was entitled to call his father's wife by the title she held in his father's house. And she was Emberfell's, by birth and by something else he did not know the shape of, and this was her hall, in her country, and he was a foreign prince at a foreign table with no letter.

He bowed as a foreign prince bows to a sovereign.

"Your Grace," he said.

She inclined her head. She did not correct him. She said that Emberfell welcomed the prince of Randillia and his people, and that she hoped the harbour house was adequate, and she said it in a voice that was entirely correct and entirely cold, and waved him to the chair at her right hand.

It was the right bid. He knew it was the right bid because she took it without a flicker. He had not expected it to feel like a door closing.

She did not speak to him again for most of the meal.

She spoke to the woman at her left, who was grey and heavy-shouldered and wore the harbour's badge, about the dues on the salt boats. She spoke across the table to a thin man who was the head of the great works at the foot of the mountain, about a furnace that had cracked. She spoke to a knight-captain about the watch. She was courteous to Kaldo's people when they spoke to her, and asked Saban a question about the coasting trade that told Emeric she already knew the answer, and asked Ferra nothing, and looked at Ward, standing against the wall behind Emeric's chair, once, in exactly the way she had looked at the three hooded men's feet on the quay.

And once, between the fish and the meat, her eyes went to the brooch at his shoulder, and stayed there.

It was longer than courtesy allowed. It was long enough that he saw her see the grey stone in the middle of the silver star, and something go across her face and away again, the way a cloud's shadow goes across a field. Then she looked back at the head of the great works and asked about the furnace.

She did not say anything about it. Not in the hall.

After the meal she stood, and the table stood, and she said that she would speak with the prince alone.

It was a small room off the end of the hall, with one lamp and two chairs and a table with a jug on it, and a window on the mountain, which was glowing faintly now at its top in the dark, a dull red like the last of a forge. Ward stood outside the door. Her own knight stood across from him on the other side of it, a tall woman with her helm under her arm, and the two of them looked at each other once and then at the wall.

Rowena shut the door.

She did not sit down at once. She went to the window and stood at it with her back to him for a moment, and he watched her shoulders, and saw the moment the hall went out of them. It was the same thing he had seen on the quay, when she let go of whatever she had been holding in her face and decided a thing was not going to be a fight. Then she turned round, and she was somebody else.

She sat down, and poured two cups from the jug, and pushed one across the table to him.

"Your Grace," he said, and began to thank her.

"Stop that."

He stopped.

"In there," said Rowena, "you're a prince at my table and I'm a sovereign at the head of it, and you got it right, and I'm obliged to you, because if you'd got it wrong I'd have had to make you pay for it in front of twenty people who'd have enjoyed it." She picked up her cup. "In here I knew you when you were small. Don't *Your Grace* me in here."

"Then what?"

"Rowena will do." She looked at him over the cup. "It did for your mother."

He sat down.

She watched him drink.

He knew she was doing it and did not know what to do about it. It was wine, dark and strong and a good deal better than anything Kaldo had ever poured, and he drank it the way he drank anything, which was a little at a time, and set the cup down between, and did not reach for it again until he had finished what he was saying. He had done it that way for ten years. He had not thought about how he did it since the night he woke up in the capital with somebody else's life in his head.

She watched him drink half the cup like that, and set it down.

"You weren't drinking," Rowena said. "Were you."

It was not a question, whatever shape it had.

He could not lie to her. He had decided that on the ship, somewhere over the coast, without meaning to decide it. And he could not tell her. The truth had only ever existed at the volume of a whisper, in one room, between two people, and one of the two was his father, and whatever had made it necessary was still alive and still somewhere and had not stopped being what it was because he had gone south. It was not his to say to anybody, and least of all to one of his father's queens, in a foreign country, in a room with a door.

So he gave her something true.

"You corrected my grip," he said, "when I was twelve. Nobody else bothered."

She looked at him for a long time.

He watched her read it. He had watched a great many people read a thing he had said and come out of it with the wrong answer, and he watched her come out of it with the right one, or near enough, and it happened in her face a piece at a time: the boy on the yard, and the eight years of the drunk, and the cup half full on the table in front of her, and the shape of what did not fit.

"I left," she said slowly, "because of your mother. And I stayed as long as I did because of my son. And the whole of the nine years between, I watched Amara's boy go to the bad in front of the whole court, and his father let him, and nobody lifted a finger." She put her cup down. "I thought he'd thrown you away."

He said nothing. There was nothing he could say that was not either a lie or the thing itself.

"I see," said Rowena.

She did not ask who. He had been waiting for her to ask who, and had been ready, and she did not. She sat back in her chair and looked at him across the table and the lamp, and he understood that she had worked out as much of it as she was ever going to be told, and had decided that was the part she was owed, and was not going to ask him for the rest.

"Your mother had one like that," she said.

She was looking at the brooch.

"On a cord," Rowena said. "Round her neck. Grey." She looked at the stone a moment longer.

She did not say what it was, or whether she had ever seen another. Emeric waited for more and there was no more.

She told him instead about his mother, which he had not expected.

It was a small thing. It was the kind of thing nobody tells a man about his mother because nobody thinks it is worth telling. Amara had hummed when she worked, Rowena said, whatever the work was, sewing or letters or the accounts of her household, and she had never once known she did it, and if anybody told her she would stop and look astonished and deny it, and then start again inside a minute. Rowena had told her a hundred times. It had never taken.

"You do it," said Rowena. "At the table. When you were listening to the harbour-mistress. I don't suppose you know."

He had not known.

On the other side of the door, Ward had heard it. Emeric knew he had, because the door was not thick, and because there had been a small sound on the other side of it, a boot shifting on stone, at the moment she said Amara's name. He did not look at the door. He did not think about the thing he had never asked Ward, which was the day his mother died, and he did not ask it now, with Rowena across the table. It was not tonight's.

She talked about her son freely.

Zale was somewhere past the edge of the map, she said, or he had been at the last letter, which was a year old. He wrote when he remembered and he did not often remember. His letters were very long and were mostly about weather and people he had met in taverns, and sometimes there was a line at the end about whether he was well, which he generally was.

"He's happy," she said. "That's the whole of it. He'd have been miserable anywhere they could have found him, and they'd have found him in the capital. So he went where they couldn't." She said it without any bitterness at all. "He was always better at that sum than any of us."

Emeric very nearly asked about Maeve.

He had the question most of the way to his mouth. Maeve had not come to his farewell, and nobody had had an answer for it, and he had asked twice and been given a bow and then a different servant, and he had never found out, and had stopped asking. And the woman across the table from him was Maeve's mother.

He did not ask. He closed his mouth on it.

Rowena saw him not ask. He saw her see it. She looked at him for a moment, and did not say anything, and let it go.

His eyes went to the scar again.

They had gone to it at dinner, three times, and on the quay, and he had not meant them to any of those times, and they did it again now in the lamplight. It was pale and straight and a little raised, from below her eye to the line of her jaw.

"Ask," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"I know you weren't. You've looked at it seven times. I'm a fighter. I see where a man's eyes go before he knows they've gone there." She said it flatly, as a report. "So I'll tell you, and then you can stop."

She told him the way a knight gives an account of a skirmish to a captain who was not there: in order, with nothing added.

Her brother had been meant to have the seat. Their father had said so. And he had it, and she was in the capital, and they were both content with that for a long time. Then she came home. And he could not bear her home. He wanted her gone and he could not make her go, and he could not make her disappear, so he needed it done in public, in the arena, by the old law, which said the land went to the strongest.

"He called me out," she said. "I went. I beat him." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the table. "I let him up. I told him the seat was his and I'd go back to the capital in a month and he'd not see me again. And I turned round to walk out of the arena."

She touched the scar with one finger, from the top to the bottom, once.

"He had a knife in his boot. I heard him move. I turned in time for my cheek and not my throat." She took her finger away. "So he's dead, and I have his chair."

Emeric did not say anything.

"And you're wondering," Rowena said, "why a woman with a court full of mages walks about with a cut like this on her face."

He had been.

"Because I had to stand up in front of my people that afternoon," she said. "The same day. A woman who had just killed her brother in front of them, and I was not going to do it with a bandage across half my face and let every one of them wonder what was under it. So my healers closed it. They put their hands on it and cast into it, three of them, for an hour, and by the evening it was shut." She shrugged, very slightly. "They closed it in a day. Left alone, it would have been a thread."

He understood it before she had finished. He had understood it since the night he sat across from Armand and said out loud what the tracery was.

Mana did not mend. It paid. It paid a body to do what the body was already doing, faster, and a body paid to build a thing in a hurry built it badly. It built scar. That was the whole of the tracery on a knight's arms, the price of the right total put in at a rate the body could not take, and it was the whole of the pale line down her face. Three healers for an hour, and a wound that would have closed in a fortnight closed by evening, and the tissue laid down in a rush and set that way. A slow stitch would have left a thread. The fast close had left this.

And no mage alive could take it off her now. A scar was healing that had already finished. There was nothing left in it for mana to hurry. Cast into it again and all it would do was lay down more of the same.

He thought of something else, then, and did not say it either.

He thought that one day he might have to fight a brother. He had one who wanted the chair, and who was kind to him in public, in front of everybody, for twenty years, and who had never once in all that time done anything that anybody could point to. He thought about an arena, and turning to walk out of it. He put the thought away where he kept the things that were not tonight's, and it went, but not all the way.

"If you ever decide you don't want it," he said, "come to Kaldo. By then I'll be able to fix it."

She looked at him.

"Fix it how?"

He did not know how to answer that in a way she would understand, or in a way that would not tell her things he could not tell her. He knew how it would be done. He knew roughly what it would take. He knew that the slow way left no mark, because Tobin's arms were clean, and that Imani could close a wound finer than any healer in the capital because he had once taught her to in forty minutes in a room that did not exist. He did not yet know how to put those together, and he had just promised her a thing he could not yet do.

"Slowly," he said.

Rowena looked at him a while longer, and then at the lamp.

"I've got used to it," she said.

She did not say no.

It was late when she stood up, and he stood with her, and she looked at him across the lamp, and something in her face changed again, back toward the quay.

"You walked through my yard today," she said.

"On the way to the harbour house."

"With your hand on my wall."

He said nothing.

"Tomorrow," said Rowena. "In the morning. My yard. Steel only. No casting, no armour." She said it the way she would have set the terms of a drill. "My rule."

"Yes," he said.

He said it at once. He heard himself say it too fast, and so did she, and she looked at him for the length of a breath with her head a little on one side, the way she had looked at a boy on a yard once when she had not yet decided what he was worth.

Then she opened the door, and Ward and her knight both came off the wall at once, and she went out past them into the hall without another word.
