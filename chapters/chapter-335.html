Chapter 335 - What His Patron Wanted

Ward hung the sack from the K Amara's after davit on the twenty-sixth of January, in the dark before the morning watch, with a lantern on the hook beside it; and when Emeric came up the ladder with his hands wrapped, Ward was sitting on a coil of hawser with his collar up, the way he had sat on the cart shed's post a year ago almost to the day, with a different strap three weeks off the same hand.

"Three hundred," said Ward.

"Imani said nothing heavy."

"Imani said a fortnight. It's been three weeks and two days. I asked her." Ward put his hands in his armpits. "She said if you were going to hit something you might as well hit something that couldn't hit back."

So Emeric hit it. The first dozen came off the right hand like a man knocking on a door he was not sure of; then the two new knuckles took it in their row and complained and went on taking it, and by the two hundred he was thinking about nothing at all, which was what the sack had always been for.

At two hundred and sixty Ward stopped counting. Saban had come up the after ladder in a heavy coat and stood at the head of it, as a man stands at the edge of a dance he has no intention of joining.

"Corbin," said Emeric.

"His Highness." Saban sat on the hawser beside Ward, who did not move up. "Thirteen days in his own wing, the west end over the long gallery. Osric's two at the gallery doors and two on the stair. His mother dines with him Thursdays and Sundays and talks about the weather, and he lets her. Your father hasn't been up." He turned his hat. "He's asked for one thing: the old yard behind the east range swept, the little one with the trough. He's down there every morning at seven in the frost with a long sword and no coat. Osric's two say he's good. They didn't expect him to be."

He had carried three sentences since December like coins nobody had asked him to spend: Ask your brother what the Light showed him; somebody in that yard sent him the road; twenty-eight years, and I've never asked him, and you won't either. He had said he would not ask. He laid them side by side again and did not add them up.

"Council sits on the first," said Saban, standing. "The old lord with the white hair, the one who put his hand on Grier's arm, has asked the clerk for the August roll. He wants to know whether the law they read against you reads the same backwards."



The council sat on the first of February under sixty-four candles the chancery had bought itself.

The king sat in the black chair with his boots either side of four pale marks, and Emeric stood at the foot of the dais on the left. Corbin came in by the great west door with two of Osric's people a pace behind him, in a plain dark coat and no red at all, and walked the length of the hall to the foot of the crown's table, where a man stood when the council had a question for him. He did not look at the chair.

When the rolls were done, the old lord rose on the third temporal bench, with a voice that had been loud once and kept the habit, and asked the clerk to read the law the council had laid against the house of Kaldo in August.

The clerk read it: that whoever moves against the king's person, by force or by fraud, moves against the crown; and that no man who has moved against the crown may wear it, nor his name stand in the line of it.

"By force," said the old lord. "That was the word in August. We've since heard the other word. A voice was bought, and the king was put in a room with a lock on it by a bought voice." He turned to the foot of the crown's table. "The under-purser said in this hall he carried the money on a warrant, and couldn't say whose. I'd like to know whose."

Keller sat with his hands folded on an empty sheet and did not look up. He had not been asked.

"Mine," said Corbin.

He said it to the clerk, as a man gives his name at a door.

"The warrant was mine, my lords. The purser had it from my hand, and the money with it, and I knew what it was for." He let a breath go by, the length of a courtesy. "I read that law into your record myself in August, against my father's son. I'd think less of this council if it read it any other way against his other one."

It was his mother's sentence again, and nobody in the hall but Emeric knew it.

Nobody asked for a division. The clerk wrote that His Highness the Prince Corbin had moved against the king's person by fraud, by his own word in council, and that by the law of the crown's person his name no longer stood in the line of the crown. Emeric heard the pen, and saw Corbin hear it. Nobody asked aloud who stood next. Everybody in the hall could count.

The king said nothing. He looked down the crown's table at his eldest son for as long as the pen took, and his face did nothing; then he nodded to the clerk. Corbin bowed to the dais the exact depth the custom asked of a subject and went back down the hall, unhurried, his hands behind his back.



The line came up to the K Amara a little after nine that night by a palace footman: one sheet, unsealed, in a large handsome hand taught to make a signature carry across a room.

I'll walk out for Emeric. Alone, and nobody else. The old yard, tomorrow, at ten. C.

Ward read it over his shoulder, which he had never done in thirteen years.

"No," said Ward. "Walk out. That's what they say in the capital when two men take it outside. Osric and six in the morning, up his stair, and he walks to the boat between them."

"In front of the servants," said Emeric. "Six of ours in black plate on his stair the morning after the council took his name out of the book, and by dinner the whole city has it that Kaldo dragged the king's eldest out of his father's house by the arms." He folded the sheet. "He's asking to walk out of it on his own feet. I'd like him to."

"He's asking for you."

"Because I'm the one he wants to hit. He's had a sword in his hand since he was nine, and he's heard what I am, and so has the lower town. Ten minutes. I'll let him hit me for nine of them."

"You think it'll be easy. That's the part I don't like." Ward turned his collar up. "Where do I stand?"

"Inside the gate. Nobody comes in. You don't come in unless I'm down and not getting up, or I say your name."

"And if he's down and not getting up?"

"Then you still don't."

"Those are orders," said Ward, as if reading them off a slate in a hand he did not like, and went below; and Emeric heard him on the ladder, three rungs, and stop, and go on, which was how Ward slammed a door.



The old yard was smaller than he remembered, which every yard is.

Raked sand forty paces each way inside a wall a little higher than a man; an iron gate under a stone arch; a weapons rack under a pent roof with the old ash blades gone grey; a stone trough on a flagged apron by the gate, with a pump over it and a skin of ice closing again where somebody had broken it. And the long south wall, pocked from end to end at chest height with small round craters close as the marks on a dice, where a young woman with her hair in a ringed tail had stood every morning for years and put a charged blade's point into the stone to see how deep it would go. Emeric had sat on that wall's coping at five, swinging his heels, watching her.

He came in at ten in a shirt and a dark coat with a blunted practice blade from the K Amara's rack. The suit was on Bram's bench seven hundred miles away with both its hearts broken.

Ward shut the gate and stood inside it with his back to the iron.

Corbin was by the trough in an old quilted arming coat faded from crown red to brick, a plain long sword bare in his right hand. He was bigger than Emeric remembered, heavier than a soldier, the gold hair pushed back with a wet hand; their father's height and their father's eyes, and six years the elder, though at forty-two he looked thirty-five. At the open throat and the wrists the tracery stood up under the skin in raised silver bands, unlit.

In his left sleeve there were three small stones. Emeric felt them before he looked, three held notes like coals in a closed fist: fire, earth, and something sharper, the same three he had felt in a lay brother's sleeve in the autumn. He said nothing about the sleeve.

"You came alone," said Corbin. "Mother said you would." He looked past him at the gate. "Nearly."

"Ward stays."

"Of course he does. I'd think..." Something went across his face that might have been a smile at himself. "No. I've said that one enough." He lifted the point. "I'll walk out. Afterward."

He was good.

Emeric had been told so and had not believed it, which was his own fault. The first cut came from high on the right with no wind-up at all, and the shock of it on the practice blade went to his shoulder. It went on like that: long cuts off the shoulder, a thrust out of nowhere, the point turning in the air the way a fish turns; thirty-three years of mornings, with a knight's weight behind every blow since the floor at sixteen. Emeric took them on the blade and on a plate the size of a dinner tray that came up out of the air before his left forearm when he wanted it. He stepped. He gave ground and took it back. He did not once cut.

He felt Corbin feel it through the steel. Every blow taken; none returned.

"Even now," said Corbin. He was breathing a little harder. "Even now you're holding back. I can feel it in the steel." The plate rang like a pan. "You think I'm a joke."

"I'm holding back," said Emeric. "I am."

Corbin's face went quite blank, as it never did in a room, and then red.



He stopped fencing. What came in its place was a big man swinging steel as hard as he could, wide, from the hip, with the floor's gift pushed into every swing; each one tore the cold air like a sheet. The silver at Corbin's wrists began to glow, faint, the colour of a coal under ash. The practice blade bent on the first heavy one.

"All that training," said Corbin, in the voice built for an audience of three, with an audience of one at the gate. "Every morning since I could hold one. And he still looks at you. What have you done? Tell me. Things just happen to you, and they go your way."

His left hand went to his right wrist as he recovered, the way a man straightens a cuff, and his thumb went down inside the sleeve.

The fire came off his forearm at a yard's range, no glyph, a lay brother's trick in a prince's sleeve. Emeric had it before it had finished being fire, by feel, and turned it back on itself, and it went into nothing a hand from his eyes with a soft cough, like a lamp blown out in the next room. Grey dust fell out of Corbin's cuff. Emeric saw him feel the first stone go dark.

"Your mother died," said Corbin, lower. "I know. Everyone knows. He made sure everyone knew; he wore it every day for twenty-eight years where we could see it. Mine's alive." A cut. "Mine's alive, and I've never once been her son. I've been her argument."

His thumb went down again. The yard floor got up under Emeric's feet in a long swell meant to throw him, and he let his knees go soft and rode it like a deck in a beam wind, and came down standing. Two of three. Augustine's gifts. He watched his brother count them.

"My father died too," said Corbin, quite quietly. "Nobody buried him. I see him every day in the corridor. He's dead to me and I'm dead to him, and we still sit down together when they ring for dinner."



Emeric decided then how it would end. Not by breaking him. He would make Corbin empty himself; a knight empties himself by pushing, and the way to make a man push is to make the ground cost. Shaping was cheap and enhancing was dear. Emeric began to shape.

A rut two fingers deep where Corbin's lead foot came down, so he caught himself with a push he had not meant to spend. A lip of packed stone under his heel, no higher than a doorsill, there and gone. Emeric gave ground in long exchanges so Corbin had to chase across ground that would not lie still, and brought the trough's water over its lip and slicked it across the flags in a dark tongue across the line Corbin wanted; in the frost the flags went white at the edges at once. Corbin came across it anyway, because he would not go round, and his boots went, and he pushed, and the silver at his throat came up a shade brighter for the cost.

"Fourteen years," he said, shouting now and not knowing it. "You built a duchy out of a ruin. You've two women who chose you. You beat the pox. You fed the north when the hoppers came, and you put my name on a bounty for eggs and made a fool of me in every parish in the kingdom. You laughed at me at that dinner." The plate; a step. "And he knew for two years the floor didn't have to kill anybody, and sent the crown's own boys south to you, and never told his heir. And he still writes to you. Waylon I could understand. Waylon sleeps with the horses. Why Emeric?"

Emeric let it go into the plate.

"I sat that chair for a year," said Corbin, and it came out low, not as a weapon. "I was fourteen. Nobody said thank you. They took the footstool away because it looked childish."

Zale, by a fire, years ago: he had them take it away again. Emeric did not say it. Both could be true of a boy of fourteen.

"And then he came home," said Corbin, "and went straight up the stairs to your door."

"He did," said Emeric.

Corbin made a sound and came at him, and the next lip of stone took his heel and put him on one knee, and he came up out of it with a push that threw sand to the wall. "You want to end us. You and your printers and your schools and your knights with no tracery on them. I am us. Don't you understand? I am us. What did you think I'd do?"

His hand went to his sleeve the last time.

Emeric could have turned the lightning as he had the fire. He let it come instead, onto a plate the size of a door; it cracked off the four walls and threw a white light on the pocked stone, and the plate drank it and rang and stood, and the sand at his boots was not even marked. Ward had seen it. That was the point: Corbin's best shot had landed in front of a witness and done nothing.

Corbin stood with his left arm still out, looking at the plate.

"You got to disappoint him," he said, without the voice, without anything round it. "I never had that."



Then the tracery lit.

All of it at once, wrists and throat and along the jaw to the ear, bright as wire in a forge, and going on past where it had any business to go. Emeric had seen where a light like that went: two dock-quarter boys on Imani's ward in a bad summer, who had forced it until the threads in them split from the wrists to the heart. Draining them had mended nothing.

Corbin did not know there was a line. Nobody had ever let him near enough to one to find out.

Emeric stopped being careful of himself. He let the plate go and went in, three yards in one knight's step, to get a hand on him; and Corbin, who was good, cut across the line of the closing arm. Emeric put his left forearm up into it, because that is what an arm does. The long sword opened it from the wrist halfway to the elbow, through coat and shirt and skin and the meat under them, until he felt the steel grate and knew what it had found.

His right hand closed on Corbin's sword wrist, and he drained him.

It was the easiest thing he had done all morning and the ugliest. He found the overfull lake in his brother, bright and splitting at its banks, and opened a way out of it through his own hand, and let it go: not into himself but past himself and out, into the sand and the frost and the yard's cold air, the way you open a sluice. The silver went out at the jaw first, then the throat, then the arms, a band at a time like lamps along a passage when the servants have gone to bed. When the last went out under his fingers he stopped, and left his brother what any man in the street had, and not a drop under it.

The sword fell on the sand. Corbin went down after it to his knees and knelt with his hands open on his thighs, pulling at the air. Emeric stood over him, bleeding into the sand in a steady rope off his left fingers.



Corbin went for the sword.

His hand closed on the grip, and Emeric understood with great cold clarity that this was the most dangerous moment in the yard. A commoner with a long sword could still open a knight's belly, and a knight with any mana in his hands could break a commoner like a dry stick, and there was nothing left between those two things for either of them to stand on.

He put his boot on the blade, took it from under Corbin's hand, and threw it into the trough through the new ice, and the bent practice blade after it. Then he took his own mana down, all of it, held down the way a man holds a struggling dog, until there was nothing in his hands but his hands. No plate. Nothing round the fist. The cut arm was much louder without it.

"Get up," he said. "No steel. Nothing out of your hands but your hands. Fight me like men."

Corbin laughed once, high, not a laugh he had ever let out in a room. Then he got up and came.

He came the way every knight in Randillia had come since the square went into the drill: low and fast, both hands open, for the wrist and the elbow and the belt, to get the hip in and put the other man on his back. Drained, he was still a big strong man who had wrestled every week of his life, and if his hands closed on a man with a cut arm, that man would go down and stay there.

Emeric put the jab in his face.

He stood the way a man named Ray had stood him in a room over a tire shop: side on, left foot forward, right heel up, hands at his cheekbones, elbows in over the ribs, chin behind the left shoulder; a stance nobody in this city had ever stood in. The left came out twice and home, quick, not hard, a fist on the end of a measuring rod exactly where Corbin wanted to put his hands; and by the time Corbin's hands came up to clear it Emeric was a step to the side, throwing a few drops of blood across the frost.

Corbin came again and Emeric slid away along the sand. The third time Corbin got a fistful of his shirt, and Emeric turned his shoulder out of it the way you turn out of a clinch you do not want; and as Corbin reached again the low ribs under the reaching arm were open, the floating ones, and Emeric put two short left hooks in them with the hip behind them.

Corbin made a sound like a man stepping into cold water, and his elbow came down to cover them. "You," he said. "You..."

He had the breath for a grievance. Emeric put the right hand straight into the soft hollow under his breastbone, and the new knuckles told him about it, and the sentence went out of Corbin in one piece with the air that would have carried it. He folded over the fist and stood bent, mouth open, getting nothing. Emeric stepped back and let him find it. It took a while.

"Even now," said Corbin, when he could, with a hand on his knee. "Even with your hands. You've nothing in them. I can feel you've nothing in them, and it's still..."

It was true, and Emeric did not answer it. Holding the mana down did not take back the knight's bone under the fist. He had only taken away what could be taken.

And this was why it had never once worked with Ward. Between a tap and a burying there was a finger's width, and in a body packed as dense as Ward's it was not there to find. But the man in front of him had been drained to a body of the kind the skeleton on Ray's wall had been drawn for, and the finger's width was there, and Emeric could feel exactly where.

Corbin reached for the clinch. Emeric let the reach come halfway, until his weight was on the front foot and his right side opened under the last of the ribs; and turned his back heel as if he were stamping out a coal, and the hip came round behind it, and the left hook went in short and flat and low into the place where the cage gave out. The one Ray had loved best.

Corbin's face did nothing. He stood with his hands half up, as if somebody had called his name from another room; and for a full second, while Emeric stepped away, he went on standing. Then his legs stopped holding him, both at once, and he sat down on his heels with an expression of enormous astonishment and a hand flat on his right side.

At the gate, Ward shifted his feet on the flags, once.

Corbin got up. It took a long time, and he stood swaying and put his hands up, badly, like a man trying somebody else's stance.

"Again," he said.

He could have ended it with the point of the chin, or the temple. He knew exactly what either did to a man with nothing in him, and he did not. The kidneys were open every time Corbin turned; he did not touch them. Not in Ray's gym. Not anywhere.

Corbin came in the last time, and Emeric slipped the reaching hand and put his right into the soft hinge behind the jaw, under the ear, and pulled it as it landed, the way you pull a door you only mean to latch. The lights went out of Corbin's eyes and came back, and he went down on his knees in the sand and did not get up again.

"What have you ever done?" he said to the sand. His face was red and wet and he did not seem to know it. "You've won. Say it. What have you ever done?"

Emeric crouched in front of him, slowly, because his own knees had begun to have opinions.

"I can't say it," he said. "It'd take a year." He held out his right hand, the one that was not bleeding, palm up between them. "I can show you."

Corbin looked at the hand as if it were a trick he could not see the back of; then up, with their father's eyes.

"Show me, then," he said, and put his hand in it, hard, like a man taking up a glove somebody has thrown down.



The room was where it always was: the long table, the shelves, the drawings, the slow clean light. He was there a breath before Corbin, because it was his, and he used the breath. He went to the window at the end that did not look out on anything in this world, and put its shutter to, and dropped the bar; then stood with both boots on the boards over the stair Armand had shown him on the night of his stone, and did not move off them.

Then he let his brother in.

Corbin stood inside the door like a man in a strange house at night with somebody else holding the lamp, understanding slowly that he was not in a yard any more and had not walked anywhere to leave it.

"Where is this?"

"Mine. Stay close. I'll open the doors."

In his own house he held the cue. Some doors stayed shut, and Corbin did not know they were there.

A carriage in sunlight, a blue coat with the buttons done up wrong, a road going up; and a little way down the road the room stopped at a wall, flat and grey, with no door and no crack. The road went into it as a road goes into a cliff.

"That's what 'your mother died' looks like from in here," said Emeric. "I was seven. I put it there myself. I've never once got through."

A corridor roped off. A cook's face, then another's. A tutor gone between a Monday and a Friday. Three bedrooms inside seven days, and two men on the door. "Four," said Emeric. "In fourteen months. While you were sitting the chair. They weren't trying to kill me. They were trying to take me." And the third bedroom in the evening, a man sitting on the edge of a boy's bed, which he had never done, with a face held pleasant over something enormous and furious; leaning in close, so the boy of eight adjusted his shoulders to be held and was wrong; and whispering.

Corbin went very still.

"He told me to be a joke," said Emeric. "A recluse, then a drunk, so nobody would think me worth the trouble of taking. Fourteen years. Every laugh you ever got off me at a dinner, I was paid for." A gallery at night, the same man older, a foot away, giving instructions in the order a man gives them. "And the exile was his. He'd waited three years for a reason ugly enough. You didn't win it. He let you think you had."

A river town under a grey sky, the aqueduct running a finger deep, doors marked for the plague. Sela's boy Tam on a door with a stick, and then not standing anywhere. A line through Harl's name in a book. A girl under the dynamo house with an apron over her. Dunnel on the coast road with a post he never delivered. Emeric gave them their names as they went by. "That's some of what it cost."

A vaulted room under a counting house, the flags scrubbed in long arcs; a ring painted round a drain; eight iron stands with eight stones; two rows of chairs; a child's shoe on its side by the drain, worn through at the toe. "Wayford," said Emeric. "The five houses, bleeding people for the stones. Children. That's the night I decided I'd have power, enough that nobody would build a room like this again in my reach. I wanted it and I went and got it. I'm not sorry."

A woman on a quay at evening with a ledger against her chest, counting hulls in. A woman going down a long ward with her sleeves rolled. Ward at the gate of every yard. A smith's hands black to the wrist round a pair of tongs. He did not explain it. Corbin could see the tool marks on all of it.

The last was the crown as he had always seen it from below the dais: a great dark weight that made a man sit still in a black chair for thirty years and never once say what he meant in a room with a door. He let Corbin feel what he felt looking at it, which was what a man feels looking at a beam he will carry up a ladder the rest of his life.

"I never wanted it," he said, flat, as he would have given Ferra the figures. "Here's the sum. You were first in line. Zale left. I was the drunk. If you'd been decent, if you'd been ordinary, it was yours. Nobody was coming for it."

"Everybody's carrying something," said Emeric. "Some people carry it forward. Some people put it on the next man and stand on him to see over. So he failed you. He did. I'm not going to argue it. Then stop waiting for him to fix it, and be your own man." He watched his brother's face. "You let one grown man's neglect of a boy turn you into a grown man doing the same thing to a boy. I was seven when you started. You were very good at it."

He was quiet a while. "He was in a bind you couldn't see. Maybe there was a better way through it. There probably was. It's done. I can't fix yesterday, and neither can you. I can do something about today."

Corbin looked at him a long time, his big hands hanging.

"You want to see mine?" His voice had gone rough and ordinary, a voice nobody had ever heard him use. "Look, then."



A given read goes by the giver's own roads. Emeric did not choose the doors in this house. He held the lamp and went where his brother's mind went when it was asked.

Within a dozen steps he knew something was wrong with it. Some of the rooms were too bright.

A council hall, a boy's feet a hand off the floor, and a low black stool carried off by a clerk saying kindly that it looked childish, Highness. A man going up a stair two at a time to a door with a guard on it, not once looking back. This yard, an arms master's hand on a twelve-year-old's shoulder, that's it, Zale, and a boy of fourteen at the gate turning and going. A long table at night, a man rising before the cups with a letter, his face bored, going out.

They were lit like rooms with a lamp in every corner, every edge sharp as fresh-cut glass. And they were worn: a path worn smooth across each floor like the step of a church, in at the door and straight to the thing that hurt, and round, and out. Beside every one, where the rest of the house should have been, there was fog: shapes under dust sheets, a table with people laughing at it, a man's hand on a boy's head, a smell of oranges. Nobody had ever been walked in there.

Emeric looked at the floor. In the worn path of the stair room, in the dust, there were footprints. Two sets. One was Corbin's, a boy's and a youth's and a man's, hundreds of them over one another. The other was a single grown man's tread, light and even and unhurried, that came in at the door a half pace ahead of the boy's every time and went to the thing that hurt and stood beside it, waiting for the boy to come and look, and went out ahead of him again. It was in every bright room, worn as deep as the boy's own. And it had a note on it that Emeric knew as he would have known a man's cough on a dark stair. He had laid a thread on it in a square in December and followed it down into a dead man's cellar.

He said nothing, and waited. Corbin was walking with him and saw what the lamp showed. He stood in the door of his own bright room and looked down at the two sets of steps, side by side, the other always a half pace ahead.

"Who else has been in here?" he said.

Emeric did not answer. He felt Corbin go back through the bright rooms looking at the floors, and then further back, to a quiet room with a closed door and a chair, a warm hand on his head, a voice: shall we go and look at it again, my son, the stair, it will help to look. Again. And again. Hundreds of times. Twenty-eight years.

"He walked me," said Corbin. "He walked me through it."

The bright rooms did not change; nobody could change them. But they looked to him now like a field a man has always thought his own and finds a stranger's path worn across it to the gate.

He went to the other bright rooms himself, fast, the way a man goes round his house at night when he has found one window forced. Their father's study, with the green-shaded lamp; a small boy with gold eyes slipping in at the door and across the carpet; and their father bent close over him with his face soft as Emeric had never in his life seen it soft, whispering, the two of them heads together over something kept from everyone.

Emeric knew it at once; he built rooms like this himself, for Bram. A built room holds only what its builder thought to put in it. This one was bright and clean and smelled of nothing; their father's study had smelled of ink and lamp oil and the old dog who slept under the desk, and there was no dog. The boy's face was exactly right, and his hands at his sides were blank, smooth, unfinished, like a doll's, because whoever made him had looked at his face and never at his hands. And they were seeing it from the doorway, at a grown man's height. Emeric's eye was a foot above the boy's head.

He did not say anything. Corbin looked a long time, and then walked round the end of the desk, behind their bent father, to where the chair should have been and the shelf of rolls behind it.

There was nothing. Not darkness: nothing, the way a painted flat stops when you go round the back of it on a stage, and there is only unpainted wood and rope.

Corbin stood behind the desk and turned and looked at the doorway he had seen this room from all his life.

"I was never in that door," he said. "I was nine. I'd have been looking up."

"I did go in," said Emeric, because he would not have the truth taken from his brother along with the lie. "Often. To look at the maps. He never once bent down to me like that. I used to sit under the map table and he'd forget I was in the room."

"It was the Light," said Corbin, quite flat. "He put his hand on me and said the Light showed the truth to those who asked. He said you don't repeat what the Light shows you in confidence. I never did. Not to my mother. I thought I was the only one in the world who knew what Father was."

There were three more, every one seen from a doorway at a man's height, empty behind, clean of any smell. After the second he stopped going round the back.

On another world there was a name for a grown man who finds the one child nobody notices and notices him, gives him small secrets and then bigger ones, and tells him his brothers will take everything; and men there had once walked frightened people through the same rooms until they remembered things that never happened. Augustine had not needed anything that never happened. He had enough that did. Emeric kept all of it behind the barred shutter. Corbin had the footprints and the back of the stage.

"There," said Emeric. It was the only time he pointed.

The long table, the night Corbin was made Lieutenant: the bored face going out with its letter. At the edge of the lamplight where the fog came up, there was a corner nobody had ever been walked to. Emeric could not sharpen it; nobody could. He could only turn the lamp a little toward it.

Corbin went, out of the worn path for the first time, across a floor with no footprints on it, his or anyone's. And because he was looking, it came. Not much. A man in a doorway at the end of the hall with a letter in his hand, stopping on his way out to turn his head and look back down the long table, past the lords and the candles, at the young man at its end in the Lieutenant's new grey. For about a breath. Not bored. Something else, that a man would only see if he had been let look. Then he went out.

"I don't know what that is," said Corbin.

"No," said Emeric. "Neither do I."

Going back, Corbin stopped at a fog with a voice in it: his mother's, cold and exact, saying once and plainly that the man uses people and leaves them in pieces, and whatever he promises you he is promising to somebody else as well. Nobody had walked him there. The only path worn into her room went to one bright thing, her mouth saying Amara's name as if it tasted of something, and the rest left to go grey.

"She told me," said Corbin. "And I thought she was jealous of him."

Emeric thought of a tall woman who had bought her son's life without once saying please, and of the promise she had bought with it, which he was keeping now with his mouth shut.

His hands were shaking, here, where he did not have any.

"Fuck yesterday," he said. "The man who did this to you is the one I want."

He meant it. He also knew the man was dead, and that something wore his coat now and rode his horse north with both hands on the reins, and that it was that, and not the man, he wanted. He kept that behind the shutter too.



Corbin did not answer. His hand had gone inside his coat to the breast, to a sheet; Emeric felt its shape in his brother's mind, old paper folded in four, soft at the folds, carried since August and read many times. And Corbin's mind went from the sheet to what it was about, as a mind does: past a nursery window he had only read of, down into a yard.

It was dark here. Not fog; dark. A room nobody had ever walked him to, on purpose, so that in hundreds of walks the other footprints had gone round it wide every time, the way a man walks wide of a well with no cover on it. There were no footprints near its door at all.

Corbin walked to it himself. Emeric stayed in the worn path with the lamp, and turned it nowhere.

And because Corbin was looking, it came.

A yard in morning sun, the carriages going out at the gate, a lean grey captain riding back down the column and saying something short; and a boy of thirteen on the keep steps, grown out of his coat at the wrists, sent down to wave and not waving, hearing it. The boy crossing another yard alone, quickly, to a side door. A chapel, empty, smelling of cold wax and the brass of the altar rail; and on the altar step a scrap of paper folded small, put there by the boy's hand, four words on it in pencil in a boy's letters: the falls, not the river. A man in a lay brother's grey coming in by the side door, stooping, taking the paper off the step without a look at the boy, going out.

And under it, weeks older, the kind voice, the only grown voice in that house that had ever asked the boy anything. Will you do me one small kindness, my son, that nobody else need know? Your little brother's birthday road. Leave me word of it on the chapel's altar step on the morning, and I'll pray for it from the hills, every bend. It shall be our secret.

Corbin stood in the chapel door and looked at the paper on the step.

He understood it before Emeric could have said anything, and Emeric had not meant to. He felt his brother understand it the way you feel a floor give under somebody in the next room. The ridges on both sides of the falls road, and men in the grass on them, waiting, because somebody had sent word which road. A woman in a grey travelling dress laughing at a groom, who had kissed the top of a baby's head on those steps and told him he could have the falls next year. He had been thirteen. He had thought he was asking God to keep his brother safe on a road.

Emeric did not soften it or add to it. It was the plainest room in the house.

"He asked me nicely," said Corbin, to the step, in the voice of a boy.

Then, as if something in him had come loose, he turned and went at a stumble to a small room Emeric had not noticed, neither worn nor fog, and bright for no reason anybody would think of. A gallery in the palace last spring, after the tribunal, with their father shut in his rooms. The cardinal walking beside Corbin with his left hand on his arm and his right closed at his side, talking of the lilacs, and asking in passing, as a kind man asks after a household, which room the little one slept in now, Waylon, with the house so unsettled; and which knight had his door. You'll want a man of your own on it, my son, in times like these. And Corbin, the crown's Lieutenant with the realm in his hands, saying the east wing, and that he'd put his own man there in the morning. Pleased to be asked. It was bright because it had been pleasant, a warm hand on his arm in a spring gallery, and nothing in it had hurt.

"He asked me which room Waylon slept in," said Corbin.

"Yes," said Emeric.

"I told him. I put my own man on the door. Since April." He was looking at the gallery. "I thought he was fond of the boy."

Emeric thought of a ruined right hand opening against a garden wall: the little one, he's where I left him, on the shelf. Twenty-eight years on the shelf while the one worth more breathing was in reach; and when that one went south behind a wall, the cardinal had asked which room the other slept in now. Waylon had slept most nights in a loft over a grey mare's box. Corbin's knight had minded an empty room until the summer, when Waylon went south with two horses.

"He wanted the end of her," said Corbin slowly, as if reading it off a clerk's slip to be sure of each word. "Her whole line. You, and the boy too. The road, and then the room. And I held the door open for him both times."

He looked round at Emeric, and the face he had worn into every room since he was fourteen was not on him anywhere.

Emeric took him home.



Ward had watched all of it from the gate and not moved, as he had been told: not when the sword went into the boy's arm, not when the hook went in under the prince's ribs and his own knees answered it from thirty yards. He had hated every minute in order.

Then the boy crouched in front of his brother and held out his good hand, and the prince took it, hard; and the two of them were sitting knee to knee in the sand by the dark place where the blood was, holding hands like a pair of children told to make up, and nothing happened at all.

Ward counted. It was what his head did with a thing it could not act on. One, a sparrow coming down on the rack's pent roof. Two, three. The yard tasted of something again, faint at the back of his tongue, the bakehouse at four in the morning before there is any bread; the square had tasted of it in December. He knew now the boy made it, though not how. Five, six, the blood still running off the boy's fingers and the boy not noticing. Seven. The sparrow went. Eight, nine.

At about ten, the elder began to cry.

He did it badly, as a man does who has not wept since he was a boy: all at once, his hand still in his brother's, in hard ugly gulps like a man being sick, his head going down toward the sand; and he made no attempt to hide it, because he did not seem to know there was anybody in the world to hide it from.

The boy did not let go of his hand.

Ward stood at the gate through the whole of it, until the prince was only breathing. Then the prince looked round the yard as if he had just come into it, and saw Ward.

"Captain," he said. His voice was hoarse and ordinary; he had nothing clever left to put on it. "Is there any water?"

Ward looked at the trough with the two swords in it, and went to the pump instead, and filled the tin cup on its chain and carried it across the sand to the king's eldest son. The prince drank it in both hands and held it out again without looking up, and Ward filled it again.

The boy had got up. He pulled his shirt off over his head one-handed in the frost and wrapped it round the forearm, round and round, tight, and tucked the end, badly. The blood came through it at once.

"Imani," said Ward.

"In a minute."

"Now."

"In a minute, Ward."

The prince set the cup on the sand. He put his hand inside his arming coat and brought out a sheet of paper folded in four, soft at the folds, cracked along one of them; and held it up to his brother without a word, and without looking at him.

The boy looked at it a long time before he took it with his good hand. He did not open it. He put it in the pocket of his coat and put the coat on over his bare chest and buttoned it one-handed.

The prince got up by himself, which took him three tries, and stood in his brick-red arming coat with his brother's blood on its breast, and looked at the gate.

"I said I'd walk out," he said.

"Yes, Highness," said Ward, and opened it.

The king's eldest son walked between Osric's two across the east range's court, past two scullery girls and a boy with a bucket who did not know what they were looking at; on his own feet, with his hands at his sides, as he had asked.

The boy stood at the gate with Ward and watched him all the way to the stair.

"Now," said Ward.

"Now," said Emeric.



Imani said nothing at all for the first stitch. Bettany, holding the lamp, looked at the deckhead.

She had him on the bench under the forward lamps in the K Endeavor's hospital hold with Bettany holding the lamp. She had cut the shirt off and said "Bone," in the voice she used for the price of things, and washed it twice with something that made him think with great clarity about his whole life.

"To the bone," she said at the second stitch. "Not into it. You'll keep the arm and every finger, and make a fist with it by the spring, and you don't deserve one of them. A knight's body puts itself back twice as quick as a man's, and this is the first time in thirteen years I've wished yours didn't, so you'd have time to think."

"I hit him with the other one," said Emeric. "You said, if I hit anybody. I mostly did."

The needle stopped.

"I said that about your hand. I said nothing whatever about letting a man with a sword open your arm wrist to elbow, because it didn't occur to me I'd need to. I'll have it in the codebook next time." She drew the thread through. "Ward says you put your arm up into it. I know why. I'm not arguing with the why. I'm arguing with the arm. You've only got two."

"How many?" said Emeric.

"Twenty-two. Two layers, because I want it to close like something I'd put my name to." She tied one off. "Did he walk out?"

"On his own feet."

"Well. That's something to have bled for. I'd still rather you'd bled for it less." She tied off another. "I sent a nurse up to him. One of the low ribs on his right side is cracked, and his jaw will be every colour there is by the end of the week. She strapped him. He thanked her, she says, as if she'd poured his wine."

He had known since the gate he would read the sheet here, with her hands on him and her mind on the arm, and he opened it one-handed on his knee.

Two inks. The top was old ink gone brown on paper the colour of weak tea at the edges, in a quick tall upright hand that wasted no stroke and had been written, he would have bet the duchy, standing at a window.

Harrold. On the morning of the falls, when the carriages had gone out at the gate, I took the little one up to the nursery, because he would not stop roaring and I would not have him roar at her back. The nursery window looks on the chapel yard. I saw Corbin cross it by himself and go into the chapel by the side door. He was in it no longer than it takes to say a short prayer. He came out and stood on the step with his hands at his sides as if he had forgotten what came next. A little after, a lay brother went out at the chapel yard gate on a grey mule, at a trot, which I have never in my life seen a lay brother do. I don't know what it means. I'm not writing to tell you what it means. I'm writing because I'm the only one who saw it, and because that boy has never once gone into a chapel unless somebody made him, and because if I don't write it down I'll begin to tell myself I didn't see it. R.

Under it, in black ink newer by twenty-eight years, in the same hand gone a little less upright: I wrote this the winter after and never sent it. He was a boy and you were half mad, and I didn't know which of you it would break. I send it now to a room of men I don't like, because you're in a room with a lock on it and nobody else will say it. Forgive me the delay, or don't. R.

He read it twice, the second time at the same pace as the first, the way his father had once read a slip in a counting room, as a man walks a road twice to see whether it is the same road. It was.

He had not asked. He had said he would not, and he had not; and his brother had walked to that door by himself with this in his coat, and held it out in the sand without a word. Emeric did not know what he felt about it, and it did not much matter whether he knew.

Imani had stopped stitching. She had not looked at the sheet. She was looking at his face, the needle held up out of the way between two fingers.

"Is it bad?" she said.

"It's true."

She looked at him a moment longer, and bent to the arm again.

"Then it'll keep till you've two hands to hold it with. Fold it up. You're bleeding on it."

He folded it away, and she put in the rest without another word and laid his arm across his chest in a sling, as if she were putting away a thing she valued in a drawer.

"Nothing with that hand for a fortnight," she said. "Not the sack. Not your brother. Not a pen. You can sign things with the other one. You've been practising."



Vantic had come up on the mail hull on the fourth, because the crown was a client of the House again and the crown's book had to be opened to the crown; and on the fifth, at closing, she sent a card to the landing tiers saying she would be obliged if His Grace would call.

The House kept its capital office in a narrow stone house off the goldsmiths' lane. The long room was at the back, where the depositors came in by day: a counter, a row of brass lamps, and under the shuttered window a long table where the clerks counted coin, scrubbed nearly white.

She was at it alone, in her plain dark coat and gloves, with two ledgers open side by side and turned to face the chair across from her. She looked at the sling once and said nothing about it, and nodded at the chair.

He sat and read.

The left-hand book was the capital office's discount book, letters of credit presented at this counter for coin. The paymaster of the Lieutenant's companies, by name, from September to the first week of November, presenting paper on the Lieutenant's warrant for the pay of the hired company of the Wayford road and two grey mages. Thirty-one letters. Every one drawn on the same house in the Imperial Core, a name that meant nothing to him, under the imperial exchange's countermark, and every one numbered in the corner in the same small square hand.

She did not say anything. She did not move her folded hands.

The right-hand book was a copy of the House's correspondent's book in Kesrath's port. October and the first fortnight of November: three of Kesrath's yards presenting paper for thirty boats built in a hurry, flat-bottomed with a ramp at the bow. Saban's man had been told it was good paper. Here was the paper. Twenty-eight letters. The same house, the same countermark, the same small square hand in the corner.

He went back to the numbers. Ferra would have seen it in the time it took to turn the page. They ran in one series. Four thousand one hundred and twelve to four thousand one hundred and forty-two on the left, in the paymaster's name, for the men who came down the Wayford road to his South Gate before dawn. Four thousand one hundred and forty-three to four thousand one hundred and seventy on the right, in the yards' names, for the boats that put Kesrath's men on the Claim in November. One run of paper from one desk, half to his brother and half to his brother's enemy, who was also his.

He sat back. Vantic unfolded her hands.

"The same paper," she said, as flat as a rate. "To both sides of one war, in one autumn. Whoever won owed the drawer. Whoever lost owed it too, with less to pay. That isn't a loan, Your Grace. It's a purchase. They bought the war, both ends of it, and waited to see which end they'd own."

"The Empire."

"A house in the Core, under the exchange's mark. I don't say more than the paper says. The paper says enough."

On another world men had done this so often it had stopped being worth remarking on: paid for a war they would not fight, on both sides, and owned the peace. An emperor who could not take a field from a subject king without every king looking to his own had found a way to take it anyway, by paying both to fight over it and holding both their notes. He said none of it.

"There's a third book," said Vantic, "and I haven't got it." She laid one gloved finger on the bare wood between the two ledgers, where a third would have gone. "The northern hire. The men who came down from nowhere with good boots and no master for the Church's work in Aldane's war, and went home when it was done. They were paid in coin, and coin keeps no book. I think it was the same hand. I've thought so since October." She took the finger away. "I can't prove it from anything I hold. I'm telling you that I can't, so you'll know the difference when I can."

"Will you?"

"I know the shape of their paper now." She closed both books and laid her hands on them. "Paper comes back. It's the one thing about it I've always liked."

He got up carefully. For the first time she looked straight at the sling.

"Ferra will want to know what that cost."

"Twenty-two stitches."

"She'll want it in figures," said Vantic. "That isn't one."

He went out into the lane, where it had begun to snow, small and dry, with Ward a pace behind him. At the corner Emeric stopped.

Up over the roofs the castle stood pale on its shoulder in the snow-light. One window at the west end, over the long gallery, had been lit till two in the morning every night that week; the footman said His Highness sat up reading and did not turn the pages. It was lit now.

"Osric says he ate his supper tonight," said Ward. "First time this week. Imani's nurse has been up twice to look at that rib, and he let her."

"Did he ask for anything?"

"No." Ward put his hands in his armpits. "Thought you'd want him let alone."

Emeric looked at the window a while longer. In Ostrel a thing that wore a cardinal's face, and had all a cardinal's memories, knew to the last room what had been done to the man behind that window, and expected it to stay done.

"Yes," he said. "Let him alone."

They went down to the boat.
