Chapter 185: On the Wall

The wall of New Kaldo, closed on the full circuit. Two miles, three furlongs and some yards. Four gates, sixteen towers. Third season.

Ferra wrote it at the long-room table on the evening of the day it happened, and then sat and looked at it, because it did not look like enough.

It had happened in the ordinary way. There had been a gap on the south arc, west of the south gate, where the core had been left low all summer so the carts could get the dressed stone in and out to the gate towers. On the last day of the gap there had been forty yards of it, and in the morning the carts stopped, and the earth mages came along the wall walk from both sides in their pairs and went down into the gap and lifted the core up into the line, twenty yards from the east end and twenty from the west, until the two slopes of earth met in the middle. Then the masons came in behind them with the stone. By the end of the working day the facing was up to the height of the rest and the scaffold was on it, and there was no gap anymore, anywhere, on the whole of the circle.

Tessaly Rook had closed it. She had taken the western twenty yards herself, alone, with Paskin on the eastern twenty, and when the two slopes met she had stood in the middle of them with her hands still up for a moment, as though she had forgotten to put them down. Then she had put them down, and gone and sat on a block of dressed stone at the foot of the gate tower, and nobody had gone near her for a while.

She had come back from the north a month before with the boy and with a licence in a tin case, and had gone straight to Armand's study the evening she arrived and been in there with the door shut until well after dark. When she came out, Armand had come out after her, and walked down the passage to the counting room where Ferra was working late, and asked for the box. Ferra had given it to him. He had written in it for some time with his back to her, and given it back to her locked, and said good night. Ferra had not read what he wrote. She would, when she needed to. She had not needed to yet.

Nobody cheered when the wall closed. There was no ceremony. Bram had wanted a ceremony and Emeric had said not yet, meaning not until the gates were hung, which would be the spring. A mason on the south gate tower had waved his cap. Some of the gangs had gone home early. That was all.

She wrote: Closed by T. Rook and Paskin, the last forty yards, the south arc. And then, because it was true and she thought someone should write it down, The first time since the footing that the whole circle has been one thing.

It was a city now, from the wall. That was the strange part.

From the ground it had never been a city. From the ground it had been a camp and a quarry and a mess of trenches, and then a line of footing across the flat, and then a wall at knee height, at waist height, at half height, with carts going through the gaps and the gangs' huts clustered in its lee like sheep against a hedge. You could walk along the foot of it for a morning and never see more than a hundred yards of it at once.

From the wall walk you saw all of it.

The circle went away from you on both sides, grey and new, with its sixteen towers standing out of it at their intervals like the pins in a surveyor's chain, and bent round until it came back to you across the flat. Four gates, one at each quarter, with their gate towers up to the second floor and their arches turned and empty, waiting for the oak. Inside the circle the ground fell very gently from the north towards the south, towards the lower water and the sea, which you could see from the south wall as a grey line past Fletcher's yard.

In the middle, on the rise, the palace.

It was a shell. It had a roof on its ground floor and nothing on its upper floors but scaffold, and in the centre of it, over the great room that Emeric called the long room and everybody else called the hall, the drum of the dome was going up in a ring of dressed stone with nothing on top of it yet but sky. The drum could be seen from anywhere in the circle. Ferra thought that was the point of it. Emeric had drawn the city so that wherever you were in it, you could look up and see the offices and the records and the courts, the place where the book was kept.

The church stood on the second ring, on the north side, well away from the middle, on the plot Father Doran had been shown three years ago at the datum stake, and its footings were in and nothing else.

Round the palace, the first ring canal. It was not a moat. Ward had stopped calling it a moat the day he saw the drawing properly. It was a channel of dressed stone ten feet wide, lined with the lime that set under water, fed from the aqueduct where the aqueduct came in at the north gate, and it went round the palace hill in a circle and let out at the south, into the second ring, and then the third. Each ring was wider than the last. From the rings, smaller channels ran out into the quarters between the streets, and in the quarters that had no houses in them yet, the channels ran through fields: beans and barley and cabbages, planted this spring by the families of the first quarter on the empty ground inside their own walls. Water's road, Emeric had called the rings. They were the city's drains' cousins and the city's fields' mothers, and in a siege they were the city's water, and in a fire they were the fire company's cistern, and in the summer they were where the first quarter's children swam, strictly against the rules.

The first quarter itself was the north-east, between the north gate and the east. It had streets now, laid and set, with pipes under every one of them and houses on both sides: stone below and timber above, with slate roofs from the ridge quarry and glass in every window, clear glass, that caught the light and made the quarter glitter when the sun was low. Near two thousand people lived in it. There was a market square with a tower in the middle of it, stone base and lead-lined tank and brass taps, and a school, and a bakehouse, and a house for the fire company's first-quarter watch.

There were four towers in the city. One over each quarter. Only the first quarter had houses under its tower. The other three stood over empty ground, full, with their taps locked and their pumps turning slowly in the wind, over streets that were only pegs and string, because Emeric's rule had been that nothing was built above ground until what went under it was laid, and water went under everything.

The rest was stakes. Most of the city was stakes. Streets pegged out in the grass, lots marked with white-painted posts, the lines of future sewers and mains laid in open trenches and covered over and marked with a row of stones. Room to grow. That was what the drawing said, and Ferra had watched the table argue about it for a winter and come round, one by one, to the idea that you build a wall round a city that is not there yet because walls are the thing you cannot add later, and the city will come.

The island was half empty. Ilsa's count in the summer had put the island at two thousand six hundred souls, which was what it had been the year before the debtors came, and the rooms behind the market were one family deep again, and the tannery lane where the fire had been was a lane of kitchen gardens. Tam Brewer was still in his house by the water steps. Ferra had checked.

She went to find Emeric on the wall at midday on the third day after it closed.

She did not arrange it. She thought about arranging it and decided not to, because anything she arranged she would have to wonder, afterwards, whether she had arranged it well. She rode out to the flat with the site's book in her saddle bag, as she did twice a week, and did the site's business with Weekes and the quarry foreman and the clerk of the gangs, and then she asked the south gate's foreman where the prince was, and he pointed up.

The wall walk on the south-west arc had its parapet up and the coping going on. There were masons along it every twenty yards, setting the coping stones in their beds of mortar, and a lime boy going up and down with a hod, and a cart below at the foot of the wall with a hoist and a gang, and the whole of it loud with the chink of trowels and men calling for stone. Emeric was between two gangs of masons, with a straight-edge and a level, going along the coping they had just set and looking at the bubble, and frowning at it, and moving on.

She went up the tower stair and out onto the walk and along it to him.

"The coping's a quarter inch high on the last three," he said, without looking up. "They'll say it's the mortar bed and they'll be right. I'm going to let it go."

"Let it go," Ferra said.

He looked up then. He had a smear of lime on his jaw. He put the level down on the coping and looked at her face and waited, because he could see she had not climbed the tower to talk about the coping.

The masons were twenty yards off on one side and fifteen on the other. The lime boy went past between them with his hod and did not look at either of them. It was noon and the sun was on the wall and the flat below was full of carts and gangs and dust and noise, and the sea at the far edge of the world was grey.

"I want to ask you something," Ferra said. "And I want the true answer, and I want it now, here, and then I don't want to have to ask it again."

"All right."

"How do you feel about Imani." She said it flat, as a question in a ledger, because that was the only way she could say it. "And Vantic."

He did not look surprised. She thought afterwards that he had been waiting for someone to ask him that for about a year, and had not known it would be her, and was not surprised it was.

"Why?" he said.

"Because I won't start anything I'd have to wonder about," Ferra said. "I've watched you for years. I've watched them. I don't know what's there. I won't guess. If there's something there, I'd rather know now and leave it be, than find out in five years from something you don't say."

The level lay on the coping between them. He put his hand on it, not to pick it up, just to have his hand on something.

"I like them both," he said.

She waited.

"I mean that. I like them both a great deal. Imani is the best of all of us. She's the only one of us who has never once let me get away with anything, and I have tried. Vantic is the cleverest person I've ever met, including me, and she makes me laugh, and I think she's lonely in a way she's never going to talk about." He looked at the coping. "If you'd asked me whether I could love either of them, the true answer is I don't know. Probably I could. Most people can love most people, if they let themselves."

"Go on."

"I haven't let myself." He looked up at her. "And I'm going to tell you why, and it's going to sound like arithmetic, and I know how that sounds when the question's this one. I'm going to say it anyway, because you asked for the true answer, and you'd know if I gave you a kinder one."

"Say it."

"Vantic's House lends against the city and the works and the war. It's the biggest loan the House has ever made outside the capital. One day she'll run the House, or the House will be run by whoever she lets run it, and it will be the biggest bank in the kingdom." He did not look away. "A prince can't marry his lender. Not an exiled one. Not one with a file on him in his brother's satchel. The day it was known, every figure in your book would be read as a gift from her to me or from me to her. Every loan Kaldo ever took from the House would look like a dowry. She'd lose the House or I'd lose the city, and probably both."

"And Imani."

He was quiet for a moment. A mason down the wall called for stone and the cart's hoist creaked.

"Imani took her seat at that table," he said, "so that nothing would ever be done to her again without her in the room. Twice was enough. She told me so once, in about that many words, and she's never said it again because she didn't need to." He turned the level a quarter inch on the coping. "Her job at the table is to tell me no. About bodies. The water, the ward, the cellar, the camp. She's the one who says not like that and not him and not yet. She's the only one who says it about bodies, and she's right more often than I am. And it's already hard for her. I can see how hard it is. I watched her say yes to the water too fast and I watched her know it." He looked at Ferra. "I won't make it any harder for her to say no to me. Ever. If there were ever anything between us, every time she told me no at that table she'd have to wonder if she was saying it to the prince or to me, and so would everybody else, and so would I."

The wind came off the flat and moved the hair on his forehead.

"That's the reasons," he said. "I told you they'd sound like arithmetic."

"They do."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be." Ferra found that her hand was on the coping too, a foot from his. The stone was warm from the sun. "They're good reasons. They're the reasons I'd have given, if I were you." She looked at the level. "Is that the whole answer?"

"No," he said.

She waited.

"The rest of it is," Emeric said, "that it's not close. You win by a mile. You've been winning by a mile for so long that I stopped noticing it was a race. I'm not telling you about Imani and Vantic because either of them was ever near you. I'm telling you because you asked, and because you'd want the reasons in the book." He almost smiled. "You'd want them in the book even if they didn't matter."

Ferra stood on the wall of New Kaldo at noon, with the masons twenty yards off on one side and fifteen on the other, and the lime boy coming back past them with an empty hod, and felt something happen inside her that she had no column for.

"Not the book," she said, when she could.

"No?"

"The box."

That made him laugh, once, surprised out of him, and the nearest mason looked round and then looked away again. Ferra did not laugh. She did not move her hand. After a moment he moved his, a foot along the warm coping, until the side of it was against the side of hers.

That was all. They stood like that for as long as it took the lime boy to reach the stair and go down it. Then a gang foreman shouted from the foot of the wall that the hoist rope was fraying and did the prince want to look at it, and Emeric took his hand back and picked up the level, and Ferra took her hand back and went down the stair to her horse, and rode back to the island with the site's book in her saddle bag and did not open it all afternoon.

Vantic came down for the autumn quarter day, as she always did, and sat at the loan table in the counting room with Ferra and Saban and the House's copy of the instrument and Ferra's book, and copied what she needed.

It took the morning. It always did. At noon Saban went down to see about the carts, and Vantic sat back in her chair with her pen in her fingers and looked across the table at Ferra for a while.

"Your figures are exactly the same," Vantic said.

"I should hope so."

"You aren't." Vantic put the pen down. "What is it?"

Ferra told her. She told her plainly, in about four sentences, because she had decided on the ride back from the wall that she would, and that she would do it before Vantic worked it out for herself, which Vantic would. She did not tell her what Emeric had said about the House. That was his to say or not.

Vantic listened with her hands folded on the table. When Ferra had finished she was quiet for long enough that Ferra wondered whether she had misjudged it.

Then Vantic reached across the table for the jug of small beer that stood between them for the long mornings, and poured two cups, and pushed one across to Ferra, and lifted the other.

"To the only contract in this barony," Vantic said, "that I didn't draw."

She did not drink. She held the cup up for a moment, and looked at it rather than at Ferra, and there was something in her face that Ferra had seen under every clause of every instrument Vantic had ever written for Kaldo and had never been able to read. Then she smiled, a real smile, and drank, and set the cup down, and picked up her pen.

"I'd have drawn it better," she said. "You'd have had a sunset clause." And bent to the book.

Imani did not wait to be told.

It was at supper in the long room four nights later, the whole table, and Ferra came in late from the counting room and sat down where she had sat for years, at the corner by Hobb. Then she looked at the chair beside Emeric's, which was empty because Armand had gone up early, and got up again, and moved.

She did it without thinking about it, and then thought about it, and was sitting down by the time she had finished thinking, and it was too late to do anything but sit.

Across the table, Imani put down her spoon.

"A year ago," Imani said.

Ferra looked at her.

"You moved your chair a year ago," Imani said. "Last autumn, after the war. You moved it about a foot. Every supper. You thought nobody saw." She picked up her spoon again. "I've been waiting for you to move it the rest of the way. I was going to give you till the spring."

Ward, at the table's foot, did not look up from his plate, but his mouth moved. Bram looked from one of them to the other and then at Emeric, and his eyebrows went up and stayed up. Saban, who had known since the quarter day and had said nothing, which for Saban was a feat, looked at the ceiling. Hobb went on eating.

Emeric said nothing at all. He passed Ferra the bread.

Imani ate her soup. After a while, without looking up, she said, "Good," and that was the whole of it, and it was enough.

The first ring canal was filled on the last day of the autumn.

It had been dug and lined through the summer and left dry for the lime to cure, and on the last dry day of the season Jorrin and the aqueduct gang went up to the north gate and opened the sluice where the aqueduct came in, and let the river into the city.

Ferra went up on the wall to see it. So did Emeric. They went up the north-east tower stair separately and came out onto the wall walk a little way apart and stood there, in the middle of a working day, with the masons on the east arc setting coping thirty yards off and the lime boy going up and down with his hod and a cart grinding along the road below.

The water came slowly. It came in under the north gate's empty arch along its stone channel, brown with the summer's silt, and went round the first bend of the ring with a little bow wave in front of it, pushing leaves and straw, and then filled out behind itself and went on. It took most of an hour to go round the palace hill. They stood on the wall and watched it the whole time, and did not say much. Once Emeric pointed to a place on the south side where the lining had a crack, and Ferra wrote it down. Once she asked what the flow was, and he told her.

The sun came round to the west. The water in the ring settled and cleared, the silt going down into the bed of it, and the surface of it went still and flat and took the light, so that from the north-east tower the palace stood on its hill inside a ring of sky.

Ferra looked at it. Then she looked at him, standing a foot away on the warm stone with the lime still on his jaw from somewhere, looking at his canal.

She did not move her hand this time. She did not need to. The masons were thirty yards off and the lime boy was on the stair, and it was daylight, and the whole of the city was below them, most of it still stakes.
