Chapter 185 - Dark Over the North

The K Discovery went out of the shed at dusk on the eighteenth of November under the motor, at a walking pace, with no lamp lit anywhere aboard her, and Ferra stood on the slip in her coat and watched her go and did not wave.

She had decided in the long room that she would not come. Somebody had to be in Kaldo with clean hands and a clean book if the fourth line ever had to be kept, she said; somebody who had been at her desk all night with the lamp lit and the window open, where the watch on the wall could see her. So she would be at her desk all night with the lamp lit and the window open. She had said it at the table without looking at anybody, and nobody had argued, because it was true, and because everybody at the table could see what it cost her and nobody wanted to make her say so.

At the slip, before Emeric went up the ladder, she had put a folded paper into his hand.

"For the coach," she said. "You'll know when."

He had looked at it. It was sealed with plain red wax with no mark in it, and addressed on the outside in a clerk's round hand that was not Ferra's and not Hale's and not anybody's he had ever seen in the barony's books. Lord Aurel Sallow. Nothing else.

"What does it say?"

"Nothing," said Ferra. "I wrote the outside in a hand I made up this afternoon. I've never used it before and I'll never use it again." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "The inside's blank. I sealed it on the counting room's spare wax, which half the merchants on the coast road buy from the same chandler." She looked up at the dark hull above them. "He'll open it. He'll read nothing. He'll think it's a message he can't understand, and he'll lie awake trying to understand it, and he'll never be able to show it to a court and say it came from anywhere." She took one hand out of her pocket and straightened his collar, where it had folded under the gorget of the suit. "Come home."



Seven of them went. Emeric, Ward and Rylan in their suits, the helms on their racks in the saloon. Fletcher at the bow helm, because the K Discovery would be on the water for a day and Fletcher would not let anybody else put her on the water, ever, in any weather, for any reason. Cade, who was Fletcher's second in the yard, a long silent man of forty with forearms like ship's cable and a habit of whistling one tune, very softly, all day, which nobody had ever heard the end of. Tobe from the foundry, young, broad, freckled, who had been picked for the yard because he had carried hot charged stone in the casting shed for four years without once dropping one, and who did not know that was why. Imani, with her bag and two flasks of ether in a padded case and a roll of linen and a lamp, sitting very upright in the saloon with her hands in her lap, watching the dark go by outside the iris ports with an expression Emeric had seen on her once before, on the marsh, at the end of his arm. And in the forward locker, coiled, Bram's ladder.

It was not a ladder. Bram had said so himself, delivering it, and had looked at it with a smith's dislike of anything he could not hit. It was forty feet of good hemp rope in two falls, with oak treads between them every foot, and at the bottom, on its own line, a seat: a broad canvas sling with a board in it and a strap across, the kind a sailor is swayed up in when he cannot climb. "For skirts," Bram had said. "And for anybody who's frightened and won't say so. Which is everybody, the first time." He had looked at Emeric. "You'll put her in the seat and haul her. You'll not ask her to climb forty feet of rope in the dark in her good shoes. I'll know if you do."

They went down the river under the motor, past the dock quarter and the marsh, dark, and out between the Teeth, which Fletcher took at a crawl and from memory, to the sea; and on the open sea, where there was nothing to hear her but fish, Fletcher opened her up.

She roared. She always roared, flat out; that was the price of her. But there was nobody on the black water to hear it, and she went up the coast at a mile's height and two hundred miles in the hour with the sea going by underneath like a dark floor and the shore a line of no lights at all to the west. Four hours. Emeric sat in the top cockpit beside Fletcher's second seat with his own suit on and the helm on his knees and watched the stars. Cade whistled his tune. Tobe, in the stone-keeper's station amidships, read the dials every quarter of an hour and called them through the speaking tube in a voice that was steady by the second hour and cheerful by the third. Imani did not sleep. Rylan did; he lay on the saloon bench in his plate with his arms crossed and slept as a soldier sleeps, at once and completely. Ward sat at the saloon table with his helm in front of him and did not do anything at all.

At the fourth hour Fletcher brought her in over the land.

Slow. That was the other half of it. Inland, over farms and villages and a lord's woods, she went at a walk, on the stones and the motor's quiet push, at a mile's height, so that a man in a farmyard below would have had to be looking straight up at the right handful of stars and seen them go out, and come back, and nothing else. Emeric opened his sense and let it lie out under her, wide and thin, across the dark country, the small warm notes of sleeping farms and the bright notes of dogs; and none of them woke.

The border tower stood on a bald hill at the edge of Sallow's land with a signal fire banked in a brazier on its top and one lamp lit in its door, and six notes asleep in it, and one awake.



He went down alone, on the cape.

Not on the suit's hearts. The hearts were loud enough, in a still night, for a man awake on a hilltop to hear them coming a mile off. He went over the K Discovery's rail with the hearts asleep and the cape-wing furled, and fell, and let the watchdog throw the cape at the third count, the way Sorrel had made them all do it twenty times over the river, and lay on the wing and slid down the dark toward the hill in a long silent slant with the cold air tearing at the edges of his helm, and put his heels down at a man's height above the tower's yard and stalled and dropped onto the beaten earth in front of the door, and went to one knee, and stood up.

The one awake note was in the doorway.

A big man. Bigger than Osric, and Osric was the biggest man in Kaldo's garrison. He stood in the lit door of the tower in his shirt and his boots with a sword in his hand, not raised, and looked at the black armoured shape that had just fallen out of the sky into his yard without a sound, and did not shout for his men, and did not run, and did not raise the sword.

"If you're the devil," said Sir Jabari Mensah, "you're early. I've another hour on the watch."

Emeric took the helm off.

Jabari looked at his face in the lamplight from the door for a long time. He had a broad, dark, heavy-boned face, and eyes that had not closed properly for three nights, and the look of a man who has been holding something up for so long that he has stopped noticing his arms.

"You're the one she wouldn't marry," he said.

"I'm the one who wouldn't marry her. She'll tell you it was the other way round. She's right."

"I know who you are." Jabari did not put the sword down. "I've read about you in her letters for a year. I've read about your chancellor and your healer and your bridge and your soap." He was quiet. "I told your man no. I meant it."

"I know you did."

"Then why are you in my yard?"

"Because you were right," said Emeric. "Everything you said to him was right. A girl who runs is ruined, and a knight who takes her is a thief, and they'd hang you, and she'd watch." He put the helm under his arm. "I'm not here to ask you to run. I'm not going to ask her to run either."

Jabari waited.

"Tomorrow night she's on the road to Vane's," said Emeric. "We're taking her off it. Not you; us. No knight of her father's household touches her. Nobody of her father's dies. Then she comes to Kaldo, and sleeps, and in two days she walks into Kaldo's church, in daylight, with the doors open, and Father Doran asks her in front of the whole town whether she wants to marry you. Out loud. And if she says yes, he marries you, under Kaldo's seal, and the lines go to the crown's register by the post, and to every court that asks, in writing, with witnesses." He looked at the big man in the doorway. "That isn't running. It isn't a ditch. It's a wedding. So come and ask her properly."

The fire in the brazier on the tower's top settled, and a few sparks went up.

Jabari stood in the doorway with the sword in his hand. He did not say anything for so long that Emeric heard, very faint, through the cold, Cade's whistled tune coming down from a mile above them, which could not possibly have been true.

"Does she want it?" said Jabari.

It came out rough. It was the only thing he asked. Not what it would cost him, or what would happen to his men, or whether the prince who had fallen out of the sky could be trusted; only that.

"You'll have to ask her that yourself," said Emeric. "I'm not going to answer it for her. Nobody is, ever again, if I can help it."

Jabari looked at him a moment longer. Then he lowered the sword, finally, and turned and went into the tower; and Emeric heard him going up the stair inside, heavily, and a door, and a man's voice, sleepy, asking what, and Jabari's voice, low and level, telling his sergeant something at length. When he came down again he had a coat on and a pack over one shoulder and the sword on his hip, and a folded paper in his hand, which he gave to a grey-haired man in a nightshirt who had followed him down and stood in the door blinking at the black armour in the yard.

"To his lordship," said Jabari. "When he sends to ask. Not before." He turned to Emeric. "I've resigned my post. In writing. I've given my sergeant the fire and the six men and the key to the store, and signed it." He hitched the pack. "I'll not leave a post. I'll leave a lord. That's allowed."

The sergeant looked at the paper, and at Jabari, and at Emeric, and at the dark sky, and said nothing, very sensibly.

The ladder came down out of the dark. Bram's treads first, swinging, knocking on the yard's beaten earth; and the seat on its own line beside it. Jabari looked at the ladder, and up it, into the black above the tower where it went up and up into nothing at all, with no ship and no stars at the top of it, only dark.

"I've never been higher than a horse," he said.

"The seat's for people who won't say they're frightened," said Emeric. "Bram's rule."

Jabari looked at the seat. Then he put his hands on the ladder's ropes and his boot on the first tread, and climbed, without a word, forty feet, in the dark, into nothing; and Emeric went up behind him on his hearts, slowly, and saw him come over the K Discovery's rail at the top and sit down on the deck with his back to it and his eyes shut, and Cade stop whistling for the first time in four hours and put a hand on his shoulder.



She lay the day out on the water.

That had been Fletcher's idea, at the long table, and it was a good one. There was a sea loch thirty miles west of the Sallow valley on Linus's old copy of the north country map, long and narrow and deep, with steep wooded sides and no village on it anywhere, and no road; and the K Discovery came down onto it before the first light on the morning of the nineteenth with her legs up and her foils in, and sat on the black water like a long dark fishing boat under the steep woods, and nobody in the world came to look at her. Fletcher put out her sea anchor. Cade slept. Tobe fished, off the stern, with a line Maren had packed in the galley for the purpose, and caught four, and was prouder of them than of anything Emeric had ever seen him do.

Jabari did not sleep. He sat on the deck in the cold with his back against the cabin and looked at the loch and the woods and the grey sky, and ate what Imani gave him when she gave it to him, and answered what Ward asked him about the road and the valley and Vane's house and the bend before Vane's gate where the road went through a stand of beeches, in short, exact sentences, the way Ward asked; and in the early afternoon he went to sleep sitting up in the middle of a sentence, and Imani put a blanket over him, and he did not wake for four hours.

"Three nights," said Imani, to nobody, tucking the blanket. "At least. Look at his hands." Jabari's hands lay on his knees, open, very still. "He's been holding a sword all night for three nights in case somebody came for her, on a hill nine miles from anywhere she was." She stood up. "Men."



The coach came up the valley road at dusk, as he had told Ward it would.

They had been holding for an hour, high and dark, a mile above the valley and a mile off the road to the east, over Vane's woods, where nobody looked up. Emeric lay on the deck with his helm on and his sense out wide and thin over the whole of the road below, and found it before he could see it: a cluster of notes moving slowly up the valley in the last of the light from Sallow's gate toward Vane's. Horses, a team of four, hard and bright and frightened of nothing yet. A coachman. Two notes inside the coach, one of them a girl's, small and quick and furious, and one he knew; he had stood in a hall with it in February while Imani told it what was wrong with its chest. The steward. And riding round the coach, ahead and behind and at the doors, nine more.

Nine. Not Sallow's household men. He knew the notes of household men; they were heavy and dull and incurious, men riding behind a coach because their lord told them to. These were not. These were awake. Every one of them was turning its head as it rode, he could feel them doing it, reading the woods on either side of the road; and one of them, riding a little behind the others, on the coach's near side, was not a man's note at all, or not only. It was bright and high and thin and it had a stone on it, a charged stone, in a pocket or a pouch, that he could feel the way he felt a lamp across a dark room.

"Nine," he said, aloud, to Ward. "Not his men. Hired. Somebody's. And a mage."

Ward, beside him on the deck, said nothing at all for a breath.

"Vane expected trouble," he said then. "Or her father did." He put his helm on. "Doesn't matter which. What's the mage?"

"Hedge. Weak. Air, I think. He's got a stone on him." Emeric listened. "A big one."

"A burst," said Ward.

"Yes."

Rylan, on Ward's other side, already in his helm with its wings, said, "Then he'll burst it the moment he hears us," and nobody answered him, because it was true.

They had planned for household men. They had planned for the hearts, and the dark, and coming down on the road ahead of the coach where the beeches closed over it, in front of a coachman and six or eight dull men who would see three black knights land in the road out of nowhere and do the sensible thing, which was nothing. Imani's ether for anybody who did something else. Jabari down the ladder already and waiting in the beeches, on foot, where he knew every root. Nobody of hers dies. That had been the plan.

"We go anyway," said Emeric.

"We go anyway," said Ward. "But high. Come down high. Make him burst it high." He stood up. "If he bursts it when we're on the road, we're in it with nothing. If he bursts it when we're at two hundred feet, we've got the capes."

"And if the capes don't throw?"

"Then you'll find out what Sorrel's chute is for," said Ward. "Go."



Jabari had gone down the ladder at the bend a quarter of an hour before, in the last of the light, a big dark shape on Bram's treads disappearing into the beeches. Now he was a note in the trees beside the road at the bend's tightest point, very still, the stillness of a man who has stood in a hedge in the dark before and knows how to do it.

Emeric went over the rail with the hearts awake.

He felt them catch behind his breastbone and between his shoulders, the warm hum of the stones taking his weight, and went down toward the road in a long slanting dive on the push with Ward on his left and Rylan on his right, three black shapes against a sky that was nearly as black, and the beeches coming up under them, and the road a pale ribbon through the trees, and the coach's two lamps moving on it like a pair of slow eyes. Eight hundred feet. Six hundred. Four.

The bright thin note on the coach's near side turned its head straight up at them.

It had heard. Of course it had heard; the hearts hummed and the push hissed and three men in plate falling out of a November sky made a sound like a flock of very large geese, and every head on the road turned up at once. Emeric felt the hired men's notes jump, all nine of them, a single bright shock of fright going through them like a bell. Felt the mage's hand go to the pouch.

Three hundred feet.

He felt the stone go.

It went the way a dead zone always went, the way he had felt it go on the bridge in the war and never wanted to feel again: not a sound, not a flash, not anything a man's eyes could see. A wind, out of nowhere, that moved nothing, that bent no tree and stirred no leaf and did not touch the coach lamps' flames, and went out through him and past him in a wave; and behind it, nothing. The world gone out. Every note on the road gone, every note in the trees, Jabari's, the horses', his own sense going dark round him all at once like a room when the lamps are knocked over. The hair-width to Ward, gone. The hearts behind his breastbone, gone, the warm hum of the stones stopping dead as if somebody had put a hand over them. And the whole weight of the plate coming back onto the harness in one breath, and the straps biting into his shoulders.

And falling.

His own gravity came up in him. He felt it come, the deep quiet thing that rose, the way a hand comes up when a man trips; and it came up into nothing. There was nothing to take hold of. In the dead air there was nothing outside him to push with and nothing to push against, and the thing he had held down hard on a short rope for a week of bruises over the river, so that he would not catch himself when it mattered, was not there to hold down. It was simply gone, as the stones were. He had trained himself not to reach for it. He had not needed to. It was not there to reach.

One. He fell. Two. The beeches came up. Three.

The watchdog's bellows went slack, and Bram's springs, that had been tried against the forge's reference, threw.

The cape opened behind him with a crack like a sail filling, and he fell a while longer with it open, as Sorrel had said he would, and then the wing bit, and took him out of the fall, and he lay on it, like lying on a bed, the way she had said. Cane and silk and the angle of the battens, and nothing else in the whole dead world holding him up. Below him the beeches went by, close, black, their bare tops reaching. He did not kick. He looked where he wanted to go, which was the road beyond the bend, the pale ribbon between the trees, and not at the trees, and slid toward it on the cold air with the dead stones heavy at his back.

The road came up too fast and too narrow. A cape could not land. It could only stop falling. At a hundred feet over the road's open stretch he pulled the ring on his chest, and Sorrel's fourth sheet, the one she had redrawn eleven times, threw the chute out below the cape and not into it, the way Tess had said it must and Abloh had repacked it; and the chute opened with a slam that took his breath, and the cape collapsed above it, and he came down the last hundred feet hanging under silk in the dark like a spider on a thread, and hit the road on his feet, and fell over, and got up.

Ward was down. He felt it rather than saw it; no, he did not feel anything. He saw it. A dark shape in the road twenty yards off, getting up out of a heap of silk. Rylan further, at the edge of the beeches, cutting himself loose.

The coach was coming round the bend at a canter, its lamps swinging, the hired men's horses shouldering round it, somebody shouting.



He had not fought without his sense since the boat's hold.

It was like fighting with one eye shut and the other full of smoke. He had come to depend on it as a man depends on his own hearing, so completely that he did not know he depended on it until it was gone; and now there were men coming at him out of the dark on horses and on foot and he could not feel any of them, where they were, which way they would go, what their shoulders would do a heartbeat before their swords did. He could see the coach lamps and the dark shapes against them and that was all. It was how every other man in the world had fought, always. It was how Ward had fought for thirty years.

Read it, and wait for the second thing.

There was no first thing to read. So he waited.

The first rider came at him down the road with his sword out, a dark shape against the lamps, and Emeric did not move until the horse was on him, and then went inside the cut, under it, close against the horse's shoulder, and took the man's sword arm at the elbow with both hands and pulled, and the man came out of the saddle over him and hit the road. The horse went on. The man got up. Emeric hit him once, in the side of the head, with his gauntlet, and he went down and stayed down.

That was the hybrid. The one thing a dead zone could not take, because it was not outside him; the mana turned inward, into his own arms and legs, the way a knight's ran, with no glyph and no light and nothing at all for anybody on that road to see. He had held it back in the capital and paid for it. He did not hold it back here. It came up into his arms like heat into iron and he let it, and the next man who came at him on foot with a short sword he simply took the sword from, by the blade, in his gauntlet, and threw it into the beeches, and put the man down with the flat of his hand.

Behind him in the dark he could hear Ward. He could not see him; he could hear him, the particular sound of Ward fighting, which was very little sound at all. A grunt. Something heavy falling. A man's voice shouting a name and stopping. Rylan was louder, and laughed once, which was Rylan.

And Jabari.

Jabari came out of the beeches at the bend with no armour and no stone and no suit and nothing but a knight's body and a sword and a road he had ridden a thousand times, and he went through the hired men at the coach's near side the way the river went through a weir. Emeric saw it by the coach lamps in pieces, a shape and a shape and a shape. A man coming round the coach's back wheel with a crossbow, and Jabari already there, because he knew exactly where the wheel was and the man had to look for it. A horse shying at a root in the road's verge, a root Jabari had stepped over without looking down. Two men at once and the bank of the ditch behind them, which Jabari put them in. He fought the way Ward fought, with very little sound and no flourish, and he fought on his own ground, and on his own ground in the dark he was the best of all four of them, and Emeric, with his sense dead and only his eyes, could see it.

The coach had stopped.

It had not stopped because the coachman had stopped it. The coachman was in the ditch with his hands over his head. It had stopped because the team, four big frightened horses, had tried to go on up the road at a gallop with the hired men shouting at them and the whip going, and had gone on without it. The traces were cut. All four, clean, at the bars. The coach stood in the road on its own wheels with its pole down in the mud and the team gone into the dark, and its door open, and in the door, with her skirts held up in one hand and a pair of sewing scissors in the other, Isolde Sallow.

She had cut them from the box. She had climbed out of the coach's window onto the front of it while the coach was still moving and the coachman was still screaming, and leaned down over the team's backs in the dark with her sewing scissors, and cut the traces one by one at the bars, so that nobody could whip the horses on to Vane's gate with her inside. Emeric worked that out later. At the time all he saw was a girl in a green travelling coat standing in the door of a coach with no horses, with her hair coming down and a pair of scissors in her hand.

And behind her in the coach, the steward.

He had a knife. A small one, a gentleman's knife, the kind a man carries to cut a pen; and he had it out, and he had his other hand on Isolde's arm, pulling her back into the coach, and his face in the lamplight was the face of a man who has been told what will happen to him if the girl is not delivered.

Isolde turned round in the door, unhooked the coach lamp from its bracket beside her head, and broke it over his wrist.

The glass went. The oil went, burning, over his hand and his cuff and the coach's floor, a sheet of yellow flame; and the steward screamed and let go of her and of the knife, and fell back into the coach beating at his sleeve. Isolde jumped down into the road. The burning oil lit the whole of the bend for a breath, bright as noon: the beeches, the road, the stopped coach, the hired men down in the ditch and on the road and one still on his feet by the back wheel, with his face turned toward her in the firelight, a hard young face with a scar through one eyebrow and a mouth that had been laughing.

She looked straight at him. Emeric saw her look. She looked at that face the way Ferra looked at a line in a book she meant to remember, and then Jabari was between them, and the man went down.

And then it was over.



Nobody of hers had died. Emeric counted. He could not count with his sense, so he counted the way other men did, walking, with a dark lantern from the coach's box that Rylan lit with a flint, from shape to shape. Eight hired men and the mage. The mage was sitting in the road with his hands on his knees, looking at his empty pouch; he had burst his stone and found out that a man who bursts his own stone is a man in a dead zone with no magic and four knights round him, and he had not tried anything after that. Two of the eight had broken arms. One had a broken head, and was breathing. The rest were bruised and cut and very frightened. The coachman was in the ditch. The steward was in the coach, sobbing, holding his wrist.

The ladder came down half a mile up the road, beyond the edge of the dead air, where the K Discovery's stones could still feel the world; and Imani came down it with her bag and her lamp and walked back down the road into the dead dark on her own two feet, because ether needed nothing but a woman who knew how much to pour.

She went from man to man. She looked at the broken head for a long time, and pressed it, and said it would mend if he was let lie still, and put him under. She set the two arms, quickly, with a strap from the coach and a piece of the coach's broken step for a splint, and put them under. She looked at the steward's wrist in the coach, by the lantern, with her face doing nothing at all, and put salve on it from her bag, and bound it, and looked him in the eye while she did; and the steward looked back at her, and knew her, and did not say anything, and neither did she.

The rest she put under too. One after another, on the road, a pad and the flask and a count, with Rylan holding them still; so that when they woke in an hour, or two, they would wake in a ditch in the dark with a headache and nothing to tell anybody but that black things had come out of the sky.

The steward Ward tied to the coach's back wheel, by his good arm, firmly and not unkindly. Emeric took Ferra's folded paper out of his belt and pushed it into the steward's coat, inside, where it would be found.

"For Lord Sallow," he said.

The steward looked at the blank red wax and the clerk's hand that was nobody's, and shut his eyes.

Isolde's maid was sitting on the coach step with a shawl round her, crying quietly in the way of a woman who has decided that crying is the safest thing to be found doing. Isolde knelt in the road in front of her and took her hands and said something to her in a low voice for a long time; and when she stood up the maid had stopped crying and was nodding.

"She stays," said Isolde, to Emeric. "With the coach. She'll say she was asleep and woke and I was gone and she saw nothing. She'll say it to my father and to Vane and to my aunt." She looked down at the girl. "She'll be believed. She's very good at being believed. She's been my maid for six years."

Then she turned round in the road, in the lantern light, and looked at Jabari.

He was standing by the coach's pole, in the mud, with his sword sheathed and blood on his sleeve that was not all somebody else's, and he had not come any nearer to her than that since the oil went out. He had not said anything. He stood in the road with his big hands at his sides and looked at her, a girl in a torn green coat with her hair coming down and her sewing scissors still in her fist, and Emeric saw that it was the first time they had stood within an arm's reach of each other since she was fifteen.

Nobody else on the road moved.

"Lady Isolde," said Jabari.

His voice cracked on it. He stopped. Then he went down on one knee in the mud of the road in front of her, heavily, the way a big man kneels, and looked up at her, which Emeric thought was probably also the first time.

"I said no," said Jabari. "To running. I meant it. I'd mean it again." He swallowed. "He says there's a church. In daylight. With the doors open. And a priest who'll ask you out loud." He was quiet. "So I'm asking you first. Here. On your father's road. So you've heard it from me and not from a priest." He looked up at her. "Will you marry me?"

"Yes," said Isolde.

She said it at once. She did not wait for the end of the question; she said it on top of the last word, so that it was hard to tell where his question ended and her answer began.

Then she stepped forward and hit him in the chest, with her fist, the one with the scissors in it, hard enough that he rocked back on his heel.

"That's for the letter," she said. "So that's that. So nobody wonders." She hit him again, less hard. "I read it four times. I burned it. I cried on a horse in front of my maid. You great stupid ox." Her voice had gone. "You absolute ox."

"Yes," said Jabari, on his knee in the mud.

She put the scissors in her pocket and took his face in both her hands and kissed him, in the road, in the lantern light, in front of three knights in black plate and a healer with a flask of ether and a mage sitting in the mud with an empty pouch; and Rylan turned round and looked at the beeches with great attention, and Ward looked at the coach wheel, and Imani, kneeling by the man with the broken head, did not look away at all.



Bram's seat brought her up.

They walked the half mile up the road out of the dead air, all of them, with Jabari carrying his pack and her one small bag, which was all she had brought out of the coach: a change of clothes, a hairbrush, her mother's ring, and a book. At the end of the half mile, as they walked out of the dead zone's edge, the world came back. Emeric felt it come back into him like breath into a man who has been under water, the notes all at once, everywhere, the horses gone down the road in the dark, the sleeping hired men, an owl in the beeches, and a mile above, faint and steady, the long warm hum of the K Discovery's stones; and the hearts behind his breastbone woke, and the hair-width to Ward came open by itself, as if a door had been blown shut and swung back.

The ladder hung down out of the black. Isolde looked up it, as Jabari had at the tower, into nothing at all.

"The seat," said Emeric. "Bram's rule. For anybody in skirts. Or frightened."

"I'm not frightened," said Isolde. She looked at the canvas sling. "I'm in skirts." And she sat in it, and buckled the strap across herself as if she had done it every day of her life, and Cade and Tobe hauled her up forty feet into the dark, swinging, with her hands on the ropes; and she laughed the whole way up. Not a small laugh. The laugh of a girl who had sat in a house all her life buying ribbon and being read, and was being hauled into the sky in a sling on the night she was to have been delivered to a man of fifty-eight, which she was not going to be.

Jabari climbed behind her on the treads, and did not look down once, and at the top sat on the deck again with his back to the rail and his eyes shut, and she sat down beside him and took his hand.

They were home before dawn.

Ferra was at her desk in the counting room with the lamp lit and the window open, where the watch on the wall had seen her all night; and when Emeric came up the keep stair in the grey light with his suit off and his hair wet from the ship's basin, she did not look up from the column she was adding.

"Nobody?" she said.

"Nobody. A broken head. It'll mend."

"And the fourth line?"

"Kept."

Ferra wrote the figure at the foot of the column. Then she put her pen down beside the book, and put both her hands flat on the desk on either side of it, and sat like that for a while, looking at the window, where the sky over the river was going from grey to white.

"Good," she said. And picked the pen up again.
