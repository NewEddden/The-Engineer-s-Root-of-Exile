Chapter 118 - Who Stays

Ferra left her three a list of what could not be done without her, and it ran to two sides of a sheet.

She wrote it at first light on the eve of midwinter, in the room at the end of the passage with the window that faced south, while the three of them stood at their tables and pretended not to watch her write it. She knew their names. She had chosen all three of them, and read their pages twice before she chose them, and she thought of them, she noticed that morning, by what they did: the girl from the school, who had come top in every sum for three years and still did; the clerk from the Grayrock tally house, who had kept the only honest book in it; and Paxton's accounts man from Milford, who was slower than the other two and never wrong.

They could copy. They could tally, and keep the day-book, and carry a column from one book to another without losing a figure, and the girl could do it faster than Ferra could. They could take a letter and file it and know where it was a year later. They could do everything in the room except two things.

None of them could sign. And none of them held the map.

She held the map because that was what Chancellor meant. She knew where every figure in four baronies came from and where it went, and which of them was true and which was only correct, and which men's accounts she read twice and why. It was in her head and nowhere else, because the parts of it that could be written down were in eleven different books and the parts that could not be written down were the reason she was Chancellor. The three in the south room held pieces of it. Each held a different piece, on purpose, the way the relay stones held a piece of the code each and no post held the whole.

So the list was long. Anything over a Gold Rand waited. Any new contract waited. Anything from the capital waited, unopened, in the drawer with the lock. Anything with Paxton's name on it waited. Anything about the ore road, the salt, the lime, the tithe, the census, the Ducatis covenant, waited. She wrote it all down in her even hand and gave it to the Grayrock clerk, who was the eldest, and he read it and nodded and put it in the drawer with the lock, where it would sit on top of everything else that was waiting.

Across the passage, in the room Imani had taken for the hospital's business, Imani was doing the same thing and it was taking her a quarter of the time.

Ferra could hear it through two open doors. Imani gave her staff a list too. It was a short list, of what they might do without her, and it was mostly everything. They nodded, and asked two questions, and Imani answered them, and then they went out to do it, and Imani was already reaching for her coat.

Ferra looked at her own long list in the Grayrock clerk's drawer and thought, very clearly, that she had built a place where Kaldo stopped.

She had never thought of it that way before. In the autumn, in the long room, at the end of a quarter day, she had said *nobody reads mine*, and she had meant it as a thing she was proud of and a thing that was a little lonely, both at once. It was true. It had a third half, which she had not said, because she had not seen it until this morning with her coat half on: nobody could run it, either. If the ship went into the sea tomorrow with her on it, the three in the south room would copy and tally and keep the day-book perfectly for a very long time, and nothing in four baronies that needed a signature would ever get one again, and none of them would know which drawer to open first.

She did not write it down. She put her coat on the rest of the way and went out.

Armand was at the foot of the keep stair, in the cold, with his hands folded on the head of his stick.

Emeric gave him the sealed letter in front of her, as he had for the river in the spring and for the coast in the autumn. It said what Armand was to do if they did not come back, and in what order, and Ferra had read it and signed it and sealed it and did not need to read it again. Armand put it inside his coat without looking at it. Vesk was standing a pace behind him, the way he had stood behind him all month, and he looked at the letter going into the coat and then at nothing.

Declan was on the wall already, in his suit, with the cloak on over it. Ferra could see him from the stair, walking the stretch above the western gate the way he walked it every morning, and she thought he was walking it a little more slowly than usual so that he would be where he could see the quay when they went down it.

The coat was ready at the quay street at nine, as the tailor had promised it and not a minute before.

Ferra went to pay for it, because she was going to pay for it whatever anybody said, and Osric came because he had been told to keep his opinions until it was finished and it was finished. Imani came because she was passing, and stayed.

He put it on in the tailor's back room and came out in it, and the tailor stood back, and nobody said anything for a moment, which the tailor took correctly.

It was charcoal and silver, and there was one colour in it. The coat came to his knees and closed double over his chest, with lapels, and its skirts were split behind for riding. The collar stood high and closed at the throat with silver frogs. Down the front, over the fastening, the tailor had set a panel of grey brocade, and down the panel ran a column of small medallions worked in silver cord, one under another from the collar to the sash. The cuffs were edged in silver. A broad sash was wrapped round his waist and tied at the side. On his right shoulder there was a fringed silver epaulette, and from it a double chain of silver ran across his breast to his left shoulder, where a half-cape was pinned and fell behind him to the backs of his knees. The half-cape was the colour. It was blue, a blue so dark it went black under a lamp and only showed itself blue when he moved, and Saban had chosen it off a Milford bolt for exactly that reason. Fitted dark trousers, and tall grey boots.

The half-cape was pinned by a brooch.

Ferra had not seen the brooch before. It was a silver star, eight points, rough at the back where it had been worked by hand, and in the middle of it was set a grey stone, smooth, worn soft at its edges, the kind of stone a man picks out of a stream and throws back. She knew that stone. He had carried it every day for ten years, and she had seen it in his hand more than once when he thought nobody was looking.

"Bram," Emeric said, when he saw her looking. "Last night."

Bram had come to the long room the evening before, after the forge was lit, with a silver star he had cut from a sheet that afternoon and nothing else, and asked for the stone. Emeric had given it to him. Bram had sat at the end of the table under the lamp with a jeweller's loupe he had borrowed from the goldsmith and a set of fine tools, and set it by hand in two hours, and when Emeric said that the forge would have done it in ten minutes Bram had not looked up.

"Not everything's a job for your forge," Bram had said. And then, when he had finished and was handing it across: "It comes out. There's a catch at the back. You'll want it for the other thing."

Ferra looked at the stone in its star and did not say anything, and wrote the coat in her book instead: *Coat, court, for Emberfell. Three Gold Rand.* She said the figure out loud as she wrote it, as a figure, and the tailor, who had expected to be paid in the spring, stood very straight.

Osric had been waiting.

"The epaulette," he said.

"It's finished," said Emeric. "You may have your opinion."

"It's one epaulette. A man with one epaulette looks as if he's been in a fight and lost the other."

"That's the fashion."

"It's a fashion," Osric agreed. "For men who have lost fights."

Imani had been looking at the collar the whole time, the high one with the silver frogs that closed at his throat.

"Eat before the dinner," she said. "Properly. Nobody breathes in a collar like that, and a man who hasn't eaten and can't breathe falls over in a hall." She said it to Emeric as a dosage. "I'll not be picking a prince off a queen's floor."

They went down the river an hour later, all of them, and the island went away behind them round the bend, and Ferra did not look back at it, because she had decided in the morning that she would not.

At the lower water the ship lay in the lagoon where they had left her, black and low with her lamps lit and nothing coming out of her stack, and they went out to her in the boat and sat down in her saloon, at the table he had drawn the size of the long room's, in the warm, with the dials on the wall and the smell of bread coming aft through the new door from the galley. It was the only table they would have for a week. Armand and Bram were not at it. Their chairs at the long room's table were empty tonight, and would be for a week, and the table aboard her did not have places for them.

Ward brought the promise.

He did it before anything else, before Ferra had opened the book, and he did it because he was the one who had said *a warship* in the autumn and been answered.

"You told us," he said to Emeric. "The first time she goes anywhere, she carries nothing anybody on the ground could look up and be afraid of. Not a weapon. Not a man in harness." He did not look at the armoury door. He did not need to. "Six suits have come off the forge since. Which is it?"

Nobody said anything. Ferra had her pen over the page and did not move it.

"I'll give you my reading," Ward said. "A knight doesn't leave his sword. Not in a friend's harbour and not in his own bed. A sword on a knight's hip on a quay full of knights wearing theirs frightens nobody. They'll all have one." He put one hand flat on the table. "What would frighten a harbour isn't a sword. It's her."

"The promise was about her," Emeric said. "It holds for her. She carries no weapon of her own. Nothing mounted on her, nothing that points out of her, nothing that could be aimed from her at anybody on the ground." He looked round the table. "Her people go as they are. Knights wear their swords. The suits go on under the capes, drawn as cloaks, with the hoods up and the helms left on their shelves in the hold. Nobody on that quay sees plate or a stone's light. Nobody has anything to look up at."

Ward considered it, and nodded once.

Ferra turned back a page in the book, to the promise, which she had written down in the autumn word for word as he said it. Under it, on the next page, was the amendment to the coal, two days old, in her own hand. She turned forward again and wrote under that: *The ship, unarmed. Her people, as they are.* And the date.

She looked at the two amendments, one above the other.

A promise with a correction in the margin, in her hand, dated. And a rule with a correction under it, in her hand, dated, two days before. She had never crossed anything out in her life, and she did not now, and both of the old lines stood above the new ones exactly as they had been said. She noticed that it was the second in two days. She did not write that down.

Then she wrote the ceiling, because somebody had to, and it was hers to write.

For the river in the spring, and again for the coast in the autumn, the risk had been accepted with both halves of the key in one hull. In the autumn it had been accepted with two of the six aboard and refused at three, and Saban had sulked about it for a day. Tonight it was four. Ward and Imani and Saban and herself. And Emeric. And both halves of the relay key, his and hers, in one iron hull, going over the sea to a foreign harbour.

She wrote it in the same form as the river and the coast, because the form was the point of it. *Risk accepted*, and the date, and *the holder of the barony aboard with four of the Standing Order and both halves of the key; the Secretary of the Arcane holds Kaldo under the sealed letter.* Then, under it, the new ceiling and the reason for it, because a ceiling moved without a reason written beside it was not a ceiling at all. *A treaty wants its signatories in the room. A ship that has crossed the band is no longer a trial.*

Saban was going this time. He did not say anything about it, not one word, not even about the day he had sulked, which Ferra thought was a good deal worse than if he had.

She went round the table for the reasons, because they belonged in the book, and they gave them one at a time, each in their own voice.

"The levers," said Ward. "And because."

"A prince with one guard in a foreign hall has no guard at all," said Osric. "I read that somewhere. I think it was Ward."

"She has a hot, crowded town and nobody's ever told her what's in her drains," said Imani. "And I want to be in the room when things are decided. You know that. It's in the book."

"If the deal happens, the lane's mine," said Saban. "I'd rather be there when they draw it."

"She signs treaties," said Emeric, and pointed his pen at Ferra, and Ferra wrote herself down without comment.

Fletcher said the helm, and that it was his ship. Orrin said the stones, and that somebody had to fill the house bank every evening, and that it was him, and that he had been promised the cushion. Tobe said the engine, and that Bram had said he could, and went red. From the galley, through the open door, without looking up from whatever she was doing at the hob, Maren said that somebody had to feed them, and that was the whole of her reason and more than most people had.

Vantic had not given hers at the table.

She had given it to Ferra, an hour before, on the beach, the two of them walking up from the boat with their books under their arms. Vantic had come down to Kaldo for the winter quarter day, as she came every quarter, and had gone through the barony's books with Ferra the day before, a day early, because the quarter day would find the Chancellor two miles over the sea. She had said, walking, that a treaty that committed Kaldo's revenue to a foreign lane would change the security on her House's loan. That her House would want to know how. That she would rather see the thing it was committing to than read about it after, because she had learned in the autumn, on a beach not far from this one, that she saw a thing differently before she read about it. She had said all of that as if she were reading it off a page, and Ferra had written it down later in the same words, because they were true. Whether they were all of it, Ferra did not know.

Emeric had offered her a piece of the jewellery, that morning, at the keep: a chain like Saban's, charged beads in gold. Vantic had looked at it for a moment and declined it, politely. She was the House of Ducatis's, she said, and not Kaldo's envoy, and it would not do for anybody at Emberfell to be confused about which. Ferra had noted it in the book, without comment, under the collar and the cuffs and the chain.

The cabins were hers to give, and she gave them in the form she would have used for billeting men on a march.

The stern cabin was Emeric's. Fletcher had built that bed for a prince, and she was not going to argue with a shipwright about a bed. She would share with Imani, in the first cabin to port. Ward and Osric had the cabin nearest the companion, because Ward had asked for it without saying why and she had not needed him to. Vantic had a cabin to herself, the second to starboard, and Ferra wrote the reason beside it, because it was a true one: a creditor's agent keeps a report, and a report kept in a shared cabin gets read. Maren had the fourth. Fletcher and Saban and Orrin and Tobe went forward to the crew's berths, and Fletcher said that a master slept where she moved most anyway, and that he had always meant to try one of those berths.

That left six berths empty, as the scale had said it would.

The draft terms were in her strongbox in the stern cabin, and they opened with grain.

She had read them three times. They were good terms. They were fair to both sides, and clear, and every figure in them was one she would have put her name to. And they began with grain, because grain was what Kaldo's ledger had more of than it knew what to do with: tonnage, prices, a year's surplus that could go down the coast on the coasting boats and come back as sulphur. It was a very good opening for a barony with too much grain.

Ferra did not know what Emberfell had too little of. Nobody at this table knew. Saban's brief was the coasting men's brief, what they said in the fish market at the lower water, and Saban was the first to say it was years old.

So she wrote it in the minute book, under the promise and the coal, in a line of its own, dated. *Offer what she lacks, not what we have too much of. We do not know what she lacks.* F.

She turned the book and put it in front of Emeric and waited.

He read it. He read it twice, the way he read her objections, which was more carefully than he read almost anything else, and she watched him think about it and decide.

"It stays," he said. "Grain costs us nothing to offer. It opens a table. If she doesn't want it, she'll say so, and then we're sitting at the table, and we can ask her what she does want."

Ferra took the book back. She wrote his answer beside her objection, in the same hand, in his words: *Grain costs nothing to offer and opens a table.* And she left both lines exactly where they were.

It was not the first objection of hers that he had overruled in writing. There was a page near the front of the oldest ledger in the barony, from his first year, where she had written one about a man named Cobb, and dated it, and filed it, and it was still there, and it had never once been collected.

Saban was in the hold with his samples when she went down to check the strongbox's hasp, sitting on the armoury bench under the one lamp with the crate open in front of him and the bar of steel across his knees.

He was not packing them. They had been packed for three days. He was looking at them.

"Glass," he said, when he saw her. "Paper. Ink. Printed pages. Soap. Steel." He touched each as he said it. "It's a good crate. It's the best crate I've ever taken anywhere." He put the steel down. "And I'd give the whole of it for one cask of something I've never been able to buy."

Ferra sat down on the other end of the bench.

"There's a pitch," Saban said, "that comes out of the ground in Duvrenne. Not tree pitch. Stone pitch. Black. The ground-houses up there sell it to the big yards in the south, and I've never once seen a barrel of it this side of the coast, because nobody on our water could afford one. It doesn't go soft in the sun the way pine tar does. A seam paid with it stays paid." He looked at the lamp. "And it burns."

"Where did you see it?"

"I smelled it. The whole of Duvrenne's harbour smelled of it, the time I went up for the grain. It's in the tar. Every counting house on the front had a pot of it somewhere." He shrugged. "I didn't buy any. It wasn't grain. I'd gone to sell grain."

"What's it for?" Ferra said.

"Boats," said Saban. "Walls. Burning."

She did not write it down. It was not cargo, and it was not in the terms, and it was not anything she could put in a column. Emeric was forward with Fletcher at the helm, going over the night's run, and did not hear any of it. Saban closed the crate after a while, and latched it, and the two of them went back up.

The dispatch book came last. It always did.

She opened it at the saloon table, after the others had gone forward to the day room, and wrote the day's line, and then turned back to see what else was open.

Two lines, on facing pages, each without its answer.

On the left, a week old: *To the sovereign of Emberfell. By coaster out of the lower water, west. Kaldo's seal and the Chancellor's.* It would reach Emberfell a fortnight after they did. She had known that when she wrote it and had sent it anyway.

On the right, much older: *To the Crown. By the notary's packet, Milford.* It was the answer Emeric had written in the first week of the autumn to the chancery's polite letter about the stone, every word of it true and none of it an answer to the question asked. It had gone down the Milford road more than three months ago. A reply to it could have come back up the same road twice by now.

None had.

She wrote the day's date beside it, in the column where a letter's arrival went, and after the date the words *nothing received*, because that was what had happened, and a thing that had happened went in the book. It was the first line in the dispatch book she had ever written that had no carrier and no hand and no seal, and described a letter that did not exist.

She looked at it for a while. Then she closed the book.

Vantic had read the ledger the day before. Ferra had watched her do it.

She had gone through every book at Ferra's table in the long room, one at a time, in the order they always went, and copied what she needed into her own. When she came to the page in the barony's main ledger with the winter's forge accounts on it, she had come to a line in Ferra's hand that said *equipment, diplomatic*, and a figure beside it, written larger than any other on the page.

Vantic's pen had stopped over it for the length of a breath.

Then she had copied it into her own book, exactly, the words and the figure and the date, and turned the page. She had not asked what the equipment was, or who it was for, or why a barony with no ambassador anywhere in the world had a line for diplomacy worth fifteen Gold Rand and more.

Ferra had seen her do exactly the same thing in the summer, with a word that was *lent*.

Neither of them said anything about it, then or on the beach, and Ferra did not write it down.

They took her out at dusk to prove her, because Fletcher said a ship that had been fitted was a ship nobody knew yet.

Not everybody went. Vantic stayed on the beach, in Fletcher's cottage, with the door open and her book on her knee, and said that she did not board a ship until she meant to go somewhere in it, and that tomorrow would do. Saban stayed with her, to make sure nobody on the beach wondered why a Ducatis woman was sitting alone in a shipwright's cottage on midwinter's eve. Imani stayed because Maren had set pans of water in the cold room's bunker and Imani wanted the first of the ice for her chest and did not trust anybody else to bring it.

Ferra went, because she had signed for every rivet in her.

They steamed out through the gap in the bar on the boiler's stones, with no smoke from the stack, which Fletcher still could not look at, and a dozen miles out in the last grey of the light she ran on the top of the swell, lightening, until the screw raced and came up, and the sea went away under her.

Ward was at the levers.

Emeric stood beside him in the day room, under the dome, with his hands at his sides. Ferra stood at the chart table with the book open under the caged lamp. Outside the ports the sea had gone dark, and then there was no sea, only the last line of light at the edge of the world, going down.

"Take your hands off," Emeric said.

Ward looked at him.

"Off the levers," said Emeric. "I want to see if she'll take it from me."

Ward did not take his hands off. He looked at Emeric for a long moment, and Ferra knew the look, because she had seen him give it to a stair before he went up it.

"You don't hold her yourself," Ward said. "You could. Why don't you?"

"The Branch," said Emeric. "Four stones and two tons, in the thaw. I held it for less than a count of ten and it cost me the day." He did not say what she weighed. Nobody had ever said what she weighed, out loud, and he did not now. "She's a great many times that. And she doesn't need me to hold her. The deciding stone holds her. All she needs is somebody at the levers."

Ward took his hands off the levers.

He did not take them far. He held them an inch away, both of them, the right over the push and the left over the lift, with his fingers open and not quite touching the brass.

The levers moved.

Ferra saw it. She was looking straight at them. The left lever went forward a notch by itself, and then another, with nobody's hand on it, the way a door moves in a draught except that there was no draught, and the ship went on going up under her feet, and the right lever came off its stop and eased forward to the first mark and held there. There was nothing touching either of them. Ward's hands hung in the air an inch from the brass, open.

"That's where it should be," said Emeric, looking at Ward's hands. "Exactly there."

Ward did not answer. He did not move his hands either, for the whole of the climb.

At the bottom of the band the air in the veins came in cold, and the probe stone behind its pane in the port vein began to glow, and the levers came back a notch each and she levelled there. They held her there for the best part of an hour, going nowhere, and then Ferra went below.

She went down the ladder from the day room into the galley, and aft through the store, and opened the cold room's door, and the air that came out of it was colder than the night outside.

In the bunker at the top, Maren's pans were ice.

Not skinned over. Ice, all the way through, white, with the cold channel from the vein running through the wall behind them and breathing out a cold that stopped the hand. Ferra stood with the door open and looked at it for a while. She had seen a great many things in this ship that she did not have a column for. This one she had.

She took one of the pans back up to the day room and set it on the chart table under the lamp and weighed the ice in it on her little brass letter scale, a pound at a time, because the stores book was going to need a line for it. *Ice, made aboard, in the band. Eleven pounds, the first pan.*

Then she looked up.

The two brass levers were moving under the dome, a notch, and back, with nobody's hand on them, and Ward's hands an inch away. And under the lamp on the chart table, her own pen was writing the date.
