Chapter 118 - The Flood

Saban came in from the north on the twenty-ninth of June, and for once he did not go to the exchange first. He went to the guardroom, and asked for Ward, and the two of them came up the keep stair together, and Emeric knew from the sound of their feet on the stone that whatever it was, Saban had already told it once and enjoyed it.

He sat down at Emeric's table without being asked and put his hat on it.

"It's gone," he said.

"What has?"

"All of it." Saban looked round for something to drink, and found nothing, and decided to tell it anyway. "My man at the crossing inn. The one who drinks with the sorters. He was in the yard on the fourteenth with a cart he'd hired for the day, because he hires a cart once a month to have a reason to be in the yard, and he saw it go." He spread his hands on the table. "Sacks. Out of the back room of the stone house, the one with the window. Not the front, where the bags go in and out every day. The back. The room nobody goes in but the master and the man who sweeps." He held up fingers. "Nine sacks. Crown canvas, the old kind with the leather tags, and so full they'd split two of them and tied them round with rope. Onto the north wagon. All at once. In the middle of the morning with the whole yard watching." He put his hands down. "And the master of the house standing at the back door in his shirt, watching them go, with a face my man says he'd not wish on a dog."

Nobody said anything.

"He got a letter," said Saban. "That's what the sorters said, after, in the inn, when they'd had enough. A letter from the chancery. A form. Asking for his returns, three years of them, for the audit, and his warrant, and his oath on it." He shrugged. "That's all. They get one every few years. Every house on the road gets one. He'd had two before and sent them back with his returns and thought nothing of it." He looked at Emeric. "But this time he had nine sacks of crown post in his back room that he'd been holding for four years for a man who paid him, and it came to him all at once what his oath on three years of returns was going to be worth when somebody from the chancery came down to count the sacks." Saban smiled. It was not a pleasant smile. "So he sent them. Every one. On the next wagon. With a note to the southern desk saying that the house regretted a delay in forwarding owing to damp in the back room, and enclosing the held correspondence."

"Damp," said Ward.

"Damp," said Saban. "Four years of it."

Emeric sat very still at the table.

Nine sacks. Every crown letter Kaldo had sent since the first autumn. The aqueduct, the first census, the soap, the plague rows, the war report in all three copies, the twin year's fifty-one crown copies, the November report with its two pages about Milford that somebody had opened and read and resealed with a seal that lacked Bram's nick. Four years of Kaldo, all at once, in nine split sacks on a wagon going north, arriving at the chancery's southern desk on a single morning like a flood coming down a dry river.

"They're buried," Saban said. "The southern desk. The tavern in the capital says three clerks have done nothing for a week but open Kaldo. Somebody went to the master of the desk and said there was a barony in the south that had been writing to the crown every week for four years, and the master said nonsense, he'd have known, and they showed him the sacks." He picked up his hat and turned it. "They say he's been in to see Keller twice."

"And Sethe," said Ward.

"Ah," said Saban, with real pleasure. "Sethe."

It had taken about a week, he said. That was the thing that he could not get over, and told three times, in three different ways, as though he were showing them a coin from three sides. A week. The sacks went north on the fourteenth, and on the seventeenth one of the three companies on retainer in the halls round Wayford sent its captain to Sethe's hall to say that the company had been offered a better retainer in the west and would be leaving at the end of the month, and he regretted it. On the eighteenth, the second. On the twentieth, the third, which did not send its captain or regret anything; it simply left in the night, and took four of Sethe's horses with it. The two mages from the north, the ones living in his hall as guests, whose wine his steward bought, rode out of Wayford on the twenty-first with their baggage, northward, without being seen to say goodbye to anybody.

On the twenty-second Sethe's steward was seen at the crossing inn, quietly, offering grain. Sethe's own grain, from his own barns, the grain a man lays in when he means to feed a war through a winter. At a price that made the corn factors at the inn look at each other.

And on the twenty-fourth two of the houses in the capital that held Sethe's paper declined to roll it over at the quarter. Politely. In writing. With regret.

"He's done," said Saban. "His second war. All of it. Three companies, two mages and a barn full of grain, and it's gone in a week, and nobody fought it. Nobody lifted a hand." He put his hat back on the table. "A clerk sent a form."

Ward was looking out of the window, at the yard, where Rylan's board was on the guardroom wall.

"A clerk sent a form," he said, quietly, as if he were trying out the shape of it.

Emeric was glad. He felt it go through him when Saban said Sethe's name, a hot hard gladness that went down into his chest and his hands, and he sat with it and let it go through. Sethe had paid a man at the crossing to pull Kaldo's post, and read it, and sent men across Milford to burn a barn and drive off ninety-one head and kill a carter and Dunnel and a boy who had wanted to see an engine. Sethe had sat in his long room at Wayford on Thursdays asking travellers about Kaldo's wall. He was done. It was good.

And then, under the gladness, slowly, something else came up, and he did not like it, and it would not go back down.

Two years. Two years of slips and folds and nicked seals, of Hale walking to the post house every Friday in the rain, of the twin going down the river in bales and casks and Fletcher's punt, of Ferra's finger going down the columns every Monday. A year of the twin mail. Dunnel dead with the twin burning in his cart. All of it built, piece by piece, for a court, for Keller's cold eye, to prove some day what had been done.

And a thin young clerk with ink on his cuffs, whose name none of them knew, had sat down at a table one morning in May and written twenty-six copies of the dullest letter in the chancery, and it had done in a week what Kaldo had not done in four years.

"You look," said Saban, watching him, "like a man who's won a horse race on a horse that wasn't his."

"Something like that."

"Hm." Saban considered him. "The clerk didn't know what he'd find," he said. "He sent the form because the twin year sat on his master's table three hours and he wrote down who was in the room. He'd never have looked at the southern road if you'd not given him something to look at it for." He put his hat on. "The horse was yours. The clerk only opened the gate."

He went down to the exchange then, to tell it all again to Ferra, and Emeric heard him on the stair, already starting.



The swimming lessons began the same week, because the river was warm enough, and because Ward said that if Kaldo was going to be summoned to court it was not going to go with nineteen men in forty-one who would drown in a ditch.

It was the third book. Water. Emeric had finished it in May, after Moving Unseen, and it was the shortest of the three and the one Ward had read slowest: how a man in armour gets out of it in water before it takes him down, which straps first; how to cross a river in kit with a pole and a rope and the men tied together; how to come up a bank at night without a splash; the drowned-man drill, which Imani had written two pages of herself, in her own hand, on how to get the water out of a man and the air back into him, and which had a drawing in it that made Osric go pale.

And swimming. Plain swimming. Which you could not learn from a book, Rylan said, any more than you could learn to ride from one.

So on a hot still afternoon at the end of June the garrison of Kaldo came down to the mill race below the works, where the water came off the wheel into a long deep pool before it went back into the river, and stood on the bank in their shirts and their drawers in a line, forty-one men, every one of whom had stood on the wall in the war, and looked at the water.

Half of Kaldo was on the other bank. Nobody had told them. Nobody had needed to.

Rylan went in first. He went in off the wheel house step in one long flat dive that hardly broke the water, and came up in the middle of the pool, and rolled over on his back, and lay there, floating, with his arms out and his face to the sky and the water lapping at his ears, and said to the sky that it was lovely, and they should all come in.

The nineteen who could swim came in. Ward came in, without a word, in his shirt, and swam the length of the pool and back with a slow heavy steady stroke and stood up in the shallows at the end with the water running off him and looked at the other twenty-two on the bank.

"In," he said.

They went in at the shallow end, where it came up to a man's chest, holding on to a rope Bram had strung across the pool on two stakes. And Rylan swam up and down the rope among them, on his back, on his front, telling them to put their faces in the water, and lie on it, it would hold them, it wanted to, and to kick, not like that, like a frog, look, and they put their faces in the water and came up choking, and lay on it and sank, and kicked like men stamping on a fire.

Osric sank.

He sank more completely than any man Emeric had ever seen. He let go of the rope when Rylan told him, and lay forward on the water as he was told, and went straight to the bottom like a sack of nails, and stayed there, and the bubbles came up, and Rylan and Declan went down after him and hauled him up by the arms, and he came up roaring and blowing and furious and stood in the chest-deep water with his hair in his eyes and said that he was a big man, and big men sank, everybody knew that, it was the bones.

"You're full of air," said Rylan, still on his back, still floating. "Same as me. You're holding on to it. Let go."

"I'll let go of you," said Osric, and lunged, and sank again.

The far bank laughed. It laughed so loud and so long that the people on the near bank started laughing at it, and Osric came up out of the pool the second time and heard it, and stood there in the water with the whole town laughing at him from both banks, and Emeric saw him decide, the way he had seen Osric decide in the war on the wall when there was a ladder coming up and only him to meet it.

He put his face in the water.

He lay forward on it. He let go of the air. And he did not float, exactly, but he did not sink either: he hung there in the pool, face down, arms out, with his back just under the surface, like a log, for a count of ten, and then came up spluttering and stood, and turned round to the far bank with his arms in the air.

The far bank cheered.

Emeric was in the water too, at the deep end, because he had said he would be. He could swim. He had learned it in a cold lake on his other world at the age of seven and in a river at Kaldo in the first summer, and he trod water in the deep end of the race in his shirt and watched the garrison learn, and after a while noticed that he was cold. Ward was not. Rylan was not. The water off the wheel was colder than it looked, colder than the June air, and the men at the rope were turning blue at the lips and their teeth were going.

He let a little of it go into his arms and his chest. Not much. Only what was needed. Told it what a fire was.

The cold went away. He floated in the deep end of the race, warm through, and the water round him did not feel like June water off a wheel any more, it felt like a bath. He did not let it show. He kept his face as blue and miserable as everybody else's, and his arms going, and his teeth set.

When he climbed out at the end he pulled the wet shirt off over his head to wring it, because it was clinging to him and the cloth was cold even if he was not, and stood on the bank in the sun with the water running off him.

He did not look across the race. He did not need to.

He had been keeping the sense open since the spring. Not far: thin, the way Armand had taught him by the fire, a breath let out round him and left there. All day, every day, at his table and in the yard and walking down the high street, the way a man on his other world might wear new spectacles from morning to night until his eyes stopped aching and forgot they had them on. The first month it had given him a headache by noon. The second, by evening. Now he forgot it was open at all, until something in it moved, and then he found that he knew where everything round him was, in every direction at once, behind him as well as in front, the way a man knows where his own hands are in the dark.

So he knew the far bank without turning round. A hundred people, more, a crowd of warmths along the stones and up on the bridge, and three of them he would have known in any crowd in the kingdom.

Imani was at the front, by the water, where she had stood all afternoon with her basket because the drowned-man drill was hers and she had wanted to see whether anybody would need it. She had been turned toward the race all afternoon, as was her work. The race was empty now. The garrison was out of it, on the near bank, wringing shirts. And Imani was still turned the same way, toward the near bank, toward where he stood, wide and warm and not moving at all.

Ferra was further up, at the foot of the bridge, with a book. He felt her turned away from the race, toward the book, small and hard and close. Then turned toward it. Then away. Then toward it again, and staying.

And on the bridge itself, above them all, a note he had never yet been able to read properly. Vantic's. It was always too quiet, too small for the size of the woman, as if she carried a lamp with her hand cupped round it; he had puzzled at it in the spring and put it away. She had been writing. He could feel the small regular movement of her hand on the book. It had stopped.

He looked down at himself, then, for no reason he could have given.

He had not looked properly in a long while. There had been no glass in the keep worth looking in, and nothing to look for in it. He had been a thin man on his other world, who sat at a desk ten hours a day and walked to the train, and a thin boy in the capital, who let his blade rot along with everything else. And what stood on the bank of the mill race in the sun was neither of those. Four years of Ward putting him on the ground every morning and making him get up. Rylan's sack and Rylan's pole since June. And under all of it, since the spring, the other thing, the mana drawn in instead of out, night after night behind the shutters, and the ache in the muscle the next morning that he had taken for nothing but an ache. His chest and his shoulders and his arms had been made into something he had not been introduced to. It was like finding a stranger's coat on his own peg and discovering that it fitted.

From somewhere along the near bank, Osric whistled. Not the tune from the capital. The other kind of whistle.

"Highness has been eating at Maren's tables," Osric said, loudly, to the whole garrison.

The garrison laughed. The far bank, which had heard, laughed louder. Emeric pulled the wet shirt back on over his head, inside out, at once, and felt it stick to him cold all the way down, and the far bank laughed harder at that.

He felt Imani turn away, at last, toward her basket. He felt Ferra's book, which had been closed, open again. On the bridge, the small regular movement of Vantic's pen began again, and went on, quite steadily, and then stopped once more, briefly, as if the pen had been put down to cover something, and went on.

Rylan was standing beside him, wringing out his own shirt, and looked at him once, sideways.

"You're not shivering," Rylan said.

"It's warm in the sun."

"It is," said Rylan comfortably, and went on wringing his shirt. "It is warm in the sun." And he said nothing else about it, then or afterward, which was so like what Ward would have done that Emeric wondered, walking back up the hill, how many things the garrison of Kaldo had seen and not said in the last four years, and found that he did not want to count.



Ivo Thorne had been grinding the same lens for a month.

He did it in a corner of the glasshouse on a bench he had built for it himself, under the one good window, with a dish of iron and a disc of the best glass he had made since he came, and a pot of paste that he made of sand ground finer and finer, and water, and his thumb. Round and round, the disc on the dish and the dish under the disc, round and over and round, for hours, and then a finer paste, and round again. Imani had told him in April to grind her a lens the slow way, the way that would work when the man who had made her little brass hinge was not in Kaldo, and Ivo had taken it as an insult to his trade and a challenge to his honour, and he had ground every day since.

"It's a good lens," he said, when Emeric came in on the first of July to look at it. He did not look up from the dish. "Best I've made. Best on the north coast. And it's no use to her."

"Why not?"

"Because it's a reading glass." Ivo held it up to the window, his good eye shut and the milky one open. "It makes a thing twice as big. Three times, if you hold it right. Her little hinge makes a thing a hundred times as big." He put it down on the felt with a look of deep personal grievance. "I can't grind a bead. Nobody can grind a bead. You'd have to grind it to the size of a mustard seed and round all over, and by the time you'd got it round you'd have ground it to nothing."

Tess was there, in her chair, by the bench. She had been coming to the glasshouse most days since the lehr was built, because Ivo talked while he worked, which Bram did not, and he talked about colour, which nobody else in Kaldo did. She had the reading glass in her hand now and was looking at her own thumb through it.

"Two," she said.

Emeric looked at her.

"Put two in a row," Tess said. "In a tube. The first one makes it three times bigger. Then the second one looks at the big one and makes that three times bigger." She held the glass up and looked through it at him, and he saw her eye, very large and grey, and then she moved it and it was gone. "Doesn't it?"

It did. It was the compound microscope, that a pair of spectacle makers in the same flat wet country as the cloth merchant had put together a lifetime before the cloth merchant's bead, by holding two lenses in a tube and looking through both at once. It was worse than the bead, at first. It had been worse than the bead for a hundred years, until somebody worked out how to grind the lenses better. And then it had been better than the bead forever.

"Yes," he said. "It does."

"Then why didn't you make her one of those?" said Tess.

"Because the bead was quicker. And better." He looked at Ivo's lens on the felt. "For now."

They built it that week. A tube of brass rolled by Bram from sheet, the length of a man's forearm, and another tube sliding inside it so the length could change, and Ivo's lens at the bottom of the inner tube, and a second lens at the top that Ivo ground in four days, smaller, under protest, swearing at it in the dialect of the north coast. A stand of iron to hold the tube upright, and a little plate under it to put the thing on, and a mirror of polished tin under the plate to catch the light from the window and throw it up.

It made a thing forty times as big. It was blurred at the edges, and coloured, with a little rainbow fringe round everything, which made Ivo swear again, at length, and say that it was the glass and not his grinding and that he would make better glass. Imani's hinge was clearer and nearly three times stronger.

But you could look through it with both eyes open, sitting down, with your head up. And you did not have to be a healer, or a prince, or have hands that did not shake, to use it. Any child in Sela's room could have put an eye to it.

Tess did. She put a drop of the mill race on the plate, and her eye to the tube, and turned the screw that slid the tubes, and sat in her chair at the bench in the glasshouse with her eye to the brass and did not move or speak for so long that Ivo stopped swearing and came to stand behind her.

"Well?" he said.

"They're everywhere," Tess said, without looking up. "In the race. In the water that turns the wheel." Her voice was quite steady. "Swimming. All of them. I put my hand in that water every day."

"So does everybody," said Ivo.

"I know," said Tess. She did not take her eye from the tube. "I'm going to draw them. All of them. Every kind." She reached for her slate without looking. "Somebody should."
