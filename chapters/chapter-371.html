Chapter 371 - Hollow Night, Year Sixteen

By noon on the seventh day she had stopped talking to anybody.

Imani had expected that. It was on the card, in her own hand, from August: Short-tempered (not her fault). What she hadn't expected was how politely Ilenne would do it. She sat at the bay house kitchen table with the shutters half closed against the light off the water, with a cup of fresh water in front of her that she drank from every few minutes and that did nothing, and when Imani asked her something she answered with one word and then looked at the cup until Imani stopped.

The sheen had gone off her a little after eleven. Imani had watched it go. It didn't go all at once. It went the way the shine goes off a wet stone on a step when the sun gets to it, from the edges in, until what was left was a grey-white that looked like chalk had been rubbed into it. By one the slits under her ears were lying shut and tight. By two she had her palms pressed into her eyes.

"The pool's there," said Imani, for the third time. "Nobody's going to write it down wrong."

"Seven," said Ilenne, into her hands.

"It's the seventh now. It's been the seventh since you woke up."

"Seven is night." She took her hands away and looked at the window, at the angle of the light on the shutter slats, and then at the clock on Fletcher's shelf, which she had learned to read in September and which she disliked, because it told her things. "Seven days. From night to night. You say, a week. A week is seven days." She put the heels of her hands back. "Not seven and a little. Not six and a lot. Seven."

So Imani sat with her. It was the last day of October, a Friday, cold and very bright, with a north wind putting a chop on the bay that slapped at the sea wall below the garden and threw spray up over the pilots' gates. Up the hill behind the house the town was getting ready for the night. You could hear it even from here: a cart going up the lower ward full of something that rattled, a woman shouting a child's name twice, then a third time with the middle name. Somebody hammering. Someone, already, two hours before dusk, trying out a fiddle.

The last soak had been on the evening of Friday the twenty-fourth. Imani had written the time. A quarter past five, an hour in the pool, out at a quarter past six; then every morning since, a fresh bath in the pilot's tin tub out of the mains, which carried her, as the card said, like a drink of water carries you, and no further. On the fourth day she'd gone dull at the edges and said nothing. On the fifth she had the headache by evening. On the sixth she'd been to the Harbour Board for her watch and back, and had sat on the Board's step afterwards for half an hour with her hood up and her eyes shut, and Saban's clerk had come out to ask was she ill, and she had said, "Dry," which the clerk had taken for a remark about the weather and agreed with.

At a quarter to five Zale came down the lane on his stick with a paper bag of hot chestnuts and let himself in by the garden gate without knocking, which he had stopped doing in September. He looked at her. He put the chestnuts on the table and didn't say anything at all, which was the right thing, and sat down on the bench under the window and took his watch out and put it on his knee.

At five he said, "That's seven."

"Night," said Ilenne.

"It's dark enough." He turned his head and looked out through the slats at the water, where the light had gone out of the sky over South Sentinel and the lamp on the end of the sea wall had come on. "That's night, love. Ask anybody."

She looked at the window for a long moment. Then she got up and went out through the kitchen door to the pool, unpinning her cloak as she went and dropping it on the step, and went down the stone stair in her shift into the cold dark water without a sound, and didn't come up.

Imani wrote it on the card. 31 Oct. Seven days, first full week out. Dull from day 4, headache day 5-6, chalky and gills tight from 11 on day 7. Pool at 5. She left a space for the rest.

Zale counted under his breath. He got to three hundred and stopped counting, because it was silly, and peeled a chestnut instead.

"Did you call her love?" said Imani.

"No," said Zale.



She came up at a quarter to six and sat on the pool's edge with her feet in the water and the lamplight from the kitchen door on her, and the sheen was back. All of it. Imani had seen it come back before, in August, and it still made her want to laugh, the way you want to laugh when a fire you've given up on catches. Ilenne turned her arm over in the light and looked at it, and then up at the two of them in the doorway, and went the faint clear brightness at the throat that Imani's card had down, since September, as pleased with herself.

"Seven," she said.

Imani filled in the space. Out at 5.45. Sheen back. A week is her number on land, as she said in August it would be at home. Soak weekly. More often if she wants. She considered the last line, and crossed out if she wants, and wrote whenever she likes, because the other one sounded like permission.

Wren came round the garden wall at six with a turnip in each hand and a third under her arm, and her breath going up white. She was nineteen now and had stopped coming to Hollow Night in costume two years ago, on the grounds that she was too old; she came every year anyway, in her motorcycle jacket, and carried things for people.

"From Bram," she said, and held out the one in her right hand. "He says it's better than last year's."

It was a big Milford turnip, white and purple, with a face cut in it that had heavy lids over the eyes and a long mouth full of crooked teeth, and inside, set in a cup of clay at the bottom, a little light stone of the kind the town used in its lamps, unlit. Ilenne took it in both hands on the pool's edge and turned it round to look at the lids. She lifted one with her finger. It moved.

"No," she said.

Wren looked at her.

"Last year," said Ilenne, "is best." She held the turnip up so that the dark of the kitchen showed through its teeth. "This is second."

"I'm not telling him that."

"I tell him."

"He'll be pleased," said Wren, who knew him. "He'll be pleased all week. He'll carve one next year with ears." She put the other two turnips down on the step, one for Imani, one for the house. "There's nine people at the top of the lane already and it's not even dark. Saban's walking up and down. He looks like he's lost a dog."



Emeric came down the hill from the castle at a little after seven with his sister, who had never seen it.

That surprised him, and it shouldn't have. Maeve had been at court every October of her life, and the court had a feast at the end of the month for the harvest's last accounts and no lanterns at all. She'd read about Kaldo's night in the Wayford paper, she said, which had called it a peasant superstition got up by the duke, and in the capital paper, which had called it a heresy, and in a letter from Waylon, who had called it the best night of the year and sent her a paper mask of something with tusks that she'd kept in a drawer.

"I thought it would be smaller," she said.

The upper ward was full to the walls. Every door with a lantern in its window had a knot of children at it, and every child had a bag, and the street lamps along Gate Avenue had been turned down at dusk by the Works so that the turnips would show, which they did: hundreds of them, on steps and sills and gateposts and on the tops of the tram shelters, all grinning, all orange or white inside depending on whether the house had a candle or a stone. The wind had dropped a little at sunset. It still came down off the hill in gusts that made the candles lie over and the children shriek and cup their hands round them. There were dragons. There were three dragons at least between the castle gate and the square, all made of sheets and sticks with three or four children inside, and one of them was trying to get its head up the step of the coffee house under the arch and couldn't, and was being advised from inside by the head's own legs.

"What's that one," said Maeve.

"A slime."

"What's a slime?"

"Something out of a book," said Emeric, which was what he always said, and which had been true for five years and was going to stop being true quite soon, somewhere past the wall, and he didn't like thinking about how.

A small girl with long paper ears stopped in front of them and held out a bag. She didn't say sweet or story. She looked at Maeve's face, and then at Emeric's, and her mouth fell open a little, and her mother came out of the crowd behind her and took her by the shoulder and said, "Sorry, your... sorry," and steered her off toward the next door, with the girl looking back over her shoulder the whole way.

"Do they always do that?"

"The small ones. The big ones have got used to me." He saw her looking along the street at the children in their ears and their locs, and remembered, and decided not to point out which costumes were meant to be him. She'd find out.

The church door had a lantern in it. It always did. Doran stood in the doorway in his old brown coat with the Gilded Eye on its chain round his neck, which he wore under protest and turned round so the eye faced his shirt whenever he thought nobody was watching, and a bowl of Maren's toffee in the crook of his arm. He'd done it every year since the first. The capital didn't know, or chose not to. There was a queue.

A boy at the head of it was dressed as something with wings: two frames of split cane with mill cloth stretched over them, painted grey, tied on with string round his chest. He was perhaps eight. He looked up at the cardinal with great seriousness and said, "Sweet or story."

"Story," said Doran. "I've had a lot of sweets tonight."

The boy took a breath. He had plainly learned it.

"There were people with wings," he said. "Here. A long time ago. Before us. They lived in the high places, Miss Sela says, and they went to the sea in winter, and nobody knows their names. And men came, and drove them off." He stopped. He had got to the end of what he'd learned, and he looked at the bowl, and then he added, because it was the last thing Sela had told them that week and it had stuck, "And we're sorry."

Doran stood quite still in the door. Then he held out the bowl, and the boy took two, which was one more than the rule, and nobody said anything, and he went off down the steps flapping his cane wings.

"That's new," said Maeve quietly.

"That's this year," said Emeric. "The school did a week."

Doran had seen them. He gave the bowl to the next child in the queue to hold, which the child did with enormous care, and came down two steps to them with his hands in his coat pockets.

"Your Majesty. Your Highness." He nodded to each of them, and to Maeve he added, "You'll forgive the toffee. It's the only part of tonight I'm allowed to do officially."

"I wasn't going to say anything," said Maeve.

"Nobody does." He looked up at his own church door, and the queue, and the boy with the bowl. "They've been coming to me all week, you know. Sela's children. For the death book. Not for the names; for the dates. They wanted to know how far back it goes. I showed them the first page. Four hundred years. They were very disappointed." He smiled. "One of them asked what was in it before that, and I said nothing, the book starts there, and she asked why, and I said because that's when we started writing it down, and she said then who was dying before."

He was quiet a moment. The wind came round the corner of the church and laid the candle over in the door lantern and let it up again.

"I've been thinking about my grandmother all week," he said. "Isn't that strange. She had all the tales. I told you once, didn't I, the proud ones under the hill. A hundred versions. I'd forgotten hers wasn't that one. Not quite." He looked at the boy with the wings, halfway down the street now, still flapping. "In hers they weren't proud. In hers they were the terrible ones. They lived in the hills and came up through the cellar floor and took children who didn't say their prayers, and the Light put them under, and that was a mercy. She'd say it with the lamp out, to make me hold on to her." He shook his head. "The terrible ones. I believed it till I was twelve. I'm sixty-three. I've been in holy orders forty-four years and I've only just this week thought to ask who told her."

Emeric heard the words go in and stop.

He'd said them himself. Not tonight; five years ago, in August, in a gallery under a hill with a lamp on a trestle, to Ferra, and to nobody else in this world. He'd been telling her what the stories had been on the other side of everything. The short broad people in the mountains. The tall ones in the woods. The winged ones, and the ones in the sea, and the ones that went into the hills at night, and little people. And terrible ones. It had been the only phrase he'd had for the bottom of the list, the ones the stories didn't have a better name for. He'd said it again before that night was out, about the costumes. And terrible ones, and silly ones, made of sheets.

It was two words. Two ordinary words. Any grandmother in either world might have put them together on a dark night to make a boy hold on to her.

He put it with the other things he didn't know what to do with.

"Who did tell her?" said Maeve.

"I don't know." Doran took his hands out of his pockets. "Her priest, I suppose. Somebody's. That's rather the point, isn't it." He went back up the steps and took the bowl back from the child who'd been holding it, and thanked her, and gave her a toffee for the trouble, and turned to the next in the queue, who was a very small slime.



They found their father at the bottom of Gate Avenue, where the lane went off downhill toward the lower ward and the steps began.

He was standing against the bank's railings with his hands in his coat pockets, watching, the way he watched everything now, very still, like a man at a window. Beside him was a tall woman in a long dark cloak with the hood well forward, so that there was nothing of her face to see but a curve of jaw when a lantern moved; and nobody in that street looked at her twice, because on this night of all nights a tall woman in a hood was nothing at all.

"You came down," said Emeric.

"Your mother wanted to see the dragons," said Harrold.

"I wanted to see the elves," said the woman in the hood. "There's one over there with your face, Emmy. And a beard. Someone's given you a beard."

He looked. There was. The child was about ten and had rope locs to his knees and a beard of black wool hooked over his ears, and was being very dignified about it.

A girl with a slime's oilcloth sack over one arm came up to the bank's railings at a run and stopped in front of the hood and looked up into it, and whatever she could see under it, it didn't seem to worry her. "What are you?"

"Dead," said Amara.

The girl was enchanted. She ran off to tell somebody.

Harrold closed his eyes briefly. Maeve, who was still getting used to her stepmother, made a small noise and turned it into a cough.

A gust came down the avenue off the hill. It went through the street like a hand through grass, and every candle along the railings lay flat and half of them went out; and at their feet, on the bottom step of the lane, a boy of five or six in a black sack, one of the crestless line, crouched over his turnip with both hands round it and watched his candle go out, and stayed crouched, and his face began to go.

It was very dark at the top of those steps with the candles out. The lane went down into it.

Harrold took one hand out of his pocket and bent down and put it over the turnip's open top, the way you'd cover a cup, and took it away again; and the turnip was lit. Not with a flame. A small steady white light sat inside it where the candle had been, and came out through the eyes and the teeth and laid the turnip's grin on the step, and didn't move when the next gust came.

The boy looked at it. He looked up at the tall man. Then he picked the turnip up in both arms and went off down the steps with it held out in front of him, very carefully, into the dark.

Harrold put his hand back in his pocket.

Maeve was looking at him. Emeric was looking at him. He looked back at both of them, one and then the other, with his face giving away exactly what it gave away in a room full of lords.

"Draught," he said.

The hood beside him didn't turn. But Emeric was a delver, and so was his mother, and he felt her laugh, a small warm quick thing in the air beside him, like a match struck behind a hand.



The stone was at the foot of the lane, where the steps came out above the lower ward and the dyers' sheds, in the angle of a wall.

Bram had cut it in February out of the grey sandstone that the old town's quay had been built of, a single slab as high as a man's chest and as wide as a door, set upright in a footing he'd poured himself on a Sunday with nobody to help him, because he'd wanted nobody. It had no picture on it. It had a line, cut in plain capitals, that Ferra had written in the margin of a proof a year ago and then crossed out the end of: FOR THE PEOPLES WHO WERE HERE FIRST. Under it the stone was smooth, and would stay smooth. Bram had said so, when Sela asked him what was going under. Nothing was going under. They didn't know the names.

By ten the lane was emptying. The children who were small enough to be carried had been carried home, and the ones who weren't were being walked up the steps by the scruff with their bags clutched to them and their faces sticky, protesting. Some of them, on the way up, stopped at the stone. Not all. Enough. They came down to it in twos and threes with their parents behind them, and stood there a moment as if they weren't sure what to do, and then put their turnips down on the ground at its foot, still lit, and went away up the steps. By half past ten there were sixty or seventy turnips round the bottom of the stone in a ragged half-ring, burning, and the line of capitals over them was lit from below, so that the letters stood out black and orange and the smooth stone underneath them looked very empty indeed.

Ferra had walked down with Imani after the castle's own door had run out of sweets. Saban was there, on the wall above the stone with his hat on his knee, where he'd been for an hour; he had wanted to see it, he said, so that he would know what he was guarding. Bram had come and stood and looked at it with his arms folded and gone away again without a word, and Tess with him on her stick. Zale was sitting on the bottom step with his stick across his knees.

Ilenne came down the lane last, with Wren.

She had her hood up and Bram's turnip in her hands, lit now, the little stone inside it set going by Wren's thumb at the bay house gate, so that the crooked teeth threw their shadow up onto the underside of the hood and onto her chin. She walked slowly. She had walked the lower ward a hundred times since August, to the market and the tram and the Board, and she walked it now as if it were new.

A family was at the stone ahead of her: a man, a woman, and two children, a boy of about four asleep on his father's shoulder with one shoe off, and a girl of seven or eight, awake, holding her turnip in front of her. The girl was in a tail. Grey mill cloth sewn tight, painted with scales in silver. The paper fin had got wet at some point in the evening and gone soft and was trailing on the steps behind her.

Emeric knew her. Last year she had been carried. This year she had walked, because she had grown, and because she'd cut the tail off at the knees so she could, with a pair of her mother's scissors, from the look of the hem.

Ilenne stopped at the foot of the steps and watched her.

The girl put her turnip down. She did it carefully, getting it level, turning it so that its face looked out at the lane and not at the stone, which was evidently important to her. Then she stood up and looked at the empty part of the stone under the letters, and put out her hand, and touched it.

Nobody had told her to. Emeric was nearly sure of that. Last year a woman in a hood had knelt on the floor of the long room and put her palm flat on the boards and said small ones touch, and Ferra had written it, and Sela had had it from Ferra, and maybe Sela had said it to the class in the week and maybe she hadn't. The girl touched the stone with her hand flat for about the length of a breath, and took it away and wiped it on her tail, and her mother said, "Come on, Lettie, it's past ten," and they went up the steps past Ilenne without looking at her.

Ilenne turned her head and watched the tail go up the lane.

"Skin," said Ferra, very low, beside Emeric, remembering. She didn't say it to anybody.

Ilenne didn't say it. She watched until the tail had gone round the corner at the top. Then she came forward to the stone, with the turnip held in front of her the way the girl had held hers, and stood in front of it, and the few people left at the foot of the lane went quiet, because she was tall and she was standing in a way that meant something, though none of them could have said what.

She put back her hood.

In the turnips' light it would have been only a woman. Long, grey-white, very still, the heavy dark hair still damp from the pool down her back. The eyes, if you were close, a shade too large. Saban, on the wall, did not move a muscle, and Emeric saw that he'd decided before he came down here, an hour ago, that if she put her hood back he would let her.

She said something in her own language.

It was long. It had the throat-catch in it, twice, and the click, and the long falling note at the end of each part of it that Zale said was what a sentence did when it was finished with; and the colour came up in her while she said it, not the dark and not the pale, but something Emeric had never seen on her, a slow deep blue-grey like the sea with the light gone off it, moving at her throat and down her arms in long bars. Her voice wasn't loud. It went up the lane anyway, between the walls, and a man three steps up who had been going home stopped where he was with his hand on the rail.

Then she said it in Kaldo. Not to them. To the stone.

"Old ones stand," she said. "I am old one. Here. Only one." She was quiet a moment. "So I say."

She said a name. It was in her own language and nobody at the foot of the lane could have said it back. Ferra had her book out and her pencil on it before the name was finished.

"This one," said Ilenne, "sing fish. Up river. Here, this river." She did not point. She didn't need to. The river was at the bottom of the town, under the bridges, black, going out. "Long, long. Before your book. Every year she sing the fish up, for the people on the land, the land people, so they eat. And they give her..." She stopped, looking for it. She lifted the turnip a little in her hands. "Light. Fire. On the shore, at night, so she see where. Every year. Long time."

The bars of colour moved in her, very slowly.

"Then one year, other men. Fire on the shore, same. She come. Sing." She took a breath. "This one did this. This one, sing. Then not."

That was all. She didn't say the rest. Everybody at the foot of the lane who'd been in the long room a year ago could see the rest on the second stone, the one with the fires on the shore and the shapes in the shallows, and the ones who hadn't been there didn't need it. She stood a moment longer. Then she crouched, with the turnip, and set it down at the foot of the stone among the town's sixty or seventy, in the middle, in front of the empty part under the letters, and turned it so that Bram's crooked face looked out at the lane.

She put her hand flat on the stone. She kept it there longer than the girl had.

Then she stood up and put her hood back up, and came back to the foot of the steps, and Zale got up off his step on his stick and moved along without being asked so that she could sit down, and she sat.

The man on the third step went on up the lane, slowly, with his hand on the rail. At the top he stopped and looked back down, for a while, at the stone and the lights, and then went on.

Nobody at the foot of the lane said anything for a long time.

Ferra was writing. Emeric could see over her shoulder in the light from the stone: the name, in her small marks, too many of them, and a line through it and a second try under it, and a third. And then, under that, in plain Kaldo: Sang the fish up the river for the people on the land. They lit fires on the shore for her. One year other men lit them. She looked at it. She didn't add anything.

"Is that right?" she said to Ilenne. "The name."

Ilenne leaned over and looked at the page upside down, at the three tries.

"No," she said. She took the pencil out of Ferra's fingers, which nobody else alive was allowed to do, and under the three tries she drew a fourth: not letters at all, but one of her people's marks, the curving kind she had drawn seven of in the same book in August, and then a second beside it. "This."

"I can't say that."

"No," Ilenne agreed. "Nobody here can." She gave the pencil back. "You keep it. Next year, one more."

"Next year you'll be home," said Ferra.

Ilenne looked at the stone, at the half-ring of lights and Bram's turnip in the middle of it, grinning.

"Next year," she said, "someone else is old one."



They walked her back to the bay house at eleven, the long way, down past the dyers' sheds and along the foot of the sea wall, because Zale couldn't do the steps twice. The wind had gone round to the west and dropped. The bay was flat and black, and out at the mouth the current gate's first footing had its lamp lit, and beyond it the buoys over the whirlpool mines showed theirs, small and low on the water, in the places she had drawn on Ferra's chart.

At the garden gate she stopped and turned round and looked up the hill at the town, which still had a few turnips burning on its steps, high up, like the last of a fire.

"Last year," she said, "I think: they wear us. Like the hunter."

"I know," said Emeric.

"This year small one cut the tail. So she walk." She thought about it, her head on one side. Then she gave the short sound through her nose that was a laugh, and went in, and Zale went in after her to see that the range was banked, and came out again a minute later and shut the gate behind him and stood there a moment with his hand on it.

"Not a word," he said, to nobody in particular, and went off along the shingle toward the tram on his stick.

Imani took Emeric's arm on one side and Ferra took the other, and they went up through the dark lower ward toward the castle without hurrying. At the foot of the lane they passed the stone again. Most of the turnips were still lit. Somebody had put down one more since they'd left, a small badly-carved one with a face like a surprised potato, at the end of the ring, and it was burning too.

"Your father," said Imani, after a while, conversationally, "made a light come out of his hand."

"I know."

"Ferra didn't see."

"I heard," said Ferra.

"Are we going to talk about it?"

Emeric thought about the boy on the step with his turnip in his arms, going down into the dark, and his father's face, and draught.

"Not tonight," he said.

"No," said Imani. "I didn't think so." She leaned against his arm. "Your sister's going to ask you about it at six tomorrow morning, in the Works yard, with a lightning rod."

"Probably."

"And you'll say you don't know."

"I don't know."

"That's what I mean," said Imani, and Ferra laughed, and wrote nothing, and they went on up the hill.
