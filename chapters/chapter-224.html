Chapter 224 - First Fill

The last triangle went in during the last week of the spring, at the crown, and the yard men on the drum's head cheered it, because they had not been told they were not to.

Emeric heard them from his window. He looked up and saw the dome closed over the round room for the first time: a great low bowl of glass on its steel ring, fifty feet across, every pane of it catching the morning, so that from below, from the floor of the round room, the sky came down through it in a thousand small bright pieces with thin dark lines between them, like light through the leaves of a tree. Orrin's glass was very clear. From the floor you could not see the glass at all, only the lines of the frame, and the sky in them, and a yard man at the crown with his cap in his hand, waving it at somebody in the street a long way below.

Then Bram's men came up the ladders on the inside and began to go round every seam with pitch and a hot iron, and the yard men came down, and did not go up again.

They planted it that night.

There were four of them. He had thought about who, for a long time, and the answer had not changed. Ferra, because it went in the book or it did not happen. Bram, because he had built the thing and would build whatever it needed next. Armand, because Armand would want readings from the first minute, and a reading taken by anybody else would be a reading Armand did not trust. Ward stood outside the round room's door with his back to it. He had asked to. He did not come in.

The round room had changed in a month. The table had gone, and the floor under the dome was not stone any more, round its edge; Bram's men had lifted the flags in a ring a pace wide all the way round the drum's foot and filled the ring with earth from the old farm, the good dark earth off the top field that Hollis had given without asking what for, and in it Imani's people had planted her beds. Feverfew and foxglove and comfrey and a row of willow slips, and in a corner by themselves, three of the grey shrubs from the island, grown from Imani's cuttings off the one on her sill. They had been in a week and had taken. The whole round room smelled of turned earth and green things and the pitch on the seams.

In the middle of the floor, where the table had stood, there was a bed of its own. Round, a yard across, edged with dressed stone, filled with the same dark earth, raked level. Nothing in it.

Ferra took the silk out of her coat and unfolded it on her palm, and the chipped stone lay in it.

She did not hand it to him. She held it out, on the silk, and he took it off the silk himself. It was warm from her coat. It was grey and heavy for its size, heavier than it had been when it came south in the comb case, by some grains, as Ferra's scales had told her every quarter since the band. The fine grey net lay across its old broken face like frost on a pane. The chip at its corner was worn smooth.

He did not know how deep. The book did not say. The book showed a seed on the ground, and then a seed split, and a stem, and it did not show a hand or a hole. He knelt at the edge of the bed and put two fingers into the earth in the middle of it, to the second knuckle, which was the depth he would have put a bean at, in another life, in a garden he had not thought of in a very long time. He made the hole. He put the stone in it, chip up, because he wanted to know which way it lay if he ever had to dig it up again. He pushed the earth back over it with the side of his hand, and pressed it, once, flat.

That was all. There was nothing to see. A round bed of dark earth in the middle of a stone floor, with a mark in the middle where a man's hand had pressed it.

He stayed on his knees for a moment longer than he needed to.

Behind him Ferra wrote, and he heard the pencil, and did not need to see what she wrote. He knew. The date, and the hour, and the chipped stone, planted, E., in the middle bed, a finger deep, chip up. And the names of the three who saw it.

"Readings," said Armand.

He had his instruments on a tray: the reading stones in their brass cups on their rods, the ones he had made for the band and carried up in the Hod and down again in the dark for three years, and his book. He set one on its stand at the edge of the middle bed, at the height of a man's knee, and one at the height of a man's shoulder, and read them both, and wrote them down: the medium in the round room, on the ground, at night, under a glass roof, before anything was done to it. It was the same as the medium in any room in the city. Thin. Ordinary. Ground.

"That's your zero," Armand said.

Emeric got up off his knees.

They went out through the pass-box, because from that night on nobody would go out of the round room any other way.

It stood at the drum's foot on the east side, where the gallery from his rooms came down. Two doors, iron-framed and glazed, a man's width apart, with a small iron room between them the size of a privy, and in its inner door two sleeves of Imani's rubber sealed into the glass with gloves at their ends. Bram shut the round room's door behind them from inside the box, and then opened the outer door onto the gallery, and they stepped out, and he shut that too. It clicked. For as long as anybody lived in the palace, that click would be the sound of somebody coming out of the winter garden.

The collar was on the other side of the drum, on the west, low, a hand above the beds, set into one of the steel nodes of the frame where it met the ring. An iron tube as long as a man's forearm, with a flange where it met the node and a thread cut inside it, and a brass cap on its outer end. The cartridges were on a rack beside it on the gallery: six of them, filled in the band on the K Discovery over the last fortnight on a rig Bram had built bigger than the vial rig in every part, pump and jacket and fitting. Glass tubes as thick as a man's wrist and as long as his forearm, each in its steel sleeve, each with its sealed tip, clear and faintly shivering in the lamp stone's light. Each one had cost a night.

"One," said Emeric.

Bram took the brass cap off the collar and took a cartridge off the rack and slid it into the collar, sleeve first, tip inward. It went in to the start of the thread and stopped. He put his hand on the sleeve's end.

There was a mark cut in the collar's rim, a line, and a matching line on the sleeve. Zero. When the two lines met, the cartridge's tip would be at the spike.

Bram turned it. Slowly, one quarter-turn at a time, the thread taking it in, the sleeve's line coming round toward the collar's line. Emeric had his hand on the drum's stone beside the collar, and his sense in the dome, and Armand had his instruments' book open, reading the stones on their stands through the glass from the gallery.

The lines met.

It did not go in gently.

He had known it would go quickly. A vial in a heart's port went back to the medium in three breaths and filled the heart's little chamber and the cells drank; he had watched it a hundred times. He had not thought about what three breaths looked like when the vial was the length of a forearm, and the chamber was a glass room fifty feet across, and all of the vial's charge came out of the broken tip at once at one point in the wall.

It came out like water out of a burst main. He felt it in the sense as a blow, a hard single push out of the collar's mouth into the dome, not spreading, not yet, a fist of the band thrown straight in. It hit the glass nearest the collar before it hit anything else. The pane in the frame directly above the collar's mouth, a hand away, took the whole of it on its face in the first half of the first breath.

The pane cracked.

It was a small sound. A tick, like ice on a pond in the first cold. A line went across the triangle from one corner toward the middle of the opposite side and stopped halfway, and stayed there, fine as a hair and bright where the lamp caught it.

Then the fist spread. He felt it go out through the dome in the next two breaths, filling it, thinning as it went, running up the curve of the glass to the crown and down again, the way smoke fills a room from a single door, until the whole round room from the floor to the crown was full of it, thick and even and still. The stones on Armand's stands came up. Armand read them, and wrote, and read them again.

"Near a third of the band," Armand said. "At the knee. More at the shoulder." He wrote it. "One cartridge."

Nobody was looking at the stones. They were all looking at the cracked pane.

It held. The crack did not run. Emeric had his sense on it, on the glass round the crack, and the medium did not come through it; the crack was too fine, and the glass on either side of it was still glass. The dome held what was in it. But there was a line across one of Orrin's triangles, a hand above the collar, where there had not been one a breath before.

"It came out all in one place," said Bram. He was looking at the collar's mouth. "Like a hose. Straight at the nearest thing it could hit."

"Yes."

"Then don't let it." Bram put his hand on the collar. "Something over the mouth, inside. A cup. Iron. With holes in it, all round, small ones, so it comes out of a hundred places a little instead of one place all at once. Like the rose on a watering can." He was already seeing it; Emeric could tell by his hands. "It'll slow it. It won't stop it. It'll come out of the collar into the cup and out of the cup in every direction at once, and nothing gets the whole of it."

"A baffle," said Emeric.

"A cup with holes in it," said Bram, who did not like names for things he could describe.

He made it in the fitting shop at the dock that night, and they fitted it in the morning, before anything else, through the pass-box.

It was the first thing that had to go into the dome since it was filled. Emeric drew it down first, as he had told the table they would: he opened the vent on the east side, a second collar with a valve on it, into a rack of empty stones on the gallery, twelve of them, and let the dome's charge go down out of the round room into them, slowly, until Armand's stones on their stands read ground again. Then Bram went in through the pass-box with the cup in his hand, and knelt at the collar's inner mouth among the willow slips, and bolted it on, and came out again. It took a quarter of an hour. When he came out, the twelve stones on the gallery were full, and Bram's face had the look it had after a night in the band, a little too awake.

"That'll be the band in there, then," he said.

"What's left of it."

"I could feel it on my teeth." He wiped his hands. "Is the tree all right?"

There was no tree. There was a round bed of earth. Emeric put his sense into it and found the stone where he had left it, a finger deep, chip up, and it was the same as it had been. It had had a night of a third of the band, and it had not moved, and he had not expected it to. The book's seed had not woken in a night either.

They opened the second cartridge after dinner.

It went in to the zero, and the spike took the tip, and the charge came out of the collar into Bram's cup and out of the cup through its hundred holes, and Emeric felt it go into the dome not as a fist but as a breath, all round the collar at once, soft, spreading, thinning before it touched anything. It touched the glass above the collar a breath later than the first one had, and far more gently, and the glass did not tick.

He waited, with his hand on the stone of the drum. Nothing ticked. The cracked pane above the collar held its crack and did not add to it. The dome filled.

"Solved," said Bram.

"Solved," Ferra agreed, and wrote it: Baffle fitted. Second fill, no crack. And under it, because she wrote everything, The first pane, cracked at the first fill, left in; not running.

Everybody said it. Armand said it, in his way, which was to write in his book no further damage observed and not look up. Emeric said it too. He stood at the collar with his hand on the drum and said solved, and believed it, because it was. The burst had been a fist and now it was a breath, and a breath did not crack glass. It was the kind of fix that was obvious the moment somebody said it and that nobody had thought of before, and those were the best kind.

He did not think about the other panes. The ones away from the collar. The ones on the far side of the dome, that had never had a fist of anything thrown at them, and never would, and would only ever have the steady even weight of the dome's air leaning on them from inside, day and night, for as long as there was a tree. There was nothing to think about there. They had not cracked. Nothing had touched them.

Imani came to his rooms in the east range the next morning, early, before he had dressed, and knocked, and came in without waiting for an answer.

"I've been in my garden," she said.

He had known she would. She had a key to the gallery; it was her garden. She had come up at dawn to see her beds, as she would every morning from now on, and she had stood at the pass-box and looked through its two glass doors into the round room, at her feverfew and her willow slips, and at the round bed in the middle with nothing in it but a mark, and at the stones on Armand's stands. And she had put her hand on the outer door, and felt whatever a physician who had spent eight years among people who drank the barony's water felt when she put her hand on a door with the band on the other side of it.

"I can't go in," she said.

"No."

"My own garden. I can't go into it." She did not sit down. "Bram told me to use the gloves. The sleeves in the door. He said I can do most of it through the sleeves, if I'm patient, and for the rest you'll empty it, and I can go in, and then you'll fill it again." She looked at him. "What am I covering?"

He had promised her soon. It was soon.

He told her. He told her the whole of it, standing by his window with the round room below them and the dome over it bright with the morning: the book, and the drawings of the tree and the pods, eight to a pod; the grey stones that dealers sold to people who liked the look of them; the two that had woken in the band in an iron case; the wall board, and the bands on every wrist at the table, including hers, and what was in them. That there were not enough, and never would be enough, of a thing nobody knew how to make. That he had planted his mother's, last night, in the middle bed, a finger deep, to see if it would grow.

Imani listened to all of it without moving. When he had finished she stood for a while and looked down through the gallery window at the round room and the round bed in the middle of it.

"So my beds are the lie," she said.

"Your beds are true. They're the only true thing about it anybody outside the table will ever see." He turned from the window. "Nobody who looks in there will be told a word that isn't so. It's a winter garden. It's yours. You'll grow what you grow in it, and people will come and see your foxglove through the glass and go away and tell their neighbours the physician has a glass house at the palace. All of that is true."

"And the rest isn't said."

"The rest isn't said."

Imani was quiet for a moment.

"That's how you do everything," she said. It was not a complaint. It was a diagnosis. "The water. The towers. Everything true, and the middle of it not said." She looked at the dome. "What does it do to the people in the palace? The ones who work under it. Your clerks. Ilsa. Me."

"Nothing, while it's shut. Glass holds. Nothing comes through."

"And when it isn't shut? When you empty it into your stones, or the box leaks, or a pane goes?"

He started to say that a pane would not go. He stopped, because he was standing at a window from which, if he looked, he could see a triangle of Orrin's glass on the west side of the dome with a fine bright line across it.

"Then some of it gets out," he said. "Into the palace. Not much, and not for long."

"You'll tell me," said Imani. "When it does. Every time. Not the next week. The same hour." She waited until he nodded. "I'm the Secretary of Health. The people in this building are my business, whatever's in the middle of it. I'll keep your garden. I'll keep your lie that isn't a lie. And you'll tell me every time the thing I'm keeping gets out."

"Every time."

She nodded, once, and went to the door. At the door she stopped.

"Your mother's," she said, without turning round.

"Yes."

"Then I'll water round it," said Imani, "through the sleeves, very carefully," and went out.

He sat at his desk afterwards with Armand's first readings in front of him.

A third of the band, at one cartridge. Near two thirds at two. Thicker at the shoulder than the knee. And the stone in the middle bed as it had been, a finger deep, chip up, doing nothing.

The book's seed had woken in thick air. It did not say how thick. He knew what the band was: he had flown in it for years, and felt it every time, and knew in his body what three miles up felt like and what two thirds of it felt like, and two thirds was not the band. The stone had woken in a summer of the band, in an iron case, a few grains' worth. It would wake faster, surely, in something thicker.

He found that what he wanted was to fill it. All of it. Every cartridge on the rack and another six tomorrow. Pack the dome with the band until it was as thick as the glass would bear, as thick as the band itself, thicker, right up to the pressure where the panes began to lean, and hold it there, and give the stone every grain of the medium it could possibly want, so that nothing it needed was missing, so that if it did not grow it would not be because he had given it too little.

He knew what that was. He had built things all his life, in two lives, and he knew the feeling of a man who does not know what the thing needs and so gives it everything, to be safe. It was not safe. It was a guess that felt like care.

He wrote on a clean sheet, Fill to what? And under it, after a while: The band. Its density at night. Measured, not remembered.

He did not have that number. Nobody did. Armand had readings of the band from the Hod's rail, three years of them, taken on the way up and the way down, in the cold, in a hurry, at no hour in particular, on nights when the racks were the point and the readings were not. They were a range, not a number. And a dome could not be set to a range.

He put the sheet on the pile for the table, and went down to the long room in the east range, where Ferra was already at her book in the worn place, and Armand was already in his chair, frowning at his own readings as if they had been taken by somebody else.

"I need the band," Emeric said. "At night. All night. One number."

Armand looked up at him over his spectacles.

"Yes," he said. "I rather thought you would."
