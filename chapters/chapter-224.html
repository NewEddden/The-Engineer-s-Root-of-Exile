Chapter 224 - Gold Roses

He dressed on the hull at seven in the morning, in the cradle at the end of the lowest tier, with the sun coming up over the city's east wall and the casters on the terrace stopping on their way to their hulls to watch, because word had gone round that the Kaldo boat had a duke aboard and that the duke was dressing in the bows like a bargeman.

Ward would not let him dress anywhere else. The cabin was the women's until ten; the tier master's house had a window on the street. So he stood in the bows in his shirt with a canvas screen Saban had rigged between the stem post and the rail, and the casters on the terrace looked at the canvas, and he got into the clothes behind it one piece at a time, as Abloh had shown him in the sail loft at Kaldo, slowly, because he had never in his life worn anything with this many parts.

The shirt was black, with full sleeves gathered into deep cuffs and a fall of black lace at the throat that stood up stiff from the starch and scratched, pinned at the collar with a small black stone. The trousers were black and fitted and went into black boots laced high up the calf. The waistcoat was black silk, with a row of small gold buttons and gold worked up both fronts from the hem to the collar, vine and leaf and rose, and at its waist on either side a cluster of gold roses worked so close that the silk under them did not show; and a chain from the lowest button to a gold watch on his hip, its case engraved with the same rose; and on its left breast, over the heart, between the outer silk and the lining, in a pocket of its own that Abloh had sewn shut, Bram's steel.

He put his hand flat on it before he buttoned the waistcoat. It was a little warm from the sun, and it did not give at all.

The coat went on last. It was long, almost to the boot tops, and worn open, and it hung from the shoulders and fell in long panels that moved when he moved, black, with a high stiff collar worked in gold that stood up behind his head, and across the back and down the skirts and round the hem, gold: vine and leaf and rose, worked close, so that from a little way off the black seemed to be breaking into flower all the way down; and between the panels, when he walked, a layer of sheer gold-patterned silk underneath that showed and went and showed again.

His hair was the last of it. It came well past his shoulders now, nine years of it, locked and heavy, and it would not go under a hat of any kind; so Abloh had sent up a black cord and a little box of plain gold cuffs with the clothes, and he gathered the top of it back at the crown and tied it, and left the rest down his back, and slid a cuff onto one lock and then another, near the ends, where they would catch the light. It was the one part of the morning he managed without being shown.

He came out from behind the canvas.

Ward looked at him from the gangway for a long breath. Saban, on the cabin step, took his hat off, which he did not do for people. Old Edrin, sitting in the casting seat with a cup of Maren's tea and no intention of moving out of it all day, said, "Well, now," very softly, and nothing else.

"It's a great deal of gold," said Emeric.

"It is," said Ward. He came up the gangway and stood in front of him, and reached up, and turned the high collar down an inch on the left side where it had folded under, and turned it up again, so that it stood. "Your neck is already red."

"It's the lace."

"The healer gave you a jar."

The jar was in the pocket of the coat he had worn up from Kaldo, which was in the cabin, which was the women's until ten. He thought of it, and of Imani's hand flat over it on his chest in the dispensary, and of the cabin door, and of the time.

"Later," he said.

Ward looked at him a breath longer, with no expression at all, and went back down to the gangway.



The cabin door opened at ten.

Vantic came out first, in a dress of dark grey silk he had not seen before, plain as the one she had worn on Tuesday but cut by somebody who charged a great deal more, with a single pin at her throat; and she stood aside on the narrow deck, and did not say anything, and looked back into the cabin.

Ferra came out.

He had seen the silk. He had seen the drawing, and the rose on the tracing paper, and a sleeve through a door a hand's width open. He had not seen this. She was in black, high to the throat, close in the body, with gold piping down the front of it in a narrow line and a lattice of gold diamonds over the bodice, and gold straps crossing it; and her shoulders were bare, which he had never seen in daylight, pale as milk and freckled across the tops like her face; and the sleeves began below the shoulder, tight to the wrist, banded in gold at the elbow and the cuff, both bands sitting exactly level. Down the skirt, all the way to the hem, scattered as if somebody had thrown them, gold roses.

His roses. The same rose, pricked through from the same drawing.

Her hair was up, all of it, and for once there was no loose strand anywhere, and he understood that Vantic had done it and that it had taken a long time.

She looked at him, at the coat, down and up, slowly, as she checked a figure somebody else had given her. Then she came along the deck and stood in front of him and put her hand flat on the waistcoat, on the left side, over the place where Bram's steel was, and pressed, and felt it not give.

"Good," she said.

She went to take her hand away, and he put his own over it and kept it there, on the steel, and looked at her. He did not say anything. He looked at the shoulders, and the line of gold down the front, and the hair with no strand loose, and back up to her face, and he took his time over it, with Ward on the gangway and Saban on the step and thirty casters on the terrace; and he watched the colour start at the gold collar and go up her throat, freckle by freckle, until it reached her ears.

"Emeric." Very low. "There are people."

"There are," he agreed, and went on looking.

She got her hand back on the third try, and stood away from him, and smoothed the skirt that did not need it, and did not look at him again for some time.

Imani came out last.

She came out sideways, because the door was narrow and she had a jar in one hand, and she stood on the deck in the sun in black: plain, close in the body, high at the throat with no lace at it anywhere, the sleeves to the wrist and not a finger longer, and cut, he saw, so that they would push up past the elbow without tearing. There was no gold on it but one thing. On the left breast, high, worked so close that the silk under it did not show, a single gold rose, the same rose, open, with its leaves; a thumb's width to the left of where Abloh had first put it. Her hair was her own, in a ward knot, done her own way; she had let Vantic near her sleeves and not near her head.

She looked at him, at the coat, all of it, as she looked at a patient who had dressed himself to go home early. Then she pulled the cork out of the jar with her teeth.

"Chin up."

He put his chin up. She did his neck there on the deck, in front of Ward and Saban and Old Edrin and the whole of the lowest tier, thin, under the collar at the back and the sides; her fingers were cold and the ointment colder, and the lace scratched the back of her hand, and she said something to it under her breath.

"There," she said, and corked the jar, and put it in the pocket of her dress, which Abloh had given her without being asked.

She was still close, with her hand still at his collar. He caught her wrist there before she could take it down, lightly, his thumb on the inside of it where she was always finding other people's pulses, and found hers. It was quick.

"Hm," he said.

Imani took her wrist back. "Don't you dare count me," she said, and went to stand by Ferra, and her ears, he saw, had gone the same way.

On the terrace, at the foot of the cradle, three of the casters who had stopped to watch the Kaldo boat had taken off their caps, and one of them said something to the other two, and they all three looked at the roses on Ferra's skirt and on his coat, and at the one rose on Imani's breast, and back again, and Emeric saw them read it, and saw them not know how to finish it.

"There," said Ferra, very quietly, to nobody. "Read."

"Count," said Imani.



The palace's great hall had been built by a king who wanted the people walking up it to feel every yard, and it was very long.

They waited at the foot of it, behind the doors, in a stone passage with Ward and Saban and a chamberlain in the king's colours, while the court went in ahead of them. The chamberlain had said at nine o'clock that the custom provided for the duke's lady; Ferra had asked to see where it said one, and he had gone away with his book and come back without it. The court was announced. That was the custom. Every house that entered the hall on an investiture day was named by the herald at the doors as it went in, its lord and its lady and its heir, so that the man who was to be girded would hear, before he walked, who had come to watch him do it.

The herald's voice came back to them down the passage, flat and enormous, off the hall's high stone.

The house of this. The house of that. Lords Emeric knew from the rolls and lords he had sat at table with in his old life, drunk, and lords he had never heard of. Lord Aurel Sallow, and the Lady Isolde Mensah, and Sir Jabari Mensah, her husband. The heads of the old houses of the north. His Grace of Aldane.

Emeric did not move when that one came. Ward did not either.

No bishop. No cardinal. The benches where the lords spiritual sat stood empty down the whole right side of the hall's head, because every one of them was a week's ride north at Ostrel for the Feast of the Ascent and the reading of the names, Augustine among them, and the man who had set the date had known it when he set it.

The heads of the great houses of the city. The guilds' masters. And then the herald drew breath, and there was a small pause before the next name, of the kind a herald leaves for a name he has not said before; and the voice came down the passage again.

"Livia Ducatis, of the House of Ducatis. Head of the House."

He kept his face still. It was the hardest thing he had done that day, and he had dressed in a thing with twelve buttons behind a sheet of canvas in front of thirty casters. He kept his eyes on the door at the passage's end and his hands still and his face unchanged, and he felt, beside him, Ferra not keep hers still, because she did not need to: he felt her, through her hand on his arm, go suddenly and entirely straight, and her fingers close once on his sleeve and open again, and heard her breathe out, very slowly, all of it, through her nose. On his other arm Imani did not move at all; but her fingers pressed his sleeve once, and he knew she had felt Ferra's go.

She had known since Tuesday. He did not have to look at her to know that. It had been in her face at the cabin door, very bright and very fierce, and gone before he could read it.

On the other side of the doors, through the stone, he heard the hall make a sound. Not loud. The sound of two hundred people turning their heads.

Ferra's hand was still tight on his sleeve. Under the edge of the coat, where the chamberlain could not see it, he put his own hand over hers and held it, and then reached across himself for Imani's and did the same, so that he stood in the passage with a woman's hand under each of his like a man holding two birds.

"Ferra." She did not look up. "You've stood in front of Keller with a ledger. You've stood in front of me with a veto." He felt her breathe. "These people can't read."

That got a sound out of her, very small, through her nose. On his other side Imani said, without moving her head, "They can count."

"Then let them count to two," said Emeric, which was Ferra's line from the dispensary, and he felt both of them hear it. He squeezed both hands once, and let go.

"Highness," said the chamberlain.

The doors opened.



He walked up the hall with Ferra on his left arm and Imani on his right, and it was very long.

The court stood on either side of the aisle in its colours, two hundred of it and more, the north on the left and the city on the right and the guilds at the back; and every face turned as they came in, and followed them up the hall, and he felt every one of them in his sense like a field of warm notes leaning one way in a wind. He did not look at them. He looked at the dais.

His father was on it.

Harrold sat in the king's chair under the canopy at the top of three steps, in the crown's red with the circlet on his head, and Corbin stood at his right hand a step down, and on the king's left, a step down, the chair that was the first queen's stood empty. Nobody in the hall remarked on it. She never came down.

Nine years. Emeric had not seen his father's face since the banquet hall on the night of his exile, and before that a small room with a man listening in the corridor and a voice that died at arm's length. It had not changed. That was the first thing, and it went into him like cold water. He had come up the hall braced for an old man, and there was not a line on that face he could not have drawn from memory: the same iron at the temples and nowhere else, the same set of the mouth, the same hands lying still on the chair's arms. Nine years had grown his own hair down his back and put a duchy on his shoulders, and they had gone past his father like water past a rock. A man with that much mana in him barely aged at all. Emeric had known it as a rule for years. He had not felt it until now. The second thing was the eyes, which were gold, as his were, and were watching him come up the hall with no expression in them at all, as they had watched him go down another hall nine years ago with a chest of crystals and the court laughing.

And then they went from his face to his coat, and down it, to the roses on its skirts, and across, to the women on his arms, and to the roses on them.

Harrold stopped.

It was nothing anybody else in the hall could have seen. He was sitting still, and he went on sitting still. But Emeric was forty feet from him and closing, and he had his sense out across the dais as he had it out across everything, and he felt his father's note, which he had not felt since he was a boy and had not known he remembered, and it was the one he remembered: slow and close and hard as a fist. And it stopped. For the space of one breath, between one step of Emeric's and the next, the whole of it went still, the way a man's breath stops when he has been struck somewhere nobody can see; and then it went on.

Emeric did not know why. He had no idea at all. He kept walking.

There was another note on the dais, a step down at the king's right hand, and it had not been watching Emeric at all. It had been watching the king's face. When Harrold's note stopped, that one went still too, a breath after, and stayed still longer, a held, careful stillness, like a man standing at a door he has stood at before and has never once been asked through. Then Corbin turned his head a little toward the herald, as if the herald had said something, and it moved on.

At the foot of the steps both their hands left his arms, as the chamberlain had told them they would, and they stepped aside to the left, to the places kept for them at the front of the court, where Isolde was, three paces off, with her hands folded at her waist in a particular way; and Emeric saw Ferra see Isolde's hands, and fold her own the same. Imani did not fold hers. She stood with them at her sides, as she stood at the foot of a bed.

He did not go up at once. He turned his head and looked at them first, at Ferra with her hands folded like Isolde's and at Imani with hers at her sides, one and then the other, for longer than the custom had any room for, at the foot of the king's steps with the king above him and the Lieutenant of the realm a step below the king, both watching. He felt Corbin's note sharpen on the back of his neck. He felt his father's not move at all. He did not much care about either. Ferra's chin came up a fraction and her face went pink to the hairline, in front of the whole north; and Imani looked back at him with an expression that said, quite clearly, that she would have words with him later about this, and the corner of her mouth gave her away.

Then he went up the steps alone and knelt.



The rest of it was the custom, and the custom was old, and he had read it on the hull on the way north, three pages of it in the chancery's hand, and it went exactly as the pages said.

The herald read the patent. The chancery's keeper held up the seal. Ward came up the steps behind him with the sword on his two hands, Bram's sword in its black scabbard with the gold and black roses on its guard, and knelt and held it up, and the king took it from him.

Emeric saw his father look at the guard. He saw it from below, kneeling, with his eyes where they ought to be, on the king's knees. Harrold's thumb moved over one of the roses on the guard, one of the gold ones, once, as a man touches a thing to see if it is real.

Then the king stood and buckled it on.

He did it himself. He bent and put the belt round his son's waist over the long coat, and drew it through the buckle, and pulled it tight, his hands at Emeric's hip, close; the first time in nine years his father had touched him, and the only time in his life he could remember his father's hands doing a thing for him themselves rather than through another man. They were steady and quick and impersonal and they did it well. The sword's weight settled against Emeric's left leg.

Two of the chamberlain's men came up the steps with the mantle.

It was the crown's mantle for a duke, heavy, red, lined with white fur, long enough to lie on the steps behind him when he stood, and fastened at the throat with a chain; and the king took it from them and laid it over his son's shoulders himself, and settled it, and it fell to the floor on either side of him in great stiff folds, so heavy that Emeric felt it press his shoulders down and lie along the outsides of his arms to the wrist like a pair of hands holding them.

His father's face was a foot from his, settling the chain at his throat. When the chain sat, his hands did not go back at once. They went down over the mantle's shoulders and patted them flat, twice, the way a man pats down a boy's coat at a door before he lets him out into the weather; and on the second pat the right hand stopped on Emeric's arm under the fur, and pressed, and found what was there.

"You've got strong," said Harrold. It was barely a voice at all.

Emeric could not have answered if he had tried. His father nodded, once, very slightly, as if something had been confirmed that he had been told in letters and had not quite believed, and took his hand away.

"Don't stay for the cups," said Harrold.

He said it under his breath, without moving his lips more than a man moves them to breathe, in a voice that died before it reached the chamberlain's men at Emeric's back; in the voice from the small room. His eyes went past Emeric's shoulder, to the front of the court, to the roses on one black dress and the one rose on the other, and came back.

"Two," said his father, to the chain. "One more and they'll say you're my son."

Emeric very nearly laughed, on his knees, under the crown's mantle, in front of two hundred people. He felt it come up and caught it behind his teeth. His father's face had not changed at all; but there was a breath, before it shut, when something at the corner of the king's mouth was not quite still, and then it was gone, and the face was the king's again, because the hall was full of eyes and the walls of it had ears, and Harrold had known that longer than Emeric had been alive.

Then he stepped back, and raised his own voice so that it went to the walls, round and carrying, to name his son Duke of Kaldo before his court, which it did, and the court answered it as the custom said.

Emeric stood up in the mantle, and turned round, and looked down the long hall at two hundred people looking at him.

He had heard the warning. He had understood it exactly. A king who had said four things to his son in nine years, three of them in the last minute, and the one that was an instruction was: go. Leave the feast before the cups go round. Whatever is going to happen in my hall tonight, do not be in it.

He thought of a hull in a cradle at the end of the lowest tier, with an old man at its seat who could lift it off in a breath, and the whole of the dark south behind it.

He stayed.



The peers came up to him one by one to bend the knee to the crown's will in the matter, as Aldane's sheet had said they must, and Aldane came with them.

He came twelfth, by precedence, which put him among the old northern houses. He was a heavy man in his fifties, square, with a grey beard cut square, and a good plain coat with no gold on it anywhere, as though in comment; and he came up the steps and stopped before Emeric and bent his knee the exact depth the custom asked and not a hair further, and straightened, and looked Emeric in the face. His eyes were small and pale and perfectly steady, and there was nothing in his note at all but a long cold dislike, held very still, like a man holding a full cup.

Then his eyes went past Emeric's shoulder.

Ward was there. Ward was always there. He stood at Emeric's left shoulder a pace back, in plain black, with his hands at his sides, and his face was as it always was, and he was looking at Aldane.

Aldane looked at him.

Something went through his note. Emeric felt it go: not the cold dislike, which stayed exactly where it was, but something under it, quick and hot and old, that came up and was put down again so fast that if Emeric had not had his sense out he would not have known it was there. Aldane's face did not change. His eyes stayed on Ward's for a breath, two; and then they moved away. He looked away first. He turned and went down the steps, heavily, and back to his place among the northern houses, and did not look up at the dais again.

A duke, Emeric thought, who did not care to be stared at by another man's guard. He put the dislike with the rest, with the toys at the banquet and the stillness in the chancery window and the six articles, and turned to the next man on the steps.

Behind him Ward had not moved at all. His note had not either. It had been a wall throughout, as it always was, and it went on being one.



The feast was in the same hall, at long tables carried in and set out down both sides of it while the court stood in the passages, and the duke sat at the high table at the king's left hand, in the place below the first queen's empty chair, in the mantle, because the custom said the mantle was worn until the cups.

It was very heavy. He ate in it. He found that he could not lift his arms more than a hand's width from his sides without the whole weight of it coming with them, and that the fur at the neck sat exactly over the lace at his throat, so that between them they had his skin raw before the second course. Ferra sat at his left, the place kept for her, with her back very straight and her hands in her lap and her book, for the first time in his memory at a table, nowhere at all. Imani sat beyond her, in a place the chamberlain had found in the end by moving a bishop's nephew, and ate everything that was put in front of her with the attention she gave a meal at the end of a long shift, because it was one, and because she would not give the hall the satisfaction of watching her not eat. Ward stood behind his chair. He would not sit. The chamberlain had asked him three times and then given up.

Under the table, under the fur, he found Ferra's hands where she had folded them in her lap and put one of his over both, and left it there through the soup. She did not look at him. After a while her fingers turned up under his and held on.

"Eat," he said, at the second course.

"They're watching."

"They were going to watch anyway. They might as well watch you eat." He put a slice off the joint on her plate himself, in front of the lord on her left, who looked at it as if a duke serving his Chancellor were a thing he would be describing to his wife. "You haven't had anything since the bread on the hull."

She looked at the plate. Then she took her hands out from under his, and picked up her knife and fork, and ate; carefully at first, and then, when nobody fell down dead and the hall went on talking, properly, as she ate at Maren's table at the end of a day at the books, with the attention of a woman who had been hungry and had been told she might stop pretending she was not. By the third course she was taking bread from the basket without waiting for it to be passed.

He liked that more than he could have said. On Earth he had sat across too many tables from women who ordered a salad and picked at it because somebody had told them a lady wasn't hungry in company, and it had always seemed to him a small sad lie that everyone agreed to tell. Imani had never told it in her life. Ferra, it seemed, was done telling it. He hoped they would both go on as they liked, in this hall and every hall after it, within reason, and let him stand where the looks landed. There was nobody in that room who would dare lay a finger on either of them. He had chosen them, both, for good, in front of everyone, and every person in the hall had watched him do it.

On the king's right, Corbin ate little and talked to the lords on his own side and did not look along the table once.

Beyond Corbin, at the far end of the king's side, sat Maeve.

He had not known she would be there. She went home to Emberfell in the spring; Rowena had said so, by a fire, more than a year ago. But she was there, in green, at the end of the high table, and she had stayed on in the capital past the spring for a thing that would take one afternoon; and when he looked along the table at her she was already looking at him, and she lifted her cup an inch off the cloth and put it down again and went back to her dinner, as if she did it every day. Her note, when he let his sense go that far, was the one he remembered: warm, clean, uncomplicated, and pleased with itself.

The hall was loud. It got louder. Two hundred people and a hundred servants with dishes and ewers and jugs going up and down between the tables, and the musicians in the gallery, and the noise of it coming back off the stone; and in Emeric's sense, under the noise, the whole hall was a roar of notes, hundreds of them, warm and quick and close together and moving, every one of them distinct if he went looking for it and all of them together like a wind in a wood. He could not watch all of them. Nobody could have. He watched the high table, and the steps, and the servants who came up the steps; and he let the rest of it go by.

Ferra got up at the fourth course. Imani did not; she had no intention of going anywhere duchesses were.

She did it as the court's ladies were getting up, as the custom of the feast allowed between the fourth course and the fifth, to go to the withdrawing room off the hall's side and come back; she touched his arm under the mantle, once, and went, and he watched her go down the dais steps and along the side of the hall, very straight, in the roses, and every head along that side turned as she passed.

She was gone about the time it takes to go to a withdrawing room and come back.

She came back the same way, along the side of the hall, past the tables, past the servants going up and down; and on the way, by a pillar, a woman in a dark blue dress stepped out from the tables to meet her, and the two of them stood together by the pillar for perhaps a dozen breaths with their heads close, as women do at a feast, and then the woman in blue went back to her table and Ferra came on.

Emeric saw who it was. Isolde.

Ferra came up the dais steps and round behind the high table to her chair, and as she passed behind Ward she did not stop and did not turn her head; but she looked at him. Emeric saw it from the side of his eye. She looked at Ward for perhaps the length of one breath, straight in the face, with an expression on her own that was perfectly calm, and then she looked away, down the hall, toward the far end of the table on the left side, toward the jugs.

And Ward moved.

Not much. Not fast. He took a pace to his right, behind Emeric's chair, so that he stood between the chair and the end of the dais where the steps came up, instead of behind it; and his note did a thing Emeric had felt it do twice in nine years, in a lane in Kaldo in the dark and at the top of a cellar stair in Wayford, and never in a hall: it narrowed. All of it. As if the whole slow wall of it had turned on its foundations to face one way.

Ferra sat down. She put her hands in her lap. She did not look at Emeric.

"Don't drink anything," she said, very quietly, without moving her lips, looking down the hall. Beyond her Imani had heard it; Emeric saw her lay her hand flat over the mouth of her cup, as she covered a jar with a fly in the ward.

He did not ask why. He did not need to ask why. He had felt Ward turn.

He let his sense go out down the hall along the line of Ferra's eyes, thin, past the tables, toward the far end of the left side where the jugs stood on their trestle against the wall, and the servants came and went from it with their ewers; and there were a dozen of them there, two dozen, notes like any others, men's notes, busy and tired and quick; and he could not tell which. They were all men. They all had jugs.

"Which?" he said, as quietly as she had.

"The scar," said Ferra. "Through the left eyebrow." She did not move. "I wrote it down in a gate yard two Decembers ago. She knew him across the hall by the way he holds his mouth."



The cups came at the sixth course.

It was the custom. The king rose, and the hall rose with him, with a long noise of benches going back on stone, and the servants came up from the trestles at the hall's foot with the great jugs to fill the cups for the king's health and the new duke's, a man to each section of the high table, as it had been done at every investiture in that hall for two hundred years; and the man who came up the dais steps with a jug to fill the cups at the left end of the high table, where the duke stood in his mantle and the Chancellor of Kaldo beside him, was young, in the palace's brown, with his hair cut short, and as he reached the top step and turned along the table toward them the lamplight came across his face, and Emeric saw a fine pale line running up through his left eyebrow, and a mouth that looked as though it had been laughing a moment ago and would again.

Ward was a pace and a half away. Behind the chair. Between the chair and the steps.

The young man came along the table with the jug. He filled the cup of the lord at the end, whose name Emeric did not know. He came to Imani's place, and she took her hand off her cup and let him fill it, and watched his hands do it. He came to Ferra's place, and lifted the jug toward her cup, and she did not lift her cup, and he did not seem to notice; he came on, the jug in his right hand, to the duke's place, and his left hand was low at his side against his leg in the brown cloth, and Emeric could not see it.

He felt it.

He had held the man's note since the top of the steps, thin and close, the way he held Ward's on the practice floor, and it had been a servant's note all the way along the table, busy and tired, a man doing a thing he had done a thousand times. Two places from the duke's it changed. It went tight and bright and high all at once, like a string turned up, like the shoulders of a man on a road in the dark the instant before his sword moved; and the whole of it gathered low and to the left, where the hand was. Emeric knew where it was going, and when, as clearly as if the man had said it aloud.

His arms were no use to him. The mantle had them, the whole red weight of it on his shoulders and along his arms to the wrists, and the sword was at his left hip under the fur where his hand could not have reached it if he had had a week. That was the custom too. The mantle was worn until the cups. He left his arms where they were.

The jug came up over his cup.

"Left hand," Emeric said, quietly, to the man's face. "I wouldn't."

The man's eyes came up. For the space of a breath his note did a thing Emeric had never felt a paid man's do: it missed, like a foot missing a stair in the dark. Then it found the stair again. He had been paid, and he was three courses into the work, and a man does not put down three courses of work for a sentence.

The jug fell. The left hand came up from his side with something short and dark in it, a blade no longer than a hand, and came at the heart under the mantle's edge, straight, very fast, a blow that had been practised; and the heart was not there. Emeric had turned when the note found its stair, a quarter turn on the balls of his feet with the mantle swinging heavy round him, chest away, the turn Ward had drilled into him until his body did it before he had finished thinking it. What met the knife was the air at his left side. It went through the silk of the waistcoat under his arm with a small dry tearing, through the lace, past his ribs by the width of a finger, and buried itself to the grip in the fur of the mantle's lining, and stuck there.

And Ward had the man.

Emeric felt it rather than saw it, with his face turned away: Ward's note coming round the end of the chair in one movement, while the blade was still going in, and taking the man's knife wrist from behind in one hand and the back of his neck in the other; and when the blade caught in the fur Ward simply went on with the movement it had started, up and round and down, so that the young man's arm went up behind his back to the shoulder blade with a sound like a green stick and his face went down on the high table among the cups, hard, and stayed there.

All of that in the time it took the jug to hit the dais and break.



The hall made the sound a hall makes. Two hundred people shouting, and benches going over, and somebody screaming in the gallery, and the musicians stopping all at once in the middle of a bar.

The king's guard were at the high table before the screaming stopped: six of them, in the crown's red, who had stood behind the king's chair all evening without anybody noticing them, as Ward stood behind Emeric's; and their captain put his hand on Ward's arm, and Ward looked at the hand, and at the captain, and did not let go of the man.

"Mine," said a voice beside the king. Corbin. He had come round the table's end, quickly, white, with two of his own household behind him in the Lieutenant's grey. "The Lieutenant's guard will take him. Captain..."

"The crown's guard."

The king had not raised his voice. He did not need to. He was standing at the centre of the high table where he had risen for the cups, with his cup still in his hand, and he had not moved at all through any of it, and he did not move now. He said it to the captain in red.

"The crown's guard will hold him, in the crown's cells, under the crown's lock," said Harrold. "Nobody else will have the key. The crown's magistrate will see him in the morning." His eyes went to Corbin for a breath. Then back to the captain. "Take him."

Corbin stood very still. Then he said, "Of course, sir," and stepped back, and his two men in grey stepped back with him. Two hundred people had watched the Lieutenant of the realm told no at his father's table, over a knife meant for his brother, and not one of them would remember it by morning. Emeric thought Corbin would remember it for some time.

Ward let go. The captain's men took the young man up off the table by both arms, his face bloody, his left arm hanging, and he looked round once at the hall as they turned him, with the mouth that had been laughing, and it was not laughing now, and it was not frightened either; it was nothing at all, the face of a man who had done a day's work and been paid for it and had always known what the work might cost. Then they took him down the dais steps and through the side door, and the door shut.

Ward drew the knife out of the mantle's lining, carefully, the way a man draws a thorn, and looked at it, and gave it to the captain's last man, and came back and stood behind Emeric's chair.

Emeric looked down at himself.

The waistcoat was cut open on the left side from the edge of the breast to under the arm, a long clean slit in the black silk, and at the front of the slit, under the torn lace, a curved grey edge of steel showed, clean, not a mark on it. There was no blood. He put his hand on it. It was warm.

Ferra put her hand on top of his.

She did not say anything. She had not moved from her place. Her cup stood on the table in front of her where it had stood all evening, full of the wine it had been filled with at the first course, and her hand was on his on the steel, very hard, and her face was white to the lips and perfectly calm, and her eyes, when he looked at them, were not calm at all.

"Good," she said, as she had said on the hull, and went to take her hand away.

He turned his own over under it and caught it, and held it against the steel, and did not let it go; there at the high table, with the king standing three places off and the crown's guard taking a man down the steps and the whole hall looking. He held it until he felt her pulse in the ends of her fingers come down from where it had been, and her breath go out of her, once, all of it, and her hand stop being a fist.

"I'm here," he said, low. "I knew where he was the whole time."

"I know." She did not look at him. "I'm cross with you anyway." And she took her hand back, and folded it with the other, and was the Chancellor of Kaldo again.

Imani came round behind Ferra's chair. She did not ask. She put her hand in at the slit in the waistcoat, flat, against his ribs where the blade had gone by, and pressed, and said, "Breathe in," and he breathed in, and she said, "Again," and he did. Then she took his wrist, there at the high table, in front of the king and the whole hall, and counted it with her lips moving very slightly, as if the hall were a ward and the king were the man in the next bed.

"Not a mark," she said. She let go. "Ninety-four. Which is high, for a man who knew it was coming." She looked at him. "You'll live." She went to go. He caught two of her fingers as they left his wrist, below the edge of the table, and held them for the length of a breath; and they were not quite steady, which they always were, in a ward, with a man bleeding on the table. She looked down at him. Then she pressed his fingers back, hard, once, and went back round Ferra's chair to her own place and sat down and picked up her fork.



He finished the feast.

He did not decide to. He found that he had sat down, in the mantle, with the slit in his waistcoat and the steel showing in it, and that the hall was looking at him; and that the king had sat down too, and the court was sitting down, slowly, a bench at a time, because the king had; and that the servants had come back up from the trestles with new jugs, white, careful, and were filling the cups again, every one of them for the king's health and the new duke's, because the custom was not finished and nobody knew what else to do.

He drank the king's health. He drank it from a cup Ward had filled himself, from a jug Ward had carried up from the trestle himself and tasted. He drank his own. He sat through the seventh course and the eighth, and talked to the lord on his left about that lord's river, which had flooded in the spring, and ate the things that were put in front of him, and his hand did not shake on his knife once that anybody could see; and two hundred people watched him do it, from one end of the long hall to the other, a duke who had had a knife put at him at his own girding by a servant, and had talked to the man first, and had not left the hall, and was eating his pudding.

Ferra did not touch her cup. Not the one in front of her, nor the one Ward filled for her. She sat beside him through the seventh course and the eighth, very straight, with her hands in her lap and the roses on her skirt, and talked to the lady on her other side about the price of silk; and her cup stood in front of her full, all evening, and was full when the tables were carried out. Imani ate the seventh course and the eighth and drank water from a jug Ward had tasted, and once, when the lady across from her asked what it was like in the south, said, "Wet," and went on eating.

At the foot of the hall, across the tables, Aldane sat among the northern houses and ate nothing and did not once look up at the dais.



The king went at midnight, as the custom said he went, and the hall rose for him and stood while he went down the dais steps and out by the door behind the canopy with the six in red round him; and when the door had shut the hall breathed out, and the noise came back up into the roof all at once.

Emeric stood at the high table while the chamberlain's men came for the mantle. They were slow about it. The chain at the throat had been made for a man who meant to keep it on, and while the first of them worked at the clasp and the second held up the fur behind, the end of the high table filled round him: Ferra and Imani from their places, Ward behind; and from the other end, along the table from the king's empty chair, unhurried, with three lords of the north a pace behind him and a cup in his hand, Corbin.

Emeric knew the walk before he knew the face: a man crossing a room to be kind to somebody in front of witnesses. The three lords were the witnesses. The nearest tables went quiet as he came, because a prince speaking in that voice was meant to be heard.

"Brother." Warm. Carrying. He had always had a lovely voice for it. "You've had a long day."

"I have."

"We'll do better by you next time." Corbin looked at the slit in the waistcoat, and the grey edge of the steel in it, with grave concern. "It's what comes of being so long away. The house forgets how to keep you." One of the lords behind him smiled into his beard. "Though I see the south has kept you very well." His eyes went past Emeric's shoulder to Ferra, and then to Imani, and came back, pleasantly, as a man's eyes come back from a pair of horses he has been invited to admire. "Two of them. We'd heard there was a Chancellor. Nobody told us about the..." he let the smallest breath go by, "...physician."

The lords laughed. Not loudly. Correctly.

"You'll stay with us now you're home, of course," said Corbin. "Father's had your old rooms aired." He raised his cup a little. "I'm sure we can find the ladies something."

It was maintenance. Emeric had felt it done to him for fifteen years and it was perfect of its kind, as it had always been: there was nothing in it a man could call an insult in any court in the realm, and every lord within twenty feet had heard the Chancellor of Kaldo and its physician called a pair of somethings that would need a room found for them. He felt Ferra behind his left shoulder go quite still. He did not have to look at Imani. He could feel her note without going out for it, close and hard and perfectly level, the note of a woman who had said in a dispensary in April that a court would count, and had been right.

The drunk prince would have laughed. That had been the part, and he had played it well: when Corbin said a thing like this, the drunk prince laughed loudest and said something worse about himself, and the court went home and wrote down that the third son was a good-natured fool, which was what the part was for. He could feel the laugh where it used to be. It would still have worked. The court was waiting for it.

And in every letter that left the city that week, Ferra and Imani would be what Corbin had called them, with the duke of Kaldo laughing beside them.

Corbin was looking up at him. That was the other thing Emeric noticed, and noticed Corbin notice: he was looking up. Not far. A little. He was looking at a man whose shoulders filled a coat that had been cut to them, and whose hands, at his sides, were a smith's hands now and a soldier's, and whose eyes were not swimming; and something behind Corbin's grave kind face did a small sum and did not like the total.

"That's kind," said Emeric. "I'll decline. I've a house." He let it sit, as Corbin had. "I've had one for nine years. I built most of it."

"Of course," said Corbin, smiling.

"But I understand why you'd offer." Emeric looked at him quite pleasantly, and at the three lords, and at the quiet tables, and back. "It's extremely comfortable to stay home, brother. It doesn't make you any less of a man, if you have so many years under your belt and still live with your parents."

Nobody said anything at all.

Then somebody laughed: once, out loud, a short bark of a laugh that got out before its owner could catch it, from the end of the dais where the steps came up. And then one of Corbin's own three made a sound into his cup and turned it, too late, into a cough; and the nearest table went, and the one past it, not loudly, but all the way along, the sound a hall makes when it has been handed something to repeat.

Corbin's face did not change. It was a very good face, and he had had it a long time. But Emeric felt his note, close enough to touch, go white and high for the space of a breath, as a flame does when a door opens on it; and then smooth again, all at once, as if a hand had been passed over it.

"Good night, Your Grace," said Corbin warmly, and inclined his head the exact depth the custom asked, and went back along the high table with his lords behind him, who did not look at one another.

The chamberlain's man got the clasp undone at last. The mantle came off Emeric's shoulders all at once, and he felt suddenly light enough to float.

Two cold fingers found his wrist.

"Ninety-eight," said Imani. "You enjoyed that."

"Somewhat."

Ferra had not moved. She was looking after Corbin down the table, quite calmly, with her hands folded at her waist as Isolde folded hers.

"I'll not write that down," she said.

"No?"

"I'll remember it."

He put his hand flat on the small of her back, over the gold lattice, where the lords going away down the table could see it if they turned their heads, and kept it there. Some of them turned their heads. Ferra did not move away from it. She leaned back into it, very slightly, the width of a sheet of paper, and went on looking calmly after Corbin.

At the end of the dais, by the steps, a young man in a dark riding coat with straw on one sleeve was turning away. Emeric saw him for less than a breath, a square shoulder and a short beard and, as he turned, the eyes, gold, catching the lamplight; and then he was going down the steps into the crowd, quickly, with his head down, as a man goes who has laughed at the wrong moment in the wrong hall and would rather nobody worked out which of them it was.

Emeric knew who it was. He had known the laugh before he saw the eyes, which made no sense at all, since the last time he had heard it, it had belonged to a boy of four.

"Well, he's gone," said somebody behind him. "He was always quick when he'd done something."

Maeve had come round the back of the high table the long way, past the king's empty chair and Corbin's, without hurrying, with her cup still in her hand. She did not curtsey to the new duke. She put the cup down on the table, and put her arms round his neck, and held on; in front of the end of the high table and every lord still standing at it, as if there were nobody there at all, which was how she had always done it.

"Third time," she said into his collar. "I was in the wrong place twice. I wasn't going to be a third time." She let go, and held him off at arm's length, and looked at him, the shoulders and the hair and the gold, and her eyebrows went up and stayed up. "Look at you."

"Maeve."

"Don't Maeve me, I've had to hear about you from strangers for a year." She looked past him. "And I've had to hear about them from Corbin, which is worse." She went round him, quite deliberately, and stood in front of Ferra and Imani, and looked them both over without pretending she wasn't doing it. "I'm his sister. The nice one, which is a low bar when you've met Corbin."

"Your Highness," said Ferra.

"Maeve. Please. I've been Your Highness all evening and I'm sick of it." She looked at the dress, the lattice and the level bands, with real appreciation. "Somebody spent days on those sleeves." Then at Imani's single rose, and at Imani's hand, which had his pulse in it a moment ago. "And you're the physician. You took his pulse at the high table in front of my father." She sounded delighted. "Nobody's ever done that. He'll be thinking about it for a month."

"Twice," said Imani. "Ninety-four, and then ninety-eight, after your brother."

Maeve laughed out loud, and the lords still at the table turned their heads, and she did not care about that either.

"I'll tell you something," she said to the two of them, lowering her voice not at all. "When he was nineteen I'd have bet my mother's horses he'd never get one woman to look at him twice. Not one. He couldn't keep a candle lit." She looked at Ferra, and at Imani, and back at Emeric, and shook her head slowly. "And he's gone south and come back with two. And beautiful. And clever, I'm told, both of you, cleverer than him, which wouldn't have been hard then." She picked her cup up off the table. "Crazy world."

Ferra's mouth was doing something it did not usually do in public. Imani had given up and was smiling.

Then she turned round on him, and the delight went out of her face as if somebody had shut a door on it, and what was behind it was not delight.

"Nine years," she said. "Not a line. Not to me. Not to Waylon. I had to learn you'd taken a barony from a soap peddler at the Emberfell market."

"A letter from Kaldo puts your name in a book," he said. "Corbin's, or the cardinal's, or whoever's opening the post that month. An exiled brother who writes home is a faction. I thought you were safer without one."

Maeve looked at him for a long moment.

"That's the stupidest thing you've ever said to me," she said, "and I was there for the drunk years." She leaned in, so the lords at the table had to work for it. "I have a grey household and a mother in Emberfell and a father who reads his own children's letters before breakfast. My name's been in every book in this city since I could walk. I'd have liked to be in yours." She straightened. "Safer. You decided that for me. You always did that."

He had no answer that was better than hers, so he did not give one.

"So write to me," said Maeve, to the other two. "Both of you. Not him, he writes like a ledger." Ferra opened her mouth, and Maeve said, "I know, you write the ledgers. Write to me anyway." She touched Emeric's arm, once, going past, and was gone round the end of the table, in her green, toward the hall door; and a lord of the north who had stood aside for her looked after her and then at the Kaldo end of the table with the expression of a man revising a letter in his head.



It was well past midnight when the court went, and he came out of the hall into the passage behind the dais with his shoulders still remembering the mantle.

The passage was long and dim, lit by a lamp at each end, and it went toward the stair where Ward had said the carriage would be. Ward was at the far end of it with Saban, talking to the captain of the king's guard. Ferra and Imani had gone ahead with Isolde. The passage between was empty.

Not quite. Halfway along it, by a window that looked down on a courtyard with a dry fountain in it, a woman in dark grey silk was standing with her hands folded in front of her, waiting.

He stopped.

Vantic did not move toward him. She stood by the window as she had stood in a good many doorways in Kaldo with a case of books, quite still, looking at him; and she had a ring on her right hand now that she had not had on Tuesday, heavy and plain, on the second finger, with no stone in it.

"Your Grace," she said.

"Livia Ducatis," he said. "Head of the House."

"Yes." She did not smile. She looked at the slit in his waistcoat, and at the grey edge of the steel in it, and back up at his face. "On Tuesday. At four. The partners sat for eight hours and I answered one question for seven of them." She was quiet. "My father heard them out, and gave it to me before the lamps were lit, and went to bed." She did not look away. "I could have told you. On the hull. On Tuesday night, in the cabin, with the door open. Ferra knew; she read it off my face in the doorway, as she reads everything." She was quiet for a breath. "I didn't tell you."

"No."

"I wanted you to hear it," said Vantic, "in a room where you couldn't answer it."

The lamp at the far end of the passage moved; Saban, turning, with his hat in his hand. Neither of them looked.

He understood it. He had stood behind the doors that morning with the herald's voice coming down the stone, and heard the name, and kept his face still because there were two hundred people on the other side of the doors and a chamberlain at his elbow and the whole of the day ahead of him; and he had not been able to say one word to her, or go to her, or do anything at all but stand there and hear it. Which was the point. She had not wanted his congratulations, or his face, or his thanks, or anything he might have said to her in a counting room in Kaldo that would have made it a thing between the two of them. She had wanted the hall to say it, and him to hear the hall say it, as everybody else did.

Once. In her life.

"It was a good room for it," he said.

"It was the best room in the kingdom for it." She unfolded her hands. And then she was Vantic again, all at once, as a door shuts; the voice the same, the face the same, and the thing behind them gone back where she kept it. "The House will want to discuss the duchy's paper, Your Grace. The patent changes your standing with the crown's lenders. My eldest brother holds the crown's book and will want to know whether Kaldo means to borrow on its new style." She inclined her head the exact depth the custom asked. "I'd suggest you don't. Not yet. We can talk about it at the quarter day."

"At the quarter day," said Emeric.

"Good night, Your Grace."

She went past him down the passage toward the far stair, not hurrying, in her grey, with the ring on her hand; and at the end of the passage Saban stood aside for her and bowed, very low, lower than Emeric had ever seen him bow to anybody, and she nodded to him as she passed and went down the stair and was gone.

Emeric stood by the window over the dry fountain a while longer with his hand on the cut in his waistcoat.

Then a page in the king's household livery came up the passage from the other end, the end by the hall, and stopped in front of him, and bowed.

"Your Grace," said the page. "His Majesty asks if you'll come to him."
