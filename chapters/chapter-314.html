Chapter 314 - Black Hull

Commencement fell on the last Friday of September, and it took all day.

It had taken an hour the first year. Fourteen names, the third year, read by Sela from the roll in front of the teachers' bench in her dark dress, and each child standing up and coming forward and saying a name, and the square laughing when Paskin shouted from the back that the girl was still falling on the door. The square had been half full. Emeric had sat on the bench beside Bram and known every one of the fourteen by sight.

This year Ottoline had the count on cards in a tray on the end of the bench, in ward order, with the four baronies' schools in their own rows behind Kaldo's: six hundred and forty-one. She had told Sela the number a week ago, and Sela had gone a little pale and then said that she had read the roll every year since there was a roll and would read it this year if it took her till dark; and it did.

The square was full by seven. Not half full. The benches went back from the teachers' bench in rows across the whole of it, to the records hall steps on one side and the plane trees on the other, with the year's children on the front ones in their best and their families packed in behind them as far as the tram stop, and boys up the plane trees, and people at every window round the square that would open; and on the east side, where the Grayrock train came in, a block of benches that the Grayrock people had taken by walking up from the station at six with their own cushions and sitting on them till everybody understood that they were not going to move. Milford had come too, fewer, in a knot by the fountain. Wayford had sent its school's first two on the morning train with a schoolmaster who looked as if he had not slept.

The teachers' bench was not a bench any more. It was three, in a long row under an awning, because everybody in Kaldo who had a job taught at the school and everybody who taught had a right to sit there; and the six and Armand were in the middle of the front one with Sela at the table in front of them, and behind them the masters and mistresses of everything Kaldo did, the works and the wards and the port and the printing office and the line room and the mill, in their good coats, two hundred of them, fanning themselves with the printed roll.

The king sat at the back of the square.

He had been asked to the bench. He had said, as he said to everything now, that he would like for once to watch a thing from where most people watched it; and he sat on the end of a bench by the records hall steps among the dye works' families, in his brown coat, with his hat on his knee, and Waylon beside him; and for the first hour nobody near him knew who he was, and then somebody did, and the news went along the bench and the benches round it like a draught along a corridor, and people stopped looking at the front and looked at him; and Harrold, who had been looked at all his life, looked at the front, steadily, until they gave up and looked there too.

Sela began at eight.

"Anna Abbot," she said. "Ward one."

A girl in the front row stood up, very straight, with her hands in fists at her sides, and came to the table, and said, "The mill. Under Mistress Dunn." And Sela wrote it, and said, "The mill," aloud for the square, and the girl went back to her bench, and somebody behind her whooped; and Sela said, "Benet Abbot. Ward one," and a boy who was plainly the girl's brother stood up and said, "The mill," too, before he had even got to the table, and the square laughed.

That was the first.

Emeric had sat through nine of these. He found, by the tenth name, that he was not going to be able to sit through this one the way he had sat through those: as a man on a bench, pleased, a little bored, glancing along the row at Ferra to see whether she was writing. He could not do it because he could not keep up. The names came, and the wards, and the choices, faster than he could put faces to them, and he did not know most of the faces anyway. He knew the parents. He knew the dye works and the tram sheds and the north quay and the tan yard in the lower ward. He had seen most of these children, at one time or another, as small noise at a school door or a heap on a tram seat or a face in the line at the records hall in August. And now they stood up one after another out of the benches in their best, girls and boys, carters' children and lightermen's and the mill's and the miners', and walked to Sela's table and said a name, and went back, and he did not know them. He had stopped knowing them some time ago. He had not noticed when.

The works, under Bram: a hundred and fifty by the end of the day, Ottoline said, and Bram stood up for the first of them and sat down again only when Ferra pulled his coat. Medicine, under Imani: ninety, more than had ever chosen anything but the works; and Imani stood for every one of them, at her place on the bench, so that by eleven her feet hurt and she stood anyway. The books, under Ferra. Trade, under Saban, who sat with his hat on his knee and nodded at each of his like a man accepting a bale he had already inspected. The craft and the clothing works, under Abloh, who could not hear his own name said seventy times without his eyes going wet, and was embarrassed about it, and blew his nose loudly at every tenth. Knighthood and flight, under Ward: eighty, and Ward looked at each of them as they said his name with the same face he gave a new blade, which nobody in the square but Emeric could read, and which said that he would find out.

The stones, under Armand: four.

Armand had been looking forward to it, Emeric knew. He had told the long room in August that he expected nobody, since nobody ever chose the stones, and that he would be very gracious about it; and when the first of the four stood up at a little after ten, a thin dark girl from the port who had been in the Seers' Table since she was eleven, and walked to the table and said in a carrying voice, "The stones. Under Master Armand," he went perfectly still on the bench. By the fourth he had his long hand over his mouth. Ferra, without looking up from her book, passed him a handkerchief along the bench, and he took it, and used it, and said to nobody that the dust from the square was very bad this year.

And there were two new names, that had never been said in that square before.

The first was Sela's own. Teaching: under Mistress Sela. She had never had a department. She had had a room, and then a building, and then a school, and every master in Kaldo had come through her door to teach in it; but there had never been a table at Commencement where a child could stand and say that they wanted to do what she did. There was one this year because Ferra had put it on the roll in July and not told her. So Sela had to read them out herself. The first was a boy from ward four, at a quarter to ten, who came to the table and looked at her and said, "Teaching. Under you, Mistress"; and Sela opened her mouth to read it and could not. She sat with the pen. The square waited. The boy stood in front of her table, going red, until Imani got up from the bench behind, and leaned over Sela's shoulder, and read it out for her in her clear ward voice, "Teaching, under Mistress Sela," and put her hand on Sela's back for a moment, and sat down again.

Sela read the rest of them herself. Sixty. Her voice was perfectly steady for every one.

The second was Maren's.

Maren had not wanted a department. Maren had said so to Ferra in the kitchen, at length, with a ladle; she had said that she was a cook and not a school, that she had no time to stand in a square and be looked at, and that if any child of hers wanted to learn to cook they could come to the back door at five like anybody else and peel what they were given. Ferra had written cookery on the roll anyway. And it had turned out, when Ottoline went through her cards in the spring, that a great many children had already come to the back door at five. Some of them had been coming for years. Some of them had gone out of it into the town and opened cook shops and bake ovens and pie stalls at the tram stops, and the town had eaten their food and never thought to ask where they learned to make it, and found out today.

Forty-five of them said her name. Maren was at the back of the square by the kitchen cart that had brought the bread down for noon, in her apron, with her arms folded, and she heard her name the first time and went dark red from the neck up and stayed that colour for the rest of the morning. At the tenth she took her apron off. At the twentieth she put it back on and went behind the cart and began cutting bread, very hard, with her back to the square. At the forty-fifth she came out from behind the cart and stood with the bread knife in her hand and looked at the forty-fifth, a big shy boy from the tan yard, as if she would like to hit him with it; and the square, which had been waiting for her to do something since the first, cheered so loudly that the pigeons went up off the hall roof all at once.



Ivo filmed it.

He had the motion camera on its legs on the records hall steps, above the crowd, at the height of a man's shoulder, and he stood behind it all day turning the crank: the same handle he had turned in his yard for a month to learn the count, two turns a second, smooth, so that the frames would come down through the gate at a rate the eye would take for a thing moving. Fennick had brought him the roll that morning in a flat tin, at six, at the steps, and stood over him while he threaded it. It was the first clear roll off the drum. It had come out of Fennick's acetate vats and been poured thin onto the polished drum the Forge had turned for it and peeled off at the far side clear as window glass; and Fennick had wanted, in July, to make it out of the gun-cotton he made his smokeless from, because he had the gun-cotton and it was easy, and Emeric had said no before Fennick had finished the sentence, and Fennick had said no too before Emeric finished his, because Fennick made the gun-cotton and knew what it did in a hot gate. So it was the slow way, cotton linters out of the forced field and acid out of the coke works, and it had taken three months; and it was the first of it, in a tin, on the steps.

Nobody in the square knew what Ivo was doing. They thought he was taking a picture, a very slow one. Children kept coming up the steps to look into the front of the box, and Ivo kept saying, without taking his hand off the crank, "Out of it. Out of it. Thank you. Out of it."

Merrin was beside him with a horn on a pole and a second box on the step at her feet, connected to the horn by a cable, that hummed when it was warm and did nothing else that anybody could see. It did not work yet, she said, to anybody who asked; she was there to see it not work properly. She stood all day with the horn held up over the steps toward Sela's table like a woman fishing, and changed its angle every hour, and wrote in a book.



The holdings began after noon.

This was the other new thing. Sela read the choosing as she always read it, the name and the ward and the department; and then, for some of them, there was a fourth word, which had not been read in that square before. A place. Every master in Kaldo who had a team out on the holdings that year, at Grayrock or Milford or Wayford or the island, had asked for hands, and Ferra had made a column of them, and a child could choose a department and a place both, and be posted there with the department's kit on their back the week after.

"Hob Varney," read Sela. "Ward six. The works. Wayford."

A cheer went up from the knot round the Wayford schoolmaster. Hob Varney, a square red boy, looked round in astonishment at being cheered by people he had never met.

The clothing works had the kit laid out on trestles by the hall steps for the holdings' choosers to take away: canvas jackets and trousers, the works' cut, with the pockets where Abloh had put them after asking forty people where they wanted their pockets; boots with steel in the toe; gloves; a bag. They went up to the trestles straight from the table and came away with their arms full and their faces amazed, and their mothers took the bags off them and went through them on the benches, item by item, and held the boots up to the light.

At a quarter past two Sela came to the Grayrock rows.

Grayrock had had its own school for three years. Bryda had built it in the old baron's tithe barn, with the barn's doors left as they were because she would not pay to change a thing that worked, and Sela had sent teachers up on the line every Monday and fetched them back every Friday, and Bram had sent his works' masters up to teach by turns; and in three years it had not yet had a child old enough to choose. This year it had nine. They had come down on the six o'clock train with their families and their cushions and sat in the front of the Grayrock block since seven, in a row, looking straight ahead, as if they had been told what would happen to them if they did anything else.

The first of them was a girl.

"Edda Rowe," read Sela. "Grayrock school."

She stood up. She was fifteen or sixteen, thin, in a brown dress that had been somebody's before it was hers and had been let down twice, with her hair in a plait so tight it pulled at the corners of her eyes; and she walked from the end of the Grayrock row to Sela's table, the whole length of the square, with every bench on the east side gone dead quiet behind her, and stood at the table, and did not look at Sela. She looked over Sela's head, at the bench, at the place on it where Imani stood.

"Medicine," she said. "Under Mistress Imani. Grayrock."

Imani stood a little straighter.

"Grayrock's clinic," said the girl, in case anybody had not understood, and her voice went up at the end and came down again. "Home. When I'm done. That's what I want written."

Sela wrote it. Then she read it out, for the square, as she had read the others, the name and the department and the place; and the Grayrock benches came up off their cushions.

"Edda Rowe. Medicine. Grayrock."

Emeric had heard that square cheer for a good many things in thirteen years. He had heard it for First Light, and the trams, and the express; he had heard it cheer him, once or twice, though not often, and he had not much liked it. He had never heard a noise like the one the Grayrock block made for Edda Rowe. It was not a cheer exactly. It was a sound that four hundred people make when something they have been waiting for since before they knew they were waiting has happened, a long, rising, cracking roar, with things in it that were not glad at all, and it went on and on, and the girl stood at the table in her let-down dress and did not turn round, because she could not.

At the end of the Grayrock block, on the aisle, standing, in black, with her hands folded at her waist, was Bryda.

She was not cheering. She had not moved. She had built the school in her husband's barn after he was dead, and paid for its roof out of the barony's first good year, and argued Ferra down over its slates; and she stood on the aisle with her plain face perfectly still and watched the girl's back at Sela's table, and her eyes ran, steadily, down both sides of her face, and she did not wipe them and did not look away.

Edda Rowe went back down the square to the Grayrock rows with her chin up and her plait swinging. On the records hall steps Ivo's hand went round on the crank, two turns a second, smooth, and Merrin, beside him, had her horn up over the steps and was very still, the way a woman fishing is still when the line moves.

Emeric looked along the bench at Imani. She was standing for the girl, and her face had gone the way it went at a bedside sometimes, when a thing she had done for years for nobody in particular turned out to have been done for somebody.

She saw him look. She did not look back. She said, very quietly, still standing, "Ninety. I've ninety. And beds for twenty I haven't used." And then, quieter still: "I'm going to need more than twenty."



Sela read the last name at a quarter to seven, by lamplight, with her voice gone to a thread; and the square, which had been in its seats eleven hours, rose all together and gave her a noise that was not for anybody's child, and she sat at the table with the roll in front of her and the pen in her hand and let it go over her with her eyes shut.

They ate afterward at Maren's on the Gate Avenue, the six and Armand and the king and Waylon, at the long table at the back, because Ferra had booked it in July without asking anybody, and because Maren would be there to be thanked and could not escape.

It was the first eating house in Kaldo with its food on a board on the wall and the prices beside each dish in copper. That had been Ferra's idea too. Maren had objected to it when it was proposed and objected to it when it was painted and objected to it nightly since, on the grounds that a cook should cook what was good that day and a board was a promise, and a promise was a thing a cook would end up keeping at the expense of the food; and when the six came in at half past seven she was standing in front of it in her apron with a cloth in her hand, rubbing out the eel pie with her back to the room, because the eels had not been good, and writing something else instead.

She did not turn round.

"I'm not talking about it," she said to the board.

"About what?" said Ferra.

"Any of it." Maren wrote mutton, very hard, so that the chalk squeaked. "Sit down. You're letting the heat out."

The king had never in his life eaten in a room where anybody could come in off the street. He sat at the end of the long table with his back to the wall, where he could see the door from habit, and read Maren's board twice through, slowly, with the attention he had given Ferra's drains; and when the girl came to ask what he would have he said the mutton, please, and looked at the price, and then at Ferra, and said, "Is that right? For that much?" and Ferra said that it was, and that Maren lost a little on it on purpose so that dock men could eat there, and the king read the board a third time.

From the north quay, through the open door, came the smell of the fish stall on the corner, fat and vinegar and hot batter; a boy at the counter was handing a dock man his fish and fried potato in a paper cone. Emeric knew what it was. He had known what it was the day the stall opened and he had walked past it, and stopped, and stood on the corner like a man struck, and walked on without a word. He did not say anything now. He watched the dock man go off up the quay eating out of the cone, and turned back to the table, and found Imani watching him, and said nothing to her either; and she did not ask.

Maren came out at the end with the pudding herself, which she never did, and put it down in the middle of the table, and stood over it with the cloth.

"Forty-five," she said, to the pudding. "That boy from the tan yard. He can't boil an egg."

"He'll learn," said Ferra.

"He'll learn because I'll make him." Maren looked round the table, at all of them, and lastly at the king, who looked back at her with polite and complete attention; and her colour came up again, all the way, as it had in the square. "I said I wasn't talking about it," she said, and went back to her kitchen, and they heard her through the hatch, a moment later, shouting at somebody about the eels.



The Cradle came up on the second of October, on South Sentinel, in the morning, level, the first time.

It had been the old races' pier. It lay along the inner shore of the big east bay where they had found it in the survey in the winter of the year before, under the weed, settled in the shallows below the old quay and the carved face of rock above it: a slab of worked grey stone six hundred feet long and two hundred wide, fifteen feet through, hollowed underneath into cells like a hull, so that it was lighter than anybody looking at it would have believed; and round its rim, sunk into its edge at even spaces, sixteen gravity stones, each as tall as a man, that had held it a few feet above the water for the old fishers' boats to tie up to, for as long as there had been mana enough to pay them. Then the mana had thinned, and they had not been paid, and the pier had come down gently onto the sand of the bay with whatever was on it, and lain there, and the weed had grown over it, and the stones in its rim had gone to sleep, very nearly empty and not quite.

For eighteen months Kaldo had fed them. Ferra had counted every flask: liquid mana from the condensing plant at the secret dock and from the Hod's runs to the Band, carried out to the island in the Hod's hold and poured, a flask at a time, into the cups Bram had cut round each rim stone's foot, and taken up by the stone, slowly, the way dry ground takes a slow rain, until each one stopped taking and the flask stayed full. A stone took what it could hold and no more. There was no overloading it. Bram and Fletcher had cleared the weed off its deck and the sand out of its cells and patched the cells that had cracked in the long settling, and sealed them; and in September the last stone had stopped taking, and Ferra had drawn a line under the column in the stone book, and written full, and the date.

So on the second of October they lifted it.

There was not much to see. That was the thing Emeric remembered afterward. Bram and Fletcher stood on the old quay, and Emeric with them, and Ferra with the stone book, and a dozen of the island's crew along the shore; and Emeric went down onto the deck of the slab at its landward corner, with the bay water a foot deep over his boots, and laid his hand flat on the nearest of the rim stones, and went into it from the inside.

The lines were there. He had read them first in the Catch, the island's fish trap, two winters ago, beside the live pull that still ran: the old lines that tied the sixteen together, a single web of instructions that went round the whole rim from stone to stone and through the slab between them, written so long ago that the language of them was a thing he could read only the way a man reads a hand he half knows. Rise. How far. Hold. Lower. How fast. Each stone's line answering the next, so that sixteen were one. He did not change any of it. He only woke it, as a man wakes a house, by opening a door in it, and stepped back off the slab into the bay.

The water round the slab's edge went flat.

Then the slab rose out of it. All of it, all at once, the whole six hundred feet of it coming up out of the bay level as a table, slowly, without a sound, the water pouring off its deck in a long sheet on every side and out of the cells beneath through the old drain holes in a hundred small falls; and it went up a foot, two, three, and stopped, exactly level, three feet above the water of the bay, and stayed there, hanging, dripping, with the sea running back under it and the sunlight going in under its edge.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Fletcher had walked down the quay steps to the water. He crouched at the edge and looked along the underside of the slab, along the whole length of it, with his eye at the level of the water, the way a shipwright sights along a keel; and then he stood up and walked to the far end of the quay and did it again from there.

"She's dead level," he said. "End to end. Across." He looked at Emeric. "Not a hair."

"First time," said Bram.

"First time."

Bram said nothing more. He folded his arms and looked at the slab hanging in the bay in front of him, six hundred feet of somebody else's work that had been lying in the weed for longer than his family had a name, and had just done the thing it was made to do without anybody having to tell it twice; and Emeric, standing beside him, felt his note go very quiet, the way it went in front of a machine on his floor that he thought was better than he could have made.

Emeric was not looking at the slab.

He was still feeling the lines, from where he stood, with his sense: sixteen stones, sixteen hands of instructions, all of them the same hand. The same turn of the old writing at the end of each line, the same way of putting a number, the same little habit, like a man who always dots his letters a certain way, in the place where each stone said where level was. Whoever had written them had written all sixteen, alone, at one sitting perhaps, a long time ago, with the slab in front of them; and so all sixteen agreed about where level was without anybody having had to make them, because there had only ever been one idea of it.

He noticed it, and kept it, and did not yet know why.



The island's flying school had been running for twelve days.

Ward ran it from a hut on the bluff above the bay with a table, a stove, the rolls, and a window that looked down on the water, and he came out of it only to stand on the beach. There were a hundred knights on the island and the second hundred due on the Hod at the end of the week, and every one of them, on his first morning, had gone into the shallows to old Wicket in his shirt.

Wicket had not changed his opinion of knights. He had changed his opinion of some knights, one at a time, and he kept a list of them in his head which he would not say aloud; and by the second week the first fifty had been through his hands and could lie on the sea and swim the length of the old quay and back, badly, without being taken by the hair, and the big sergeant from Grayrock with the neck like a bollard had learned to float on his back and could be seen doing it in the evening, in the bay, alone, with his eyes shut and an expression of great peace.

The dead-zone drop was on the eighth day. Emeric had not been there, and was glad not to have been. Fennick had gone up on the Hod with a stick of ten in their suits clipped on along her rail and a stone in a tin, and over the bay at three thousand feet he had burst it under them; and the suits had gone dead, every node, all at once, as Fennick and Ward had said they would; and the ten had fallen, as Fennick and Ward had said they would, through a wind that did not move their hair, for three counts, until each suit's watchdog felt its hearts go dead and fired its chute. Ten chutes had opened. Ward had counted them from the beach. Ten men had come down into the bay under them, swearing, and the boats had fished them out one by one, and two of them had been sick in the boats and one of them had wept and three of them had asked to go again; and Ward had written all of it down.

"Nobody likes it," said Ward, on the beach, when Emeric came. "That's right. They're not meant to like it. They're meant to have done it."

And there was Saint Nobody.

Fletcher had built her in a month, at Ward's request, to the lines Emeric had drawn for Armand at the long table in July: the white hull, as near as he could remember it from one look in the dark at the crossing, an oak hull on old pontoon floats, whitewashed, with panes of violet glass let into her prow where her lift stones would have sat if she had any. She did not fly. She sat on her floats in the bay, moored fore and aft, and rode the swell, and was boarded. That was what she was for. The knights went up in the Hod and hull two and came down on her deck out of the air in their suits, in sticks, by day and then by night, and took her from bow to stern, and then took her again; and on the fourth day somebody, nobody would say who, had painted a bishop's mitre on her bow in white lead over the whitewash, very large, with a cross-hatching of gold on it out of a pot of Abloh's sign paint; and on the fifth day somebody else had written under it, in neat capitals, SAINT NOBODY, and she had been Saint Nobody ever since.

Ferra had heard. Ferra had opened a line in the island's book for her, under repairs, and written: one hull, sainted.

The hooks went across on the tenth day and failed on the eleventh. Emeric heard about that too, when he came. The hook was a grapple on a line, and the line ran back to a winch on hull two's deck that hauled the white hull in alongside once the hook bit, so that the boarders could cross; and on the eleventh the hook bit, and the winch took up, and the ratchet that held the drum slipped on the third pawl, and the drum ran free, and the line went out with a knight on the end of it who had clipped onto it for the crossing, and took him off hull two's deck backwards into the bay from forty feet. He had come up laughing. The ratchet had not.

Tess came out on the Hod on the second of October to look at it, because Ward had asked for her by name.

She was on the beach in the afternoon when the Cradle stood dripping in the bay behind her, sitting on an upturned dory with her stick across her knees and the winch's ratchet in her lap, out of its housing, in pieces on a cloth; and she was not looking at the Cradle. She had looked at it once, when it rose, and then gone back to the ratchet, because the ratchet was hers and the Cradle was not.

"It's the pawl spring," she said, when Emeric came to stand over her. "It's too weak. When the drum's loaded it holds, and when the line goes slack for a breath, when the hull swings in, the pawl lifts and the spring can't get it back down before the load comes on again. So it skips." She held up a small coiled spring between two fingers. "This is a spring for a gate latch. Somebody took it out of a drawer."

"Can you fix it?"

Tess gave him a look of such pure scorn that the knight who had been dragged into the bay, who was sitting on the sand nearby with a blanket round his shoulders, laughed out loud.



The K Amara lifted for the first time on the fifth of October, in the long shed at the airship yard, and listed to port.

She was the biggest thing Kaldo had ever built that moved. Four hundred and twenty feet of her, ninety in the beam, all of it black welded steel, the welders' black, left unpainted, as the yard had always said the first armed hull would be: a long black armoured hull with a knife of a prow and, at her stern, a domed after-castle with a glazed observatory on its crown and a tower going up out of it with a mast and rigging, and between them her long open middle deck, the market deck, with its flat aprons fore and aft; and round the after-castle, at the level of its lowest windows, a running track, because Emeric had drawn it there in the year he first drew her and nobody had ever had the heart to take it out. She had lain in her cradle in the long shed since the spring of the year before. Her lift stones had been coded and seated in her spine by the six and Tess in the delve, a station at a time, thirty-two stations, over eight months, while Emeric was building everything else; and every one of them had been tested on the bench, and every one of them was sound.

On the fifth they lifted her a foot off her cradle in the long shed, with the doors shut, and she went up level, and at a foot she began to roll.

It was slow. It was so slow that for a breath nobody on the shed floor believed it: a long, easy lean to port, like a ship heeling to a breeze, the whole black length of her going over by degrees until her port aprons were a yard lower than her starboard and the men on her deck were holding on to the rail. Fletcher's voice came down off her tower, very calm, and the lift stones took her down again, slowly, onto her cradle, and she settled with a groan of steel on oak that went right through the shed and up into the roof, and lay there.

Two stations disagreed. Everybody could see that much. The port side wanted to sit lower than the starboard by a thing that, at a foot, was nothing, and at a hundred feet would be a ship lying on her side.

"Rebond them," said Bram.

They rebonded them. It took four days. The six and Tess went into every station on her port side again, one at a time, in the delve, and checked every line against the bench test and found every line sound; and wrote each one again from the beginning anyway, because Bram said so; and on the ninth of October she was lifted again.



She had her crew aboard for the second lift. That was Rylan's doing.

"If she's going over, I want them on her when she does," he said, in the shed, the evening before. "Once. In the shed. With the doors shut and Fletcher on the helm and the floor six feet down. They'll learn more off one roll at a foot than off a month of me shouting." He flexed the burned arm. "And the boots want trying."

The mag boots had come from Tess's line the week before, three hundred pairs, in a row of crates down the side of the shed. They looked like nothing much. Work boots with thick soles and a steel plate on the heel, and a small brass lever at each ankle; and in the sole of each, where nobody could see it, a magnet that held with no power at all, and a little cell in the heel to cancel it, and a switch under the heel that was the thing Tess had cared about. The heel came up, the switch closed, a pulse of current went through the coil and the magnet let go of the deck. The heel came down, the switch opened, and the magnet held again. So one foot was always held, and a man could walk on a steel deck in a roll as if it were a floor, one locked step after another, like a climber on a wall.

"If the cell dies," Tess had said, at the long table, when Ferra asked, "the foot stays stuck. Not free. Stuck. You pull the lever." She had been very firm about that. "Stuck is better. You can't fall off a ship stuck."

That was the boots. Under them, in the deck, were the deck hold's stones, anchored under the plating between the frames, pulling every man aboard down onto the steel through any turn the ship might make; and on every man's body was his trim harness, with its belt stone in the middle of his back, set to carry part of his weight in the straps, so that the deck hold's pull was a firm hand on the shoulders and not a weight, and a man could walk and run in it. And on every harness was a clip on a lanyard; and along every rail on the ship was a line, the jackline, running bow to stern; and the rule, Rylan's rule, the only one on the ship he would hit a man for breaking, was that nobody stood on a deck that might move without his clip on the line.

"The boots hold your feet," Rylan said, to the whole crew, on the shed floor, in the morning, standing on a crate. "The line holds the rest of you. The hold's magic and the harness is magic and they'll both go dead the minute anybody pops a stone near this ship, and when they do, the boots'll still hold and the line'll still hold, because the boots are iron and the line's rope." He looked at them. "Clip on. Every time. Before you put your foot on the deck. Before you breathe. Clip on."

Three hundred men and women in boots and harness clipped on, and went up the gangways onto the K Amara, and spread along her deck to their stations, and the gangways came away.

Tolliver did not clip on.

He was a deckhand, nineteen, a port boy off the lighters who had been on the K Amara's rolls since August and had never been on a deck higher than a lighter's, and he was so taken up with the boots, walking up and down by the forward rail feeling the heel switch click and the magnet catch with every step, that he walked the whole length of the market deck to his station and stood at it with his clip hanging from his harness at his hip like a dropped glove.

Rylan saw. Rylan saw everything on a deck; he had been seeing everything on Kaldo's decks for ten years. He did not say anything. He walked down the deck in his own boots, click, catch, click, catch, to where Tolliver stood, and stopped beside him, and looked at the clip hanging at Tolliver's hip.

Fletcher had taken her up off the cradle by a yard, as Rylan had asked, and was holding her there to let the crew feel her hang before she went any higher. Rylan looked up at the tower and lifted one hand, and Fletcher, on the helm, saw the hand and did what he had been asked to do in the shed the night before if Rylan ever lifted it; and the deck under Tolliver's feet tilted, a few degrees, gently, to starboard, for a slow count of three, and came level again.

Tolliver's boots held. The rest of him went over sideways from the knees like a tree, with his arms going round, and fetched up against the rail with a thump, and hung there over it from the waist with his feet still locked to the deck behind him, staring down at the shed floor a long way below.

Rylan bent down beside him and picked up the clip and clipped it to the jackline.

"Before you breathe," he said, pleasantly, and walked back up the deck to his own station; and nobody on the K Amara, for the rest of that day, or the rest of that year, was ever seen on a deck without his clip on the line.

Then they lifted her the rest of the way.

She went up, level. Two yards. Three. Four, with the shed floor dropping away under her and three hundred people on her deck holding very still. And at four feet she began, slowly, as before, to roll to port.

The boots held. The line held. The deck hold pulled every soul aboard down onto the steel and the harnesses took their weight, and nobody fell, and nobody slid, and nobody came off a rail; Rylan would say afterward that it was the best thing he had ever seen a crew do, and that he had not seen it because he was looking at the shed floor. The K Amara rolled. She went over to port, slow and steady and long, and Fletcher took her down again; and she settled onto her cradle with the same long groan of steel on oak, and lay there, listing very slightly even on the cradle, as if she had an opinion.

Emeric was on the shed floor by the forward cradle. He had his sense in her, as he had had it in her the first time, in all thirty-two stations at once, and he felt the roll begin; and this time, because he was listening for it, he felt where it came from.

It was not a fault. He felt that straight away. Every station did what it had been told. It was that two of them, aft on the port side, had been told something slightly different about where level was than all the rest. Not wrong. Different. As if the person who wrote them had stood in a different place to look.

And on the second of October, in the bay at South Sentinel, a slab of stone six hundred feet long had come up out of the sea level the first time, because one hand had written every line in it.

He knew. He knew the way he knew the right size of a beam from across a room. He turned round on the shed floor and walked out of the shed.

He walked out of the side door into the yard, past the stacks of plate, and stood in the yard with his hands in his pockets and his back to the shed, looking at the river, and did not go back in. He could feel them all in there. Bram's note, slow and sore. Fletcher coming down the tower ladder. The crew unclipping, carefully, one at a time, Rylan's voice going down the deck. And Tess, by the trim table at the shed's after end, where the stations' readings came down to a board of needles on wires, in her chair, with her bench book open on her knees and her stick across the arms, not moving.

After a while he felt her chair come out of the side door behind him and stop.

"You've got the face," said Tess.

He did not turn round.

"The one you get," she said, "when you know a thing and you've decided not to say it." She rolled the chair forward until she was beside him, and looked at the river too. "You walked out so you couldn't."

"Yes."

"That's stupid," said Tess. "It's also right." She opened the bench book on her knees. "Do you want to know what it is, or do you know?"

"I think I know."

"Then you can tell me if I'm right." She did not wait for him to say yes. She turned the book toward him, and it was a page of figures, the needles' readings at every station, both lifts, written down in her square hand as the board gave them; and down the side of the page, against stations nineteen and twenty, port, aft, she had drawn a bracket, and written beside it, two hands.

"It's not the stones," she said. "Every stone does what it's told. It's what they were told level was. Thirty of them were written by the rest of us. Nineteen and twenty, port, aft, were written by Bram." She ran her finger along a row. "Bram wrote his in the shed, on the cradle, in March, against the spirit level on the cradle beam. Because that's what Bram does. He puts a level on a thing. And the cradle's level." She looked up. "The shed floor isn't."

"It falls to the river."

"One in two hundred. For the drains. He built it like that. Fletcher told him to." Tess shut the book. "So Bram's level is the shed floor's level, near enough, because the cradle sits on the floor, and he trued the cradle to the floor and not to the sky. And everybody else wrote level to her keel, in the delve, off the drawings, because that's what you do in the delve, you don't look at a spirit level, you look at her. And her keel was laid true." She put her hands flat on the book. "So two stations think she's level when she's leaning one in two hundred to port. And at a foot that's nothing and at four it's a roll. And at a hundred feet she'd be on her ear." She looked at him. "Is that it?"

He looked at her for a long moment.

"I didn't know it was Bram," he said. "Or the floor. I knew it was two hands."

Tess considered this.

"Good," she said. "Then I found it." She turned her chair round. "I'm going to go and tell Bram. He'll want to hit something. You stay out here till he's done."



The Hod three went up her gangway on the twelfth, with their kit, to her aprons.

They had flown on the Hod for three years. Arthur, Mira and Bren: Ward's three, the ones he had given to Ned Tallis because the Hod needed hands and they needed the air, and who had gone up the Hod's ladder as young knights and come down it, over three years, as something Ward did not yet have a word for and would not use one for until he did. They had been on every Band run since. They had been on the Isle in the year of the camp. They had been on the river road in July, in black wool suits with nothing on their faces, and nobody on the far bank had seen them come or go.

Now the K Amara wanted apron knights, and Ward had given her the best he had.

Ned Tallis saw them off at the foot of the gangway. He was Ned, so there was nothing sentimental in it. He shook each of them by the hand, hard, and told Bren that if he ever heard of him breaking anything on that black barge's aprons that belonged to the Hod he would come aboard and break it back; and then he turned round to the Hod's crew book, which Noll Finch was holding open for him on the gangway's newel post, and took the pen.

Noll Finch was nineteen. He had been fifteen at his Commencement, the youngest of the forty that year and the smallest, a thin boy with arms like a lath and a big quiet head, who had come to the bottom of every sheet Ward ever posted and stood in front of it with his hands in his pockets while the others laughed; and who had kept coming to the yard, every morning, at the bottom of every sheet, for four years, without once being any good, until he was. He was still thin. He would always be thin. He had signed for the Hod in August, as a hand, under Ned, because Ward had said he might and nobody else had wanted the place, and the place had been the only one there was.

Ned wrote him in. Noll Finch, knight, the Hod, and the date. Then he handed the pen to Noll, and Noll signed under it, in a careful square hand, and looked at his own name in the Hod's book under the three that had been in it before him; and Mira, from halfway up the K Amara's gangway, looked back down at him, and said, "Bottom of the sheet," in the voice she had used to the boy who had laughed at him four years ago on the well step.

Noll went red. Then he grinned, enormously, all at once, the way a boy grins who has been trying not to for three days.

"Top of the book," he said.



Imani scanned the apron knights that afternoon, on the K Amara's forward apron, for the mark.

The aprons and the market deck were not iron-skinned. The crew spaces were; she had seen to that herself, and set the cabin mark on the K Amara's brass bypass for the weakest aboard, as she set it on every ship. But the aprons were open to the sky, and the knights who flew off them would be in the Band for hours at a time in their suits, breathing what the Band gave them; and Imani wanted every one of their numbers in her book before they did. So she sat on a crate on the apron with the scanner in its case at her feet and the hollow sense open, and the hundred apron knights came to her in a line, one by one, and held out an arm, and she read it, slowly, the way she read everything now, and wrote it down; and Bettany stood beside her with the card box.

Rylan came to sit beside her at four, when the line was nearly done, with a mug of tea for each of them, and watched.

The tracery came up under her hand on the old knights the way it came up under the scanner's: the silver lines in the skin of the forearm, branching up from the wrist, where the rite had cracked them and they had held. She wrote it down. She had been writing it down for a year, every knight in the garrison, three views, and since August she had had a reason for it, and had not yet said it to anybody but Ward.

"Kal never had a son," said Rylan.

Imani looked up.

"The old baron. The first one. Kal." Rylan blew on his tea. "The old men in the port always said it was the rite did it. That he'd been a knight, and a good one, the best they had, and he took the land instead of a captaincy, and then he couldn't get a son on anybody, and he left the whole barony to his steward's people. Wexler's lot." He drank. "They said the rite takes it out of you. That's what my father said. That's why there's so few knights' sons."

"Nobody knows that," said Imani.

"No."

"Nobody's ever counted it. Not the Church. Not the tower. It's a thing old men say in the port." She looked at the next arm in the line, a woman of the apron knights with her sleeve rolled, and read it, and wrote. "Some knights have no children. Some have. Some men who aren't knights have no children either. Until somebody writes all of them down, it's a story."

"You'll write all of them down."

"Somebody will."

Rylan nodded, as if that settled it. Then he grinned at the apron, at nothing.

"I've two," he said.

"I know you've two."

"After the rite. Both of them. Finn and Wren." He held out his own arm, the good one, the one that was not shiny, with its sleeve pushed up and the old silver in the skin of it. "So it's a story, then."

Imani read his arm, though she had read it a dozen times before, and wrote it down.

"It's a story you're in," she said. "Drink your tea."



He went down to the secret dock that night, late, because Tess was there.

The secret dock lay at the far end of the lake behind its wall, where nobody went who had not sworn the ships' oath and nobody could see over: the slip the K Discovery had gone off in the dark, and the stone shed, and the condensing plant in its brick house the size of a barn against the wall, with its chimney and its hum. The lamps were lit along the hard. The water beyond them was black, and the reeds blacker.

The new thing was not a ship. That was what anybody seeing it would have noticed first, and what had made Fletcher, the first time he came down to it, stand on the slip for some time with his hands in his pockets. It was the column from the K Amara, the second one, the better one, the one Bram had built in the winter with the new valve seat and the packing that didn't weep and the double-walled steel tank, and had rubbed NOT YET off the lid of its box with three strokes of his sleeve; and it was standing upright on the hard with its tank hung beneath it in a pit dug into the ground and lined with brick, and round it, at the height of a man's head above the ground, there was an iron platform. A deep one, a hull with a flat top, sealed, built on the hard round the column out of plate the yard had rolled for the K Amara and had to spare. There was no ship round it. There were no masts, no stones in a spine, no helm, nothing at all to say what it was or where it would go; only the column going up through the middle of it like the trunk of a tree through a raft, and the beginnings of a house on the platform's top, a frame of steel ribs, half plated. The house would hold eighteen or twenty people when it was done. The column would make liquid mana out of the air four miles up, from the Band itself, and not on the ground out of the Band's leavings.

Ferra had been carrying it in her tally since the spring, as the column, second; and in the column beside it, where she wrote what a thing was for, she had written once the Well, and it had been the Well ever since.

Tess was in the pit under the platform, by the tank, in her chair, with a lamp.

She had a band engine in pieces on the floor round her: one of Bram's, the kind he had built for the works' pumps the winter before, two iron weights in two greased sleeves with a gravity stone seated in a cup in each, and a cast ring with a wave cut in its face that the weights rode, one falling as the other rose, so that the ring turned and turned a shaft; and the whole of it in a riveted iron box that was filled, when it was running, with Band air, and burned liquid mana, and turned as steadily as a stove burns coal. Up there, Tess said, the box would fill itself. Up there the fuel was what the station made. The engines would drive the compressors and the compressors would make the fuel and the fuel would drive the engines, and the plant would run as long as the Band was the Band, which was a long time.

"And when it stops?" Emeric had asked her, at the long table in September.

"It won't stop."

"When it stops."

Tess had looked at him, and then at Bram, who had said, before she could, the thing he said about every band engine he had ever built: that a band engine in a dead zone isn't dead, it's shut, and you can't trust a shut thing to keep anybody breathing. So the air and the heat and the scrubber and the radio and the tank's cooling, the things that kept people alive four miles up, ran on a diesel set and a room of batteries in the platform's hull, and never on the band engines at all. If the plant stopped, the station lost its income. It did not lose its air.

"What must not stop," Tess said now, from the pit, without looking up, "goes on what cannot stop." She had a spanner in one hand and the engine's deciding stone in the other. "I'm putting that on a plate on the wall up there. In brass. So they read it every morning."

"Bram said it first."

"Bram can have his name under it." She set the stone into its cup. "I need a second one of these. A decider. Each engine needs its own. Ferra's going to make a face."

"Ferra's already made it. She put two in the stone book in September."

Tess looked up at him at last. In the lamp her face was tired and pleased, the way it was at the end of a day when something had worked; and he saw, on the floor beside the chair, the other thing she had brought down with her, in a padded box with its lid open, that she had not been working on and was keeping near her anyway.

The stone from the cove. Clear as water, faint blue at the thick edge, the size of a goose's egg, asleep.

"I started it last night," said Tess. "While you were up the shed with her lift stones." She did not look at it. "The register. Every soul who'll ever live up there, so the shell knows them, and lets them through. And the lattice. And the pin." She turned back to the engine. "Six weeks. Perhaps five. I can hold it now. I couldn't have, before."

Before her stone, she meant. Before April, and the dome, and the weeks on the straw by the tree. She could go into a stone now and stay in it for as long as Armand could, and come out of it with her head clear; and the station's stone would have one hand's writing on it from end to end, her hand, and no other.

He thought about saying so. He thought about the Cradle hanging in the bay, level, and sixteen stones that agreed about where level was because one person had written all of them. He did not say it. She would work it out for herself, and she would rather.

"Do you want tea?" he said instead.

"You always make tea," said Tess. "Every time something happens, you make tea."

"Do you want it?"

"Yes," said Tess, and bent over the engine again with the lamp.
