Chapter 36 - Inside the Walls

They walked it for three days and Ward ran the survey.

That had been settled in about nine words on the first morning, in the yard, before they crossed. Emeric had started to lay out how he wanted to do it and had got two sentences in and then heard himself, and stopped, and put it a different way.

"You have done this. I have read about it."

"Yes," Ward said.

"Then you run it and I carry the slate."

Osric, behind them, did not say anything, and Declan did not say anything, and both of them heard it, and by the second day the difference in how the four of them moved around him was measurable. Emeric noticed and did not comment. There is nothing a man can add to a thing like that except damage.

The island was excellent. That was the first finding and it took about an hour to confirm.

Water on every side, and not a ford in any of it. Walls sitting on rock rather than on made ground, which meant no mining and no ram worth the trouble of bringing. One crossing, the Left Bridge, thirty feet wide at the neck with the Western Gate at the far end of it, which is a killing floor with a door on it. A keep with sightlines down both branches and along the whole west bank.

Ward walked the wall head twice and gave his assessment leaning on the parapet with his arms folded.

"Nobody takes this by storming it. Not with two hundred. Not with six hundred."

"Good."

"It is not good."

Emeric waited.

"If nobody can take it by storming it," Ward said, "then nobody is going to try. You have not built a problem for them. You have chosen for them."

On the third day, on the west bank, in the middle of the second division with the ground still hard, Ward stopped walking and asked him the question instead of answering it.

"If you were Renner. What do you do."

"You want me to say it."

"I want you to find it."

So Emeric stood in a field in the cold and did it properly, which meant not reaching for the answer he wanted.

He would not assault the bridge. Only an idiot assaults a thirty-foot neck with a gate at the end, and whatever else Renner was, eleven years of skimming a vein and spending it on men was not the career of an idiot.

He would come down the river road, because it was the only road, and he would stop on the west bank where the ground was open and level and the water was easy to get at.

And then he would sit down. And then he would eat.

Emeric turned round on the spot and looked at what was standing behind him.

The nine divisions. The drying shed. The silo. The pump-house, and the tower on the western rise, which was the highest thing west of the water and visible from four miles up the road. The mill. The foundry, across the branch on the outskirts, brick and three furnaces and a pattern shop and everything in the barony that mattered.

Every one of them outside the walls. Every one of them sited by him, personally, with reasons he could still recite, and every reason had been a good one.

The tower went on the rise because water runs downhill. The pump-house went on the arm because that is where the current is. The foundry went across the branch because a foundry catches fire and you do not put three furnaces inside a wall with eighteen hundred people behind it. The silo went next to the field because grain is heavy and you do not cart it over a bridge twice.

Not one of those decisions had been wrong. Every single one of them had been the correct decision for a barony at peace, made by a man building a place to live in, and he had made all of them in the first two years while he was too busy keeping people alive to think about anything else, and he would make most of them again.

And the result was that an army sitting on the west bank would be sitting on Kaldo's grain, Kaldo's water, Kaldo's mill and Kaldo's iron, in a field that fed them, with a roof over the ones who wanted one.

"A besieger with our own stores under him is not besieging," he said.

Ward waited.

"He is being catered."

"Yes."

It landed the way a thing lands when nobody else did it to you. There was no enemy in it. There was no bad luck in it. Three years of competent work, arranged by him, had built the most comfortable siege camp in the southern half of the kingdom and put a tower on the highest ground to mark it.

The arithmetic took ten minutes and nobody enjoyed any of it.

A siege living off Kaldo's own harvest does not run for six weeks. It runs until the harvest runs out, which with the surplus they had just brought in would be well past the winter. Call it two hundred men, comfortable, fed out of a field they did not plant.

Against forty-four in harness and a town.

And nothing coming. That was the part with no answer in it. A siege is broken by somebody arriving, and there was no somebody. The Crown had exiled him here and would not cross the kingdom for him and both he and his father knew it. There was no ally. There was not one man outside those walls who would have crossed a room for him, which he had noticed as a draught in the autumn and which was now a number on a slate.

So the war could not be allowed to become a siege.

That decision was made in a cold field in early spring, weeks before a single man was on a road, and everything that happened afterward came out of it.

The hauling took eleven days and used every cart in the barony.

The garrison worked it, the Works crews worked it, the venture men worked it, and for eleven days the Left Bridge had a queue on it from first light to dark, one way loaded and one way empty, with two of Ferra's clerks at the gate counting everything that went through.

Forty men in plate spent eleven days lifting sacks. Osric complained about it continuously and at length and worked harder than anybody, which was the shape of Osric.

And when it was inside, Emeric did not stack it in one place.

He had written the rule eighteen months earlier, in a book, on a wet afternoon, and it had nothing to do with war. Many small sealed bins rather than one large one. Limed, damp-sealed, walked with a rod before sealing. Distributed, because the single worst thing that can happen to a barony's grain is that all of it is in the same building when the weevils get into it, or the damp does, or a spark does.

He had written it for weevils. He used it now for two hundred men and it fitted without a single alteration, and he noted that in the margin because it was the only interesting thing about a dull fortnight.

Cellars, a dozen of them, under houses whose owners agreed and were paid. The keep undercroft. Six buildings that did not look remotely like granaries, including the back of the soap shop and the old customs shed by the port. And the parish crypt, which Doran offered before anyone asked him for it, walking up on the fourth morning and saying it was dry and it was cold and it was consecrated ground so nobody would break into it, and then going back to work without waiting to be thanked.

Nobody outside the barony knew where any of it went. Most people inside the barony knew where one of them was, because they had carried sacks into it and then gone home. There were four people who could have named them all and Emeric had thought about that number carefully and had decided four was right.

The old granary was left standing and left empty.

Swept, shut, locked, the yard around it kept tidy. It was the largest building outside the walls after the foundry and it had been the barony's grain store for ninety years and every man who had ever worked the west bank knew what it was for.

Ferra asked whether he wanted it pulled down for the timber and he said no, and she asked why, and he said that an empty building that looks full is worth considerably more than a pile of salvage, and that he could not be seen to be hiding anything, and both of those were true and neither of them was the reason.

He did not say the reason. She did not ask again.

The crop stayed in the ground, and he told them himself.

He could have sent Farris. Farris would have done it well and the venture men would have taken it from him, and Emeric went instead, into the long room over the threshing floor with forty-odd men in it who had put their own seed into that arrangement, and stood up and gave them the hard version.

The crop could not be cut early. There was no version of cutting it early that produced anything worth having. Green grain is not food and will not store and will not sow.

So it stays where it is. And if the coalition comes before harvest, it burns, and there is nothing anyone in this room can do to prevent that, and there will be no pretending afterward that anybody could have.

Then he priced it in front of them, because they had a right to the number.

The seed for next spring was safe, sealed, inside the walls, all of it. The winter reserve was safe. The town would eat, every hearth, at the full ration, through the winter and into the spring.

And there would be no surplus for a year. Not a small surplus. None. The soap trade was already stopping and the field was the only other thing the barony had, and a year with no surplus and no soap is a year where the money that comes in is the money that was already in the cellar.

"That is not starvation," he said. "I want that understood, because it is the one thing I can promise. Nobody in this barony is going to be hungry."

He let that sit for a second.

"It is also not recovery. It is a year where we stand still."

Nobody was reassured. Nobody had come to be reassured.

Hollis stood up near the back with his hat in his hands.

"The child wage," he said.

The room went quiet in a different way.

The barony had been paying it for two years. Every household with a child under eleven, a small sum every quarter against the child not being put to work, and it had been Ferra's design and Emeric's insistence and it had cost more than either of them had expected and it had taken a year for anybody to believe it was real.

And it came out of the surplus. Every quarter of it came out of the surplus, and Hollis had eleven acres and no schooling and had worked out in about four seconds what a year with no surplus meant for a fund that came out of the surplus.

"The wage holds," Emeric said.

"Out of what."

"Out of the reserve, if it has to be. Out of the foundry. I will find it. The school waits."

There had been a school promised for two years. It had been promised at the hall, in front of people, more than once, and everyone in that room knew it and a good third of them had children who had been counting on it.

"The school waits," Emeric said again. "I am not going to soften that."

Hollis thought about it, standing up, with the whole room watching him.

"I'll hold you to the wage," he said.

"Do."

And he did, eleven months later, in a much worse room, and Emeric paid it.

The rest of the work was unglamorous and correct and took three weeks.

The foundry could not be moved, and that was the end of that conversation. He hardened what could be hardened, which was not much: a ditch, a bank, shutters on the pattern shop, the good stock moved into the town. He planned denial for the rest, which meant a written order about what got broken and in what order and by whom if it came to it, and he wrote it out and gave a copy to Bram and watched Bram read it and neither of them said anything at all afterward.

And he accepted, on paper, in the book, that if the foundry fell he lost the thing everything else depended on. He made himself write that sentence.

Water inside the walls got checked twice and then checked again, because Imani came and stood in his doorway and was fierce about it.

The Mere sits above the town. Everything that goes into it comes past the town on its way down, and everything the town drinks comes out of it. Two hundred men camped on the west bank was two hundred men upstream of eighteen hundred people, and she had buried nine hundred already and she was not going to do it again over something anybody could have thought about in advance.

"I am not asking you to be clever," she said. "I am asking you to be suspicious. Every well inside the wall. Every cistern. And a man on the intake who is not a boy."

"Done."

"And when I tell you to stop drinking from something, you will not ask me for a reason first."

"Done."

She set up for wounded in the room behind the church and then told him flatly what she had and what she did not, and the list of what she did not was short in the particular way that means people are going to die of ordinary things.

Not enough linen. No poppy at all, none in the barony, none available anywhere she could reach. Two pairs of hands including her own, and four women she had trained herself who were good and were not enough. She gave it without drama and without asking him to fix it, which was how he knew she had already decided none of it could be fixed in the time available.

Doran opened the parish hall as a second space and said almost nothing about it. He moved the benches himself over two days with a boy helping him, and when Emeric came to see it he found it swept, laid out in rows, with water at both ends and a bucket of sand by the door, and Doran already gone.

The bridge was the one thing that did not get settled.

Ward wanted it prepared for denial, which meant cut the timbers most of the way through, stack the fuel, and have four men whose only job on the day was to stand there and wait to be told.

Emeric agreed.

And then found himself out on it at odd hours, twice, three times, looking at the water going under it, because a bridge that nobody can cross is a siege by another name. Drop it and the town is safe and the town is also sealed, with everything it owns on the wrong side of the water and two hundred men living in it.

He did not resolve it. He left it prepared and unresolved and he was aware that leaving a thing unresolved is itself a decision, and he wrote that down too and did not like the look of it on the page.

Petram asked for a weapon at the end of a work party.

Not in a scene arranged for it. It was the ninth day of the hauling, at the gate, in the dark, with the carts going back out empty and men sitting on the kerb too tired to stand up. Petram had been carrying sacks for nine days on a commuted work detail with a guard who had stopped bothering to watch him around the fourth day.

"When it comes," he said, "give me something."

That was all of it. He did not argue a case. He did not offer a reason.

"No," Emeric said.

And then gave him the real one, because the kind one would have been an insult to both of them.

"Not because of what I think. I think you would stand. I have thought that for about a year." He was too tired to make it sound like anything. "You put poison in my cup. Every man in this town knows it. In four weeks this town is going to be frightened, and a frightened town with an armed poisoner in it does one thing, and it does it fast, and it does not do it to me. It does it to you, and then it does it to somebody else, and then I have a second problem behind my own wall while there are two hundred men on the bank."

Petram listened to all of it.

"Aye," he said.

He did not ask again.

He worked the ward through the whole of it, carrying, fetching, holding men down for Imani, and he was still under sentence the entire time and the sentence was never commuted and he never once asked for it to be.

By the end of the month every store in the barony was on the island, in bins nobody could name all of, under a town that did not yet know it was going to be looked at.

And the crop that paid for the next year stood green in the fields on the west bank, on both sides of the river road, exactly where anybody coming down it would have to walk through it.
