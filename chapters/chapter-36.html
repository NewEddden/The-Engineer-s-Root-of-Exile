Chapter 36 - Rotten Eggs

The first thing the hill needed was wood, and the wood was in the baron's woods, and the baron's woods had been fined for as long as anybody in the hamlet had been alive.

Emeric lifted the fine on the second morning, standing in the street with the old miner and half the hamlet listening: for the workings, for fire on the face and timber for the roofs, and for nothing else. He said it twice, as he had said the ash price twice in the lower market, because a thing said once to a crowd was a rumor by the next day. He would have Hale write it out in the barony's form when he got home. Hale would call it a waiver and not a precedent.

The old miner heard him out and then asked who would pay for the wood if the next baron put the fine back.

"Then it will be in writing," Emeric said.

"There's always a next baron," said the old miner, and went to find his axe.

They chose the nearest tunnel, the one Emeric had waded into, because its seam ran level for a few yards before it turned down and its water could be bailed. By noon there was a stack of cordwood built against the face as high as a man's chest, split small and packed tight, with kindling under it and the tunnel mouth cleared so the smoke could get out. The old miner lit it himself. Then everybody came out and sat on the spoil heap in the sun and waited.

It burned all afternoon. The smoke rolled out of the tunnel mouth thick and grey and then thinner, and the men sat and ate and argued about whose turn it was to fetch water from the stream, and nobody was in any hurry. Toward evening, when the fire had burned down to a bed of coals and the face behind it was too hot to stand near, they went in with buckets in a line and threw cold stream water on the rock all at once.

The face cracked with a noise like a roof beam going. Emeric had not been ready for how loud it would be. Steam came boiling out of the mouth, and behind it a smell of wet ash and hot stone and, faintly, rotten eggs, and when it cleared the men went in with bars and wedges and began to lever off what the fire had loosened.

A hand's depth, across the face. Perhaps a little more. A day's wood and a day's waiting for a hand's depth of rock.

"That's a good one," the old miner said, with real satisfaction, and Emeric did not doubt that it was.



On the second day he asked to light it.

They let him, the way men let a guest carve. He lit the kindling from a brand off the cooking fire like anybody else, and stood back while the stack caught, and then he did not stand back any further. He reached into the fire with his mind the way he had reached into the laundry hearth in October, and took hold of the flame, and leaned on it.

It was not a large fire to hold. It was a stubborn one. Left alone, a fire in a tunnel went where fires went, up, and most of its heat went up with the smoke into the roof and out of the mouth. He would not let it. He flattened it and drove it forward against the face in a sheet, hard, and kept it there, on one patch of rock about the size of a door, and did not let it drift.

The fire glyph stood in the air in front of his hand, faint in the smoke. Behind him the men had stopped talking.

He held it for most of an hour. By the end the sweat was running into his eyes and there was a pressure behind them like a thumb, and he let the flame go back to being a fire and sat down on the spoil heap rather suddenly. The quench, when they threw it, cracked twice instead of once, and the men came out of the steam with their bars and stood looking at the face.

Two hands' depth. Nearer three in the middle, where he had held it hardest.

Nobody said anything about it. The old miner looked at the face, and then at Emeric sitting on the heap with his head in his hands, and then went in and started levering off rock.

On the third morning Emeric came up the hill from the hamlet and found the stack built and the kindling laid and the whole crew sitting on the spoil heap with the hamlet's fire-pot smoking beside them. Nobody had put it to the kindling. They were waiting for him, and nobody had told them to, and he stood at the bottom of the heap and looked at the unlit stack and did not like it as much as he would have expected to.

He lit it anyway. He held it for an hour, and got his three hands' depth, and that night he lay awake in the hamlet's spare house with a headache.



The water was the other thing.

Once the face was moving, the seam turned down within a week, as every seam on that hill turned down, and the tunnel floor began to fill. The men bailed it with buckets passed hand to hand, and lost ground every hour they bailed.

The hamlet had a carpenter, the man who had timbered the workings when there were workings to timber. He listened to what Emeric wanted with his arms folded, and asked three questions, all of them good, and then built it in five days out of a split larch trunk, two bored logs, a beam and some leather off a dead goat. Two treadles under a beam, a man on each, rocking it like a seesaw. The beam worked two wooden plungers in the bored logs, one going down while the other came up, and leather flaps inside the logs let the water past one way and not the other. A pipe of hollowed poles ran from the logs up the tunnel and out of the mouth onto the hillside.

It worked. Two men treading could lift the water about as fast as the hill let it in, in April, if they did not stop. When they stopped to change over, the water gained. When they stopped to eat, it gained more. The carpenter stood at the tunnel mouth watching the pipe spit and gurgle onto the heather and said that in November it would be under water to the roof, pump or no pump, and he was very likely right.

It kept the lower working dry enough to dig, just. Emeric looked at the two men on the treadles, red in the face and rocking, and thought about a thing that would tread all night without eating and not mind the dark, and put the thought away until there was iron to build it from.

On the last day, going along the heaps with Bram, he found the other ores.

The miners had thrown them away. They were in the spoil with everything else that was not iron: a dark brown stuff, glassy and heavy, that broke in shining faces like burnt sugar, and here and there small grey cubes, bright as new pewter and far heavier than they looked. The old miner had no name for the brown. The grey he called lead, and said his grandfather had sold some of it to the church for the roof, and that the brown had never gone anywhere, because nobody wanted it.

Emeric filled both his coat pockets with it.



Bram smelted the hill ore the week they got home.

He roasted it first, on an open heap behind the forge, for two days, and the whole lane complained, and the smell got into the bread at the baker's on the corner. Then he took it down in the little bloomery hearth he used for scrap, with Emeric watching and Tess on the bellows, and pulled out a bloom of iron the size of a loaf, spongy and grey, and beat it into a bar.

The bar looked sound. Bram heated it again and began to draw it out, and on the third blow at a good red heat it cracked across, ragged, along half its width.

He did not say anything. He took another heat, and tried the other end, and it cracked again, at the same color.

"Told you," he said.

"Sulfur," Emeric said. "It makes the iron crumble when it's worked hot. Cold, it's sound enough. Hot, it falls apart along the grain. There's a word for it."

"There's a word for it in Kaldo too." Bram dropped the bar in the slack tub and it hissed. "Rubbish. My father called it rubbish. That's why nobody bought it twice." He looked at the cracked bar under the water with an expression of deep, private vindication. "You'll make pots of it. Grates. Weights for a scale. You'll not make a plow of it, nor a nail, nor anything that wants a hammer on it hot, and every plow and nail in this barony still comes down that river from Grayrock at a fifth over."

He had known it since the day Bram first said rotten eggs. He had hoped otherwise anyway, as far as the bloomery, and the hope had gone into the slack tub with the bar.

"So what was the hill for?" Bram said.

Emeric took a lump of the roasted ore off the heap, the one the whole lane hated, and turned it over. Bright patches in it, gone dull in the roasting. Rust. The glitter of the seam.

"The stink," he said.

Bram looked at him as though he had finally said the thing Bram had suspected him of being able to say all along.



The dyehouse stood on the lane above the shut cooperage, a street back from the canal ring, and it had been shut since the cloth trade died.

Hale found the key on the second day of looking. Inside it was one long stone room with a floor stained in streaks of every color cloth had ever been dyed in Kaldo, gone brown with time, and down both walls a row of great vats set into the floor, lead-lined, each with a bung at the bottom and a stone channel under the bung, and the channels running together into one drain that went out under the end wall toward the ring. The vats were dry. Some of them had birds' nests in them. The roof was mostly there.

A woman lived in the end house of the lane who had dyed in it since she was a girl, and Rylan brought her up because Emeric asked who had. She was small and bent and her hands were stained to the wrist with something that had never washed out, and she walked the length of the room, slowly, putting her hand on the rim of every vat, and stopped at the fourth one from the end.

"This was the black," she said.

Emeric showed her a lump of the green salt he had made on Bram's hearth the night before, from a bucket of spoil washed through with water and boiled down with scrap iron until green crystals came out on the sides of the pan as it cooled. It had taken him half the night and burned a hole in his sleeve.

She knew it at once.

"Copperas," she said. She took it from his hand and licked her finger and touched it and tasted it, and made the face of a woman meeting an old enemy. "Black for mourning. You put that with oak galls and the wool goes black as a priest. We bought it by the cask." She gave it back. "Off the hill, when I was a girl. Then off the river."

It came off the hill again within a fortnight.

The spoil heaps had been lying in the rain for a hundred years doing half the work, the sulfur in them turning slowly to something that washed out, which was why the grass on them was sour and the goats would not eat it. The hamlet's carts brought it down by the load, along with the ore. In the dyehouse it went into the vats with water carried from the canal ring, and stood, and the liquor that ran out of the bungs came out the color of weak tea and tasted of ink. That liquor went into a lead pan, one of the dyers' own, over a fire with a heap of Bram's cracked hill-iron in it, the bars that would not forge, which was the best use anybody had found for them, and boiled down, and was ladled into the fourth vat from the end to cool. In the morning the sides of the vat were furred with green crystals the size of a fingernail, like ice on a window.

Tess grew them on strings.

Nobody had told her to. She hung lengths of cord into the cooling liquor from a stick laid across the vat, and in the morning each cord had grown a long lumpy green finger of crystal, and she lifted them out one at a time and laid them on a board and measured each one against her own hand, and wrote the measurements on the board in chalk.



The acid came last, and it came slowly.

He dried the green crystals over the dyehouse fire until they went white and crumbled, and packed the powder into a fat clay jar he bought from a potter in the market, sealed to a clay pipe with clay and a strip of wet linen, and the pipe ran down into a second jar standing in a bucket of cold water. Then he built a fire round the first jar and kept it at a red heat for a whole day, and nothing came, and the seal cracked, and he did it again.

The second time it came. White fumes first, choking, so that Tess and Bram both went out into the yard coughing. Then, late in the afternoon, a few drops in the bottom of the cold jar. Then more. By dark there was a finger's depth of something clear and oily in it that did not look like anything at all.

He lifted the jar out of the bucket, very carefully, with both hands, and set it on the bench, and showed Tess.

He let a single drop of it fall on the end of the bench. The wood went brown while she leaned in, and black while she watched.

"Never with your hands," he said. "Never in a cup that's had anything else in it. It goes into water, slowly. Never water into it, or it spits. If it touches you, water, a great deal of water, at once, and then go to Imani." He said it the way Abloh said the lye rules in the yard, in the same order every time, and made her say it back to him.

She said it back. Then she put her two hands round the jar, carefully, as he had, and lifted it an inch off the bench, and put it down again.

"It's heavy," she said.

"Nearly twice what water is."

Bram, in the doorway with a wet cloth over his nose and mouth, looked at the finger's depth of oil in the jar, and at the black place on the bench, and at the prince.

"Two days on a cart," he said through the cloth. "Two days of that stink in my lane, and now this. For a thumb of something that eats wood."

"Yes."

"What's it for?"

"A jar," Emeric said.

Bram waited for the rest of it, and when it did not come he took the cloth away from his face and went out, and they heard him say something to the lane about princes that the lane seemed to agree with.

Tess was looking at the drain in the dyehouse floor, the stone channel under the vats that ran out under the end wall toward the canal ring.

"Where does it go?" she said.

He had been about to tell her. He did not need to, and it pleased him more than anything in the jar.

"Into a pit of lime first," he said. "Then nowhere it can hurt anybody."
