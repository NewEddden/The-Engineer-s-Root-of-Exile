Chapter 36 - From Her Own Hand

The trouble arrived in February wearing the dullest possible clothes, which was a list of documents.

Vantic made his return to his house once a year, in the second week of February, and the return was not a report and not a letter. It was a schedule with a column for every figure his house was carrying and a column beside it saying where that figure had come from, and the second column had eleven permitted words in it and Vantic had explained all eleven in October and Emeric had not listened properly because it had been October and there had been a great deal else.

"The harvest," Vantic said. "Last year's. Four hundred acres cut in the last fortnight of July, delivered into the granary between the twenty-eighth of July and the eleventh of August, burned on the nineteenth of August. It appears three times in the security documents and once in the loss schedule and the figure is the same in all four places, which I have checked."

"Then what's the difficulty."

"The second column." Vantic turned the sheet round. "Every other line on this page says seen, or counted, or weighed by factor, or primary record inspected. That line says asserted."

Ferra put her pen down, which she did not do often.

"The tally box," she said.

"The tally box."

Emeric had to go and stand in the ash to remember it, and did, on a cold morning with the walls still up to shoulder height and the new frame going in over the top of them, because the second granary was being built on the bones of the first at Corliss's insistence and against Emeric's instinct and Corliss had been right about the footings.

The tally box had sat inside the door on a shelf at the height of a man's elbow. It was where the boy stood who called the load, because a boy calling a load has to be able to see the cart and reach the box without walking, and it held the day's sheets until the week was up and the week's sheets went to the castle in a satchel on a Sunday.

Except in harvest, when nobody walked anything to the castle on a Sunday, because in harvest every hand in Kaldo was in a field, and the sheets stayed in the box until the cutting was done and the counting could be done properly by somebody who was not asleep.

Fifteen days of intake. Two hundred and seventeen deliveries. Every household in the field, by name, by date, by cart, weighed on the beam and called out loud by a fourteen-year-old named Tam Hesk who had a good voice and could not read, with the mark of the man delivering it against every line.

It had been eleven feet from the door on a wooden shelf.

"Say what asserted costs me," Emeric said, back in the long room.

"In itself, nothing. It is not a doubt. It is a word that means the figure came out of the borrower's mouth." Vantic was entirely level about it. "What it costs you is next year. A house carrying an asserted figure at the head of a security takes a reserve against it, because it must, because eleven barons in ninety years have asserted a harvest and four of them were lying. The reserve is a fraction and I can tell you the fraction and you will not enjoy it, and it does not come off the debt. It comes off what my house believes it is holding, which is the number every term you ever ask me for is priced against."

"For how long."

"Until a figure with a primary record behind it replaces it, which is August, or until eleven years are up." He shrugged with one shoulder, which for Vantic was an outburst. "It is a bad year to be carrying it and it is the year you have."

"And there's no version where the mill's counts stand in."

"The mill counted what it ground, which was a third of it, months later, and by then some of it had been eaten and some of it had been the seed and some of it was in the hands of men who had walked here from Wayford. It is a good record of a different thing."

Ferra had been quiet for a while, which meant she had been working.

"I can produce them," she said.

Vantic looked at her.

"The sheets burned," he said.

"The sheets burned. I can produce them." She closed the book on her finger. "I wrote them. Not the calling, Tam called them, but the sheets came to me and I ruled every one of them and I have the totals, and I have the venture's part allocations which are in the field's own book at Dunnel's house and did not burn, and I have the mill, and I have the weighbeam man, who is alive and has an excellent memory for carts and none at all for names, and I have my own hand and I know what is in it." She said it without any drama, which was worse. "Give me four evenings."

"Alone?"

"No." Ferra did not look at Emeric. "Not alone."

What she did not say in front of the factor, and said to Emeric afterward on the stair, was that she had been at it since October.

"Since the week he arrived?"

"Since the week after the fire." She stopped on the landing. "I stood in that ash on the twentieth of August and I understood something that I have not enjoyed understanding, which is that I have kept the books of this barony for eleven years in one hand on one kind of paper in two buildings, and that a barony is a thing which burns. So I began keeping my own workings twice. Not the ledger, the ledger is the ledger. The workings underneath it. How I got to a total and out of what, written down as a thing anyone could walk back up."

"You never told me."

"It is not a project. It is four extra hours a week and a second box." She started up the stairs again. "You would have called it something, and had a name painted on it, and I would have had to have opinions about the name."

They did it over four evenings in the long room with the door shut.

What it looked like from outside was a woman at a table with a great deal of paper and a man sitting across from her doing nothing whatsoever for two hours at a stretch, and Ward, who put his head in on the second evening, came away with the impression that his lord had taken up sitting.

What it was, was slow. She went in with the venture's allocations and the mill's counts and her own workings and the totals she had never doubted, and she came out every night with paper, because a thing that could not be got back onto paper did not count and both of them had agreed on that in a room a year ago and neither of them had moved on it since.

On the first evening she did the shape: fifteen days, sixty-one households, the order the divisions had been cut in, which cart had been where. On the second she did the households that had delivered once and were therefore easy. On the third she did the households that had delivered four and five times, which were the large ones and the awkward ones, and she stopped twice and made him wait while she went and stood at the window.

On the fourth evening she was faster than she had been on any of the others and he did not like the look of it, and said so.

"I know," she said. "I have noticed it too. It is the last fifth of it and there is nothing left to be surprised by and I am filling in a shape I have already drawn." She put the pen down. "That is the part I do not trust and I am going to mark it."

She brought it to the long room on the twenty-fourth. Forty-one pages, ruled the way the burned sheets had been ruled, two hundred and seventeen deliveries by household and by date and by weight, with the mark of the delivering man reproduced nowhere because a mark is a man's own and she would not draw another person's mark.

The totals came out to the figure in the security documents, exactly, to the pound.

Emeric read the whole of it and felt something he had not felt since before the fire, which was pleasure of a very clean kind.

"That's the thing," he said. "Ferra, that is the thing. I lost the building. I didn't lose the year."

"It is not the building."

"It's better than the building. A building is a building." He had his hand flat on the pages. "Every man who has ever run anything has had the same nightmare, which is that the fire takes the record and the record is the only thing that says what happened, and everybody has always solved it by building a stronger box. You've solved it by being the box."

"Do not put that on a wall," Ferra said.

Vantic read it for a long time.

He read it the way he read everything, which was without expression and out of order, going to page thirty-one and then to page four and then to the fold, and he was at it for the better part of an hour and a half while the fire went down and nobody added to it.

Then he squared the pages and put his hands on top of them.

"By what method," he said.

"I wrote them," Ferra said. "I know what I wrote."

"That is not a method, mistress. That is a claim about a memory." Nothing unkind in it at all, which was the difficulty with him. "Take me through the eleventh of August. Sixteen deliveries. Give me the ninth of them."

She gave him the ninth of them.

"And how do you know it was not the tenth."

"Because the tenth is Halloway's second cart and Halloway's second cart is always after the small ones, because he holds it back so the small ones are not standing in a queue behind a full wagon in the heat. He has done that every year I have kept the book and he did it when the old baron kept it and his father did it before him. It is not written anywhere. It is a habit of a man's, and it puts him ninth or tenth on eleven days out of fifteen, and on the eleventh of August I have him tenth."

"That is a good answer," Vantic said. "It is also an answer about how a man behaves and not about what happened on the day."

"Yes."

"There are eleven marks in this."

"There are eleven places where I could not get there and would not pretend." She did not soften it either. "Nine of them are in the last fifth, which I did fastest and trusted least. Two of them are on the sixth of August, which was the day of the storm, when three carts came in together at dusk and Tam called them out of order and I have never known which of the three was which. The totals for the three are right and the split between them is a guess and I have written guessed beside it in the margin."

Vantic turned to the sixth of August and looked at the word.

"You understand my difficulty," he said.

"I understand that a set of books with eleven honest gaps in it is more convincing than a set with none," Ferra said, "and that a person setting out to deceive you, if she were any good, would put eleven gaps in."

"Yes."

"And that you cannot tell the difference."

"I cannot tell the difference."

"No," Ferra said. "Neither can I."

The fire made the noise a fire makes and nobody said anything for a while, and Emeric, who had opened his mouth, discovered that everything he had been about to say was a defence of a woman who had not asked for one and had just said a truer thing than any of it.

Vantic put the pages down.

"I will take it," he said, "and I will not write asserted, and I will not write primary record inspected, because it is neither and my house has words for both and it has never given me a word for this." He got his own sheet out and ruled a line. "I am going to write that the figure is supported by a reconstruction produced by the borrower's own officer from the borrower's own workings, that the reconstruction agrees to the pound with a figure recorded before the loss, that it carries eleven declared uncertainties, and that in my opinion it is honest work. Then I am going to sign my name under the word opinion, which is a thing I have done four times in nine years."

"Does it stop the reserve," Emeric said.

"It halves it, at a guess, and the guess is my house's and not mine." He capped the ink. "Do not thank me. It is the truthful entry and it happens to be the useful one, and if it had been the other way about I would have written the other one."

He put the case under his arm at the door and then stopped, and took out his book, and wrote something in it standing up, and did not say what it was.

Emeric noticed, because he had noticed the first time, in October, when the man had done exactly that after asking about the file and had gone away without explaining it. It had turned out well the first time. He watched him write and let himself be pleased about it in a small private way that he did not examine, because there were forty-one pages on his table that had come out of a fire, and a woman at the end of the table who had made them, and a reserve halved on a stranger's opinion, and it had been a good night.

Ferra stayed to gather the paper.

"I want to say the thing I did not say in front of him," she said.

"Go on."

"Nothing on those pages is a lie and I would take it in front of a magistrate." She stacked them square. "And if I had wanted to put a hundred and forty bushels somewhere and cover it, I would have done it the same way, in the same hand, with the same eleven marks, and it would have taken me the same four evenings. I would like that said out loud once by somebody in this barony, and then I would like it not said again."

"It's said."

"Good." She took the pages away to the box. "The old baron never read a line I wrote in eleven years, and I used to think about that with some bitterness, and it turns out that what I was resenting was the only thing keeping me honest, which was nobody being able to tell."
