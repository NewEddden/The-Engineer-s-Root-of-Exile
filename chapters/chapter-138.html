Chapter 138 - Steps Back

Imani knew before anyone told her. Nobody told her. Nobody had to.

It was the table, on the first Monday of February. Emeric came into the hall a little late, from the cellar, and sat down in his place, and Ferra was already in hers, across from him, with the barony's book open in front of her, a new book, because the old one had been dried in front of her fire for three days and was not yet fit to write in. Her hair was pinned up hard. It was always pinned up, one way or another, any way at all, with one strand coming down. This morning it was pinned up hard and tight and high, the way Imani pinned hers for the ward, and the ends of it, where they showed under the pins, were black.

Not dark. Black. Ink black. The barony's ink, which did not wash out of hair in three days, or in thirty, whatever you did to it.

Emeric did not look at it. He did not look at Ferra at all, more than he would have on any Monday. She did not look at him. They sat across the table from each other and did the business of the four baronies for an hour and a half, the copper and the ore and the crown's third and the new standpipes for Wayford, and neither of them said a word to the other that was not the business, and when Ferra wanted the ore figures she held her hand out for them across the table without looking up, as she had done every Monday for a year, and he put them in it.

He put them in her hand. Not on the table.

And at the end of the table, by the door, with her sleeves pinned for the ward, Imani looked at the ore figures go from his hand into Ferra's. And then at Ferra's hair. And then at him.

It was not a long look. It was the look she gave a wound when she came to a bed, the whole of it, once, before she touched anything. And he saw her see it. All of it. Whatever there was to see, she saw, in the length of that one look, at the end of a table, on a Monday, in the middle of the price of copper.

Then she looked down at her own papers, and went on with the business, and said the right things about the Wayford standpipes in the right order, and when the table rose she went out first, by the door she always sat by, to the ward.



She did not come to the hospital ground that evening.

He went. He went because they had said again tomorrow in December and it had been again tomorrow every evening since, five nights in seven, at the bench in the tool shed at the top of the turned ground, under the lamp, with the canal water and the bead and the plan paper. He had stopped needing to be told. He went up the slope at dusk with his charcoal in his pocket and the lamp in the shed was lit and the bench was set out, the bead on its linen, the jar of the day's water, a stack of plan paper turned to its blank side.

The girl from the north well was at the bench. Imani's first apprentice. Fifteen, with her two plaits, sitting on Imani's stool with the bead to her eye and a stick of charcoal in her other hand, drawing.

She looked up when he came in. She looked at him the way a girl of fifteen looks at a prince who has come into a room where she has been told to expect him.

"Healer says," she said, "she'll do the drawings with me now. In the evenings. Here." She held up the charcoal, as if he might not know what it was for. "So you needn't. She says thank you." She looked at the page in front of her. "She says you've got the hospital and the chair and the mine and four baronies and you don't need to be sitting up drawing tadpoles with her till the second candle." She stopped. "That's what she said. Tadpoles."

He stood in the door of the shed.

"Where is she?" he said.

"At the ward." The girl looked at him. "She said you'd ask. She said to say at the ward, and that she's well, and that she'll see you at the table." She put the bead back to her eye. "And that she'll want the heating schedule for Tess's chair by Thursday, because she's worked it out."

He did not go to the ward.

He knew what it was. He had known it, if he was honest, since the end of the table that morning, when she looked at the ore figures go from his hand into Ferra's and then at Ferra's hair. He stood in the door of the shed at the top of the hospital ground in the cold, with the lamp behind him and the girl from the north well bent over the bead, and the whole of it came to him at once, all of it, the way the town had come up out of the glass in the room above Kaldo when Ferra put the census in.

The ward in February. Her face a hand's breadth from his. What are you. I said I'd ask again. I'm asking. And he had stood there and said nothing for so long that the nothing became the answer. And she had remembered You walked, and stepped back, one step, carefully, the way she stepped back from a bed when she had done all she could.

She had not stepped back from him then. Not all the way. She had come to the hospital ground with a jar of water. She had let him hold her wrist. She had said thank you for not counting.

And he had never once told her anything. Not what he was. Not what the room was, until she was in it. Not the gravity. Not the heat in his hands. Not the stable roof, though she had held his bare foot in her lap on the keep stair and asked him, quite quietly, to tell her one day. He had given her a bead of glass made in a night with three magics she did not know he had, and let her think it was a gift, and it had been a gift, and it had also been the nearest thing to an answer he could give without giving one. She had known that. She had decided not to mind.

And this morning she had seen him put the ore figures in Ferra's hand, and Ferra's hair black at the ends, and known that whatever he had not told her, he had told Ferra.

That was all. She did not need a speech. She did not need anybody to tell her anything. She had seen it, the way she saw a wound, and done what she did with a wound, which was the next thing that needed doing. She had sent the girl from the north well to the shed with the charcoal, and a message that was kind, and true, and left him nothing at all to answer.

She cared about him. He knew that. He thought he had always known it, from the first summer, from her hand flat on her own door frame telling him to go home. She cared about him, and it was not enough to make the rest of it right for her. A prince's wife. The holder's wife. A woman who had been the barony's healer since she was nineteen, and buried a husband, and held the lower ward together through the plague with her own hands, sitting at the holder's right at the high table in green wool like Isolde Sallow, while somebody else ran her ward. She would never have done it. He thought he had known that too.

And still it hurt. It hurt more than he had any right to, standing in the shed door in the cold. And he could see, quite clearly, his part in it. Not the choice. The choice was Ferra, and he would make it again. But the not telling. The year of not telling, after she had given him the chair by her fire and whose it had been. She had paid first, in the ward in the second year, before she asked what he was. He had never paid her back.

"Are you all right?" said the girl from the north well, from the bench.

He looked at her. Fifteen. With the bead in her hand.

"What are you drawing?" he said.

"A long one. With a tail at the front." She held up the page. "Healer says it's the same one that's in the children at Wayford. In the ditch water. She says I'm to draw every one of them that's in the canal, so she can count them."

"Then draw it," said Emeric. "Carefully. She'll check it."

"She checks everything," said the girl, and bent back to the bead.

He went down the slope in the dark.

He did not see Imani until the Thursday, at the ward, when he brought her the heating schedule for Tess's chair, and she took it from him at the desk, and read it, all of it, and corrected two figures with her own pen, and gave it back, and said it would do. She was exactly as she had always been. Dry. Exact. On her own terms. She asked after the mine. She told him the cooper's chest had cleared. She did not step away from him, or look away from him, or say anything that was not true.

At the end of it, when he had the schedule back in his hand and was turning to go, she said his name.

He turned.

Imani looked at him across the desk, under the lamp, with the register open in front of her.

"Good," she said.

That was all. She said it the way she said a wound had closed clean. And she looked down at the register and picked up her pen, and he stood there, and understood that it was the only thing she was going to say about it, ever, and that it was meant, and that it was meant to be enough, and that it was very nearly the kindest thing anybody had ever said to him.

He went out of the side door into the cold.



The first message on the Grayrock line came on the sixth, at noon, into the line room at the top of the keep, on the new needle Tess had put on the board for it, and the board man wrote it down off the needle letter by letter in the book and then stood up from his stool and ran down the keep stair, which no board man had ever done, to find somebody to show.

It was from Bryda's hall. Forty miles. In the time it took to boil a kettle.

It said: The steward of Grayrock to the holder. The assessor arrived this morning. He has a cold and a bad temper and has asked for the books. Ilsa has given him them. He is reading them now. He does not like them. They are correct.

Ferra, reading it off the board book in the line room, put her hand over her mouth.



The Seers' Table began on the ninth, in the long room, after school, and it began as a punishment, or at least that was what the children who were given it thought it was.

Twelve of them. The oldest still in Sela's room, the year below the first commencement, fifteen and sixteen, the ones who had not yet chosen and would choose in September. Emeric picked them himself, with Sela, on her advice: the ones who asked questions in class that were not about the lesson, and drew in the margins of their slates, and could not sit still. He gave them Ivo's tube microscope, the one Tess had built with Ivo's lens, on its iron stand with its tin mirror. And a box of the new sized paper, five hundred sheets. And a job.

Maren's questions. She had been asking them for years, of anybody who would listen, at the kitchen door: why milk soured faster in summer, and why one batch of bread rose and the next, from the same barm, sat in the tin like a stone. Nobody had ever answered them.

"Find out," said Emeric, standing in front of the twelve of them in the long room with the microscope on the table. "Draw everything you can find under the glass. Everything. Milk. Barm. Bread dough. Water from every well. Scrapings off the cheese. Anything you like. Label every drawing. Date it. Say where you got it." He put his hand on the box of paper. "When you've filled these, there's more."

The twelve of them looked at the microscope, and at the paper, and at each other, with the faces of children who have been given an enormous quantity of copying to do and told it is a privilege.

"Why?" said one of them, a boy from the dock quarter with ink on his chin.

"Because Maren wants to know why her bread won't rise."

The boy looked at him with deep suspicion. Then he went and put his eye to the microscope, because it was there, and nobody had told him not to, and was very quiet for some time.

By the end of the week they had named themselves. They called it the Seers' Table, after the long table in the long room where they sat, because, the boy from the dock quarter said, they were the only people in Kaldo who could see. Emeric thought it was the worst name he had ever heard and told them so, and called it the microscope class to their faces, every time, and they called it the Seers' Table to his, and both names stuck.



That night, alone, behind the shutters, he did a thing he had been thinking about for a year.

It had started with the board. With a lamp stone filling under his hand in the ward, and Imani watching him do it without looking at it. Mana went out of a man told what to be: fire, air, a weight taken off a coin. Or into a stone, told what to do. But it could go out of a man told nothing at all. He had done it every night since the spring, to keep his sense open: let it out thin, with no glyph and no shape, and leave it there. Mana with no instruction. It went out of him, and it went into the air, and it stayed there round him, thin, for as long as he kept letting it, and then it was gone.

On his other world there had been air that men made into a liquid. You took it and squeezed it and cooled it until it could not stay a gas any more, and it ran, pale blue, in a flask, and boiled away the moment it was let go. He had read about it in the trial, from the inside, as if he had made it with his own hands. Air was made of things that would do that, if you made them cold enough and pressed them hard enough. And what went out of him, told nothing, felt to his sense like air.

So he took a glass jar from Ivo's glasshouse, a good one, clear, with a wide mouth, and a collar of leather that Abloh had made to his drawing, that fitted the mouth of the jar on one side and his own wrist on the other, close, sealed with tallow. He put his left hand into the jar. He tied the collar round his wrist and sealed it. And he let the mana go out of his hand into the jar, told nothing, and kept letting it, and did not let it thin, and did not let it go anywhere, because there was nowhere in the jar for it to go.

It filled the jar. He felt it fill. He felt it press, the way air presses in a bladder that is blown too full. And he went on letting it, and pressing, and then, because it was the only thing he could think of that was like cold for mana, he told it, very hard, all at once, to be still.

It became liquid.

He felt it happen. The press in the jar let go, all at once, and something ran down the inside of the glass round his hand like water down a window, and pooled in the bottom of the jar round his fingers.

He lit the candle with his other hand and looked.

Pale blue. The colour of a winter sky an hour after sunset. A little pool of it in the bottom of the jar, round his fingers, a finger's depth, perhaps two ounces. It did not move like water. It moved more slowly, and it caught the candle's light and held it, so that it seemed to glow faintly from inside, and his fingers in it felt cool, and very slightly tingling, the way a hand tingles when the blood is coming back into it.

And he was empty.

He had not noticed it while he was doing it. He noticed it now, sitting at his table with his hand in a jar, all at once: hollow, cold, light, the way he had felt after the first nights in the delve in the first winter, the way he had felt at the bottom of the cursed gallery with the hood of good air gone thin. Two ounces of pale blue in a jar had taken out of him most of what he had.

He sat and looked at it.

Then, because he wanted to see it better, he untied the collar, and pulled his hand out of the jar.

The moment his hand came out, the moment the seal broke, it was gone.

Not spilled. Gone. The little pale blue pool in the bottom of the jar went up, all of it, at once, into the air, the way water goes up off a hot iron, and he felt it go, a cool breath across his face and his hands, like the air before rain, and then nothing. The jar was empty. Dry. Not a drop.

He sat there with his wet hand and the empty jar and the candle and laughed, very quietly, so the board man would not hear.



He took the next jar to Armand.

He made it the next night, sealed this time, his hand out of it before he made it liquid, with a long glass tube through the collar instead of his wrist, that Ivo had drawn for him, so that he could push the mana down the tube into the jar and then seal the tube in the candle flame, and keep it. It took him most of the night and left him so drained that he slept until noon. But he had it. A jar the size of a fist, sealed, with an inch of pale blue in the bottom, glowing very faintly in the dark of his coat pocket.

He put it on the board across Armand's knees.

Armand looked at it for some time without touching it.

"You've been building things without me again," he said.

"I wanted to surprise you."

"You did." Armand did not look up from the jar. "I'm surprised. I'm surprised and I'm offended, and I'm eighty, and I'll be offended for as long as I like." He was quiet. "How long did it take you?"

"Two nights."

"Two nights." Armand looked at the pale blue in the jar. He looked at it the way he had looked at the watch stones on the bench in the room, a long time, a teacher looking at a thing a student has done that he had not taught him. Then something at the corner of his mouth moved, and he put out his hand and touched the glass, very lightly, with one finger. "Well," he said. "It's very pretty."

"You've seen it before."

"I've seen it before," said Armand. He took his finger away. "Every crystal theorist in the tower has seen it before. It's in the tower's book. It's a curiosity. Students make it, in their third year, to show off, and then never make it again." He folded his hands on the board. "Shall I tell you why?"

"Yes."

Armand told him. Six reasons. He counted them off on his fingers in the lecture-hall voice, the way he had counted off the eleven tests of the grey stone in his notebook, and Emeric sat on the stool and listened to every one.

It cost more to make than it held. A mage spent more of himself pressing it into a jar than the jar would give back. His own reservoir refilled itself for nothing in a day, so bottling it was like bottling water beside a well. A mana stone did the same thing better: it held a charge as long as you liked, and could be told what to do with it, and did not vanish when you opened it. Liquid mana could not be told anything; it was not a spell and no spell could be cast from it, it was fuel with no engine. It would not stay. Break the seal and it was gone, as Emeric had seen. No soldier could carry it, no caravan could ship it, no battle would ever be won with it, because the first dropped flask was the last. And sixth.

"Drinking it kills people," said Armand. "A man eats food with mana in it and he stops, because he's full. That's the body's brake. This has no brake. It's all mana and nothing else, and a man who drinks it takes all of it at once, faster than any rite." He looked at the jar. "I knew a student who did. Third year. To see. He got the tracery on his arms in an hour, the silver, ring over ring, and then on his chest, and his neck, and then he died, badly, in the tower's infirmary, with four masters standing round him who could do nothing." He was quiet. "It's in the tower's book too. With his name."

Emeric sat on the stool.

"All six," he said. "None of them holds."

Armand looked at him.

"Not if you're not the source," said Emeric. "Every one of your reasons is about a mage making it out of himself and spending himself to do it. If it came from somewhere else. Somewhere there was more of it than any man could ever spend. Then it doesn't matter what it costs to make. It doesn't matter that a stone does it better, because it's not instead of a stone, it's what you fill the stones with." He looked at the jar. "If there were somewhere you could just take it from."

Armand did not say anything for a long while.

Then he said: "The climbs."

He told it once. Emeric never heard him tell it again.

When Armand was a young man, before the tower, before anything, a low apprentice to a tower master, the tower had sent mages up. Up into the sky. Because there was a thing every mage who flew knew and nobody could explain: that the higher you went, the thicker the mana felt round you. Denser. Richer. As if the sky were full of it, the way the sea is full of salt. And the tower had wanted to know how far up it went, and how thick it got.

So they climbed. In pairs. One mage flying, with gravity under him and air pushing, and the other on his back, riding, with a sack of charged stones. When the flier ran low, he drained a stone into himself by touch and kept going. When the stones were gone, they changed places. Up and up. Two miles. Three. The air went thin and cold, colder than any winter, and a man's breath went short and his head went light, and some of them saw things that were not there and laughed for no reason. Keeping warm cost. Holding height cost. Every breath up there cost. And the mana got thicker the higher they went, so thick that a man could feel it on his skin like fog.

"And they couldn't use it," said Armand. "That was the joke. The thickest mana anybody had ever felt, all round them, and they couldn't take it. Their own reservoirs filled from it, a little, but they were spending faster than they filled, holding up two men in thin air. And the stones." He shook his head. "They took empty stones up to fill. The stones wouldn't take it. Not up there. A stone up there would take a twentieth of a charge in the time it took a man to fall." He looked at the jar. "So they came down. Every time. Exhausted, half frozen, half mad from the thin air, and empty-handed. And told the tower there was more mana in the sky than in all the stones in the kingdom, and they couldn't bring any of it home."

"What happened?"

"The tower was working on it," said Armand. "My master was. He made the last climb himself. He had ideas." He was quiet. "And then the Church closed it. A letter came down to the tower. Wasted funds. Wasted research. Wasted men, who could be doing better things for the realm than freezing in the sky. And the tower stopped." He looked at the fire. "I thought it was strange, at the time. I was nobody. An apprentice. I thought it was strange, and I didn't say so, and I forgot about it for fifty years." He looked back at the jar. "Until just now."

Emeric looked at the jar too.

A jar of something that would not stay, that could not be carried, that was worthless if a man made it out of himself. And somewhere above Kaldo, two miles up, three, more, a sky so full of it that men had felt it on their skin like fog, and could not bring any home.

A jar would bring it home. If the jar were sealed. If somebody up there could press it in.

"Don't," said Armand.

Emeric looked up.

"Whatever you're thinking," said Armand. "Not this week. Not this year. You've a gravity mine full of bad air and a chair for a girl and four baronies and a woman with ink in her hair." He picked up the jar off his board and held it out. "Take your pretty jar. Go to bed." He held it a moment longer before Emeric took it. "And then, when you've slept, come back and tell me what you were thinking. All of it. Before you do any of it. I'd like to be in it this time."



The chair failed for the fourth time on the twelfth, indoors, in the cart shed, and buried the prince in silk.

It was the parachute. Tess had wanted it from the first drawing, for the stairs: if the chair ever lost its lift going up or down the keep stair, or the river stair, or any stair, a silk canopy packed in a box at the back of the seat would throw itself open on a spring and a bellows and let the chair down slow. Abloh had sewn it. Three yards across, of the finest silk the coast house could sell, which had come up on Somebody Came's last run in a parcel the size of a loaf at a price Ferra had written in the book in red.

The chair's stone was charged every morning by Vesk, in the charging room, before the test, because Vesk's charge was the steadiest in Kaldo. That morning Vesk had charged it as he always did. And at noon, with Emeric in the chair a hand's width off the shed floor, testing the stick's new notch, the charge ran out.

It did not run out the way a stone runs out on a long night, slowly. Vesk's charge had been a morning's charge, for a morning's test, and the morning had gone long, and Emeric had not looked at the time, and the stone simply stopped. All at once. A hand's width off the floor.

The chair dropped a hand's width onto the shed floor with a thump. Which would have been nothing.

Except that the parachute was set to throw itself open the moment the lift failed. And the lift had failed. And the bellows that was meant to throw the canopy up and out into the air of a stairwell, with forty feet of drop under it to fill, threw it up and out into the air of a cart shed with a hand's width of drop under it and nothing to fill it at all, and three yards of the finest silk on the coast came out of the box at the back of the chair with a whump, and went up, and hung, and then came down, softly, slowly, all of it, over the chair and the prince in it, and settled.

He sat under it. It was very quiet under the silk. It was pale, and it smelled faintly of the coast house's cedar box, and it was the softest thing that had ever been on top of him.

From outside it, after a while, he heard the shed door open.

Footsteps. A pause. Then Saban's voice, dry, commercial, from somewhere near the door.

"What," said Saban, "does that cost? By the yard?"
