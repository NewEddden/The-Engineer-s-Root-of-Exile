Chapter 138 - Whose Line

Ferra was not angry. That was what he could not get used to.

She was in the counting room at eight the next morning, as she was every morning, with the barony's book open in front of her fire upstairs to dry and a fresh one on the table, and her hair pinned up hard and high, and she said good morning to him when he came in, and held out her hand for the Grayrock figures, and he gave them to her, and she checked them and initialled them and gave them back. She was exact. She was civil. She asked him about the mine, and the crown's assessor, and the copper for the boat. She did not say anything that was not the business, and she did not look at him more than the business needed, and she did not once, in the whole of that week, raise her eyes to his face and leave them there.

She had asked him a question in her doorway with ink in her hair, and he had not answered it, and she was waiting. That was all. She was not going to ask again. Ferra did not ask twice for anything. She would do the business of four baronies with him, every day, exactly and well, for as long as it took him to come up her stair and say whose.

He went up her stair twice that week. At night, late, with the light under her door. Both times he stood in the passage under her name and the candle that nobody admitted to keeping lit, and both times he had the sentence ready, and both times, at the door, the other name came up behind it. And he stood there until the candle on the sill had burned down a finger's breadth, and went back down.

She would have heard him. She heard everything on that stair. She did not open the door.



The first message on the Grayrock line came on the sixth, at noon, into the line room at the top of the keep, on the new needle Tess had put on the board for it, and the board man wrote it down off the needle letter by letter in the book and then stood up from his stool and ran down the keep stair, which no board man had ever done, to find somebody to show.

It was from Bryda's hall. Forty miles. In the time it took to boil a kettle.

It said: The steward of Grayrock to the holder. The assessor arrived this morning. He has a cold and a bad temper and has asked for the books. Ilsa has given him them. He is reading them now. He does not like them. They are correct.

Ferra, reading it off the board book in the line room, put her hand over her mouth.

She laughed. Behind her hand, once, short. And then she looked up, over her hand, and he was standing on the other side of the board, and for the first time in two days she looked straight at him, with the laugh still in her eyes, and he saw her forget for a breath that she was waiting for anything.

Then she remembered. He watched it come back into her face. She took her hand away from her mouth and gave the board book back to the board man and went down the stair.



Imani saw it before anyone told her. Nobody told her. There was nothing yet that anybody could have told.

It was the table, on the eleventh, the second Monday of February. Emeric came into the hall a little late, from the cellar, and sat down in his place, and Ferra was already in hers, across from him, with the barony's book open in front of her, a new book, because the old one had been dried in front of her fire for a week and was not yet fit to write in. Her hair was pinned up hard. It was always pinned up, one way or another, any way at all, with one strand coming down. This morning it was pinned up hard and tight and high, the way Imani pinned hers for the ward, and the ends of it, where they showed under the pins, were black.

Not dark. Black. Ink black. The barony's ink, which did not wash out of hair in a week, or in thirty days, whatever you did to it.

Emeric did not look at it. He did not look at Ferra at all, more than he would have on any Monday. She did not look at him. She had not, more than the business needed, for a week. They sat across the table from each other and did the business of the four baronies for an hour and a half, the copper and the ore and the crown's third and the new standpipes for Wayford, and neither of them said a word to the other that was not the business, and when Ferra wanted the ore figures she held her hand out for them across the table without looking up, as she had done every Monday for a year, and he put them in it.

He put them in her hand. Not on the table.

And at the end of the table, by the door, with her sleeves pinned for the ward, Imani looked at the ore figures go from his hand into Ferra's. And then at Ferra's hair. And then at him.

It was not a long look. It was the look she gave a wound when she came to a bed, the whole of it, once, before she touched anything. And he saw her see it. Or see what there was to see from the end of a table: the ink, and a hand held out, and a woman who did not look at him. She saw it the way she saw a wound, whole, once, in the middle of the price of copper. And he sat across from Ferra with the ore figures gone out of his hand and did not know how to tell Imani that it was not the whole, or what the whole was.

Then she looked down at her own papers, and went on with the business, and said the right things about the Wayford standpipes in the right order, and when the table rose she went out first, by the door she always sat by, to the ward.



She did not come to the hospital ground that evening.

He went. He went because they had said again tomorrow in December and it had been again tomorrow every evening since, five nights in seven, at the bench in the tool shed at the top of the turned ground, under the lamp, with the canal water and the bead and the plan paper. He had stopped needing to be told. He went up the slope at dusk with his charcoal in his pocket and the lamp in the shed was lit and the bench was set out, the bead on its linen, the jar of the day's water, a stack of plan paper turned to its blank side.

The girl from the north well was at the bench. Imani's first apprentice. Fifteen, with her two plaits, sitting on Imani's stool with the bead to her eye and a stick of charcoal in her other hand, drawing.

She looked up when he came in. She looked at him the way a girl of fifteen looks at a prince who has come into a room where she has been told to expect him.

"Healer says," she said, "she'll do the drawings with me now. In the evenings. Here." She held up the charcoal, as if he might not know what it was for. "So you needn't. She says thank you." She looked at the page in front of her. "She says you've got the hospital and the chair and the mine and four baronies and you don't need to be sitting up drawing tadpoles with her till the second candle." She stopped. "That's what she said. Tadpoles."

He stood in the door of the shed.

"Where is she?" he said.

"At the ward." The girl looked at him. "She said you'd ask. She said to say at the ward, and that she's well, and that she'll see you at the table." She put the bead back to her eye. "And that she'll want the heating schedule for Tess's chair by Thursday, because she's worked it out."

He did not go to the ward.

He knew what it was. He stood in the door of the shed at the top of the hospital ground in the cold, with the lamp behind him and the girl from the north well bent over the bead, and it came to him whole, the way the town had come up out of the glass in the room above Kaldo when Ferra put the census in.

Imani had seen ink in Ferra's hair, and the ore figures go into Ferra's hand, and drawn the only line a sensible woman could draw from them. And she had done what she did with a wound: the next thing that needed doing. She had sent the girl with the charcoal and a message that was kind, and true, and left him nothing to answer. She thought it was settled. She thought he had chosen.

And Ferra was in her room in the keep with the barony's book dried stiff in front of her fire, waiting for him to tell her whose line was missing. She had been waiting a week. She did the business with him at the table and in the counting room, exactly and well, and held her hand out for figures, and did not ask again, because she had asked once and Ferra did not ask twice.

He had not chosen. That was the joke. He had not chosen anybody. He had stood in a doorway with two names in his mouth and said neither, and he was losing both of them anyway, one to a wrong guess and one to a right one, and it was nobody's doing but his own.

He was good at deciding. That was the other joke. He had decided a sewer and a war and a hanging and a mine. He decided things at that table every Monday that four baronies lived or died by, and he did not lose sleep over most of them, because there was a right answer and he could usually see it. He could not see this one. On his other world he had been a thin man at a desk ten hours a day, who had walked out with two women in his whole life and lost both of them by forgetting to notice they were there. He had not learned anything about this. He had not had to. He had two lives behind him and had learned nothing about this in either, and he stood in the door of a tool shed like a boy of fifteen, with his heart going and nothing in his hands.

He wanted them both.

He made himself think it in words, in the cold, so that he could look at it. He wanted Ferra, who knew him, who read him upside down and corrected him and had kissed him to stop a speech and laughed in the ink. And he wanted Imani, who had held his wrist until his hands stopped shaking, and told him he was right with the wrong legs, and asked him what he was with her face a hand's breadth from his. He did not want one more than the other. He had tried, every night for a week, to find which, the way he would have tried a column, and it would not come out. It came out two.

The world would let him. That was the worst of it. In Randillia a man with four baronies could take two wives, or three, and the court would not raise an eyebrow; half the old houses in the capital had a second wife somewhere, for land or for children or for love, and nobody thought it strange. But he had not been raised in Randillia. Not really. Not the part of him that was doing the wanting. The part of him that was doing the wanting had been raised in a country on the other side of death where a man had one wife, and a man who wanted two women was a man who could not make up his mind, and there was a word for him, and it was not a kind one.

And it was selfish. He knew that too. To want them both because choosing one would hurt the other, and he could not stand to be the one who did the hurting. To want them both because he wanted them both. He could dress it up however he liked, at the shed door in the cold, and under the dressing it was a man who wanted not to lose anything.

He could tell them. He thought it, and felt the fear come up behind it, cold. He could go to each of them and say the true thing, the thing he could not say in Ferra's doorway: there are two lines. And then he would watch the two most exact women in the four baronies hear it, and do the sum, and come to the only answer a sensible woman could, which was that he was a man who could not choose and was asking them to make it easy for him. And he would lose them both. Not one. Both. Tonight he was only losing them slowly.

And if he did not tell them, and chose, and chose wrong.

He stood in the door of the shed and went round it again, and again, the way the chair had gone round in the yard with nothing to stop it, and did not find the stick.

"Are you all right?" said the girl from the north well, from the bench.

He looked at her. Fifteen. With the bead in her hand.

"What are you drawing?" he said.

"A long one. With a tail at the front." She held up the page. "Healer says it's the same one that's in the children at Wayford. In the ditch water. She says I'm to draw every one of them that's in the canal, so she can count them."

"Then draw it," said Emeric. "Carefully. She'll check it."

"She checks everything," said the girl, and bent back to the bead.

He went down the slope in the dark.

He did not see Imani until the Thursday, at the ward, when he brought her the heating schedule for Tess's chair, and she took it from him at the desk, and read it, all of it, and corrected two figures with her own pen, and gave it back, and said it would do. She was exactly as she had always been. Dry. Exact. On her own terms. She asked after the mine. She told him the cooper's chest had cleared. She did not step away from him, or look away from him, or say anything that was not true.

She did not say his name. Not once. She said Highness, twice, which she had not called him since the first summer.

He went out of the side door into the cold.



The Seers' Table began on the twelfth, in the long room, after school, and it began as a punishment, or at least that was what the children who were given it thought it was.

Twelve of them. The oldest still in Sela's room, the year below the first commencement, fifteen and sixteen, the ones who had not yet chosen and would choose in September. Emeric picked them himself, with Sela, on her advice: the ones who asked questions in class that were not about the lesson, and drew in the margins of their slates, and could not sit still. He gave them Ivo's tube microscope, the one Tess had built with Ivo's lens, on its iron stand with its tin mirror. And a box of the new sized paper, five hundred sheets. And a job.

Maren's questions. She had been asking them for years, of anybody who would listen, at the kitchen door: why milk soured faster in summer, and why one batch of bread rose and the next, from the same barm, sat in the tin like a stone. Nobody had ever answered them.

"Find out," said Emeric, standing in front of the twelve of them in the long room with the microscope on the table. "Draw everything you can find under the glass. Everything. Milk. Barm. Bread dough. Water from every well. Scrapings off the cheese. Anything you like. Label every drawing. Date it. Say where you got it." He put his hand on the box of paper. "When you've filled these, there's more."

The twelve of them looked at the microscope, and at the paper, and at each other, with the faces of children who have been given an enormous quantity of copying to do and told it is a privilege.

"Why?" said one of them, a boy from the dock quarter with ink on his chin.

"Because Maren wants to know why her bread won't rise."

The boy looked at him with deep suspicion. Then he went and put his eye to the microscope, because it was there, and nobody had told him not to, and was very quiet for some time.

By the end of the week they had named themselves. They called it the Seers' Table, after the long table in the long room where they sat, because, the boy from the dock quarter said, they were the only people in Kaldo who could see. Emeric thought it was the worst name he had ever heard and told them so, and called it the microscope class to their faces, every time, and they called it the Seers' Table to his, and both names stuck.



The chair failed for the fourth time on the fifteenth, indoors, in the cart shed, and buried the prince in silk.

It was the parachute. Tess had wanted it from the first drawing, for the stairs: if the chair ever lost its lift going up or down the keep stair, or the river stair, or any stair, a silk canopy packed in a box at the back of the seat would throw itself open on a spring and a bellows and let the chair down slow. Abloh had sewn it. Three yards across, of the finest silk the coast house could sell, which had come up on Somebody Came's last run in a parcel the size of a loaf at a price Ferra had written in the book in red.

The chair's stone was charged every morning by Vesk, in the charging room, before the test, because Vesk's charge was the steadiest in Kaldo. That morning Vesk had charged it as he always did. And at noon, with Emeric in the chair a hand's width off the shed floor, testing the stick's new notch, the charge ran out.

It did not run out the way a stone runs out on a long night, slowly. Vesk's charge had been a morning's charge, for a morning's test, and the morning had gone long, and Emeric had not looked at the time, and the stone simply stopped. All at once. A hand's width off the floor.

The chair dropped a hand's width onto the shed floor with a thump. Which would have been nothing.

Except that the parachute was set to throw itself open the moment the lift failed. And the lift had failed. And the bellows that was meant to throw the canopy up and out into the air of a stairwell, with forty feet of drop under it to fill, threw it up and out into the air of a cart shed with a hand's width of drop under it and nothing to fill it at all, and three yards of the finest silk on the coast came out of the box at the back of the chair with a whump, and went up, and hung, and then came down, softly, slowly, all of it, over the chair and the prince in it, and settled.

He sat under it. It was very quiet under the silk. It was pale, and it smelled faintly of the coast house's cedar box, and it was the softest thing that had ever been on top of him.

From outside it, after a while, he heard the shed door open.

Footsteps. A pause. Then Saban's voice, dry, commercial, from somewhere near the door.

"What," said Saban, "does that cost? By the yard?"



He went up her stair on the night of the seventeenth, late, with the light under her door, and this time he knocked.

She said come in. She was at the trestle. The old book was on the shelf now, dry, its pages gone stiff and wavy and grey at the edges, and the new one was open in front of her. She did not look up. She went on writing until she reached the end of the line, and blotted it, and put her pen down, and then she looked up.

"Whose," she said.

He had not sat down. He did not think he could.

"Two," he said.

Ferra did not move.

"There are two lines missing," said Emeric. "Not one. I came up your stair two weeks ago with one of them ready and the other came up behind it, and I couldn't say either, because I couldn't say one without lying about the other." He made himself go on looking at her. "Yours. And Imani's."

The fire ticked. Ferra's hands lay flat on either side of the book.

"I don't know how to choose," he said. "I've tried. Every night. I've tried it the way you'd try a column, and it doesn't come out one. It comes out two." He heard his voice and did not stop it. "I know what that sounds like. Where I was raised, a man who says that is a man who can't make up his mind and wants somebody else to make it easy for him. And maybe that's what I am. I'm good at choosing everything else. I chose a war. I've never chosen this. I've never had to. I'm no good at it. I'd rather lose a hand than see you hurt, or her, and the only way I can see not to hurt one of you is not to choose, and that's the most selfish thing I've ever wanted in my life, and I know it." He stopped. "And I was frightened to tell you. Because if I told you, and her, I thought I'd lose you both. And I'm telling you anyway. Because you asked me whose, and you deserved an answer, and that's the only true one I've got."

He stopped. He had not made a speech. He had said it in the order it came, badly, and it was out, all of it, in her room, and he could not take it back.

Ferra sat at the trestle and looked at him.

She did not say anything for so long that he could hear the board man's stool creak in the line room two floors above.

"I know," she said.

He stared at her.

"Not that. Not what you've just said. I didn't know you'd say it. I didn't know you knew it." She looked down at the book. "But I've had a column on it since the summer of the war. Since the night she came to my table with your coat over her arm, boiled, and asked you how she got into that bed, and you said she walked." She was quiet. "I didn't understand any of it. I understood that I didn't. I wrote it down."

"Ferra."

"I'm not angry." She said it carefully, the way she read a figure back to make sure of it. "I thought I would be. I thought, if he ever says it, I'll be angry. I'm not." She put her hand flat on the book. "I'm something. I don't know what it's called yet. I'll need to find out."

She was white. The freckles stood out across her nose. Her hand on the book was quite steady, and she was looking at it, and not at him.

"Have you told her?" she said.

"No."

Ferra looked up.

"Then tell her," she said. "Before you tell me anything else. Before you stand in my room one more night." Her voice did not rise. "I won't carry it for you. I won't know a thing about her that she doesn't know about herself. Not this. I've kept everybody's secrets in this barony for nearly five years, in my head and in my box and in my cipher, and I'll keep anything you like. But I won't keep that one. It isn't mine. It's hers." She held his eyes. "Tell her the way you've told me. All of it. Badly, if that's the only way you can."

"And then?"

"And then I don't know," said Ferra. "And neither will you. And you'll have to wait, the way I've waited two weeks." She picked up her pen. "Go away now, Emeric. Please. I need to think, and I can't do it with you standing there."

He went. At the door, with his hand on the latch, he did not turn round, because he did not think he could bear to see her face if it was doing something.

Behind him he heard her pen touch the page, and stop. And not move again until he had shut the door.
