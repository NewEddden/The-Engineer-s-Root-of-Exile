Chapter 138 - Under the Eaves

The ship came up from the south on the first dark night after the dinners, with Osric at the levers and Declan and Rylan asleep in the armoury, and came down to the lamp behind the thorn a mile out of Wayford at the third bell of the night, and was gone up again before the dew had settled on the grass she had pressed flat.

Ward had walked out with the lamp. He had walked out with it before, and he did it the same way every time: the south road as far as the last house, then the edge of Lisle's wood, then the sheep track up the bare hill, not fast, the lamp shuttered, stopping at the wood's edge to listen for a count of fifty. The prince walked behind him. At the top Ward set the lamp behind the thorn and opened the hood a hand's width, facing south, and they stood and waited, and after a while the sky over them was not empty.

She came down on her legs without a sound he could have told from the wind. The hatch opened in the top deck. Osric put his head up, and saw who it was, and put his head down again.

In the armoury, while she climbed, Emeric took the levers from Osric the way he took them, which was by not touching them: he sat down on the stool at the bench by the forward bulkhead with his hand flat on the breast of his own suit on its frame, and shut his eyes, and after that the levers moved when he wanted them to and not before. Osric watched him do it for a moment, and then went and began to put his suit on.

Ward was already in his.

It was cold at the top.

It was always cold at the top, cold enough that the frost came up white on the rail under a man's hand and stayed, and the stars stood very close and very hard and did not twinkle, and the air was thin in a way that a man did not notice until he tried to do something. Ward noticed it at the hatch. He had his helm under his arm and his hood back, and he was going down the line of them with the satchels, and he found that he had put his hand on Declan's satchel buckle and was still looking at it.

He made himself finish the buckle. It took him longer than it should have.

"Count them," he said.

Declan opened his satchel and put his hand in and counted the knots by feel, lips moving, in the starlight. "Four."

"Count them again."

Declan counted them again. He took them out this time, one at a time, and set them on the deck in a row, and looked at the row. There were three. He looked at them for a moment longer, and put them back, and shut the satchel, and did not say anything, which for Declan was a good deal.

"We go down," Ward said. "Not the other way. It gets better as we go down."

He looked along the line of them. Osric, steady, working his gauntlets on finger by finger, slowly. Rylan, already done, standing by the hatch coaming with his helm on and the pad on his off hand turned out to the dark, not moving. Declan, with his three. On every hip, on the belt, clear of the front heart and clear of the pack, a rack of three tin tubes in a row.

Nobody touched them. Ward had not thought anybody would.

"Stones dark from the rail," he said. "Glide only. Lighten if you're coming in short. Nobody burns over the town. If you have to burn, you go out over the wood first, and you burn there, low, and you walk back." He put his helm on. "We meet at the thorn at the fifth bell. Anybody not at the thorn at the fifth bell, we go and look for him, and we do not go and look for him in the air."

He went over the rail first.

The ship was nearly three miles up and Wayford was a scatter of five lamps in the dark to the north, and he fell away from her in a long slant with the cape stiff under his arms and the wind loud in his hood and nothing lit on him anywhere. The air came back as he went down. He could feel it come back, the way a man feels a room warm, a little at a time and then all at once; somewhere below the first mile his hands were his own again, and he opened and closed the right one inside its gauntlet to be sure of it, and it was.

He looked back once. Three shapes against the stars behind him, dark, strung out in a line, not close. Nobody's stones were showing. Good.

They came in over Lisle's wood from the south at the height of a church tower, and over the south edge of the town at the height of a house, and the square opened in front of them with the Staple on its west side and the one lamp in its upper window, and Ward tipped the cape and came round in a long slow turn over the rooftops on the north side, Lighten, very little, just enough to flatten the last of it, and put his boots on the ridge of Placit's roof, and was still.

Nobody below him moved.

He waited. The three others came in behind him, one and then one and then one, onto the ridges he had given them in the pattern store on the model Nevin had made from Saban's sketches, which was not a good model, because Nevin had never seen Wayford and Saban could not draw. Declan's ridge was two houses east. Osric's was across the square, on Strade's. Rylan's was the Staple.

The rooms they wanted were the rooms where men wrote.

Saban had got them out of kitchens and stables over a fortnight, a window at a time: which room in Placit's house was the room where his lordship did his letters in the mornings, and which was his steward's, and where Hollin kept his books, and whether Strade wrote upstairs or down. It was all in the pattern store on scraps of paper pinned to Nevin's model, and none of it was anything a servant would have thought twice about saying to a pleasant man buying a jug of beer. His lordship writes in the little room at the back, the one over the kitchen, because it's warm. Her ladyship's always up in the front room over the porch, you can see the light from the square half the night.

A stone could not hear through a wall the thickness of Placit's. It could hear down a chimney.

Ward went along Placit's ridge on his pads with his stones dark, pad and boot and boot and pad, felt the stack at the back of the house with his free hand before he trusted it, and found it sound, good brick in good mortar. Four pots. The third from the left was the little room at the back, over the kitchen, and it was warm under his palm; somebody had banked the fire in there before they went to bed. He set his left boot and his right on the brick of the stack itself, one above the other, and set the pad on his off hand on the slates beside it, and hung there on the side of the stack with both his other hand and the knot free.

The knot went in under the lip of the pot, on the side away from the square, where the soot had built up in a black crust over fifty years. He pressed it into the crust. He pressed crust over it with his thumb. When he took his hand away it was a lump of soot on a chimney pot like every other lump of soot on every other chimney pot in the south, and there was no light from it anywhere, because Bram had made the casings so there would not be.

Then the pad. Then the boots. The small soft pop of each of them, one, two, three, which he had stopped hearing on the second night at the keep and heard now, on Placit's roof, as though somebody had struck a bell.

Nobody below him moved.

The Staple was Rylan's because the Staple was the hardest.

It was two storeys of old stone round a square hall, with a slate roof pitched steep over the weighing floor and a flat leaded walk behind a parapet on the side of the square, and the ram stood on its iron rod at the peak of the roof over the arches, turning a little in the wind. The lamp in the upper window was on the square side, directly under the walk. There was a man behind it. They had known there would be. Saban had never found out who sat up there all night or why, only that somebody always did, and that on the square side there was nothing a man on the roof could do that the man behind the lamp would not hear if he was listening.

Ward watched from Placit's ridge.

Rylan came up the back of the Staple, the side away from the square, on the pads, where the stone was old and dressed and sound, and went up the pitch of the roof on his belly with his pads on the slates, and at the top he did not stand. He lay along the ridge, flat, under the ram, and reached up.

The Staple's writing was done in the room at the front, over the lamp, and the room at the front was under a leaded walk that a man could not stand on without the lead talking. So the Staple's knot went where nobody could stand at all. It would listen, and it would pass on what the others heard, both; there was nothing between it and the room over the lamp but thirty feet of rafters and slates and a lath ceiling, which was near the edge of what a knot could hear through and inside it. It went on the iron rod of the vane, below the ram, in the collar where the rod went through its bearing and years of rust and pigeon lime had built up round it, and Rylan put it there and pressed the lime over it with the heel of his hand, and lay still.

Below him, on the square side, a door opened onto the walk.

Ward saw it. He saw the light from the lamp come out across the lead in a long yellow slab, and a man step out into it in his shirtsleeves, a clerk, not young, with a pen still in his hand. The man went to the parapet and stood there, and looked out at the square, and at the sky over it, and stretched with both arms above his head the way a man does who has been bent over a desk for three hours, and yawned.

Then he looked up.

He looked straight up, past the ridge of his own roof, at the ram, at the stars behind it. He was looking at nothing. He was a man who had been looking at small figures in bad light for three hours and was resting his eyes on the biggest thing he could find, and the biggest thing he could find was the sky. Rylan lay along the ridge in black plate under a black cape, not thirty feet above him and a little to one side, against the black of the roof, and did not move. He did not breathe that Ward could see.

The clerk looked at the sky for about as long as it took to count to ten. Then he went in, and shut the door on the walk, and the slab of light went away.

Rylan lay still for a long count after that. Then he came down the back of the Staple the way he had gone up it, slowly, without a sound, and went away across the roofs toward the Ambry house with the next knot in his hand, to the dovecote in the Ambry yard, where the chain came home.

He had not been seen because nobody was looking for him, Ward thought. That was all. It was not anything they had done. The clerk had not been looking for a man on the roof because there had never in the history of the world been a man on that roof, and a man who is not expecting to see a thing does not see it. It would hold as long as that held, and not an hour longer.

The second night was Hollin's, and Dray's, and Hollin had a dog.

Saban had not known about the dog. It was a new dog, a big brindled yard dog chained in the angle between Hollin's kitchen and his stable, and it had not been there the week before, because it had been bought at the Milford fair four days ago, at a price Hollin had very likely told somebody at dinner. It did not bark. It stood up at the end of its chain in the dark with its head up, the moment Ward put his boots on the stable roof, and looked at him, and made a noise down in its chest that was not a bark yet.

Ward stayed where he was.

He stayed there on the stable roof with his stones dark and did not move and did not look at the dog, because a dog that is looked at knows it has been seen, and after a long while the dog lay down again at the end of its chain with its head on its paws and its eyes open. Declan, on the roof of the house, did not come down. Ward had not needed to tell him. He had needed, on the first night, to tell Declan three separate times with a look to stop talking, because Declan on a roof in the dark whispered, a thin running breath of a whisper that went on and on and was mostly about how cold it was and how the slates were greasy and what he would say to Saban about the model, and on the third night at the keep he had done it all the way up the wall. Tonight he was silent. Tonight he was on Hollin's roof with a dog under him and the wind in the wrong direction, and he went round the back of Hollin's chimney like a cat round a wall and put the knot in and came away, and it was the best Ward had ever seen him do anything.

Dray's house was easier. Dray's house had no dog, and a stack that nobody had swept in years, and a light burning in the room below it past the fourth bell, where the youngest of the five sat up late over something. Osric put the knot in with Dray's light coming up the flue under his hand, warm, and came away.

At the fifth bell they were all at the thorn.

The third night went wrong once.

It was Strade's night. Osric had done the first knot on her stack over the front room on the first night, the room over the porch where her light burned half the night, and it had gone in clean. On the third night Emeric wanted a second, at the back, over a room Saban's boy said was her steward's, because the first knot's reach did not cover the back of the house and a steward writes letters too. Ward gave it to Declan, because Declan had done Hollin's, and Rylan went with him, because the back of Strade's house was old brick and Ward did not like it.

Ward was on the corner of the square when it happened, on the roof of the Ducatis room, keeping the watch.

He saw Declan go up the back wall of Strade's house under the eave, pad and boot and boot and pad, and stop under the gutter, and reach up for the eave with his off hand, and set the pad. He saw Declan put his weight on it. He saw the pad come away from the wall with a piece of the wall on it, and Declan go backwards off the brick with nothing under him.

Rylan had him.

Rylan was three feet to one side on the same wall, and he had not been watching Declan, he had been watching the wall above him, and he put out his free hand without looking and took Declan by the back of his belt as he came off and held him. That was all. Declan swung once, a short arc at the end of Rylan's arm, and came back to the wall, and got a boot on it, and then the other, and the pads took, and he hung there against the brick for a moment with Rylan's hand still in his belt and his own hand, the off hand, empty, with a lump of old mortar the size of a fist still stuck to its pad. Nobody lit a stone.

Then Rylan let go of the belt, and Declan peeled the mortar off his pad and put it in his satchel, because it had to go somewhere and could not be dropped on a flagged yard at the third bell, and felt the wall above him with his hand, twice, the way the prince had said, and found a better place, and went on up.

At the thorn afterward Declan did not say anything about it. He did not say anything about anything. He took his helm off and sat down on the grass with his back against the thorn and his helm in his lap, and after a while he said, to Rylan, who was standing a little way off, "Thank you."

Rylan did not say anything. He nodded, once.

Ward saw it and filed it where he filed things.

The knots went in over three nights: seven that listened and eight that only passed things on, strung from the houses back through the town to the Ambry dovecote by roofs and a church tower and a stable vane and, once, the high end of a tannery drying shed. The last of them went in on the third night in the place Emeric had asked for after the second, when he had put his hand on the root in the grey of the morning and found that Hollin's knot reached it only in fits, like a man shouting across a river in a wind. It was a quarter of a mile of open ground, near enough, from Hollin's to the next. But it was not open ground. There was a malt house in the way.

Emeric named each of them into the root in the mornings, with the door locked and Wenna and Dace at the table under the window not watching him do it. PLACIT. STRADE, FRONT. STRADE, BACK. HOLLIN. DRAY. VENNOR. STAPLE. And the eight that only passed things on he named by where they were, the dovecote and the tower and the vane, so that if one of them failed he would know which, and he closed his hand over the root on the last morning and felt the whole of it, fifteen knots in a long crooked line through a sleeping town, each one there and humming very faintly with its two small stones taking turns, and he took his hand away.

Then nothing happened.

Nothing happened for three weeks.

He stayed. There were reasons to stay and they were real ones. Placit asked him to sit at the Staple's table on a weighing day, to learn, and he sat there for an afternoon and learned how a bale of rags was graded and what was taken on it and by whom, which he had known already from Ferra's copies of Sethe's letters and let Placit teach him anyway. Dray took him out to see a horse. The shop in front of the ink house sold paper across its counter to the clerks of the town, who came in to look at it and went away with a quire, and then came back for another. Wendel boiled. Hobb wrote the boilings down. Once, on a dark night, Emeric walked out to the thorn with Ward and was in Kaldo in an hour for a table that could not wait, and back in his bed at the inn before the bell, and Hobb on the landing had read two chapters to his door and drunk a posset.

And every morning he let himself into the back room, and the two printers stood on their trestles in the lime with their carriages parked in the corners of their beds and their heads full, and on each bed lay the same clean sheet that had lain there the morning before.

Wenna read her book. Dace oiled the rails, every day, whether they needed it or not, because Emeric had told him on the first evening that a rail left dry would stick, and nobody had ever told him to stop.

In the second week Emeric began to doubt the stones.

He did not say so. He went up onto the roof of the ink house one night with Ward and put his hand on the knot in the dovecote, which was the one he could reach, and felt it there, humming, awake, one of its two stones working and the other sitting by. He went through what he had written into the listening stones in his head, and could not find a fault in it, and that proved nothing, because a man who could find the fault in his own work would not have written it in the first place. He went back over the tests in the Kaldo yard, Pim at his bench and Osric pacing, and the thirty yards and the quarter mile, and could not find a fault in those either. He began to think about brass, and about soot, and about whether a knot pressed into fifty years of soot in a chimney pot could hear anything at all through the soot, and he had not tested that, and he knew he had not.

In the third week he tested it.

He took a bottle of the T ink out of his coat in his room at the inn and a pen and a board with a sheet pinned to it, and put the board under his coat, and went out after the second bell of the night by the yard door. He walked down the junction road to the square, and along the north side of the square past the Staple, where the lamp was lit, and past Placit's house, slowly, a gentleman walking off his supper, and in the shadow of Placit's porch, with the board against his chest under his coat and his back to the wall of Placit's little room over the kitchen, he wrote four words on the sheet by feel. Then he put the pen away, and walked on round the square, and back up the junction road, and let himself into the ink house by the yard door with his own key, and into the back room.

The left-hand printer was moving.

It was nearly done. The carriage was going back and forth across the foot of the sheet in short dry clicks, blotting, and then it stopped, and parked itself in the corner, and the sheet lay on the bed under the lamp.

PLACIT. T2. And the root's count.

Under it, four words in his own hand, crooked, because he had written them against his chest in the dark without looking: nobody is using it.

Dace, who had been asleep in his chair, woke up and looked at the sheet and then at Emeric and did not say anything. Emeric folded the sheet in four and put it in the stove.

"They're still buying the old ink," Saban said.

It was the next evening, at the inn table, with Ward and Hobb and the inn's dinner in front of them and Saban's boy gone out to the stable to see to something that did not need seeing to.

"Through Lisle's man," Saban said. "The same as always. It comes down from the north with the wool, in the spring and the autumn, and Lisle's factor sells it on at the Staple's back door to anybody who wants it, and all five of them want it, and have done for thirty years. Placit told them at his table to put your bottles on a shelf until they knew what was in them. Dray's steward told my boy. He was very put out about it. His lordship wanted to try the new ink and wasn't let." He turned his cup. "It's not a plot. It's old men and a gift from a stranger. They've got the old ink in the house, and they'll use it till it's gone, and then they'll see."

"And when is it gone?"

"When Lisle's man comes down with the autumn wool, they'll buy more, and it won't be gone." Saban drank. "Unless one of them's cheap."

Emeric looked at him.

"Hollin," Saban said, "has bought his last jar."

It came on a Thursday, at about the fourth bell of the afternoon, with the shop open and the town going about its business on the other side of the wall, and Emeric was not in the room when it began. Wenna came to the inn for him. She came up the stairs faster than a clerk walks, and stood in his doorway with her hand on the frame, and said, "It's moving," and went away again before he could get up.

When he got there the right-hand printer had nearly finished its page.

HOLLIN. 3. The count.

It was a list.

It was not Hollin's hand. It was a round, careful, laborious hand, a hand that had been taught its letters late and held its pen too hard, and it listed sheets, eleven, and napkins, four dozen, and the green hangings from the back parlour, and his lordship's shirts, nine, one to be turned at the collar, and it ended with a note that the laundress at the stream was to be told the soap was not to be the Kaldo soap because it took the colour out of the green. That was the whole of it. That was the first page the net ever gave them, and it was Lord Hollin's housekeeper counting his linen in his ink.

Wenna read it with her finger under each line, the way Ferra read everything. Dace read it over her shoulder. Emeric read it standing, twice, and then took it off the bed and put it on the table under the window, on the left side, where Ferra's note that came north with them had said the first sheet should go, and put Wenna's book on it to keep it flat.

Nobody said anything. After a moment Dace laughed, once, quietly, through his nose, and Wenna did not, and then did.

The second page came the next morning, in Hollin's own hand, which was square and fast and pressed hard at the ends of words, to a man at Milford, about a horse: its price, which was too high, and its hocks, which were not what he had been told, and whether the man at Milford thought he was a fool, which it was plain that Hollin thought he did. The third was Hollin again, about the price of rags. The fourth, that evening, was the same.

On the fifth day DRAY. 4. came down the right-hand printer: a letter to a horse dealer, in a young man's fast untidy hand that sloped uphill across the sheet. Dray's steward had told Dray that Hollin was writing in his, and Dray had had his own bottles down off the shelf inside the hour.

By the end of the week it was coming every day.

Ward went with him to the ink house on the last night of that week, late, because Emeric had not been able to sleep and had got up and dressed and Ward had heard him on the stair.

The shop was dark and the house was quiet, and they went through by the yard door and along the passage, and Emeric turned his key in Bram's lock and opened the door on the back room, and stood there.

The lamp on the table under the window was turned down low. Dace had gone up to his bed over the shop. Wenna had not. She was asleep in her chair at the end of the table, with her cheek on her hand and her book face down in her lap and a shawl round her shoulders that had slipped, and on the table beside her, weighted with a clay cup, were two small stacks of printed sheets, Hollin's and Dray's, and beyond them along the table's edge clean places for the other houses, with nothing on them yet.

In the dim light at the far end of the room the left-hand printer was moving.

Its carriage went back and forth across the bed in short dry clicks, blotting, with nobody watching it, at two in the morning, in a town where every soul in five great houses was asleep; and somewhere out in the dark of the square, in one of them, somebody was not.

Ward stood in the doorway behind him and did not say anything. After a while Emeric went in, quietly, so as not to wake her, and stood beside the printer and waited for the page.
