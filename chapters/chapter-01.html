Chapter 1 - Live Normally

The savings graph had started as a joke. Marcus built it in the second month of unemployment, back when he still thought of the gap as a scheduling problem: one column for the balance, one for the burn, a line chart he colored blue because blue looked calmer than red. By the sixth month the joke had a slope he could read without the axis labels. The line met zero in March.

He looked at that point the way he would have looked at a crack running out from a weld toe, mostly curious about how long it had been growing before anyone thought to measure it.

Eleven years as a mechanical engineer, and the market had decided it could manage without him for half a year.

The Meridian Bio listing came through a forum he didn't remember joining. Direct skill induction trial. Paid participants, healthy adults, no prior conditions. Eleven hundred a month plus mileage. Meridian was publicly traded, which meant there was a quarterly report somewhere promising shareholders that this would work, and Marcus read the report before he read the consent packet, because the report was where they'd tell the truth.

The report said very little. The packet said a great deal, most of it in the passive voice.

He signed in the second week of March.



The trial room had a reclining chair, a headset that sat over the ears and wrapped the back of the skull, and a tech who called him Fourteen from the first day. There were twenty-six of them, the tech said, all listed by number. Nobody met anybody else. Ninety minutes a week. Lie back, close your eyes, don't fight the hum.

"What does it do?" Marcus asked.

"You'll get the summary in your packet."

He had read the summary. It said the sessions delivered structured stimulation intended to support the acquisition of complex skills. It did not say which skills. It said participants should live normally between sessions and report anything unusual on the weekly form.

The intake form had eleven questions. The first was about headaches. The rest were about nausea, sleep, appetite, mood, dizziness, ringing in the ears, unusual tastes. Every one of them asked what might go wrong with him. None of them asked what he could do.

He filled it out carefully and handed it back, and the tech clipped it face down on a stack without looking at it.

That was the detail he kept turning over on the drive home. A company spends money on an eleven-question form and then doesn't read the answers. Either the answers didn't matter, or somebody else read them later, or the form existed so that it would exist. He couldn't decide which, and he didn't like any of them.

That night he took a spiral notebook out of the kitchen drawer where he kept takeout menus and dead batteries, wrote the date at the top of the first page, and started a file.



Denholm called him back within the month. Ninety-some people, a mid-sized shop that built assemblies for other people's machines, a hiring manager who apologized twice for how long it had taken. Marcus took the job and kept the trial. The sessions were Thursday evenings. Nobody at Denholm needed to know where he went on Thursdays.

He didn't notice the first thing. He found it afterward.

After session four he went to a diner because he didn't want to cook, and when the waitress came to clear his plate she asked if he wanted to keep the napkin. He looked down. He'd drawn a load path across it in ballpoint, a cantilevered bracket with the forces resolved through two bolts and a bearing face, neat arrows, a moment arm marked off. He didn't remember picking up the pen. The bracket wasn't anything he was working on.

He said no, she could take it. Then he said wait, and folded it into his jacket.

It went into the notebook that night. Session 4. Unprompted technical sketch. No memory of starting it. Content correct.

After session six it was harder to file as a curiosity.

He was on a call with Denholm's design team, half-listening, looking at a drawing package for a drive assembly someone had shared on screen. Three parts met at one interface: a bracket boss, the outside diameter of a sub-frame shaft, and the thread engagement on the fasteners that held them together. Each tolerance was fine on its own. Marcus looked at the three of them together and saw, without working it out, that at the bad end of all three the stack would lock. The shaft would seize in the boss on assembly, and nobody would find out until the first production run.

He said so. There was a pause on the call long enough for someone to unmute and mute again.

Someone ran the numbers. He was right. Denholm revised the drawing in two days.

The interface check on that drawing belonged to Doyle.



Doyle had been at Denholm eleven years, and his initials were on the interface check of every drawing that left the building. Marcus knew it from a single glance, which was all most things seemed to take now. He could close his eyes and see the stamp, the date beside it, the slight leftward lean of the D.

At the end of the month a note went into Marcus's file. It praised his technical contribution in one sentence and then spent a paragraph on communication and professionalism, on the importance of raising concerns through appropriate channels, on team cohesion. It was signed by his manager. It didn't need Doyle's name on it.

Marcus read it twice, said thank you for the feedback, and was pleasant to everyone for six weeks.

In those six weeks, on his lunch breaks, he went through eleven years of approval stamps in the shared archive. He didn't take notes, and didn't need to. What he found was simple once it was laid out: Doyle had stopped doing the interface check years ago and kept signing for it. There was a stretch in 2019 where his initials appeared on enough drawings, over eleven days, that doing the checks properly would have taken thirty hours a day.

He didn't take it to anyone. He sat down at lunch on a Thursday beside a quality engineer he liked and asked her one question, the kind a new hire asks about process. Whether interface checks were time-logged anywhere, or just signed. Whether anyone had ever audited how long they took.

She looked at him for a second longer than the question deserved. Then she said she didn't think so, and that it was a good question.

Quality ran an audit in June. By the time Marcus went in for session fourteen, Doyle was on a performance plan and nobody at his end of the building was saying his name.

Marcus noticed that he felt nothing about it. He noticed himself noticing, and he wrote that down too, in one line. Then he closed the notebook. A man with a good filing system can go a very long time without reading his own folders.



The tenth session was the week of Kevin's kid's birthday.

Kevin sat two desks over and had a laugh that carried. The party was in a backyard with a bouncy castle sagging on one side because nobody had staked it properly. Marcus brought a gift he'd chosen by reading the box. He stood by the cooler holding a paper plate and listened to Kevin's father-in-law tell another guest about his new heart medication, the name and the dose, and how he took it with his evening glass of something.

Marcus knew, flatly and completely, what the interaction would do to him. He knew the mechanism. He knew roughly how many days it would take to show up and what it would look like when it did.

He had never studied medicine or read a pharmacology text in his life.

He waited until the old man went to refill his drink and told Kevin, quietly, that he'd had a relative on something similar, and that it might be worth having his father-in-law ask the pharmacist about the evening drink. Kevin said huh, thanks, he would. Then Kevin gave him the look the quality engineer had given him, a beat too long, and Marcus went to watch the bouncy castle.

The lie had come out smooth. That went in the notebook as well.

Session twelve was smaller, and worse. He was walking a shop floor with a technician, and three steps before it happened he saw the man catch his boot on a floor-mounted pipe and go down onto his wrist. He said "pipe" a fraction before the man's foot got there. The technician stepped over it, looked back at it, looked at Marcus, and laughed it off.

Marcus didn't laugh. He'd seen the fall, start to finish, before the foot had moved. Instinct was the body flinching. This had run on ahead of him and come back with a clean result.



By the end of May the file had sections.

He hadn't planned them. They grew on their own, as filing systems do when the same kind of paper keeps arriving. The first section was his own trade. The trial had only sharpened his mechanical work, a stone taken to a blade that was already there. The rest were things he had never studied.

Materials. A cracked bracket sat on the scrap rack outside the test bay, and he picked it up on his way past and read the fracture face like a page: fatigue, started at a sharp inside corner, helped along by a heat treatment somebody had cut short. He put it back and told nobody, because he couldn't have said how he knew.

Software. The test rig's controller had been throwing the same fault for a week, and the man who'd written its code had left the company. Marcus opened the code on a Tuesday, in a language he had never learned, and found the mistake in twenty minutes. He told them he'd taken a course once.

Electrical. He knew a motor on the same rig had a shorted winding from the note in its hum, before anyone put a meter on it.

Chemistry. A coating supplier's batch kept failing to cure, and he knew why from the data sheet, two days before their lab did.

And medicine, from Kevin's backyard.

Six fields, one of them his. Each of the new ones had arrived whole, judgment attached, like a trade learned over twenty years. He had never held a scalpel or stood at a lab bench, and it didn't seem to matter. When he pictured the work he could feel his way through it, motion by motion, where the resistance would come and what came next, clearly enough that he trusted his hands to follow. Slowly. Nothing in him was quick at any of it. And each one felt faintly like someone else's handwriting that he could read perfectly.

That was the first kind of knowing. The second was stranger.

He remembered everything he had ever read. That went further back than the trial. The thermodynamics text from his second year of college, page by page, coffee ring and all. A children's book about volcanoes. A paperback history of canals he'd read on a delayed flight eight years ago and couldn't have quoted a line of the week after. All of it was there now.

But the books came back as pages, not as understanding. He could recite the canal book. He could not have built a lock from it without doing all the work of understanding it over again, and it was written for a world that already had cement and surveyors. And he could only find a thing if he remembered which book it was in.

He wrote the distinction down in two columns. It seemed important to keep them apart.



There were no side effects. That was the joke he kept to himself every Thursday when he filled in the weekly form: no headaches, no nausea, sleep fine, appetite fine, mood fine. All true. The form only wanted to know what was going wrong with him, and nothing was.

He reported none of the rest.

Page four of the consent packet, paragraph two, assigned to Meridian Bio all rights to any data, discoveries, methods or outcomes arising from participation in the study. He'd read it the first night. He read it again the night of the napkin. Outcomes arising from participation. If he walked in and said the headset had taught him medicine, the outcome arising from participation was him, and he had a fair idea what a publicly traded company did with an outcome like that. They would pull him out of the cohort and into a different room, one with more instruments and fewer chairs, and they would be very polite about it.

The monthly cognitive battery came at week four. He took it straight, because he didn't know yet that he shouldn't. The tech read the score once, then again, then wrote something on a different sheet.

At week eight he scattered errors through it like gravel. Not too many. A missed sequence here, a slow answer there, enough to land the score four points above week four, a gentle upward curve, consistent with cohort. The tech read it once and moved on.



He tried, for about a week, to get rich.

Cards were solved in an afternoon, which was exactly the problem: a casino would notice that before it noticed anything else. Options needed capital and information, and he had neither, only a better-than-average sense of which way a thing would fall once it was already falling. Patents he actually filed, four of them, small mechanical improvements that came to him whole in the shower. Under his employment agreement Denholm owned all four. He found that funny for longer than he expected to.

What he had left was the file.

By June it was about forty pages, written small. Dates, sessions, incidents, what he'd known and when, what he couldn't account for. Page by page it stopped being a log and became an argument, and the argument was simple enough to fit on the last page in three lines.

Meridian thought it was selling a shortcut. Train a technician in a month instead of a year. That was what the quarterly report promised, as far as it promised anything.

What Meridian actually had was a way to move the valuable part of a person somewhere else and keep it. And once you could do that, the person was the cheap part.

He sat with the notebook open at his kitchen table and read that line several times. It was the most important thing he'd ever written, and it would never make him a dollar. He kept writing anyway.



Session fourteen ended at seven-twenty. He walked home because the evening was warm and because walking was when the file wrote itself.

He was three pages ahead in his head, working out how many of the other twenty-five numbers were having the same months he was, and whether any of them were stupid enough to say so, when he saw the girl.

She was four, maybe, in a yellow jacket too heavy for the weather, and she had stepped off the curb after something only she could see. Her mother was ten feet behind her looking at a phone. The car coming down the lane was doing perhaps thirty, and the man driving it was looking at nothing at all, as people do on a road they've driven every day for years.

Marcus saw the whole of it before any of it happened: the bumper, the jacket, where she would go and how far.

He was already moving. He got both hands on the yellow jacket and shoved, hard, harder than you should ever push a child, and she went sprawling onto the grass verge and started to scream, and the scream was the best sound he had heard in months.

The car took him at the hip.

For about four seconds he was somewhere above it. He could see the street, the mother with her hand over her mouth, the driver getting out, the girl on the grass with mud on the yellow sleeve. It was very quiet, and he was very clear.

He thought about the notebook in the kitchen drawer. Forty pages, small handwriting, and in a week or so a landlord would hire someone to clear the apartment, and the someone would put the drawer's contents into a bag with the takeout menus and the dead batteries and carry it all down to the dumpster.

He thought, with something close to amusement, that the bills were settled either way.

Then the clarity went, and the quiet went with it.



He was standing.

The floor was stone. The ceiling was very high, and from it hung long banners threaded through with gold. There were people all around him, a great many of them, in clothes that didn't belong to any year he knew, and every one of them was looking at him.

At the far end of the hall, on a raised seat, a man wearing a crown was speaking. He was speaking down, the way a man speaks from a height to someone below him who has disappointed him for a long time, and he had already been speaking for a while.
