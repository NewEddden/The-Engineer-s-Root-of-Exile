Chapter 1 - Live Normally

Things had never come hard to Marcus, and he had been waiting most of his life for the bill.

He did not think of it that way when he was young. When he was young he thought of it the way a boy thinks of being tall: a fact about him, nothing he had done, nothing anyone could take back. He read early and fast. He took apart the toaster at six and put it back together at six and a half, and it worked, and his mother stood in the kitchen doorway holding the crumb tray he had not needed and did not say anything for a while. In school he learned things once. He finished high school at fifteen, an engineering degree at eighteen and a master's at nineteen, and people stopped calling him bright and started calling him a genius, which he pretended to find embarrassing. Physics was a language he already half spoke. Anything he could get his hands on, he could make his hands do, given a week, and usually less.

Not everything. He was a poor speller and a worse singer, he could not draw a face to save his life, and three years of Spanish had left him able to order a coffee and apologize. But the things that bent to him bent all the way, and by eighteen he had stopped noticing the effort other people put into them, which is the first step to not respecting it.

He assumed it was all going somewhere. That was the honest version. Given enough time he would do something large, and the large thing would bring the rest along behind it, the name, the money, the apartment with the long windows, the kind of life where other people's calendars bent around his. He did not hope for it. He planned for it, the way you plan for weather that has been forecast.

Underneath all of it, from as far back as he could remember, he had felt slightly off. He could never have said how. It was like wearing a coat that fit everywhere it was supposed to and still sat wrong on the shoulders. He mentioned it once to a college roommate, late, after the kind of night where people mention things, and the roommate said everybody felt that way, and Marcus said yes, of course, and knew that was not what he meant.

The bill came when he was twenty, and it came in an envelope.

The first seizure had been small enough to miss. He was at the bench in the graduate lab, soldering a breakout board for somebody else's electrode array, and the room skipped. That was all. One moment the iron was over the third pad and the next it was over the seventh, and the joints in between were done, and done well, and he had no memory of doing them. He sat looking at them for a long time. Then he did the eighth pad, because it needed doing.

The second one put him on the floor of a grocery store with a cut over his eye and a teenager in an apron holding his head off the tile. After that there were scans.

The neurologist was a tired, decent woman who had clearly given the speech before and had clearly never liked it. She put the images up side by side. Small bright marks in the white matter, scattered through the regions that handled memory and the learning of movement, the places where a body files what it knows how to do. Old, she said, most of them. Some of them very old. She would have guessed childhood, maybe earlier, if he had brought her a childhood scan to compare. He had not. He had been adopted as a baby by a couple already well into their forties, both of them dead now, and the nearest thing he owned to a family medical history was one page from the agency with most of its boxes left blank.

The new ones were the problem. There were new ones, at the edges of the old, and they were spreading. She did not have a name for it that she trusted. She had three names she did not trust, and a referral, and a number she gave him only because he asked for it twice: three to five years before the decline got serious, and she would be glad to be wrong.

He drove home and sat in the car in the lot behind his building for an hour with the engine off. Then he went upstairs, opened a beer he did not drink, and laughed out loud in the empty kitchen, once, at the ceiling.

"Well," he said. "There's the tax."

It was a joke. He made it a dozen times over the next year, to the few people he told, and it always got the small sorry laugh it was built for. He did not tell any of them that it was also the first time in his life he had admitted out loud that the gifts had come from somewhere, and that he had never once paid for them.



There had been people to mention things to, once. Six of them, after college, the kind of close where nobody needed a reason to call. Then weddings, and houses two hours out, and children, one after another, and nobody had done anything wrong. The group chat was still there. These days it was mostly pictures of other people's kids, and he liked every one of them, and could not remember the last time a question in it had been for him.

That was not quite the whole of it, and he knew it. Jonah had asked him to stand up at his wedding, the spring before the diagnosis, and Marcus had said yes and then missed the rehearsal dinner for a deadline that could have moved. He had been sure, at the time, that Jonah understood. Things had always been easy for Marcus, and he had got into the habit of assuming they were easy for the people around him too, including forgiving him. Jonah sent a card when he heard about the scans. It was a kind card. It was the longest thing Jonah had written him in two years.

The gym was the one thing he had not cut. It was a boxing gym over a tire shop by the rail yard, two rings and a wall of heavy bags patched with duct tape, run by a man named Ray who had stopped telling him he ought to compete somewhere in his second year and started telling him to keep his right hand home. Marcus had walked in at nineteen to work off a semester of vending machine dinners and stayed because nothing else in his week told him the truth so fast. Drop your hands and you got hit. Square up and you got hit. Nobody on the other end of a jab cared what your transcript said.

Ray had a chiropractor's skeleton poster taped up by the speed bags, curling at the corners, and he taught off it like scripture. The chin was a lever: turn the head fast enough and the brain hit the inside of its own skull, and the lights went out without any help from the rest of you. The liver sat under the right ribs where the cage gave out, and a left hook there dropped a man a full second after it landed, standing, wondering where his legs had gone. The solar plexus took the air out of him. The floating ribs broke. The kidneys you did not touch, not in Ray's gym, not unless you wanted to find another one.

After the scans the neurologist took sparring away from him. He had known she would. A brain that was already writing its own bright marks into itself did not need help from a plumber's overhand right on a Saturday. He told Ray in the parking lot, standing by Ray's truck, and Ray looked at him for a while and then said, "Bags, then," and that was the end of the conversation. So it was bags, three nights a week, and pads with Ray when Ray felt like it, and sitting on the apron on Saturdays watching other people get the truth told to them. He kept paying the sixty dollars. He would have paid more.



Meridian started at the kitchen table, because that was where everything he had ever started had started.

The logic was simple, and he had worked it backward from the end the way he worked everything. Nobody was going to cure what he had in three to five years, because nobody was working on it. It was too rare to fund and too strange to name. To get a team of real specialists on it, people who could read the scans and model the lesions and design the trials and build the instruments, you would need ten years just to train them, and the people who could train them were spread across four countries and mostly retired. He did not have ten years. He did not have money either, not the kind that buys specialists. What he had was a doctorate half finished in neural interfaces and hands that could build almost anything he could see.

So the problem was not the disease. The problem was that expertise moved too slowly. It moved one apprentice at a time, over decades, and it died with the people who carried it. If a working method could be lifted out of a trained mind and set down in another, you could build the team in months. Then you could build ten.

Everyone he explained it to said the same thing in different words, which was that it could not be done. Memory was not a file. Skill was not a file. The brain did not store a trade in any one place you could copy from, and even if it did, there was no way to write it back. He knew all the arguments. He could make them better than the people making them to him. And he did not believe them, not for a minute, and he could never explain why not.

That bothered him more than anything else about the whole business. He was a man who could show his work. Here he could not. He was simply sure, the way you are sure of the floor in a dark room you have lived in for years, and when people asked him where the certainty came from he gave them the published literature, which was thin, and the early animal work, which was thinner, and they went away thinking he was brave or deluded. He was neither. He knew it could be done the way he knew his own name.

He named the company after the line on a map, the one everything else is measured from, because it sounded like a place to stand. Meridian Bio. Two years old by the spring he died, and still small enough to fit in one rented floor above a dental practice: five people, a grant that renewed on prayer, one investor who had known his father, and a rig in the back room that nobody outside the company had ever seen work.

Dana Okafor ran the rig. She was six years older than him and a year behind him in the program, the only person he had ever met who learned as fast as he did and remembered to be kind about it, and she had joined him the week he told her about the scans, without being asked. Theo wrote the software. Ines handled the donors and the ethics paperwork and the long careful letters to review boards, and was the only one of them who ever told Marcus no to his face and made it stick.

The donors were the part he was proudest of. Retired people, mostly, at the end of long careers: a metallurgist who had spent forty years reading fracture faces for an aircraft maker, a hardware engineer, a chemist from a coatings firm, a man who had wired power plants, and a neurologist named Adebayo who had seen two cases like Marcus's in his whole life and wanted to see a third one live. They sat in the chair for an hour a week while the rig recorded them working problems they had spent their lives on, and they did it for nothing, or for lunch. Ines kept them anonymous on every document the company produced. Donor one, donor two. The numbers were her idea. She said a person ought to be able to give something away without having their name ground into it.

By the second winter the rig could take a donor's working method and hold it. That was the half nobody had believed, and they had done it, and it sat in the back room on four racks with a fan that whined, and they could not publish it, because the other half was writing it into somebody, and there was no ethical way to try that on anyone for years.

Ines had the timeline on a whiteboard. Animal work, then more animal work, then review, then a phase one with healthy volunteers, then a wait. Six years at the inside. Eight if anybody important got nervous. She wrote it out in green marker and underlined the end of it and then looked at him, because she already knew what he was going to say.

He waited until the others had gone home to say it to her, which was its own kind of cowardice, and he noticed that and filed it.

"There's one subject who can consent to everything," he said. "Who knows the risks better than any review board ever will. Who doesn't have six years."

"No," said Ines.

"I'm not asking you to run it."

"I know you're not. You're asking me to not stop you. That's worse." She capped the marker. "You think because everything's always worked for you, this will. That's not a reason, Marcus. That's a habit."

He did not have an answer to that, and she did not wait for one. She resigned the following Monday, in a letter that was kind and very precise and did not leave him anything to argue with, and she shook his hand at the door and told him she hoped she would hear he had been right. Dana ran the first session three days later. Theo stayed late to watch the numbers, because he said somebody ought to be looking at them who was not in the chair.



The intake form had eleven questions. Ines had written it before she left.

Headaches. Nausea. Dizziness, disturbed sleep, appetite change, ringing in the ears, numbness in the hands or feet. Mood changes noted by yourself or by others. Difficulty concentrating. Question eleven asked whether he had experienced anything he considered unusual, and gave him four ruled lines for it.

Disturbed sleep made him stop for a second. He had one dream that came back: a wall at night, wind coming off water somewhere below, and a woman beside him talking about bridges. He had never once seen her face. But he had been having it since he was eight, so it was not a change, and the form asked about changes.

He wrote No eleven times, signed the log, and handed the clipboard to Dana.

Dana read every line, because she was Dana, and then she put him in the chair.

After the first session the plan told him to live normally. They would monitor for changes. Dana said it to him out loud, reading off her own protocol, and then laughed at herself, because the man who had written the protocol was sitting in front of her with sensors glued behind his ears.

"Live normally," he said. "I'll give it a try."

Normally had not included much dating, even before. That one had never taken, and not for lack of offers. Women looked at him twice in grocery lines. Men did too, now and then, and said so more plainly. At nineteen he had borrowed a guitar and learned four songs in one winter because that was what boys did to get a girl to look up, and later he did the rest of it too, the apps, the dinners friends set up, the second drinks. Most of the dates went well. Some ended at a door with somebody leaning in, and he could have gone inside. His body was willing enough; it usually was. That was never the problem.

The problem was that his heart never moved. He could feel the moment where it was meant to turn, the look across the table that was supposed to become something, and it stayed exactly where it was. Pushing on past that point would have been manners, nothing else. He'd have been kissing someone so as not to be rude. He couldn't do that to them, and he found he couldn't do it to himself either, so he stopped at doors and said good night and meant it kindly. The companion models in the mall kiosks, built from the ground up to be wanted, moved him even less. He had looked the words up, late, more than once. None of them fit. Not this, not that, maybe nothing at all.

Most nights he settled on nothing: he was a man who simply didn't. Some nights he didn't believe it, because the missing had a shape to it. It felt less like an absence than like somebody he had lost. Or two. He never knew who, and he filed it with the tune.

The woman who raised him used to say he had hummed before he could talk: one tune, the same few bars, over and over, nothing she had ever sung him and nothing on any radio. Three notes stepping down, and one long one that climbed and stopped. He still did it at the bench without noticing. Dana had started humming it back at him in the second year, to tease him, and gotten it wrong in the same place every time, and he could not tell her how it went after that, because he had never once got to the end of it either.



What nobody had warned him about, because nobody could have, was how well it worked.

Halfway through session four he caught himself sketching a load path on a napkin at a diner counter, tracing a force down through a stack of components, member to member, all the way to the ground. That one was his already. That had always been his. But he had done it in the margin of a napkin waiting on his eggs without noticing he had started, and the lines came out cleaner than his lines had ever come out, and that part was not his.

By session six the rig itself had started to look wrong to him. It was a Tuesday, nothing on the calendar, and he was half listening to Theo walk through a firmware change and drawing in the margin of his notepad, the same small mark he had put in the margins of everything since grade school, a ring with one stroke through it that stopped short of the far side, when his eye caught on the mount for the coil housing and would not let go. Three parts. Three suppliers. Each tolerance fine on its own. Stacked worst case on worst case, the assembly would not close, and it had not been closing for months. That was the vibration they had been chasing since autumn.

He waited for the doubt. It usually showed up right about here, the second-guessing, the surely somebody already checked this. It did not come. What came instead was certainty, quiet and ordinary, the same certainty you have that a door you just walked through was a door.

"Can we back up," he said, cutting Theo off mid-sentence without entirely meaning to, and showed him.

Theo looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at Marcus. "You checked that mount in October," he said. "I watched you."

"I know."

"So what's different?"

Marcus did not have a good answer, and Theo, who was not stupid, did not ask again. He wrote something in his own log that evening. Marcus did not ask to see it.



By session ten it had stopped staying in its lane entirely.

It was a Saturday, a birthday party for Dana's nephew, and Marcus was standing at the edge of a backyard holding a beer he was not really drinking when Dana's father started coughing. Wet, effortful, the kind everyone at the table politely agreed not to hear. The old man waved it off. Heart medication, a new one, doctor still sorting the dose.

He said the name of the drug and the number in the same breath, and something in Marcus clicked into place that had no business clicking. A whole shelf slid out where a second earlier there had been nothing. Contraindications. Interaction profiles. The specific mechanism by which that drug, at that dose, in a man that age with that cough, could be doing more damage than the thing it was written for.

It was Adebayo's shelf. He knew that at once, the way you know a friend's handwriting on an envelope. Medicine had been the point of loading the neurologist, and it had come in whole, the way the old man had spent forty years learning it, and it was not only the brain. Apparently it was all of it.

"You should mention that cough to whoever set the dose," he said, working to make it sound like a guess. "Sooner than your next appointment."

Dana's father squinted at him. More amused than offended, but not by much. "Dana says you build machines. You a doctor now?"

"No." Too fast. He heard it land wrong on the way out.

Across the yard Dana had gone still with a paper plate in her hand, in the particular way of someone recalculating what she knew about a man she had worked beside for six years. He caught the look and read it clean. She called her father's cardiologist herself on the Monday. She did not say anything to Marcus about it until Thursday, and what she said then was, "How many?"

"All five," he said. "So far."



By the end of May the notebook had sections.

He had not planned them. They grew the way a filing system grows when the same kind of paper keeps arriving, and when he finally sat down one night and looked at what he had, there were six, and only one of them was his.

That one was mechanical, and the rig had not so much added to it as sharpened it, a stone run along an edge that was already there. The napkin, the stack-up. He had been good before. He was something else now, and he could not have said where one stopped and the other started.

The other five were the donors'.

Materials came off a scrap bin behind the machine shop downstairs. A cracked bracket, tagged for the trash, and he picked it up on his way past for no reason and read the fracture face like a page: fatigue, started at a sharp inside corner nobody had bothered to radius, helped along by a heat treatment somebody had cut short to make a ship date. He put it back in the bin and told nobody, and that night he wrote donor one beside it in the notebook.

Software came on a Sunday, in Theo's code, which he had never been able to read properly. He opened it at the kitchen table and found the bug Theo had been hunting for a week, and then, without meaning to, three ways a stranger could have walked into the rig from outside, and wrote them on the back of a printout because it seemed rude to leave them lying open. When he looked up, the apartment had gone dark around him and he could not have said when.

Electrical he heard. The rig's cooling fan had a shorted winding, and he knew it from the note in its hum, a little flat of where it ought to sit, a day before anybody put a meter on it. When the rewound motor ran clean he put his hand flat on the housing, the way he did with anything he had finished, and said, "That'll hold," to nobody in particular.

Chemistry was the dental practice downstairs, whose new resin kept failing to cure. He read the data sheet over the receptionist's shoulder in the elevator and knew why, and told her, and she looked at him as though he had offered to pull her teeth.

Medicine was the backyard. Medicine was also his own scans.

Each of them had come in whole, judgment and all, like a trade learned over twenty years of somebody else's working days. He had never held a scalpel or stood at a bench with a pipette, and it did not seem to matter. When he pictured the work he could feel his way through it, motion by motion, where the resistance would come and what came after, clearly enough that he trusted his hands to follow. They would follow slowly. Nothing in him was quick at any of it. And all of it read faintly like someone else's handwriting, every letter legible, none of the letters his.

That was the first kind of knowing. The second was stranger.

He remembered everything he had ever read. Not only since the sessions. The thermodynamics text from his second year of college came back page by page, coffee ring and all. So did a children's book about volcanoes, and a paperback history of canals he had read on a delayed flight eight years ago and could not have quoted a line of the week after.

But the books came back as pages. He could recite the canal book. He could not have built a lock from it without doing all the work of understanding it over again, and it had been written for a world that already had cement and surveyors. And he could only find a thing if he remembered which book it was in.

He drew a line down the middle of a fresh page and wrote the two kinds of knowing in two columns. One was what he could do. The other was what he could recite. He was careful, afterward, to keep them apart.

None of it reached the gym. He had half expected it to. The first Saturday after session four he had gone up the stairs over the tire shop with a slightly guilty curiosity, to see whether some stranger's decades would turn up in his hands on the bags. They did not. Ray held pads for him and told him his left was coming home low, which it had been coming home low since February, and Marcus sat on the apron afterward with his wraps in his lap and felt better than he had all week. The one place the two touched was Ray's poster. After session ten he stood in front of it with his wraps half on and could name everything under the skin it did not bother to show, the vessels that ran along the liver's underside, the nerve that sat behind the jaw's hinge, why the liver took its second and the chin did not. Ray caught him staring and asked if he was planning to operate. Whatever the rig was pressing into him had nothing to say about where a man's chin was. That he still had to learn the slow way, a round at a time, and it was the one thing left he was sure was his.



The cost turned up in June.

He had been waiting for one. He had budgeted for a drawback the way he budgeted for anything, and for eleven sessions the invoice never arrived, and in a system that expensive a missing bill is only a bill you have not found yet. He found it on the fourth of June, in the hospital's imaging suite, on a screen he could now read better than the technician running it.

The bright marks had moved. Not the old ones. The new ones, at the edges, which the neurologist had said were spreading, were spreading faster. In the same regions the rig wrote to. Memory, and the learning of movement. The places where a body files what it knows how to do.

He did not need anybody to explain it to him. That was the joke, if he wanted one, and he did not much. The first useful thing the neurologist in his head ever told him was that the sessions were putting the knowledge exactly where the damage was, and that the damage was answering. Three to five years had been the number before. He sat in the car in the hospital lot and worked the new one himself, from the scans, twice, and got the same answer both times, and it was not years.

He did not tell Dana that night. He told himself he would tell her at the next session, which was a lie, and he noticed it was a lie, and filed that too, because a man with a good filing system can go a very long time without reading his own folders.

What he did instead, at the kitchen table, was the thing Ines would have done. He wrote it down. All of it. The sessions and the scans and the six sections and the two columns, the timeline on the whiteboard and the one he had skipped, and then the part he had been avoiding since session four, which was what the rig was going to be once it left the back room.

Because he could see it now, with five careers in his head to see it with. He had built the machine to save one man by copying a team into him. That was the least interesting thing it could do. Compress a four-year degree into a quarter. Reskill a workforce over a weekend. Send a terraforming crew to Mars knowing everything the job needed before the ship had cleared the Moon, instead of learning it in the seven months out. That was what an investor would hear, and it was all true, and none of it was the real thing.

People had hated the androids from the first year they were sold, and for a while they had said so with bricks: a few warehouse fires, a summer when the care-home models needed police at the doors. It burned out the way those things do. The machines were cheaper than the fires, and in the end everyone learned to live with them, because there was no version of the world where they went away. Nobody ever settled whether there was anyone inside. They said they were afraid of being switched off, and they said it well, and every test anyone designed only proved how good they had become at giving people what people needed to hear. Marcus had stopped trying to decide. The androids had taken the work a body could do. What sat on four racks in his back room could take the rest.

It was a way to lift the part of a person that made them worth employing and set it down somewhere else. Ten years out and you do not hire the engineer, you license him. Twenty and whether he is alive becomes an accounting question, because the expensive part of him has already been moved and stored, and the rest of him eats.

He had built that. Not meaning to. He had built it the way he had built everything, fast and well and because he was sure, and Ines had stood at the whiteboard and told him that being sure was a habit, and he had let her walk out the door.

At two in the morning he finished the letter. It ran to forty pages, and it was addressed to Dana, and it said what the rig was, and what it had done to him, and what he thought it would do in the wrong hands, and what she should do about it if he was not there to argue. He sealed it in an envelope and put it in the kitchen drawer with the takeout menus, the dead batteries, and another sealed envelope, from an adoption agency, that had followed him through every apartment he had ever rented.

He had never opened that one. He thought about it, standing there with the drawer open. Then he shut the drawer, because there would be time, and he knew while he was thinking it that there would not.

Then he went to bed, because it was Tuesday.



He went to session fourteen anyway, and signed the log, and wrote none of it down.

He was quiet through the afternoon. Dana asked him twice whether he was all right and he said yes both times and she let him, which was not like her, and he understood later that she had already seen the June scan on the shared drive and was waiting for him to say it. He did not say it. That evening it was warm, and he went out on foot the way he did most evenings that June, the same loop of blocks around his building. By now it was automatic. He could have done it blindfolded, could have done it asleep.

There was a problem in his head and it was a good one. He had been turning it since he came down the front steps, and somewhere around the second block the street went the way the apartment had gone that Sunday with Theo's code, dark at the edges, dropped out of the frame, filed as solved. He did not decide to stop watching it. That was the thing about the focus. It never asked.

His mind came all the way back to the surface at the sight of a little girl stepping into the road.

After that nothing arrived in an order he could file.

A woman on her phone. A hand holding a smaller hand and then not holding it. A kid in a yellow jacket taking two quick delighted steps off a curb, because a curb is the most interesting geography a four-year-old knows. The math was available to him instantly and completely and it was bad, and every number in it said the same thing, and his body went anyway, some borrowed reflex getting there ahead of him.

He got the kid clear.

He did not get clear himself.

A crash, though he was not sure he heard it so much as arrived after it. Then the ground, and no pain in it, and no feeling attached to any of it, like a room going quiet around an alarm nobody had registered yet.

Then the strange unhurried part, the part nobody is supposed to get.

He seemed to be standing a few feet above the street looking down at the shape of himself, a dented bumper, a woman screaming into a phone at an operator. If there was a later, he wanted to know how long that actually lasted. It felt like minutes. Realistically it was four seconds. That gap alone was worth a session's thinking, assuming he ever got to think again.

He could not tell whether the calm was shock or whether it was the lesions or the rig, and he found he was interested in the question, which answered it about as well as anything could.

He noted that a couple of seconds before the crash the woman had been far enough into whatever was on that phone to let go of her daughter's hand. He noted the driver's face through the windshield, the particular blankness of a man dissociating at forty miles an hour. Two people in the same intersection and neither one present in it.

Three, he corrected.

He found that funnier than he probably should have.

He ran the notification order, because it was the kind of thing he ran. Dana first. She would find the letter, because she would go looking for one, and she would read every line of it, because she was Dana. Theo after that. Ray, when he did not come up the stairs on Thursday. Ines would hear from Dana, and would not say I told you, and would think it, and be right. The friends in a month or two, from each other, and they would be sorry, and mean it. Jonah would write something. It would be kind.

He waited to feel something about the whole business and nothing came, and he decided, since a man ought to land somewhere, that he would settle for not minding much. He had been budgeting for a slower ending. This one at least had a kid on the far curb in a yellow jacket, crying, alive, which was more than the scans had offered.

The last thought he had that belonged entirely to Marcus was about the drawer in the kitchen, and the two envelopes in it: one he had never opened, and one he had never said out loud.

The second to last was that the tax was paid, either way.

Then the street was not there.

He was standing in a room with ceilings too high for the space and gold-threaded banners hanging dead still in air that did not move, and there were people ringed all around him in the kind of silence a crowd only makes on purpose, and a man in a crown was talking down at him from somewhere above his eye line in a tone he recognized instantly and from no particular memory at all.

Who in the fuck is this man talking down to me, and why?
