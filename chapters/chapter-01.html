Chapter 1 - Session Fourteen

The intake form had eleven questions, and Marcus had learned to answer every one of them honestly without telling Meridian Bio a thing.

Headaches. No. Nausea, dizziness, disturbed sleep, appetite change, ringing in the ears, numbness in the hands or feet. No, no, no, no, no, no. Mood changes noted by yourself or by others. No. Difficulty concentrating.

He held the pen over that one a beat longer than the rest, the way a man pauses over a joke he can't tell in the room he's in, and wrote No.

Question eleven asked whether he had experienced anything he considered unusual. There were four ruled lines for it, which told him exactly how much unusual the study had been designed to accommodate. He could have filled a notebook. He had filled a notebook, and it was at home, in a spot he had thought about far too carefully for a notebook.

He wrote No, signed the log, and handed the clipboard back.

The tech thanked him without reading it. That part still bothered him. Fourteen weeks of a stranger's professional life being pressed into the meat behind his eyes, and the paperwork protecting all of them from that was eleven questions long and nobody checked the answers.

Direct skill induction, the recruiter had called it. Cognitive scaffolding transfer, according to the consent packet. In practice it was a headset, a chair that reclined farther than any chair needed to, and ninety minutes a week of a team's decades of engineering knowledge and method being worked into the grooves of his brain like a foot into wet sand.

Human lab rat, if you wanted to put it kindly. He mostly did.

He'd signed six months to the day after the layoffs, in a waiting room that smelled like new carpet and old coffee. The savings he'd spent a decade stacking were draining at a rate he could plot on graph paper, and had, and the line hit zero in March. He'd put applications in the week it happened. Denholm. Metalworks. Furna. A dozen others he stopped tracking. All of them took their sweet time, and rent didn't care about anyone's hiring timeline.

Then the trial invitation came, and it came laid out flat. Publicly traded company. Clean standing. The study announced to the world with the recipients listed by number and every personal detail stripped out. Somebody had made it close to impossible to quietly bury a body, and that, more than the money, was what got him to say yes.

He'd gone in skeptical, though not for the reason everyone else was. Friends kept reaching for the horror-movie version, the one where the recipient loses his mind or starts leaking out his ears. Marcus's doubt ran a layer under that, down where an engineer looks at what a system implies instead of what it's sold as. If knowledge could be pressed into a man from outside, then some working assembly of a person was portable. Nobody at the company had said that part out loud. He doubted they ever would. He sat with it a good deal longer than the consent form deserved and signed anyway, because by then there wasn't a real decision left in it.

They were testing several things at once and needed the sample wide, so they'd taken a mix. People deep in a discipline and people who'd never studied anything at all. The brain was a marvel nobody had ever put a name on the deed for. Two and a half petabytes of storage, give or take, running an absurd load of passive computation, and doing all of it on twenty watts. Twenty watts ran a desk fan.

Marcus did that arithmetic more than once in the recliner while the headset warmed up, because it beat the other arithmetic, the one that ended with him hollowed out and racked in a server room as somebody's macabre cold storage. Papers were signed either way.

The team emailed him after the first session and told him to live normally. They'd monitor for changes.

Within the month the small manufacturing firm he'd interviewed with called back and offered him a seat. Denholm paid well. The trial covered what it didn't. For a while the two schedules ran alongside each other without touching. One morning a week he drove out to the facility, put the headset on, let the chair recline past anything structurally reasonable, and let some stranger's career get pressed into him in ninety-minute doses. The other days he was the new guy, learning where the good coffee was hidden and which drawings were trustworthy and which were aspirational.

Nobody at the firm knew what he did on the mornings he came in late. Nobody at the trial knew what he was doing with what they gave him.

The division held. What nobody had warned him about was how well it worked.

He knew what this looked like from the outside. Every story about a man handed sudden genius ended one of two ways. The pill wore off and you went back to being furniture, or you got the flowers-for-a-dead-mouse treatment and kept it just long enough to watch it leave. He filed both under things to worry about after rent.

Halfway through session four he caught himself sketching a load path on a napkin at a diner counter, tracing a force down through a stack of components, member to member, all the way to the ground. That was a thing good engineers took years to do without effort, following the chain instead of reading one part in isolation. He'd done it in the margin of a napkin waiting on his eggs.

By session six, Denholm's numbers had stopped agreeing with themselves.

It was a Tuesday call, nothing on the invite marked urgent, the usual half hour with the applications engineer walking Procurement and Quality through the machined brackets for the new housing assembly. Marcus was half listening, drawing in the margin of his notepad, and then his eye caught on a tolerance callout for the mounting boss and wouldn't let go. He pulled the mating part up on the second monitor. Then the fastener spec behind that. Three components. Three drawings. Three engineers who had each done their own job correctly and never once talked to each other. Every tolerance fine on its own. Stacked worst case on worst case, the assembly wouldn't close.

He waited for the doubt. It usually showed up right about here, the second-guessing, the surely somebody already checked this. It didn't come. What came instead was certainty, quiet and ordinary, the same certainty you have that a door you just walked through was a door.

"Hey, can we back up?" He cut into the applications engineer mid-sentence without quite meaning to. "The boss tolerance on the bracket, the shaft OD on the sub-frame, the fastener's minimum engagement length. Run those worst case together and there's no clearance left. It's going to lock up."

The silence on the line came in two parts. The first was the newcomer speaking at all. New hires didn't see a problem like that, and on the rare occasion they did, they didn't have the nerve to say it out loud with three departments listening. The second part was eight people quietly pulling up drawings they'd been pretending to have memorized.

"...Let me check that," the applications engineer said, in the voice of a man who was going to be checking it for the rest of the afternoon.

He was right. Denholm took two days to confirm it and revise the drawing. It cost the company nothing but the mild indignity of Procurement asking Marcus three separate times how he'd caught it, like the answer might come out different if they kept asking.

He didn't have a good answer. Two months ago he wouldn't have known to look, and if he'd looked he wouldn't have trusted himself. No way these experts made a mistake an amateur like me could find. Now the looking just happened on its own, unprompted, the way you notice a crooked picture frame in someone else's house.

It wasn't until the drive home that it occurred to him to wonder what session ten was going to feel like, if six felt like this.

By session ten it had stopped staying in his field entirely.

It was a Saturday. A coworker's kid's birthday, of all places, and Marcus was standing at the edge of a backyard holding a beer he wasn't really drinking when the coworker's father-in-law started coughing. Wet, effortful, the kind everyone at the table politely agreed not to hear. The old man waved it off. Heart medication, a new one, doctor still sorting out the dose.

He said the name of the drug and the number in the same breath, and something in Marcus clicked into place that had no business clicking. A whole shelf slid out where a second earlier there had been nothing at all. Contraindications. Interaction profiles. The specific mechanism by which that drug, at that dose, in a man that age with that cough, could be doing more damage than the thing it was prescribed for.

He'd never studied medicine. A year ago he couldn't have reliably told you the difference between systolic and diastolic. The knowledge arrived whole anyway, confident, uninvited, same delivery as the tolerance stack-up in session six. Except this time there was no napkin, no CAD file, nothing anywhere in his life that explained why he'd know it.

"You should mention that cough to whoever adjusted the dose," he said, working to make it sound like a guess. "Sooner than your next appointment."

The old man's eyes narrowed. More amused than offended, but not by much. "You a doctor now?"

"No." Too fast. He heard it land wrong on the way out.

"Mechanical and software, right? Kevin said you build brackets and mess around with code." He wasn't letting go of it the way he was supposed to. "So how do you know what my heart medication does?"

"Read about it somewhere. Had a relative on something similar."

The lie came out smoother than the truth would have, and that bothered him more than the lie did.

Two seats down, Kevin had stopped mid-conversation to watch it happen, gone still in the specific way of a host recalculating what he actually knows about a guest he invited. Marcus caught the look and read it clean. Not suspicion yet. A data point Kevin would sit with later, the same way Marcus was learning to sit with his own.

The old man laughed it off eventually. The laugh came a beat too late to undo the pause in front of it, and he didn't bring the cough up again for the rest of the afternoon.

On the drive home Marcus did the math nobody at the trial had asked him to do out loud. Session four, structural, adjacent to his own work, explainable as an unusually good teacher. Session six, still mechanical, still his lane. Session ten, a domain he had no connection to whatsoever, arriving with the same unearned fluency as everything else.

Whatever the induction was doing had stopped respecting the fences around what he knew. It had stepped into somebody else's yard.

He got as far as opening the email client at a red light.

Then he worked it forward, the way he now worked everything forward, and shut the phone off before the light changed.

Say he told them. An effect outside the protocol was an adverse finding whether or not it felt adverse, and an adverse finding got a subject pulled while somebody senior decided what it meant. Pulled meant the stipend stopped. It also meant a very different set of appointments, because the thing he'd be reporting was not I have a headache, it was I know things you never gave me, and a company that had spent nine figures learning to move the useful parts of a person from one skull to another would not treat that as a complaint. They'd treat it as a result. He would stop being subject fourteen and start being the reason the building existed.

He'd signed away his rights to anything the study produced on page four, paragraph two. He remembered the paragraph. He remembered all of them now.

And there was the small, ugly, practical fact underneath all of it. They hadn't asked. Eleven questions about symptoms and not one about capability. He could answer every line on that form truthfully for the rest of his life and never come close to the truth.

He didn't send the email. He never sent it.

The monthly battery was the harder problem, and he solved that too, which he understood afterward was the moment he'd actually decided.

They ran a cognitive assessment every fourth session. Pattern completion, digit span, a timed spatial rotation task, a reading comprehension block that was too easy to be measuring comprehension. He'd sat the first one at week four and answered it straight, and the number that came back had been high enough that the tech running it read the screen twice.

At week eight he saw the ceiling of every item within a few seconds of reading it, and spent the rest of the hour deciding where to stop. Not low. A subject who got worse would draw more attention than one who got better. He wanted a curve, gentle and unremarkable, the kind that made a research assistant write steady improvement, consistent with cohort and move on to the next file.

The part he was proudest of, and he noticed he was proud, which he also filed away, was the distribution. Missing only the hardest items is a signature. Nobody performs like that. Real people lose two easy ones to boredom, blow a medium one on a misread, get a hard one right by accident. So he scattered them. He picked which questions to fail the way you'd pick where to put wear on a forged part.

He walked out of week eight with a score four points above week four and no interest at all from anyone in the building.

He thought about the other recipients on the drive back. Twenty-six of them, listed by number, personal details stripped. He'd spent an evening trying to find one and gotten exactly as far as the design of the disclosure had intended him to get. He couldn't ask the staff whether anyone else was experiencing this, because the asking was the disclosure. So he had one data point, himself, and no way to know whether he was the anomaly or the product.

By twelve, the focus started.

Hours at a stretch on any problem at all, the world dropping away at the edges until only the thing in front of him stayed lit. It reminded him, oddly, of the way his college friends used to describe Adderall, except there was no jaw clench at the end of it and no crash on the far side. He surfaced from six hours on a heat-transfer problem one Sunday to find the apartment dark, the coffee cold, and himself perfectly fine.

It didn't stay at his desk either. At Denholm one afternoon he watched a coworker walking a line across the design floor and saw it coming the way you see the end of a sentence before somebody finishes saying it. Somebody had left a length of small-diameter pipe on the floor. Given the man's stride, he'd roll his foot on it, lose his balance backward, and catch his head on the edge of a desk on the way down.

Part of Marcus wanted to watch. The part that liked confirming a prediction. It wanted the data, and the data cost one concussion.

He let that part lose.

"Hey," he said, already moving, "pipe on the floor, about three steps ahead of you. Watch it."

It should have been crushing. It wasn't. No pressure behind the eyes, no nosebleeds, no overload, nothing to point at and say there, that's the cost. He kept waiting for the bill. Week after week it didn't come. Take away the Thursday drive and the fact that he could answer damn near any question put to him, and there was no way to tell anything had changed at all.

So he did what any man does when he's handed something enormous and no instructions. He tried to get rich with it.

He made a list, because a list was what he had instead of a plan. Cards, first, which was the stupidest and therefore the fastest to cross off. The math was trivial and had been trivial for sixty years, which was why every casino in the country had solved it. Options next. He built the model in an evening, ran it, and found the flaw sitting where he'd have found it in anyone else's work. The model wanted information, and being smart did not produce information. He had no capital, no positions, no idea what a company's quarter looked like from the inside. Genius wasn't a telescope. It was a very good pair of hands with nothing in them.

Patents were real. Four of them, at least, sitting unclaimed in problems he'd watched Denholm walk past. Every one of them was work he'd done on Denholm's clock with Denholm's drawings, and his employment agreement was four pages long and he'd read it the way he now read everything.

What he kept circling back to, at two in the morning, in the notebook he wouldn't have been able to explain if anyone found it, was that he was already sitting on the most valuable thing in his life and had signed it away for eleven hundred a month plus mileage.

Meridian thought they were selling training. Compress a four-year degree into a quarter. Reskill a workforce over a weekend. That was the pitch to the market and it was, by a wide margin, the least interesting thing the machine could do. What they actually had was a way to lift the part of a person that made him worth employing and set it down somewhere else. Ten years out, you don't hire the engineer. You license him. Twenty, and the question of whether he's still alive becomes an accounting matter, because the expensive part of him has already been moved and stored, and the rest of him eats.

Marcus, in a recliner, at eleven hundred a month, was the working prototype of a labor market where the labor doesn't need to be kept.

He didn't write that part in the notebook. He didn't need to.

By session fourteen he had stopped worrying about the company's secret and started worrying about his own. Structural and software fit inside his own field. Medicine didn't, and neither did a pipe he'd seen three steps out. If the fences came down that easily, he had no idea what was still standing on the far side of the one that used to be him.

He signed the log for the day, same as always, and wrote none of it down.

He was pensive through the afternoon, and he walked back the same route he always walked. By now it was automatic. He could have done it blindfolded, could have done it asleep.

There was a problem in his head, and it was a good one. He'd been turning it since the parking lot, and somewhere around the second block the street had gone the way the apartment went on that Sunday, dark at the edges, dropped out of the frame, quietly filed as solved. He didn't decide to stop watching it. That was the thing about the focus. It never asked.

His mind snapped all the way back to the surface at the sight of a little girl stepping into the road.

After that, nothing arrived in an order he could file.

A woman on her phone. A hand holding a smaller hand and then not holding it. A kid in a yellow jacket taking two quick delighted steps off a curb, because a curb is the most interesting geography a four-year-old knows. Marcus was moving before he decided to move, some borrowed reflex getting there ahead of him.

He got the kid clear.

He didn't get clear himself.

A crash, though he wasn't sure he heard it so much as arrived after it. Then the ground, and no pain in it, and no feeling attached to any of it, like a room going quiet around an alarm nobody had registered yet.

Then came the strange unhurried part, the part nobody's supposed to get. He seemed to be standing a few feet above the street looking down at the shape of himself, a dented bumper, a woman screaming into a phone at a 911 operator. If there was a later, he wanted to know how long that had actually lasted. It felt like minutes. Realistically it was four seconds, maybe less. That gap alone was worth a whole session's thinking, assuming he ever got to think again.

He noted, without much feeling, that a couple of seconds before the crash the woman had been far enough into whatever was on that phone to let go of her daughter's hand. He noted the driver's face through the windshield, the particular blankness of a man dissociating at forty miles an hour. Two people in the same intersection, neither one of them present in it.

Three, he corrected.

He'd seen a man's fall three steps before it happened, and he hadn't seen a car he'd been walking toward for half a block. He found the irony funnier than he probably should have. Had he been paying attention, he could have had the girl clear and been clear himself.

So there it was. The bill, arriving all at once, at the end.

Then the shout came.

"Emeric!"

A man on a high ledge, calling a name that wasn't his.

One moment there'd been a street, a car, a woman, a child, bystanders arriving from three directions at once. The next he was standing in a room with ceilings too high for the space and gold-threaded banners hanging dead still in air that didn't move. The man shouting wore a crown. His face had gone rigid with something that could have been rage or could have been disappointment.

Who in the flying fuck is this man yelling at me, and why?

That was the last clear thought that belonged entirely to Marcus.

In the same breath, a dam went.

Twenty-two years arrived at once, not as a story but as a texture, the way a smell arrives before you've decided to notice it. He knew the banners were his father's colors. He knew the shape of this room the way you know your childhood kitchen with your eyes shut, hip-checking the same drawer for twenty years without once looking at it. He knew his mother was dead, and that one came with a name attached and no face to hang it on. Amara. He knew, distantly, that his hands were wrong. Too small. The whole frame was smaller than it should have been.

The disconnect sat underneath everything else, and he couldn't get a measurement on it. Was he the man from Earth or the man from here? Who got whose memory?

Why the king was shouting came half a second later, in fragments. A woman's face, a diplomat, going cold at a party he couldn't quite picture. A room full of people turning at once. The held-breath quality of a scandal that hadn't finished happening yet. It came the way the medicine had come at the barbecue, whole and unearned.

All of it added up to a young man who had not been listening to a word his father said. By the time his attention actually landed in the room, the sentence was already halfway out.

"---- have made your choice for you, since you haven't made any for yourself in twenty-two years."

Marcus reached for the thing that had carried him through the last three months, the reflex that took a problem apart while he was still looking at it, and found it exactly where he'd left it and completely useless. It was a very good pair of hands with nothing in them. He didn't know the room. He didn't know the man. He didn't know what the sentence had been before the half of it he'd caught, and there was no drawing to pull up on a second monitor.

He had been dead four seconds ago by his own best accounting and was now apparently twenty-two years old and possibly a prince. He opened his mouth.

Nothing came.

The walk to his chambers took longer than it should have, mostly because Marcus, no, Emeric, kept stopping to look at things he had no business remembering. A tapestry. A crack running through one particular column, the same crack he'd traced with a fingernail at nine. The memories gave every ordinary object in the castle a doubled quality, new and old at once, and he still couldn't settle which of them was doing the looking. Marcus of Earth or Emeric of Ostenne.

His feet knew where to turn before he did, and he let them. Arguing with his own legs was a fight he'd lose. The guard walked half a step behind him, silent, hands loose at his sides in the way of a man who'd spent fifteen years making sure his hands were never far from something. He stood somewhere around six-six, built like a wall that had been given a grudge.

The name surfaced on its own. Ward.

Fifteen years. He'd known this man since he was a child.

"Do you have any thoughts on today's events?" Emeric asked, mostly to hear what his own voice did in this body.

"Nothing comes to mind."

That was the whole answer. Flat, complete, delivered without a flicker, and so exactly the shape of fifteen years of this same dynamic that Emeric nearly laughed.

Nearly.

"Fuck," he said instead, quiet, to himself. "This can't be. An isekai?"

Ward didn't ask what the word meant. He kept walking, half a step back, a man who had stopped being surprised by this prince a long time ago.

The room, his room, apparently, was bigger than his old apartment and colder than he expected. A trunk already sat open at the foot of the bed, which meant someone had known how the conversation with his father would end before it started. He worked through it on autopilot, hands folding while the rest of him sat somewhere behind his own eyes, still catching up.

He was nearly done when his hand closed on something small at the back of a drawer he hadn't consciously decided to open.

A crystal. Dull, greyish white, the kind of thing that belonged in a kid's rock collection instead of a prince's chambers. He knew, in the same effortless way he now seemed to know everything, that the household considered it worthless. Decorative. A trinket he'd stopped bothering with years ago. There was a reason he'd kept it. That part stayed just out of reach, sliding off whenever he turned toward it.

He almost put it back. His fingers closed instead, and something in his chest went loose that he hadn't noticed was tight. Not relief. Quieter than relief. Closer to recognition, with comfort somewhere underneath it. He had no idea why. He pocketed it anyway.

The rest of tomorrow arrived the same way everything else had that day, unasked. A cart, a road running east, an escort. Six guards for the whole journey to Kaldo, which some older part of him, the part that had spent a childhood counting the men at his brothers' backs, flagged as an insult wearing the clothes of a courtesy. Corbin rode with twenty. Even Waylon, younger than him, rated a dozen. Six was the number you assigned when you wanted a thing moved out of sight without being seen to spend on it.

Ward would be one of them. That much came back clean and certain, the same way the name had come on the stairs. The other five stayed blurred. Two he could nearly place, faces off the yard drills of the last year. The rest were strangers in his father's colors, men assigned to him rather than chosen by him, and he was too far behind his own eyes to decide yet whether that difference was going to cost him anything.

It felt like the kind of thing that would.

He was leaving early, so he went to sleep. Maybe the whole thing was a dream, and he'd wake on Earth carrying the memories of a man from somewhere else.

He didn't.
