Chapter 1 - Live Normally

By March, Marcus had gotten good at three things, and none of them paid.

The first was the application. He had a template now, a real one, with slots for the things hiring managers pretended to read. Six months of practice had taught him that the cover letter was a ritual object, like a wax seal, and that nobody had opened one since the invention of the applicant tracking system. He wrote them anyway. Denholm. Metalworks. Furna. A dozen others he had stopped tracking, which was its own small mercy, because the ones he did track sat in a spreadsheet with a column labeled STATUS and he had been forced to invent a value for it beyond no. He settled on silence. It felt more accurate and slightly less like being told.

The second was the graph. He had plotted his savings against time in October, drawn the line out, and found the day it hit zero. March. He checked it the way other men checked the weather. It never moved. Six months of his life had gone sideways and unpredictable in every respect but that, and the line came down exactly as drawn, every week, on schedule. It was the only part of his life still performing to spec and he had a hard time not admiring it.

The third was a game about building a civilization.

He was not proud of the hours. He knew the number because the launcher told him, and he had stopped looking at the launcher. Somewhere in there was a bronze-age grain economy he had tuned for four hundred hours until it ran without him, granary to mill to road to market, and the whole thing hummed along under his hands while a man with a mechanical engineering degree and eleven years of experience could not get a phone screen to check brackets for money. He mentioned this to nobody. There was nobody in the apartment to mention it to, and saying it out loud to himself had started to feel like a thing a man does before he stops shaving.

The trial invitation came in the second week of March, and it came laid out flat, which was what got him.

Publicly traded company. Clean standing. The study announced to the world in a press release, recipients listed by number, every personal detail stripped out. Twenty-six of them, himself included. Somebody at Meridian Bio had gone to real trouble to make it close to impossible to quietly bury a body, and Marcus had spent enough of his career reading specifications to know the difference between a document written to protect people and a document written to protect a company from the consequences of not protecting people. This one was the second kind. It was still better than most.

Direct skill induction, the recruiter called it. Cognitive scaffolding transfer, according to the consent packet. In practice it was a headset, a chair that reclined farther than any chair needed to, and ninety minutes a week of some other engineer's decades of method being pressed into the meat behind his eyes like a foot into wet sand.

Human lab rat, if you wanted to put it kindly. He mostly did.

He went in skeptical, though not for the reason everyone else was. His friends kept reaching for the horror-movie version, the one where the recipient loses his mind or starts leaking out his ears. Marcus's doubt ran a layer under that, down where an engineer looks at what a system implies instead of what it is sold as. If knowledge could be pressed into a man from outside, then some working assembly of a person was portable. Nobody at the company had said that part out loud. He doubted they ever would.

He sat with it a good deal longer than the consent form deserved, and signed anyway, because by then there was not a real decision left in it.

The intake form had eleven questions.

Headaches. Nausea. Dizziness, disturbed sleep, appetite change, ringing in the ears, numbness in the hands or feet. Mood changes noted by yourself or by others. Difficulty concentrating. Question eleven asked whether he had experienced anything he considered unusual, and gave him four ruled lines for it, which told him precisely how much unusual the study had been designed to accommodate.

He wrote No eleven times, signed the log, and handed the clipboard back.

The tech thanked him without reading it.

That was the part that stayed with him on the drive home, and it stayed because it was not sinister. The room was warm. The carpet was new. The woman at the desk asked about his weekend and meant it. Nobody in that building was doing anything to him that he had not been told about in writing, and the paperwork standing between twenty-six people and whatever this actually was ran to eleven questions about symptoms and zero about capability, and nobody checked the answers.

He was not angry about it. Anger was a poor instrument and he had never gotten much out of it. What he did instead, that night, at the kitchen table, in a notebook he bought for the purpose, was start a file.

He told himself it was diligence. A subject in a trial should keep records. That was true, and it was the reason he would have given anyone who asked, and it was not the reason. The reason was that eleven questions about symptoms and none about capability described a company that wanted to know what the process did to people and did not want to know what it gave them, and there were only so many ways to arrive at a form like that, and all of them were decisions somebody made on purpose.

He wrote down the date, the session number, the eleven questions in order, and the name of the tech who had not read his answers.

Then he went to bed, because it was Tuesday.

The team emailed him after the first session and told him to live normally. They would monitor for changes.

He took it at face value. There was no reason not to. Eat, sleep, work, date, and let the instrument in the chair do the measuring.

Within the month Denholm called back and offered him a seat.

He kept both. They had said live normally, and a man with a job was living considerably more normally than a man without one. One morning a week he drove out to the facility, put on the headset, let the chair recline past anything structurally reasonable, and got ninety minutes of a stranger's career worked into him. The other days he was the new guy, learning where the good coffee was hidden and which drawings were trustworthy and which were aspirational.

Nobody at the firm knew what he did on the mornings he came in late. Nobody at the trial knew what he was doing with what they gave him.

The division held. What nobody had warned him about was how well it worked.

Halfway through session four he caught himself sketching a load path on a napkin at a diner counter, tracing a force down through a stack of components, member to member, all the way to the ground. That was a thing good engineers took years to learn to do without effort, following the chain instead of reading one part in isolation. He had done it in the margin of a napkin waiting on his eggs, and had not noticed starting.

By session six, Denholm's numbers had stopped agreeing with themselves.

It was a Tuesday call, nothing on the invite marked urgent, the usual half hour with the applications engineer walking Procurement and Quality through the machined brackets for the new housing assembly. Marcus was half listening and drawing in the margin of his notepad when his eye caught on a tolerance callout for the mounting boss and would not let go. He pulled the mating part up on the second monitor. Then the fastener spec behind that. Three components, three drawings, three engineers who had each done their own job correctly and never once talked to each other. Every tolerance fine on its own. Stacked worst case on worst case, the assembly would not close.

He waited for the doubt. It usually showed up right about here, the second-guessing, the surely somebody already checked this. It did not come. What came instead was certainty, quiet and ordinary, the same certainty you have that a door you just walked through was a door.

"Can we back up," he said, cutting into the applications engineer mid-sentence without entirely meaning to. "The boss tolerance on the bracket, the shaft OD on the sub-frame, the fastener's minimum engagement. Run those worst case together and there's nothing left. It locks up."

The silence came in two parts. The first was the newcomer speaking at all. The second was eight people quietly pulling up drawings they had been pretending to have memorized.

"Let me check that," the applications engineer said, in the voice of a man who was going to be checking it for the rest of the afternoon.

He was right. Denholm took two days to confirm it and revise, and it cost the company nothing but the mild indignity of Procurement asking Marcus three separate times how he had caught it, as though the answer might come out different if they kept asking.

He did not have a good answer. Two months earlier he would not have known to look, and if he had looked he would not have trusted himself.

The interface check was Doyle's.

Marcus did not know that on the call. He learned it afterward, the way you learn things in a company of ninety people, which is to say from a man in Quality who was enjoying himself. Doyle had eleven years at Denholm and a title with the word engineering on both sides of it, and a signature block on the approval stamp of every drawing that crossed a boundary between two parts. The check that would have caught the stack-up was four hours of work and it was his, and it had been his for eleven years.

Doyle came by his desk that Thursday and was pleasant about it. Good catch. Fresh eyes. The kind of sentence that costs nothing.

The performance note went in at the end of the month.

Marcus found out because HR was obliged to tell him, which they did in a conference room with a window, using the word documentation four times. Nothing disciplinary. An observation, filed, regarding communication and professionalism in cross-functional reviews. There was a second incident cited, some meeting in April Marcus could not place at all, described in two lines that could have meant anything. His probationary period ended in nine weeks, and a note in the file at ninety days is not a note, it is a foundation.

He thanked the HR manager, who was not the problem, and asked for a copy, which she gave him.

Then he went back to his desk and was pleasant for six weeks.

He was pleasant in a way that cost him nothing, because none of it was performance. He liked most of the people at Denholm. He ate lunch at the same table he had eaten at before, which happened to be Doyle's table, and he asked Doyle about his daughter's swim meets, and listened to the answers, and remembered them. He deferred in two meetings where he did not have to. He sent Doyle a drawing markup with a note that said your call on the one item where it genuinely was.

And in the evenings, because he could now hold a revision history in his head the way other men hold a phone number, he read eleven years of approval stamps.

The pattern was not subtle once you were looking. The interface check took four hours and produced no artifact. Nobody audited it because there was nothing to audit. Doyle had signed it clean on every drawing that had crossed his desk since a reorganization in his second year, including the eleven days in a stretch of 2019 when his approvals ran at a rate that would have required him to work about thirty hours a day. Marcus did not think Doyle had ever made a conscious decision to stop doing it. He thought Doyle had skipped it once in a bad week, and nothing had happened, and a man learns what he is punished for.

Marcus wrote none of this down anywhere at Denholm.

What he did was build one sentence, and he spent most of a week building it, the way he would have spent a week on a fixture that had to work the first time.

He asked it at lunch, to the quality engineer, in the tone of a new hire who is afraid of getting something wrong. He said he had been signing the interface block on his own drawings and wanted to be sure he understood what he was signing, because it seemed to mean the same thing as the approval signature, and if it meant something different he did not want to be attesting to work he had not done.

He asked it on a Thursday. He never raised it again.

The audit came through in June, because a corrective-action audit was already scheduled and had been since January, and it went where audits go once somebody has told it which drawer to open. Doyle was on a plan by the end of the month and gone before August. There was a going-away thing in the break room. Marcus signed the card and meant what he wrote on it.

He checked himself for guilt afterward, honestly, the way he checked anything. He turned the whole business over and looked at it from underneath. The drawings had genuinely been going out unchecked. Somebody on an assembly line somewhere was measurably safer. Every fact in the chain was true and he had not told a single lie to produce any of it. He had asked one question that he was entitled to ask and that a reasonable new employee might have asked by accident.

None of that was why he had done it.

He noted the absence of guilt and filed it with everything else, the way he had filed the eleven questions and the tech who did not read them, and he did not examine the file, because a man with a good filing system can go a very long time without reading his own folders.

By session ten it had stopped staying in his field entirely.

It was a Saturday, a coworker's kid's birthday, and Marcus was standing at the edge of a backyard holding a beer he was not really drinking when the coworker's father-in-law started coughing. Wet, effortful, the kind everyone at the table politely agreed not to hear. The old man waved it off. Heart medication, a new one, doctor still sorting the dose.

He said the name of the drug and the number in the same breath, and something in Marcus clicked into place that had no business clicking. A whole shelf slid out where a second earlier there had been nothing. Contraindications. Interaction profiles. The specific mechanism by which that drug, at that dose, in a man that age with that cough, could be doing more damage than the thing it was written for.

He had never studied medicine. A year ago he could not have reliably told you the difference between systolic and diastolic.

"You should mention that cough to whoever set the dose," he said, working to make it sound like a guess. "Sooner than your next appointment."

The old man's eyes narrowed. More amused than offended, but not by much. "You a doctor now?"

"No." Too fast. He heard it land wrong on the way out.

"Kevin says you build brackets and mess with code. So how do you know what my heart medication does?"

"Read about it somewhere. Relative was on something similar."

The lie came out smoother than the truth would have, and that bothered him considerably more than the lie did.

Two seats down, Kevin had stopped mid-sentence to watch it happen, gone still in the specific way of a host recalculating what he actually knows about a guest he invited. Marcus caught the look and read it clean. Not suspicion yet. A data point Kevin would sit with later.

By twelve it was not even waiting to be asked.

He was crossing the design floor at Denholm behind a man from Quality and saw the fall three steps before it happened, the way you see the end of a sentence before somebody finishes saying it. A length of small-diameter pipe on the floor, and the man's stride, and the desk edge waiting at the far end of it.

Part of him wanted to watch. The part that liked confirming a prediction. It wanted the data, and the data cost one concussion.

He let that part lose.

"Pipe on the floor," he said, already moving. "Three steps. Watch it."

What unsettled him was what did not happen. No pressure behind the eyes. No nosebleeds. No overload, no crash, nothing to point at and say there, that is the cost. He had budgeted for a catastrophic drawback the way he budgeted for anything, and the invoice never arrived, week after week, and the absence of a bill in a system that expensive is not a gift. It is a bill you have not found yet.

He did not report any of it.

He got as far as opening the email client at a red light once, in April, and then he worked it forward the way he now worked everything forward, and shut the phone off before the light changed.

An effect outside the protocol was an adverse finding whether or not it felt adverse, and an adverse finding got a subject pulled while somebody senior decided what it meant. Pulled meant the stipend stopped. It also meant a very different set of appointments, because the thing he would be reporting was not I have a headache, it was I know things you never gave me, and a company that had spent nine figures learning to move the useful parts of a person from one skull to another would not treat that as a complaint. They would treat it as a result. He would stop being subject fourteen and start being the reason the building existed.

Page four, paragraph two, assigned them everything the study produced. He remembered the paragraph. He remembered all of them now.

The monthly battery was the harder problem, and he solved it too, which he understood afterward was the moment he had actually decided.

Pattern completion, digit span, timed spatial rotation, a reading block too easy to be measuring reading. He had sat the first one at week four and answered it straight, and the number that came back had been high enough that the tech running it read the screen twice.

At week eight he saw the ceiling of every item within a few seconds of reading it and spent the rest of the hour deciding where to stop. Not low. A subject who got worse drew more attention than one who got better. He wanted a curve, gentle and unremarkable, the kind that made a research assistant write steady improvement, consistent with cohort and move to the next file.

The part he was proudest of, and he noticed the pride and filed that too, was the distribution. Missing only the hardest items is a signature. Nobody performs like that. Real people lose two easy ones to boredom, blow a medium one on a misread, get a hard one right by accident. So he scattered them. He picked which questions to fail the way you would pick where to put wear on a forged part.

He walked out of week eight four points above week four with no interest at all from anyone in the building, and enjoyed it more than he had enjoyed anything in a year.

Then, because this is what a man does when he is handed something enormous and no instructions, he tried to get rich with it.

He made a list, a list being what he had instead of a plan. Cards first, which was the stupidest and therefore the fastest to cross off, since the math had been trivial for sixty years and every casino in the country had solved it. Options next. He built the model in an evening, ran it, and found the flaw sitting exactly where he would have found it in anyone else's work. The model wanted information, and being smart did not produce information. He had no capital, no positions, no idea what a company's quarter looked like from the inside.

Genius was not a telescope. It was a very good pair of hands with nothing in them.

Patents were real. Four of them at least, sitting unclaimed in problems he had watched Denholm walk past. Every one was work he had done on Denholm's clock with Denholm's drawings, and his employment agreement ran four pages and he had read it the way he now read everything.

The file in the kitchen drawer was the fifth item on the list and the only one that would have worked, and it would not have made him a cent.

He had, by June, about forty pages. The eleven questions. The consent language, cross-referenced against the press release, which promised the public a training product and the subject something else. The rights assignment on page four. Twenty-six numbers, and the shape of what it would take to turn a number into a person. And underneath that, the thing he could not prove and could not stop seeing, which was that Meridian thought it was selling a shortcut.

Compress a four-year degree into a quarter. Reskill a workforce over a weekend. That was the pitch, and it was by a wide margin the least interesting thing the machine could do.

What they actually had was a way to lift the part of a man that made him worth employing and set it down somewhere else. Ten years out and you do not hire the engineer, you license him. Twenty and whether he is alive becomes an accounting question, because the expensive part of him has already been moved and stored, and the rest of him eats.

He was the working prototype of a labor market where the labor did not need to be kept, and they were paying him eleven hundred a month plus mileage, and the twenty-five other numbers in that press release had signed the same four pages he had.

He knew what the file was for. He was not confused about that, at two in the morning, at a kitchen table, in a notebook he could not have explained to anyone who found it. He knew what he wanted to do to that company and he knew he could not do one inch of it yet, because the file was evidence, and evidence is inert without a lawyer, a plaintiff, a journalist, standing, money, or time. He had none of those. He had forty pages and a graph that had hit zero in March.

So he kept adding to it, and waited to be able to afford it, and went to session fourteen.

He signed the log for the day, same as always, and wrote none of it down.

He was quiet through the afternoon, and he walked back the same route he always walked. By now it was automatic. He could have done it blindfolded, could have done it asleep.

There was a problem in his head and it was a good one. He had been turning it since the parking lot, and somewhere around the second block the street went the way the apartment went on that Sunday, dark at the edges, dropped out of the frame, filed as solved. He did not decide to stop watching it. That was the thing about the focus. It never asked.

His mind came all the way back to the surface at the sight of a little girl stepping into the road.

After that nothing arrived in an order he could file.

A woman on her phone. A hand holding a smaller hand and then not holding it. A kid in a yellow jacket taking two quick delighted steps off a curb, because a curb is the most interesting geography a four-year-old knows. The math was available to him instantly and completely and it was bad, and every number in it said the same thing, and his body went anyway, some borrowed reflex getting there ahead of him.

He got the kid clear.

He did not get clear himself.

A crash, though he was not sure he heard it so much as arrived after it. Then the ground, and no pain in it, and no feeling attached to any of it, like a room going quiet around an alarm nobody had registered yet.

Then the strange unhurried part, the part nobody is supposed to get.

He seemed to be standing a few feet above the street looking down at the shape of himself, a dented bumper, a woman screaming into a phone at an operator. If there was a later, he wanted to know how long that actually lasted. It felt like minutes. Realistically it was four seconds. That gap alone was worth a session's thinking, assuming he ever got to think again.

He could not tell whether the calm was shock or whether it was the trial, and he found he was interested in the question, which answered it about as well as anything could.

He noted that a couple of seconds before the crash the woman had been far enough into whatever was on that phone to let go of her daughter's hand. He noted the driver's face through the windshield, the particular blankness of a man dissociating at forty miles an hour. Two people in the same intersection and neither one present in it.

Three, he corrected.

He had seen a man's fall three steps out, and he had not seen a car he had been walking toward for half a block. Had he been paying attention he could have had the girl clear and been clear himself. He found that funnier than he probably should have.

He waited to feel something about the whole business and nothing came, and he decided, since a man ought to land somewhere, that he would settle for not minding much.

The last thought he had that belonged entirely to Marcus was about the file in the kitchen drawer, forty pages, a landlord, and a dumpster.

The second to last was that the bills were settled either way.

Then the street was not there.

He was standing in a room with ceilings too high for the space and gold-threaded banners hanging dead still in air that did not move, and there were people ringed all around him in the kind of silence a crowd only makes on purpose, and a man in a crown was talking down at him from somewhere above his eye line in a tone he recognized instantly and from no particular memory at all.

Who in the fuck is this man talking down to me, and why?
