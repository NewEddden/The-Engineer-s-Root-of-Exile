Chapter 1 - Session Fourteen

The rain hadn't started yet. Marcus had been noticing things like that lately, which was new, and he had a fair idea what he'd paid for it.

He'd signed the consent forms three weeks after the layoffs, in a waiting room that smelled like new carpet and old coffee. Direct skill induction, the recruiter at Meridian Bio called it. Cognitive scaffolding transfer, according to the paperwork. In practice it was a headset, a chair that reclined farther than any chair needed to, and ninety minutes a week of a team's decades of engineering knowledge and method being pressed into the grooves of his brain like a foot into wet sand.

Human lab rat, if you wanted to put it kindly. He mostly did.

He'd gone in skeptical, though not for the reason everyone else was. Friends kept reaching for the horror-movie version, the one where the recipient loses his mind or starts leaking out of his ears. Marcus's doubt ran a layer under that, down where an engineer looks at what a system implies instead of what it's sold as. If knowledge could be pressed into a man from outside, then some working assembly of a person was portable. Nobody at the company had said that part out loud. He doubted they ever would.

He sat with it longer than the consent form deserved and signed anyway, because there wasn't a real decision left in it by then. His savings were four months from zero and rent had never once cared about a hiring timeline.

What actually got him over the line was the daylight. Publicly traded company, clean standing, the study announced to the world with recipients listed by number and personal details stripped. They had made it close to impossible to quietly bury a body. That mattered more to him than the money did.

Two and a half petabytes of storage, give or take, running an absurd load of passive computation, and doing all of it on twenty watts. Twenty watts ran a desk fan. Marcus did that arithmetic more than once in the recliner while the headset warmed up, because it beat the other arithmetic, the one that ended with him hollowed out and racked in a server room as somebody's cold storage. Papers were signed either way.

The trial team emailed after the first session and told him to live normally. They'd monitor for changes.

So he lived normally. Within the month a small manufacturing firm he'd interviewed with called him back. Denholm. It paid well and the trial covered what it didn't. One day a week he drove out to the facility and let a stranger's career get pressed into him in ninety-minute doses. The other days he was the new guy, learning where the good coffee was hidden and which drawings were trustworthy and which were aspirational.

What nobody had warned him about was how well it worked.

By session six, Denholm's numbers had stopped agreeing with themselves. It was a Tuesday call, nothing on the invite marked urgent, the usual half hour walking Procurement and Quality through machined brackets for a new housing assembly. Marcus was half listening when his eye caught on a tolerance callout for the mounting boss and wouldn't let go.

He pulled the mating part up on the second monitor. Then the fastener spec behind that. Three components, three drawings, three engineers who had each done their own job correctly and never once talked to each other. Every tolerance was fine on its own. Stacked worst case on worst case, the assembly wouldn't close.

He waited for the doubt. It usually showed up right about here, the second-guessing, the surely somebody already checked this. It didn't come. What came instead was certainty, quiet and ordinary, the same certainty you have that a door you just walked through was a door.

"Can we back up?" He cut in without quite meaning to. "Boss tolerance on the bracket, shaft OD on the sub-frame, the fastener's minimum engagement. Run those worst case together and there's no clearance left. It's going to lock up."

The silence came in two parts. The first was the newcomer speaking at all. The second was eight people quietly pulling up drawings they'd been pretending to have memorized.

He was right. It took two days to confirm and cost the company nothing but the mild indignity of Procurement asking him three separate times how he'd caught it, like the answer might come out different if they kept asking. He didn't have a good one. Two months earlier he wouldn't have known to look, and if he'd looked he wouldn't have trusted himself.

By session ten it had stopped staying in his field.

A coworker's kid's birthday, of all places. Marcus was standing at the edge of a backyard holding a beer he wasn't really drinking when the coworker's father-in-law started coughing. Wet, effortful, the kind everyone at the table politely agreed not to hear. The old man waved it off. Heart medication, a new one, doctor still sorting out the dose.

He said the drug and the number in the same breath, and something in Marcus clicked into place that had no business clicking. A whole shelf slid out where a second earlier there had been nothing at all. Contraindications. Interaction profiles. The specific mechanism by which that drug, at that dose, in a man that age with that cough, could be doing more damage than the thing it was prescribed for.

He had never studied medicine. A year ago he wasn't sure he could have told you the difference between systolic and diastolic.

"You should mention that cough to whoever adjusted the dose," he said, working to make it sound like a guess. "Sooner than your next appointment."

The old man's eyes narrowed. More amused than offended, but not by much. "You a doctor now?"

"No." Too fast. He heard it land wrong on the way out.

"Read about it somewhere. Had a relative on something similar."

The lie came out smoother than the truth would have, and that bothered him more than the lie did. Two seats down, his coworker had gone still in the specific way of a host recalculating what he actually knew about a guest he'd invited.

On the drive home he did the math nobody at the trial had asked him to do out loud. Session four, structural, adjacent to his own work, explainable as an unusually good teacher. Session six, still mechanical, still his lane. Session ten, a domain he had no connection to whatsoever, arriving with the same unearned fluency as everything else.

Whatever they were doing to him had stopped respecting the fences around what he knew. It had stepped into somebody else's yard.

By session fourteen he'd stopped worrying about the company's secret and started worrying about his own. If the fences came down that easily, he had no idea what was still standing on the other side of the one that used to be him.

He signed the log for the day, same as always, and wrote none of it down.

The session ended and he walked back the way he always walked. By now the route was automatic. He could have done it blindfolded, could have done it asleep.

Today wasn't going to be one of those days.

The first drops came while he was crossing, cold enough to register as information rather than weather. Then his attention snapped all the way to the surface at the sight of a little girl stepping into the road.

A woman on her phone. A hand holding a smaller hand and then not holding it. A kid in a yellow jacket taking two quick delighted steps off a curb, because a curb is the most interesting geography a four-year-old knows. Marcus was moving before he decided to move, some borrowed reflex getting there ahead of him.

He got the kid clear.

He didn't get clear himself.

A crash. Then the ground, and no pain in it, and no feeling attached to any of it, like a room going quiet around an alarm he hadn't registered yet.

Then came the strange unhurried part, the part nobody's supposed to get. He seemed to be standing a few feet above the street looking down at the shape of himself, a dented bumper, a woman screaming into a phone at an operator. If there was a later, he wanted to know how long that had actually lasted. It felt like minutes. Realistically it was four seconds, maybe less. He had no instrument for it and no way to build one, and he noted that with more irritation than fear.

He noted, without much feeling, that two seconds before the crash the woman had been far enough into whatever was on that phone to let go of her daughter's hand. He noted the driver's face through the windshield, the particular blankness of a man dissociating at forty miles an hour. Negligence in three directions, and the only person on the street running the numbers was the one about to stop.

He found that funnier than he probably should have. Had anyone else been paying attention, he could have had the girl clear and been clear himself.

The rain arrived properly. Then it didn't matter.

Then the shout came.

"Emeric!"

A man on a raised dais, calling a name that wasn't his.

One moment there'd been a street, a car, a woman, a child, bystanders arriving from three directions at once. The next he was standing in a room with ceilings too high for the space and gold-threaded banners hanging dead still in air that didn't move. The man shouting wore a crown. His face had gone rigid with something that could have been rage or could have been disappointment.

Who in the flying fuck is this man yelling at me, and why?

That was the last clear thought that belonged entirely to Marcus.

In the same breath, a dam went.

Twenty-two years arrived at once, not as a story but as a texture, the way a smell arrives before you've decided to notice it. He knew the banners were his father's colors. He knew the shape of this room the way you know your childhood kitchen with your eyes shut, hip-checking the same drawer for twenty years without once looking at it. He knew his mother was dead, and that one came with a name attached and no face to hang it on. Amara. He knew, distantly, that his hands were wrong. Too small. The whole frame was smaller than it should have been.

The disconnect sat underneath everything else and he couldn't get a measurement on it either. Was he the man from Earth or the man from here? Who got whose memory?

Why the king was shouting came half a second later, in fragments. Wine he could still taste at the back of his throat. A woman's face going cold in stages. A room full of people turning at once. A name he couldn't reach and a rank he had failed to. The held-breath quality of a scandal that hadn't finished happening yet.

It came the way the medicine had come at the barbecue. Whole, confident, unearned.

All of it added up to a young man who had not been listening to a word his father said. By the time his attention actually landed in the room, the sentence was already halfway out.

"You have not made one choice for yourself in twenty-two years. So I have made this one. Kaldo. You ride within the week."

The king said it to the room. Not to him. In the whole length of it he did not look at his son once, and the court noted that, and Emeric noted the court noting it.

Marcus, who had been dead four seconds ago by his own best accounting, was now apparently twenty-two years old and possibly a prince. He opened his mouth.

Nothing came.

The walk to his chambers took longer than it should have, mostly because Marcus, no, Emeric, kept stopping to look at things he had no business remembering. A tapestry. A crack running through one particular column, the same crack he'd traced with a fingernail at nine. The memories gave every ordinary object in the castle a doubled quality, new and old at once, and he still couldn't settle which of them was doing the looking. Marcus of Earth or Emeric of Randillia.

His feet knew where to turn before he did, and he let them. Arguing with his own legs was a fight he'd lose. The guard walked half a step behind, silent, hands loose at his sides in the way of a man who'd spent fifteen years making sure his hands were never far from something. He stood somewhere around six-six, built like a wall that had been given a grudge.

The name surfaced on its own. Ward.

Fifteen years. He'd known this man since he was a child.

"Do you have any thoughts on today's events?" Emeric asked, mostly to hear what his own voice did in this body.

"Nothing comes to mind."

That was the whole answer. Flat, complete, delivered without a flicker, and so exactly the shape of fifteen years of the same dynamic that Emeric nearly laughed.

Nearly.

He went looking for a word for what had happened to him and came back empty. He had two vocabularies now and neither of them carried one. That was the part that scared him, more than the crown or the banners or the hands. He had never in his life been unable to name a thing.

"Fuck," he said, quiet, to nobody.

Ward kept walking, half a step back, a man who had stopped being surprised by this prince a long time ago.

The room, his room, apparently, was bigger than his old apartment and colder than he expected. A trunk already sat open at the foot of the bed, which meant someone had known how the conversation with his father would end before it started.

A steward waited beside it with a ledger and counted out three thousand Rands in front of him, slowly, in a voice pitched to carry to the door. So the number could be reported accurately by anyone who cared to ask, and Emeric understood that several people would.

He arrived at the figure from two directions at once. The new memories put it next to Corbin's monthly allowance and flinched. The old ones did the sum a different way: three thousand days of another man's labor, which sounded like a fortune right up until you set it against a barony. It would keep six men and a cart on a road for a season. It would not do one useful thing at the end of that road.

Later, folding clothes on autopilot with the rest of him sitting somewhere behind his own eyes, his hand found a bundle at the bottom of the trunk that he had not put there. No note. No seal. Small, dense, and quiet, the kind of weight that only comes from things you can sell anywhere without being asked where you got them.

He sat with it for a while. Unsigned money from a court that had just exiled him could mean four things and three of them were bad. He put it back under the clothes and stopped thinking about it, which was not the same as deciding.

He was nearly done when his hand closed on something small at the back of a drawer he hadn't consciously decided to open.

A crystal. Dull, greyish white, the kind of thing that belonged in a kid's rock collection instead of a prince's chambers. He knew, in the same effortless way he now seemed to know everything, that the household considered it worthless. Decorative. A trinket he'd stopped bothering with years ago.

There was a reason he'd kept it. That part stayed just out of reach, sliding off whenever he turned toward it.

He turned it over under the lamp out of habit, checking the way he'd check any unfamiliar part. Weight, wrong for its size or right for it, he couldn't tell without a reference. No seam. No mark. No inclusion, no fracture line, nothing that suggested it had ever been worked. It gave him nothing back at all, and some inherited instinct underneath the engineer's one insisted that this was itself the strange part, without explaining why.

He almost put it back. His fingers closed instead, and something in his chest went loose that he hadn't noticed was tight. Not relief. Quieter than relief. Closer to recognition, with comfort somewhere underneath it.

He had no idea why. He pocketed it anyway.

The rest of it arrived the same way everything else had that day, unasked. A cart, a road running east, an escort. Six guards for the whole journey to Kaldo, which some older part of him, the part that had spent a childhood counting the men at his brothers' backs, flagged as an insult wearing the clothes of a courtesy. Corbin rode with twenty. Even Waylon, younger than him, rated a dozen. Six was the number you assigned when you wanted a thing moved out of sight without being seen to spend on it.

Ward would be one of them. That much came back clean and certain, the same way the name had come on the stairs. The other five stayed blurred. Two he could nearly place, faces off the yard drills of the last year. The rest were strangers in his father's colors, men assigned to him rather than chosen by him, and he was too far behind his own eyes to decide yet whether that difference was going to cost him anything.

It felt like the kind of thing that would.

There was nothing left to do with the night, so he went to sleep. Maybe the whole thing was a dream, and he'd wake up on Earth carrying the memories of a man from somewhere else.

He didn't.
