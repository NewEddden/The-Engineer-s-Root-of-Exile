Chapter 58 - The Catered Siege

"You have done this," Emeric said. "I've read about it."

He said it in the gate yard on the first morning of May, in front of Rylan and Declan and Osric, and handed Ward the town. What he handed over was a question: what a man coming to take Kaldo would see, and where he would stand, and what he would do first. Ward took it without a word, as he would have taken a blade handed to him hilt first, and the other three looked at each other and then at Ward, and moved round him differently afterward.

Rylan caught Emeric at the foot of the keep stair.

"You gave him the town," he said. "In front of Osric. Osric's telling the yard you lost a bet."

"I meant to."

"I know you meant to." Rylan considered it. "I'll let him keep telling it. It's a better story."



Ward took three days. He walked the town, inside the wall and out, alone for most of it and with Emeric for the rest. Ward walked it looking for what would break first, and how, and who would be standing there when it did.

He began at the river.

The powerhouse stood below the east bridge on the town bank, outside the wall, with the mill beside it and the race running between them and the river beyond. Ward walked round it twice. He looked at the tile roof, and the river door, and the slot in the wall where the belt came through from the wheel, and the path down from the bridge foot, and the reeds along the bank downstream where a boat could come in under the bank and nobody on the wall would see it until it was there.

"The lamps," he said.

"All eight of them. From here." Emeric heard how it sounded as he said it. "The line runs off the batteries, and the batteries are inside the wall."

"The lamps," Ward said again, and that was all he said at the powerhouse.

Then the east bridge, and across it, the dock quarter: eight hundred people in a tangle of timber houses and net lofts and tarred sheds, with no wall round any of it and never had been, and the new wharf with its pale deck, and the ore yard with its low wall that a man could step over, and below them all, round the bend of the bank, the old works and bay three, where Bram's hammer was going. Ward walked the whole of it. He walked the wharf to the end and stood there looking upriver. He stood in the door of bay three and watched the hammer and did not say anything to Bram, who did not say anything to him.

Then the west side, out through the South Gate and round under the walls: the farms, and the granary on its sixteen staddle stones on the baron's farm with the year's surplus in it. Beyond the farms the arches of the aqueduct came in across the fields from the Mere, thirty feet up, with the timber trough still sagging across the gap on its trestles where the stone had never been made good. Ward stood under the trestles and put his hand flat on one of the legs, and looked up at the trough, and then along the arches toward the Mere, and did not say anything there either. Inside the wall he looked at the wall cistern, and the pipe down the slope, and the tap at the top of the lower ward, and asked how long the cisterns would hold the town if nothing came down the channel. Emeric told him a week, if nobody washed.

Then the river road north, along the line, pole by pole, to the bend and the hut with its new roof, where the wall-crew man came out and stood up straight and reported all well in person, which made the second man laugh.

On the third evening Ward came up to the line room and stood at the window, as he had the night the line went upriver, and told Emeric what he had seen.

"Everything you have built," Ward said, "is outside the wall."

Emeric did not answer. He had known it for two days, from the way Ward walked.

"The machine that lights the wall is outside the wall. The grain is outside the wall. The water comes across the fields on legs, and some of the legs are wood. The smithy that makes the iron is across a river. Eight hundred of your people are across a river with no wall at all." Ward was looking north, at the dark where the river came down. "I would have put most of them there myself. In peace they are right."

"And now?"

"Now a man who comes down that river with your grain behind him and your water in front of him and your smithy under his feet," Ward said, "is not besieging you." He turned from the window. "He is being catered."



They worked it out at Ferra's table, because that was where the numbers were, on the evening of the fourth of May: Emeric, Ward, Ferra, and Bram in his chair by the stove, who said he was not there, and listened to every word.

There would be no siege. Ward said it first, and nobody argued. A siege was a thing a town survived if somebody came to lift it. The crown's line on Ferra's page still had nothing in the column headed answered, and there was nobody else to write to.

"Shut the gate and wait," Ward said, "and he eats you from the outside in. The mill. The dock quarter. The water. One a week. And at the end of it you open the gate yourself."

So whatever came would have to be met when it came, and finished, and everything Kaldo could not afford to lose would have to be somewhere it could not be lost.

The grain came first. Ferra had the granary's whole contents on a page, to the last measure, and she split the page into parts on the table while they watched: so much to the vaulted room under the old hall, so much to the back of the exchange, so much to the other dark market hall, the one the ward had not taken, and so much to cellars in the town she would rent for coin, a score of them. No more than a twentieth of it in any one place. If one place burned, Kaldo would be hungry, and alive.

"And the granary?" Emeric said.

"Stays where it is. Swept. Locked." Ferra did not look up. "It's on the baron's farm and everybody in Kaldo knows it's full. Anybody they talk to will tell them so."

Ward looked at her with something that on another man would have been approval.

The water was Imani's, and she would have it no other way. If the arches were cut, the cisterns held a week; after that the town went back to its wells, and the wells were the wells of the plague years. Imani would have the old wells cleaned and the boiling started before anybody cut anything.

The dock quarter was the hard part.

"They come inside," Ward said. "At the first word from the bend. Not at the second. The ones who will not come, you cannot make, and I will not send men to drag them. But the bridge is held from the town side, and once it is held, it is held."

"Eight hundred people," Ferra said.

"Eight hundred people into a town that was built for more than it holds." Ward looked at her. "You have the count of every empty house."

She did. She had walked every one of them for the copper.

"There are roofs for all of them," she said. "There aren't beds. They'll bring their own or they'll sleep on straw." She wrote straw, and then a number after it, and then crossed the number out and wrote a bigger one.

The powerhouse was where they disagreed.

Ward would not defend it. He said so plainly. It was outside the wall on a bank with reeds below it; it could be reached by water from three sides; it could be held by a few men for a little while, and then the few men would be dead and the machine would be broken anyway. "It is a thing," he said. "The wall is the town."

"The wall is the town because it's lit," Emeric said. "Your crossbows are twenty men on a wall in the dark, against however many he brings, unless they can see what they're shooting at. You put every one of those lamps where it shows a man coming at the wall, and the man in the gate stays dark. You told me so." He put his hand flat on the table. "The lamps are the defense. If the dynamo goes, the wall goes dark."

Ward was quiet.

"Six men," he said at last. "Not more. Behind the powerhouse wall, with the river door barred, with bows. If it cannot be held, they come back up the bank to the bridge foot and they do not wait to be told."

"Seven," said a voice from the stove. "Harl won't leave it."

They all looked at Bram.

"Harl won't leave his mill," Bram said. "Not for you, and not for Ward, and not for Renner. He's sat up with that machine every night since January. Two buckets. You'll not move him with a cart." He folded his arms. "Seven."

Nobody argued with that either.

Emeric's hand was still flat on the table where he had put it, across the foot of Ferra's page. She wrote the seven in beside his thumb without asking him to move it, the side of her hand against his. He took his hand back when she was done, which was too late to be any help.

The last thing was fire. The dock quarter was timber and tar and thatch.

"He will burn it," Ward said. "Whatever he cannot hold, he will burn. Every man does."

"Fire's mine," Emeric said.

It was. He could stand at the end of a burning lane and pull the fire off a roof into his hand, as he had pulled a dragon out of a laundry hearth, and send it up into the air and let it go, roof after roof, for as long as he could stand. Nobody else in Kaldo could do it. He had given the salt boatman a letter for the coast towns at the end of April, asking for anybody who could do magic and would come for coin, and had heard nothing back. It would cost him, and he had been working out how much since the night of the letter.

Ward looked at him.

"And if you are on the wall," Ward said. "Or across the river. Or dead."

"Then buckets."

"Then buckets first," said Ward, "and you after."

Emeric said yes. He meant it, as far as it went.



The grain went into the town in the first fortnight of May, every cart in the barony and every sack, and the garrison carried it, and the men who had breastplates carried it in them, because Ward wanted them used to the weight. Osric complained from the first sack to the last: that the iron rubbed, that this year's sacks were heavier than last year's, that a breastplate was for stopping a sword and not for carrying barley, and that if Renner wanted the grain so badly he could come and carry it himself. He carried more than anybody.

Hale met the first cart at the foot of the stair to the vaulted room with the key in his hand and a question, which he put to Ferra with great courtesy. Had she considered that grain drew mice? And that mice, when they had finished with the grain, were not particular? The vaulted room held every lease and boundary and debt in the barony, back further than anybody had needed to look.

Ferra had not considered it. She said so. She stood on the stair with her page and looked at the shelves of the barony's papers going back into the dark, and then wrote, under the vaulted room: crocks, lidded. The potter sold her forty of his big seconds by the end of the week, at the price of the good ones, as usual, and Hale stood at the door of the vaulted room while every crock went in and pressed every lid down himself.

Doran came up the lane to Ferra's table on the third day, which he had never done, and stood in the doorway in his black, and said that the church had a crypt, and that the crypt was dry, and that the dead in it had not, in his long acquaintance with them, eaten anything. Ferra looked at him, and then wrote the crypt onto her page, and the quantity, and Doran went away again without being thanked, which seemed to be what he wanted.

When it was done the granary on the baron's farm stood on its sixteen staddle stones with its slatted vents and its locked door and nothing in it at all, swept to the boards. The garrison knew, because the garrison had carried it. Ward had asked them, once, in the yard, not to talk about it, and none of them did.



Sela had the children.

The school's roll had gone up to a hundred and eighty in the spring, with the dock quarter's twenty-two among them, and Sela took the twenty-two first. She did not ask anybody's leave. She sat them down on the front benches at the end of a school morning and found out from each one where they lived and who they lived with and how many, and wrote it on a slate, and then she walked the empty houses inside the wall with Ferra's list in her hand and Arthur behind her carrying the slates, and gave each child a house. A real house, with a door, and a number on Ferra's list. She wrote the number on the back of each child's slate, and the child's name on the front, and made each of them say it aloud to her twice.

"When they ring the bell," she told them, "you don't go home. You go there. Your mother comes to you."

A boy from the net lofts asked what happened if his mother did not come.

"Then you wait," Sela said. "She'll come."

She told the mothers the same thing on the Saturday at the pay trestle, one at a time, and the mothers looked at the slates and at Sela and at the numbers on the backs of the slates, and most of them nodded, and some of them did not, and Sela wrote down which.



Hollis came to the gate of the soap yard on the evening of the twelfth, as he had come to other gates, with his hat in his hands.

"The child wage," he said.

Emeric had been waiting for somebody to ask it. He had not expected it to be Hollis, and then, seeing him, could not think why not.

"When they come, the school shuts," Hollis said. "Stands to reason. Can't teach letters with a war in the street." He turned the hat round once in his hands. "The wage is for the hours. If there's no hours, there's no wage. That's how the barony would see it, and I'd not blame it. I'd like to know which way you see it, before, not after."

"The wage holds," Emeric said. "School or no school. War or no war."

Hollis looked at him out of his small, still, weather-colored face.

"And when the barony's broke?" he said. "Because it will be. You don't need Ferra's book to see that."

"The wage holds."

Hollis nodded, once, and put his hat on.

"I'll hold you to it," he said, and went off down the lane in the dusk, and Emeric stood at the gate of the soap yard and watched him go, and had no doubt at all that he would.



That night Tess was at the dyehouse bench when he came in, with her bench book open and the lamp lit, looking at the wall.

The doctrine was still there, in chalk, a little smudged, and the list under it, and the lines through the jobs that something else had learned to do. Harl's belt. Bram's pipe. The old way, a hand a day. The zinc and the annealing had Bram's name against them now, and a date, and a line through each. Only flashing the field had no line, and would not get one until he had taught somebody else to do it.

Tess had added a job of her own. At the bottom of the list, under everything, in her careful hand, it said: Fires. And beside it, where the other lines had the name of whatever had learned the job: the prince.

She did not say anything when she saw him read it. She did not rub it out either.

He looked at it. Then he picked up the chalk, and did not cross it out, and put the chalk down again.
