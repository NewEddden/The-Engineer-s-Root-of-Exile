Chapter 58 - The Table

He wrote it between the eighth bell and dawn, standing up, and it came to two sheets.

Everything that was already in the training sheets stayed: the load, the ladder, the record, the rest days, the food, the measurements four times a year. He had spent two years getting the garrison to believe in those and he was not going to spend a night undoing it.

What he added was four lines, and every one of them came off a stair.

A man fights in plate with the crystals dead, once a week, until it stops being a surprise. Nothing in a dead patch works and no man in it is a knight, and the difference between a knight and a large man in good steel is that the large man has been practising.

A man gets out of a cord, wet, behind his back, and out of a hold on the ground, and stays practising both for as long as he serves.

A man goes in cold water tied, in the shallows, in front of people, until floating is the first thing his body does instead of the last.

A man carries another man down a stair and up it, in the dark, without a lamp.

At the top of the first sheet he wrote the thing the four lines were for, which he had said once already, in a church, to a priest who had wanted to make knights out of his men with oil and a book.

Soldiers. Not knights.

He did not order any of it. He had learned that the hard way with the letters, and he had learned it again from Rylan's rule going up on the board and coming back a month later as something the line men quoted at each other. What he did was post the sheets with the reason beside each line, in plain words, and say once, in the yard, that the posts that came open in this barony from now on would go to the men who met the standard, and that he was not going to argue with anybody about the timetable.

Then he left it alone and let the yard do what the yard did with anything, which was resist it for nine days and adopt it in the tenth and then behave as though it had always been there.

Rylan was in the yard at dawn on the first morning, in harness, as ordered.

He read both sheets standing, with his thumb under the lines, which took him a while and which nobody watched him do because Ward had arranged for there to be nobody there.

"This is a year of work," he said.

"It is."

"What is the fifth line?"

"There is no fifth line."

"Then write one," Rylan said. "Because there is nothing on this paper about a man standing in a stairwell being handed the choice I was handed, and there is no drill for that, and I have thought about very little else for eight days."

Emeric looked at the sheets for a while.

"There is no drill for that," he said. "There is a man you tell. That is the whole of the fifth line and it is not written on this paper, it is written somewhere else, and I am going to spend the rest of the winter building the somewhere else."

Rylan took the standard and went at it the way a man goes at a wall he intends to be on the other side of. He was in the shallows in February with his wrists tied and ice on the bank. He fought Osric in dead plate twice a week and lost for a month and then stopped losing. By the thaw there was no argument anywhere in the barony about who the hardest-trained man in it was, and the interesting part, which Ward pointed out, was that nobody in the yard resented him for it, because every man there had watched him stand in the hall and not ask for anything.

Emeric started training where people could see him, which was new.

Blade work with Ward at first light, in the yard, in front of whoever was up. He was bad at it. He had been taught properly as a boy by a master who had been paid to make a third son look adequate in a hall, and adequate in a hall was exactly what he was, and Ward took him apart for three weeks without appearing to exert himself and then began, grudgingly, to leave him alive for longer.

The rest of it stayed where it had always been.

In the delve, with the door barred and the stone in his hand, he ran the three drills with his hands behind his back and a strip of cloth over his eyes. He ran them until the count stopped meaning anything. And at the end of six weeks of it he knew precisely as much about whether a man could work blind and bound as he had known at the start, because the room rendered out of his own understanding and would agree with him all night, and a thing that agrees with you is not a test.

Armand's bench could have answered it in an afternoon.

He wrote that sentence down in the book, and the reason he was not going to use the bench, and left it unmeasured, which he disliked. It was the second thing that winter he had put in writing and left standing because the sentence that would have unlocked it was a sentence he could not begin.

The rest of the work started from one line, which he wrote on the third morning and did not change.

The wall did not fail. The single key failed.

One man was the only key. Therefore he was the only summons, so the procedure had to name him, and the procedure was on a card at eye height where anybody could read it. Therefore he was the only target, and a man who is the only target is a man whose household can be priced. Everything that had happened at the stair came out of that one property, and it was a property he had chosen twice and written the word deferred beside.

So he built the root.

It took him eleven nights to work out and it was, in the end, not a clever thing. Underneath the bound stone he set a second binding that could not be completed by one person. Two hands on it, two minds in it, each of them wanting to be there, and the work of the ring belonged to both afterward.

The property he cared about was the one at the other end of it. A key like that cannot be handed over by one of the men holding it, because handing it over is not a thing one man can do. And a key like that, he believed, could not be taken by force at all, because nothing he had ever worked with liked being forced. Pull metal past what it will take and it snaps. Push water apart and it crushes you. He had said that to Ferra in a room with two ledgers on the table, four years before, when she had asked why he would not simply pull her in and be done with it, and he had never had cause to test it and did not want one.

Bram took the first one.

He was the hand that stayed. He had not been out of the barony in six years and did not intend to be, and he was in the forge at fifth bell every day of his life including the ones he was ill on.

They did it in the overlay, standing in the casting shed with the real roof over them and the delve laid across it, which was the way they had built the flue and was the only room the two of them had ever properly shared. Emeric put it where Bram could reach it and told him what it was and what it would mean and, at some length, what it would cost him if Kaldo ever needed the ring on a night Emeric was not in it.

Bram listened to the whole of that with his arms folded.

"Give me the end of it," he said.

It went easier than anything Emeric had done all winter. Afterwards Bram stood with his hand still out and worked his fingers once, the way a man does after carrying something.

"Like taking the other end of a long beam," he said. "You feel where it wants to go."

The pump-house stone went onto the same root the following week, so that the farm had two keys as well, which meant that Ferra's oldest objection was answered eighteen months after she had made it and she did not say one word about that.

Ward took the second.

He asked three questions first, all of them about failure. What happens if I am dead. What happens if I am taken and he is not. What happens if the two of us are in the same boat, which, he pointed out, is not a hypothetical in this barony.

"Then we are back where we were on the stair," Emeric said.

"Then find a third."

He offered the third to Ferra and she turned it down inside a sentence.

"No. I am going to hold the map," she said. "If I hold the map and a key, then I am the one thing in this barony worth putting a knife in, and you will have spent a winter moving the target from your own back onto mine and calling it a system."

He built one more thing into Bram's bond and told nobody about it. Every time that key touched the ring, the root would write it down where only Emeric could read it. It took an evening. He did not have a reason for it that survived being said out loud, which was how he knew what it was, and he did it anyway and went to bed.

The map was Ferra's and she had it drawn inside a fortnight.

It set out, post by post, what each office knew, what it needed to know, and what it was never to be told, and the principle under it was one sentence long: no single post knows enough to be worth taking.

The second list was the one he had asked her for and had not thought through.

It was every soft place in the barony's own people. Who had family outside the walls. Who had debts and to whom. Whose mother was at Grayrock, whose brother drank, whose son went to a school in another barony, who could be moved by fear and who could be moved by money and who, on the evidence, could not be moved by either but had a daughter.

She brought both lists to him together and put the second one face down.

"This one is correct and it took me nine days," she said, "and I am not keeping it, and neither are you. If one person holds the map and the soft places, that person is a complete set. I told you that about the key and it is more true about paper."

So they split it, in the end, the obvious way. Ferra kept the map of who knew what. Ward took the soft places, because guarding them was already his work, and because anything coming for that list had to come through the man the whole garrison was arranged around.

Una went on Ward's list, third line, with the road and the distance and the name of the man she had married. There was nothing Kaldo could put on a door a day west in another lord's barony. What it could do was ask Saban's carters to stop at that house every run, for nothing, on the way past, and have a cup of something, and go on; which they did from that winter, for years, and never once knew why.

The table started in the second week of February and it was, at first, just the long table in the keep with six people at it who had all been in that room before.

What was new was that they came with offices and left with them.

Ferra had the books and the map. Ward had arms, the watch and the soft places. Bram had the works, and the root. Saban had trade, the roads, and the ear he had built out there and had never been asked to describe in detail. Imani had health, which she had been running for four years without anybody putting a word to it. Armand had measure, and came, and sat, and said intelligent things about every number that crossed the table.

He was not in the root and he was not in the delve and he knew there was a room he was not in, because he was not a fool and he had been watching Bram not look at him for a fortnight.

He never asked. He asked about capacity and falloff and the ninety children in the school, with great pleasure, for hours, and about nothing else at all, and Emeric let him, and both of them were aware of the shape of the silence they were being polite around.

The first real thing the table did was overrule him, and it took four minutes.

Ferra came in with the swap column and put her finger on a line of dates.

The gate stone, the one behind the backing of the board, had been on a monthly charge for a year. Since the second week of January it had wanted swapping every nineteen days.

"The nodes do not account for it," she said. "The lamps do not account for it. It is not the weather, because the pump-house stone has not moved at all, and it is not the bench correction, because I applied that in December and this starts in January." She looked up. "Something in that gatehouse is drawing and nobody at this table put it there. What is it?"

He could have given her three answers that would have held for a month. He watched himself decline to, which was not virtue, because the reason he declined was that a table you lie to is a table you have built for decoration.

So he told them.

A dead hand, wired under the root. It watched its own draw, which was the only thing a working could ever watch. If his bond went dark and stayed dark for three days, the root would wipe itself, and every node on the ring and every latch on the main would go blank at the same moment, and whoever had taken him would find that they had taken a man carrying nothing.

"It is one night of work to undo," he said, "and I would rather explain it now than have you find it in a column."

"You did not explain it now," Ferra said. "I found it in a column."

Bram spoke before anybody else, which he almost never did.

"A wall that dies when you do," he said, "dies on the night they take you. That is the one night we need it. That is the only night we need it."

"Agreed," Ward said.

Imani put her hands flat on the table. "Who is it for?" she said. "Because it is not for the barony. If they have you and the ring goes dark, they still have you, and we are blind, and the only person it costs anything is whoever is standing on the wall that night."

Saban said nothing at all and let the silence do his voting for him.

Armand, who did not know what a root was, said that as a matter of measure he would want to know why a device designed to protect a system began by disabling it, and then apologised for speaking outside his office, and nobody had the heart to tell him he had just said the whole thing in one sentence.

Emeric took it out that night, in the delve, with Bram inside the overlay watching him do it because Bram had asked to come and see it done.

The next day he found himself going over Ferra's column again at odd moments, the interval and the dates and the neatness of the reasoning that had got there from nineteen days, and noticed at about the fourth time that he was doing it, and put the book away, and did not examine the impulse any further than that.

The factor asked for the night in writing.

She did it properly, through Ferra, in a note of three lines: her house required an account of any event materially affecting the security of the barony, and she was obliged to send one.

She was given the account from the payroll steps, word for word, with the date. Men had come by water. They had failed. Two were dead and the rest had gone downriver.

She read it, said that she would forward it as written, and asked nothing else, then or afterwards. Nothing that winter cost a copper above her threshold, and nothing in it required a ruling from her, which was the first stretch of the whole arrangement in which the covenant had not been in the room.

The file he kept himself, in his own hand, in the drawer with Saban's monthly sheets.

A name, heard in a hold, spoken by a man who did not know he was being heard.

A voice: level, unhurried, no weight on any word, the flatness of a man reading a name off a list.

A river hull, tarred inside the last month, and a second boat that had taken four or five men off it in the dark.

Men who knew which day Saban's carts went out to Milford, and which house in which lane, and the name of a boy and the name of his aunt.

A charged blank the size of a fist, on a cord, on a ring bolt.

And a stone pushed past what it could hold and dropped in the channel by somebody who had known exactly what it would do to everything within forty yards, which was a thing Emeric had believed, until that night, that he might be the only man alive to have tested on purpose.

Saban's ear went out along the river after the hull: bought for cash before midwinter, probably downstream, probably by a man who did not haggle. Saban was told it was a hull and asked nothing further, because that was his office now and he was better at it than anybody at the table.

Nothing came back. He had expected nothing to come back.

He thought about taking the name down to the cells and did not.

Petram had never met the paymaster. He had said so under pressure, flatly, and gone on saying it, and it had had the shape of the truth. And Petram had given him nothing at all since the winter of the names, not one useful sentence in fourteen months, which Emeric had put down to the labour and the cold and the fact that a man runs out of things he is willing to sell.

He wrote both facts beside the name and left the line dead.

At the bottom of the sheet he put the two candidates he had, which was the whole of what he knew and he was careful to write it as thinly as he knew it. His brother's people. Or whoever had tried to kill him when he was seven, who had never lost interest and had only ever lost the target.

Under that he wrote one more line, later, on a different evening, when the house was quiet.

It was not a question and it was not addressed to anybody and it went in the drawer with the rest, and nobody who sat at his table or ate at his fire or worked in his yard ever saw it, then or afterwards.

They did not talk about it.

Rylan came back onto the roster in the last week of February and sat his nights, and Emeric came down to the gatehouse on most of them the way he always had, and the two of them worked. The card. The box. The schedule. Whether stretch six wanted its field moved now that the reeds were down.

Neither of them ever said Marrow's name aloud. Neither of them mentioned the stair. It was not avoidance exactly. It was two men who had agreed, without agreeing, that the thing was in a book with a date on it and did not need saying twice in a warm room.

The thaw came early and dirty, all the way through the first week of March, with the branches running high and brown and the ice coming off the reeds in sheets.

On a night in the middle of it, with Rylan in the chair and Emeric standing behind it going through the swap sheet, nine lit.

Rylan did not move.

He put two fingers on the edge of the board, said "Nine," to the man at the door, and sent him, and the runner went out into the cold with a lantern and the gate shut behind him. Rylan stayed in the chair, watching the other eleven, because a board nobody is reading is a board nobody has paid for.

The runner came back up out of the stair eleven minutes later, grinning, wet to the knee.

Rylan took Ferra's book off its hook and wrote one word in it, pressing too hard, so that it went through to the next page.

Otter.

Twelve lamps, two logs, a card with eleven words on it, and a ring in a wall that now answered to three people instead of one.

And in the drawer, under all of it, the loan, the covenant, the schedule of works that could not be started, and eighteen months of a curve that only held if nothing happened.
