Chapter 72 - Nothing on the Books

Bram wanted iron, and there was no iron.

He said so at Ferra's table on the second Monday in October, standing, because he had come straight from bay three and did not mean to stay. The mill roof wanted another forty straps and twice that in bolts. After the roof there was the race gate, which had been leaking since the war, and after the gate there was a list he had started in his head and stopped writing down because it depressed him. The last of the good bar had gone into Tess's wheels. Kaldo's own hills gave a red iron that cracked under the hammer if you looked at it, and no barge had come down from Grayrock since the year turned.

"The dead port," Emeric said.

"Gone into your dynamo. Gone into the scythes. Gone into the pier." Bram counted them off on a black thumb. "There's a chain left on the old slip I wouldn't hang a dog from."

Emeric looked at Ferra. She had her book open to the barony's holdings, because she always had her book open to something, and she did not look up.

"The tithe houses," he said.

She turned back two leaves without being asked, and there they were: the houses below the north well that the barony had taken in the plague years against tithes nobody had lived to pay. Thirty-four of them. Each had its own line and, at the end of each line, the tithe it had been taken for, which was what the barony carried it at.

"What would they fetch?" he said.

"Nothing." She did not have to look it up. "Nobody has bought a house below the north well since the plague. Nobody's asked."

"So they're worth nothing."

"They're carried at the tithe."

"But they're worth nothing." He put his finger on the page, on the column of figures that had been sitting in her book for three years doing nothing at all. "Brick. Roof timber, what's left of it. Hinges. Grates. Door bands. Every one of those houses has a grate in it, Bram, and a crane over the hearth and a set of bands on the door. If the barony takes something worth nothing and builds with it, what has it spent?"

Ferra had already stopped reading. He saw her see it, a good while before he got to the end of the sentence, and he saw her not like how much she liked it.

"You'd have to value them at what they'd sell for," she said. "Not what they were taken for. The whole column, on the page, with the reason. And you'd have to ask."

"I know."

"She'll see what it's for."

"I'd think less of her if she didn't."

Bram looked from one of them to the other. "Is there iron or isn't there?"

"There's thirty-four houses of it," Emeric said. "Give her the night."



The barony fed its crews at noon, one meal, from the old castle kitchen under the keep, because a man pulling slates off a roof in October did not pull many on bread and an onion. It had started in the summer as a kindness and been written into Ferra's book in August as a wage, and since then it had grown the way such things grow, until the kitchen was feeding the mill roof crew, the gate crew, the men clearing the burned lots, the garrison, and anybody else who came in at the side door with a reason Maren believed.

Ferra had found Maren in the census. A widow from the dock quarter, about forty, who had cooked for a fishing company of thirty men for twelve years before the plague took the boats and the company with them. She was broad and quick and red in the face from the range, with old burn scars shining on both forearms, and she ran the kitchen the way Ward ran the gate.

Emeric went down on the Tuesday with a sheaf of paper he had spent three evenings on.

She was boning fish at the long table, a great heap of the small bony river fish nobody bought because they were more needle than meat, and she did not stop for him. He waited. She finished the one in her hand, dropped it into a tub, wiped her knife, and held out her hand for the paper without being told what it was.

She read the first page standing. Then she pulled out a stool with her foot and sat down and read the second.

It was nothing grand. A stock made of everything the kitchen was throwing out, the heads and frames of those same fish, the bones off the garrison's mutton, onion skins and carrot tops, put on at dawn and kept just shy of the boil all day, and strained. A stew of barley and whatever the stock had in it that would keep for three days and be better on the second. Fish cakes out of the picked flesh of the bony fish, bound with mashed peas and fried on the griddle. Griddle cakes from the second-grade flour, with a little of the barm from the brewhouse to lift them. A dozen more like that, each with what it needed for forty men and how long each step took.

Maren turned to the third page and stopped at a line, and read it again.

"Didn't know nobles touched this sort of work," she said. "Thought your highness had never been closer to a kitchen than the far end of a table."

She said it without much heat, the way she might have said the fish were small this year.

"I don't cook," Emeric said.

"No." She tapped the line. "Mix the batter. Rest it a quarter of an hour." She looked up at him. "Nobody writes that who hasn't stood over a griddle and watched the first lot come out flat as a slate because he didn't."

He had stood over a great many griddles, in a different kitchen, in a different life, with a radio on the windowsill and a dog under the table hoping. He could not say a word of it. So he said nothing at all, and let the silence sit there between them on the long table with the fish.

Maren held his eye for a while. Then she folded the sheaf in half and put it in the pocket of her apron, and he could see her put the rest away too, somewhere behind her face, to be taken out and looked at later.

"The stock I can do tomorrow," she said, and picked up her knife.



The rain came up the river in the night.

It came the way the October rain always came to Kaldo, Hale said, all at once after a month of nothing, out of the south-east with the wind behind it, and it rattled the shutters of the keep until the small hours and then settled into a steady roar on the roofs that went on until morning. Emeric lay awake for some of it and listened, and thought about the wheel.

By breakfast the race wall showed a hand of new water. Tess had the measure from Bram before Emeric was down the stairs; she had sent a boy to the keep with it on a scrap of slate, in her own figures, underlined twice.

That night Ward went round the wall and lit all eight lamps.

It was the first time since August. They came up one after another along the dark curve of the wall, white and steady in their hoods, with the river loud underneath them, and in the lanes people came out of their doors and stood and looked up at them, as they had come out and looked up when the lamps went dark. They said about as much now as they had then. Then they went back in.

The rain had done one other thing. In the thatch of the dock quarter's new houses on the black ground it had found every gap the net-menders had left, and in the morning there were wet beds and wet children over there and a good deal of language. Emeric sent word across that the potter could fire roof tile at cost for anyone who wanted it, the barony paying the potter's wood. The old man with the walnut face sent back that thatch had been good enough for his grandfather. By the end of the week about half the houses had ordered tile anyway, and the old man's was not among them.



He found the boy on the gravel bar below the bridge, on the third morning after the rain, with his trousers rolled to the thigh and a sack over his shoulder.

The river had dropped back a little and left the bar raw. Everything that had lain under it all summer had been turned over, stones and silt and the green slime off them, and the boy was working his way along the edge of the water with his eyes down and his feet blue, stooping every few steps to pick something out of the gravel and drop it in the sack.

It was Arthur. Sela's youngest, the one Imani had pulled back from the fever in the first autumn, all elbows and shins now at thirteen, with his mother's way of looking at a prince as if the prince were a cart that had stopped in the wrong place.

"What are you finding?"

Arthur weighed whether to answer. Then he put his hand in the sack and brought it out closed and opened it.

Three stones lay on his palm, none bigger than a thumbnail, smooth and blue-grey and faintly clouded, like river ice. They were wet, which was no surprise, but they were wet in a way the gravel around them was not: the water stood on them in beads and did not run off.

"Water stones," Arthur said. "The flood turns them up. They're in the gravel all year but you only find them after."

Emeric took one. It was cold, colder than the river, and when he closed his hand on it the cold did not warm. Somewhere in his palm, or behind it, there was a faint sense of movement, the way you can feel a stream through the soles of your boots when you stand on a plank across it.

"What do you do with them?"

"Sell them, when there was anybody to sell to. Before." Arthur shrugged. "The old baron's man bought them for the wells, a copper for ten. Nobody's bought any since."

Emeric turned the stone over. A copper for ten. He thought about the wells, and the cistern, and the dry slope above the west farms where the aqueduct's water never reached, and none of it fit. Nothing he could think of fit. He only knew, standing on the gravel with the river going past, that he did not want these going back into the river and he did not want them going to anybody else.

"How many have you got?"

Arthur looked into the sack. "Forty. Fifty."

"I'll give you two coppers apiece. For all of them, and for any more you find this autumn."

The boy looked at him with Sela's eyes, plainly trying to work out where the trick was. When he couldn't find it, he decided the prince was simple, and was too well brought up to say so.

"What are they for?"

"I don't know yet."

Arthur considered that. Then he held the sack out, before the prince could come to his senses.



Sela lit the brazier by the north well that evening, as she had lit it on the first cold night of every winter since the plague.

Emeric came up the lane from the keep with the sack over his own shoulder, which had not been his plan, but Arthur had been paid and had gone, and there had been nobody else to carry it. She was crouched at the brazier with the tinder box, and she looked up when she heard him, and saw the sack, and saw whose sack it was, because she had sewn it.

She nodded to him. Once, short, the way you nod to a neighbour.

Then she went back to the fire.

It was not forgiveness. He had had that from her once, for Tam, the first winter, and he knew the difference. The crown that had taken her eldest son was still the crown and he was still its son. But it was more than she had given him last October, and he walked the rest of the way up the lane with the sack on his shoulder and did not look back, because looking back would have made it something she had to take back.



Bram finished the chair on the Thursday.

They carried it up the hill to the ward between them in the afternoon, Bram at the front and Emeric at the back, and it was so light that Bram kept turning round to make sure Emeric had not let go of it. Ash rails steamed and bent and pegged, a seat of stretched hide, a back that came up to the shoulder blades and stopped, a bar for the feet. The two big wheels at the back with the hand rims standing out from them, and Vesk's true circles going round without a flicker. Two small wheels at the front, larger than Emeric had drawn them, because Bram had been right about the ward floor.

Imani held the door. She looked at the chair as it went past her, and then at Emeric, and said nothing, which from Imani was a long speech.

Tess was sitting up against the wall on the bed by the window with her bench book on her knees. She looked at the chair. She looked at it for long enough that Bram began to shift his feet.

"It's for you," he said. "If you want it."

"I can see it's for me." Tess put the book down. "Who else is it going to be for? How does it go?"

Emeric showed her: the rims, the brake on each side, how to lean forward into a push and not back. Two of Imani's women lifted her across from the bed and set her down in it, and there was a bad moment while they arranged her legs on the bar, a moment she watched with her mouth shut tight and her eyes on the far wall. Then they stood back.

She put her hands on the rims and pushed, and the chair rolled forward a yard down the aisle between the beds and stopped, because she had pushed one rim harder than the other and it had turned her into the end of the next bed.

"Even," Emeric said. "Both at once."

"I know." She backed it off the bed. She pushed again, both hands, and it went straight, all the way to the end of the aisle, where she had not yet learned how to stop it, and she grabbed both rims and the chair stopped and she did not, quite. She went forward over the bar onto her hands on the boards.

Bram took a step. Imani's women took a step.

"Don't," said Tess, from the floor.

Nobody did. She got her arms under her and her hands on the seat and, with a great deal of work and none of it graceful, hauled herself up and round and back into the chair, and sat there breathing hard with her hair in her face. Then she pushed back down the aisle to the window, slowly, even, both at once, and stopped where she meant to.

Bram turned round and went out. Emeric heard him on the step outside clearing his throat for some time.

Tess pushed her hair back. "Is that all?"

It was not all. He had a bundle under his arm, tied with tape: forty pages, the first part of a book he had been writing in the evenings, in the room at the bottom of his own head where the writing went quickly and the hand afterward did not. Electricity from the beginning. What a charge was, and why the cell made one, and why a wire carried it and a string did not. He had started it in August, on the night he had sat by her bed and watched her stop writing the date under the empty space, and he had meant it to go much further than forty pages, as far as he knew, chapter by chapter, as fast as she wanted it.

He put it on her knees. She untied the tape and read the first page, and the second, and he saw the moment she stopped being polite about it and started reading properly.

"There's more of this?"

"As much as you want. As far as I know." He hesitated. "If you need me, for any of it, I'm there. Not just this."

She nodded without looking up. He was at the door when she spoke again.

"It'll wear my arms out by spring," she said. "Pushing."

"It will. You'll get stronger." He thought about it. "Or. Imagine moving this without moving your hands. A dial here, by your fingers. Forward, and something turns the wheels for you."

Tess looked at the chair, and at her hands on the rims, and then at the forty pages on her knees, which began with the cell and ended, somewhere a long way off that she could not see yet, with that.

She had not needed the picture to want it. He saw that, too, and went out.



Late, with the shutters closed and the keep asleep, he sat on the edge of his bed with a candle on the stool in front of him and tried, again, the thing that had happened once, the winter before, and never since.

Fire, pushed without a hand, still came out of him at about the strength of a breath on a spoon of soup. He set that aside. He tried air instead, which was not his, which cost him three times what fire did, and on the fourth try the thin grey ribbon of smoke off the snuffed wick bent over sideways, as if a door had opened somewhere, and stayed bent while he counted five.

Then it stood up straight again, and he had a headache like a nail behind one eye, and he lay down with his boots on and did not try a fifth time.



Ferra's revaluation went up the hill on the Monday, eleven pages in her small plain hand, every house on its own line, the old figure struck through and the new figure written in beside it with the reason: the last sale of a house below the north well, which had been in the spring before the plague. Under it, one question.

Vantic did not read the question. She read the eleven pages. She sat at Ferra's trestle in the back room of the exchange and read them for most of an hour while Ferra did something else at the other end of the trestle and Emeric stood at the window and failed to do anything at all.

Then she turned back to the first page.

"Who set this?" she said.

"I did," said Ferra.

"When?"

"Yesterday morning."

"On what?"

"On the last time anybody bought a house below the north well."

Vantic looked at the date on the last sale for a while. Then she squared the pages and put them in her case and did up the strap.

"I'll see them," she said, "before I rule."



She saw them the next afternoon.

Emeric and Bram went down to the lower ward after dinner with Ferra's list, to start on the first row, and found her already there. A woman in a plain dark coat and good boots, with a notebook, three houses ahead of them up the lane, going in at every door.

They went in at their own doors behind her. It was as bad as Ferra's book said, and worse, the way a page is never quite as bad as a room. Doors off their pins. Roofs half in, and the rain of the week before still lying in the hearths. In one house a child's shoe on the sill, small and stiff and green with damp, and nobody in that lane who would say whose. Bram walked through the first four houses talking to himself, pricing the iron out loud by eye: a grate here, a good one, a crane over the hearth, two sets of door bands, the hinges red as a sunset and sound an inch in. By the fifth house he had stopped pretending not to be pleased.

Up the lane, Vantic came out of a doorway, wrote something in her notebook, looked at the roof of the house opposite, wrote something else, and went in at the next door. She did not look round at them once.

Bram watched her go in.

"She's counting our rubbish," he said.

"She's counting Ferra's," said Emeric.



The ruling came down the hill on the Thursday, on Ferra's sheet, under the House's form and the date, in two lines.

If it is worth nothing, then nothing is under the cap.

Every item taken out of the valuation is to be shown to the House, with the date, before it is used.

Ferra read it and gave it to him. He read it twice. The second line was the price of the first, and it was a fair price, and he understood it: so that nothing could quietly become something, one hinge at a time.

Vantic was still at the trestle, writing up the week. He looked across at her, and she must have felt it, because she looked up.

It was there and gone in the space of a line: something at the corner of her mouth that had been held down for the whole of the ruling and the walk and the hour with the eleven pages, and that came up for no longer than it took her to look back down at her book. She had been beaten on her own ground by a better reading of the same figures, and she had enjoyed it.

Nobody else in the room saw it. Emeric could not have said afterward how he knew. He did not say anything. He took the sheet down to bay three, where Bram was waiting with a handcart, and gave it to him, and Bram read it slowly with his finger, and then went off up the hill with the cart to fetch the first grate in Kaldo that had ever been built out of nothing.
