Chapter 72 - Steward of Grayrock

He went back up the river road three days later with Ward and the box, and told Bryda what had been in it, because it was her house the box had been hidden in, whatever she had said in the yard.

He told her in the counting room with the door shut, and he told her all of it: the survey, the sum that had paid for it, the curse that was only rotten timber and still air, the body of gravity crystal running down into the foot of her hill, and what Ferra had said it was worth. He did not tell her the name on the commission. That was not hers to carry, and it would not help her.

She listened without interrupting. When he had finished she sat for a while with her hands flat on the table.

"Eleven years," she said. "Under the winter blankets."

"Yes."

"And the two men. The two who went in against orders." She did not ask it as a question. "Their widows were told it was the curse. I sat with one of them. I told her so myself."

He said nothing. There was nothing to say to that which would not have been an insult.

Then she told him about her husband, and she told it the way she told everything, as a specification, in order, with nothing left out and nothing dwelt on, and it was a great deal worse for it.

Renner had been generous. She said that first, and meant it. He had been a doting husband and a doting father, and he had never once raised a hand to her or to the girl, and he had given her a house that ran like a clock and praised her for it to every guest who ever sat at their table. Ledgers were a man's business. Veins were a man's business. Tallies, captains, contracts, the price of ore on the coast: a man's business, and he had meant it kindly, the way a man keeps a wife out of the rain. She had run that household without a fault for twenty years. And for twenty years she had sat at the head of its table and watched him mismanage the hill, the seams skimmed too fast and too shallow, the money going on men and horses instead of timber and pumps, the captains who flattered him kept on and the ones who argued let go, and she had seen every bit of it with a perfect clarity and had never once been allowed to say so.

"He wasn't cruel," Bryda said. "I want you to understand that. People will say he was, now, because he's dead and attainted and it's easier. He wasn't. He was kind to me every day of our marriage. The kindness was the wall."

She turned the survey's first sheet over, to look at the back of it, as if there might be something written there for her.

"That's what this is," she said. "It isn't the money. It's that he kept an empire under my own floor for years and never said a word, because it wasn't the kind of thing you'd trouble a wife with. And I only know because I handed you the box without opening it. Because I didn't want to be part of his work." She laid the sheet down. "And every man in my hall this spring is waiting to do exactly the same thing to my daughter. Kindly. With a clock."

He had the charter in his coat. He had written it at Kaldo over two nights, and Ferra had read it and found three things wrong with it and he had fixed them, and it was one sheet.

He did not take it out yet.

"You were going to tell me," he said, "that a woman with a child can't hold a working. That a hall full of captains won't take orders from Renner's widow, and a seam can't be closed by a woman's hand, and the men at the gate will only stop coming when there's a man in the chair."

Bryda looked at him steadily. It was exactly what she had been going to say. He could see her decide not to say it now that he had.

"I'll tell you the rest, then," he said. "Four years ago, the first evening at your table, I had the ore contract drawn to bind the holder of Grayrock. Not you. The holder. Whoever that turned out to be." Ward was at the window, and Emeric knew he was listening, and that was as it should be. "I did it because I'd already decided what you were. A widow minding a chair. Keeping it warm until the girl came of age, or some man married into the hill. I built every assumption I had about this place on the shape of a writ, instead of on the woman who was actually holding it. And I went on building on it for four years." He did not soften it. "It was lazy. I priced you lazily. I'm telling you so, to your face, because I owe you that at least."

Bryda did not say anything for some time. She did not thank him and she did not forgive him. She looked at him the way she looked at a tally that had come out right after being wrong for a long time, and then she said, "Go on."

He took out the charter.

It made her Steward of Grayrock, in her own name, answerable to the Baron of Kaldo and to no other man in the district. It gave her the hall, the tally house, the captains and the seams, the roads and the river landing, and the uplands, promised to her administration once the work proved out. Ilsa was to remain in Grayrock, under her mother's hand, for as long as she chose. And the stewardship was not hereditary, and it was revocable at the Baron's pleasure.

He watched her read the last clause, and saw her understand it.

"That isn't the weakness in it," he said. "That's the point of it. As long as Grayrock goes by Renner's line, or by a trust, or by anybody's marriage, it goes through you, and through Ilsa. Through your bed and through hers. Every one of those men downstairs is courting a door." He put his finger on the clause. "This takes the door away. Grayrock goes through my paper now, not through either of you. A man who marries you gets a wife. A man who marries Ilsa gets less than that, because she'll still be your daughter and he'll still have to deal with you. Neither of them gets the hill. There is nothing at the end of the courtship left to take."

Bryda read the whole sheet again, slowly, from the top.

"And you," she said. "What do you get?"

"Nothing that isn't already mine." He had decided on the road that he would not pretend otherwise, because pretending would insult her. "Grayrock's mine by attainder. The ore's mine by the contract. What I get is an administration I'd otherwise have to pay a man to run, and he'd run it worse than you, and I'd know it, and I'd pay him anyway. That's all."

She laid the sheet down.

"What may I spend without asking?" she said.

He told her. A figure, per quarter, with a clause for emergencies.

"Who may I remove without asking?"

Anybody in Grayrock's service, he said. Captains included.

"And if you revoke it," said Bryda, "what happens to Ilsa?"

She asked it third. He noticed that, and understood it, and it told him more about the kind of steward she was going to be than anything else she had said. The money first and the men second, because those were the tools, and the girl last, because the girl was the thing the tools were for.

"There's a settlement," he said. "In her name, lodged at the chancery, separate from the stewardship, the way Sethe's daughter's was separate from his estate. Grazing on the south slope and a share of the ore rate. If the charter goes, the settlement doesn't. It's not in my gift to take back." He had fought Ferra over that clause and Ferra had been right, and he had rewritten it her way. "Nobody can marry it off her either. It's hers."

Bryda sat very still.

"Then yes," she said. And then, after a moment, as if the word needed something set beside it to keep it upright: "Thank you."

Then, because she was steward now and saw no reason to wait for the clerk to read it out, she gave him the first thing on her list.

"Five of my hewers have gone down the river since the forfeiture," she said. "Young ones. Your foundry pays a Copper Rand a day more than a face does, and the roof doesn't come in on them." She said it without any complaint in it, as a line in a tally. "I won't stop them. I couldn't if I wanted to, not the ones who owe nothing. But if what's in that box is ever worked, I'll want forty men who know a hill, and the ones who know it best are the ones walking to you."

He said he would remember it. He did remember it. It did not change what the foundry paid, because he could not see how it should.

The charter was read in the hall on the fourth day, by Grayrock's own clerk, in front of every captain on the hill and every suitor who was still at her table, and the courtship was over within the week.

It ended the way a market ends when the goods are withdrawn. Nobody made a scene. The widower left first, courteously, with his condolences and a sincere wish for the steward's health. The two cousins left together. The coastal factor stayed one more night, to be seen not to leave in a hurry, and then took his bays down the hill road at a pace that suggested he had letters to write. The lord with the clock asked whether he might have the clock back, and Bryda had it wrapped for him.

Garvey was the last, and Garvey said it.

He said it in the yard, on his way to his horse, with the hawk on his fist and a dozen people in earshot, grooms and captains and two of Bryda's women, and he did not lower his voice.

"I'll say it, since nobody else will." He looked at Emeric, not at Bryda. "You killed her husband. You gutted her fighting men on your wall. You took her ore on an exclusive contract, and now it turns out you took a cursed hill with a fortune under it into the bargain. And having done all that, you've put a collar on her made of paper, and you can tear it up any morning you like, and she'll thank you for it." He settled the hawk. "Congratulations, Your Highness. It's very neat."

Every word of it was true. Emeric did not deny any of it, then or afterward.

It was Bryda who answered, from the step of her own hall.

"You offered me," she said, in the carrying voice she used for captains, "a hawk. A clock, which wasn't yours to give, because it belonged to your cousin. The upland grazing as a wedding gift to my daughter, which your brother owns, and which he tells me in writing he would not give you to graze a goat on." She counted them on her fingers, one at a time, without hurry. "Your own debts, which are eleven hundred against a house that brings in four. Your mother's good opinion, which I haven't been able to verify. And yourself." She lowered her hand. "The Baron of Kaldo offered me Grayrock. I've considered the two offers, Master Garvey, and I find I prefer his."

Somebody among the grooms laughed and turned it into a cough.

Garvey went white, and then red, and then got on his horse and rode out of the gate with his hawk and did not look back, and the yard went on watching the gate for some time after he had gone through it.

Tolley refused her on the sixth day.

She closed the east-side gallery again, the thin seam with the bad roof, by order, in writing, as Steward of Grayrock, and sent the order down by her clerk. Tolley read it at the gallery mouth in front of his men and handed it back to the clerk and went on working the seam.

It was not about the seam. Emeric had known that ten days before, standing in the damp with the picks knocking below him, and he knew it now. It was about whose name was at the bottom of the order. Tolley had taken orders from Renner and from Renner's father for thirty years, and he was not going to begin taking them from Renner's widow, charter or no charter, because to do it once was to do it always.

Ten days before, Emeric had walked past it, because Bryda had told him that protection was how it was done, and he had heard her.

This time he did not walk past it. He told himself, going down the hill road to the east gallery with Ward and six of the men he had brought, that it was different now. She had an instrument. Tolley was not defying a widow minding a chair. He was defying the Steward of Grayrock under a charter from the holder, and a charter that could be defied in its first week was not worth the paper Ferra had checked it on.

He removed Tolley at the gallery mouth, publicly, once and hard. He did it in front of the east-side men, and the clerk, and Hask, who had come up from the west side to see. He told Tolley that he was dismissed from Grayrock's service by the Steward's order, which Emeric was there to see carried out, and that his cottage would be his until the end of the quarter and not a day after, and that if any man in the gallery wished to follow him out of it he was free to do so, and then asked whether any man did. None did. Tolley stood for a long moment with his jaw working, and then took his lamp off its hook and walked up out of the gallery and down the hill, and the knocking of the picks below stopped, and did not start again.

Then Emeric left a detachment. Six men of Kaldo's forty, under Osric, quartered in the old guardhouse by the gate, to be at the Steward's disposal until further order. He wrote the order himself and handed it to Bryda, and she read it, and put it with the charter, and said that she was obliged to him.

There were two things he did not say.

The first was that soldiers standing behind a steward meant that she held her seat on his men and not on her own standing, which was exactly the thing she had refused his protection over, in plain words, in her own tally house, not a fortnight before. He had heard her then. He did not hear the echo now.

The second was that six armed men of Kaldo's, quartered permanently in a gatehouse in a town Kaldo had beaten on its own wall, were a garrison, whatever word the order used, and that everybody in Grayrock over the age of ten would call them one.

He had solved the problem and put a smaller copy of it underneath, where it would not be seen for years, and he did not notice, and neither did she.

It was Hask who said it the second time, and Hask had no angle at all.

He said it at the river landing on the last morning, while the horses were being brought down, and he said it to Emeric directly, quietly, the way an old man says a thing he has been turning over for a day.

"They'll say what that hawk fellow said, you know. In the town. Not so loud." He looked up the hill, at the hall and the head-frames and the guardhouse by the gate, where one of Osric's men was already standing in the doorway in Kaldo's plain harness. "That you killed her man and took her ore and her hill, and then put a paper on her you can tear up, and six men at her gate to make sure she keeps it on. They'll say she's yours now. Collared."

"Yes," Emeric said. "They will."

Hask waited, as if expecting more.

"It's all true," Emeric said. "Every piece of it. I won't tell you it isn't." He looked up at the hall. "The other way, one of those men downstairs finds his paper by the autumn, marries the girl and owns the hill, and her mother lives on his charity in her own house for the rest of her life. That's what it was, before. That's what I had to put against it."

Hask considered that for some time.

"Aye," he said at last. "Well. That's worse."

"It's worse," Emeric agreed. It was the most he could honestly say for what he had done, and he knew it was not very much, and he got on his horse.

He looked back once from the bend in the river road. Bryda was on the landing with Ilsa beside her, and she had her hand on her daughter's shoulder, the way she had at Renner's graveside four years ago, and the charter was in the pocket of her coat. He had made her the most secure woman in the district. He had done it with a single sheet of paper, and he could tear it in half on any morning he chose.
