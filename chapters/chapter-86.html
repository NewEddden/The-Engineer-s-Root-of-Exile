Chapter 86 - What Renner Kept

Bryda had the numbers on the table in the tally house before anybody had asked for them, and she had them in the order he would have put them in, which was the first thing he noticed and the thing he thought about longest afterward.

The shallow workings, gallery by gallery. What each had given in each of the last four years, by the cartload, off the tally house's own marks. What each would give this year if the men worked it as hard as they were working it now. What that left of the foundry's need at Kaldo, which she had from Ferra's letters, month by month, with the shortfall in a column of its own. She had walked every gallery again in the winter with Hask, with a lamp and a rod, and marked on the section where the good ore ran thin and where it ran out.

"Next harvest," she said. "The west side goes first. The north is done by the winter after. There'll be ore in this hill for a hundred years, Your Highness, but not where anybody can reach it without going down."

She put her finger on the section, on the east side, where the deep working ran down past everything else, and where a line in her own hand, pencilled very lightly, ran down beside the old galleries and marked the iron.

"That's the ore," she said. "The iron runs down with the shaft. Renner sank it deepest because it was the best iron on the hill, before he knew there was anything else down there. It's been sitting under that seal a dozen years and more with nobody taking an ounce of it." She took her finger away. "So it has to be opened. I don't see a way round that. I've looked."

He had come up the river to say exactly that, and had spent a good part of the day on the boat working out how to say it to a widow about the hole her husband had sealed, and she had said it for him, from her own end, with her own figures, before he had opened his mouth.

"I've looked too," he said.

"I thought you might have." She squared the sheets. "Ferra will want to change the rate. Deep ore costs more to raise. I'd want it changed in her place."

Ferra, who was sitting across the table with her own copy, said that she did, and named the figure, and Bryda named another, and they spent the rest of the morning between the two of them in a way he recognised from the long room at Kaldo. Two women who both did their sums before they spoke. Neither raised her voice. Neither conceded a copper she did not have to, and neither pretended to be doing anything other than what she was doing, and by the middle of the day there was a rate on the sheet that neither of them liked and both of them could defend, which was Ferra's private definition of a fair contract.

He had very little to do with any of it, and it was restful.

Bryda brought the letters after the midday meal.

She came into the tally house with an oilcloth packet in both hands, the way she had once come out of the hall with a box, and set it on the table in front of him, and stepped back from it. The oilcloth was stiff with age and blackened at the folds, and there was grit in the creases.

"The chimney in the hall smoked," she said. "It's smoked for twenty years. I asked him every winter to have it rebuilt and every winter he said it drew well enough. This winter I had it rebuilt." She said it without any weight at all. "The mason lifted the hearthstone in the counting room on Tuesday. That was under it, in a hollow cut in the rock. The mason gave it to me. I read it the same night. It's yours."

"You've read it."

"All of it. Twice." She looked at the packet. "The box was in the press behind the bed, under the winter blankets. I knew it was there for eleven years and never opened it, and he knew I wouldn't. That's where a man puts a thing he trusts his wife to leave alone." She put her hands together in front of her. "This was under a stone, in a room with a lock on it, in the rock. That's where a man puts a thing he's afraid somebody will come for."

He opened it.

Letters, perhaps thirty of them, folded small and tied in three bundles with tape. Under the letters, a narrow book bound in brown leather, a household account of the kind a man keeps for himself, with the money in and the money out in Renner's large square hand.

He read the letters first, in order. It took most of the afternoon, and nobody interrupted him, and at some point Ward brought him a cup of something and he drank it without noticing what it was.

The bundle in the box, the one he had set aside at the ford two years ago, had turned out to be the surveyors' side of things. Ferra had read it in the week after, and it had been men writing to a baron about ropes and lodgings and the weather and when they might come. It had told her nothing she did not already have.

These were not that. These were the other side, and they began earlier.

The first was dated three years before the survey. It was written in a trained hand, a secretary's hand, on paper that was far better than anything in the south, and it was not signed. It said that the writer had it on good authority that the baron's men were throwing a certain kind of stone onto his spoil, black, heavy for its size, flecked through with points of light, and that the writer would be very glad to know whether that was so. It asked for nothing but a yes or a no.

The second thanked him for his yes. It described the stone again, more closely, and told him how to tell the thing the writer was interested in from the ordinary black rock it lay among. It told him which of his workings the writer thought it most likely came from, and why. It asked him to send, by a courier who would call, three pieces of it no bigger than a walnut, and to tell nobody he had done so.

The third was about money.

He read the third twice. Then he read on, and it went on the way he had known it would from the first page, a step at a time, each letter a little further than the last and each one asking a little less politely. A survey, of a kind a baron could not afford, made by men the writer would send. The writer would bear the cost. The baron would bear the silence. And after the survey, three letters that were shorter than all the others, and that he read three times each. The working to be closed. Not filled, and not stripped: closed, with its timber in it, so that it could be opened again by men who knew what they were doing, when the time came. The men to be kept out of it. And a sentence he read until he could have said it with his eyes shut: that miners were superstitious men and would supply their own reasons, and the baron need only let them.

Renner had let them. Two men had gone in against his order and not come out, and after that the working had guarded itself.

The last letter was a thank-you. It was the warmest of all of them, and it said that the writer would not trouble the baron again for some time, and that the arrangement discussed would continue for as long as the working stayed as it was.

He put the letters down and opened the account book.

The arrangement was on every third or fourth page, each spring, for six springs, in Renner's hand, under a heading that said Rent of the east water. There was no water in the east working. There never had been. It was the only dry working on the hill, which was one of the reasons it had been the best. The sums were not large. They were enough to keep a baron of a small hill comfortable in a bad year and not enough to make him anything more than comfortable, which was exactly what a man would pay who wanted a hungry man kept on the right ground, not a rich one. After six springs the heading stopped. There was no entry to say why, and no letter.

He sat back.

Ferra had come in at some point and was sitting at the far end of the table, not reading, waiting. He passed her the account book open at the first entry. She looked at it for about as long as it took to read one line, and then went to her case and took out a sheet of her own, a sheet he had seen once before, two years ago, in the long room at Kaldo, under lamps: three payments laid side by side, arriving a little late and a little light, a factor's distance from where they started.

She laid Renner's six springs down beside her three. The same staging. The same factor's distance. The same way of arriving a little late and a little light, every time.

She did not say anything. She did not need to.

He had held the name for two years. It had been in the box: a commission, signed, for a survey, which the surveyors had kept because surveyors like to be able to prove who asked them. It had been a name on a single document, a single purchase, and he had turned it over for two years and found nothing to take hold of, a knife held by both ends.

This was not a purchase. This was six years of patient teaching and six years of rent. A broke provincial baron had not paid for a survey of the rarest stone in the kingdom out of his own purse. He had not known enough to ask for one. Somebody had found this hill from a long way off, on the strength of a rumour about the colour of some spoil, and had taught its owner what he was sitting on, and paid for the proof, and then paid him every spring to sit on it quietly and let his men believe in a curse. Not to dig it. To keep it. To keep it in the hands of a man with a small hill and large debts, who could be paid enough to be quiet and never enough to be dangerous, until whoever was paying was ready.

And then, after six springs, the paying had stopped.

He did not know why it had stopped. He did not let himself guess. He had a shape now instead of a name, and the shape was better and it was still not a thing he could put in front of anybody. Letters in a secretary's hand, unsigned. A dead man's account book with a false heading. A pattern of payments that Ferra could see and a court would call a coincidence. Against a duke.

He tied the letters up again, in their three bundles, in the order he had found them.

Bryda had been standing by the window the whole time, with her hands folded, looking out at the head-frames.

"I'm not hiding anything," she said, without turning round. "I never have. I want that understood."

"It's understood."

"Good." She turned. "Then I'll ask you for something, and it isn't money."

He waited.

"Ilsa has her settlement. The grazing and the share, lodged at the chancery, where you can't take it back and no husband can take it from her. Ferra fought you for that clause and I know she won it, and I've never thanked either of you, because I don't think it was a kindness and I don't think you meant it as one." She said it plainly, the way she said everything. "And my charter says I hold this hill until Kaldo says otherwise, and not a day longer, and it doesn't go to anybody after me. I knew that when I signed it."

"Yes."

"So when I die," Bryda said, "my daughter is a young woman with money she can't lose, standing in a hall that belongs to whoever you send. She'll be a guest in her own father's house. And once that working is open, this hill is going to be the most valuable ground in the south, and every man who comes up this river wanting a piece of it is going to look at her and see the last of Renner's line, with nothing to stand on." She looked at him directly. "She asked you once what happens to her if I die. I'm asking you now what happens to her after. Not her money. Her."

He did not answer at once.

It was the question Ilsa had asked him six years ago, at fifteen, standing beside her mother: what would happen to her. He had heard it then and for about the space of a breath had thought the worst possible thing about her, and then had understood that she was simply her mother's daughter, and was looking ahead. Now it was her mother asking it, from the other side, and it was a better question than it had been then, because the money was settled and the money had never been the thing.

"I don't have an answer yet," he said. "I'll have one before I leave the hill."

Bryda looked at him for a moment longer, and nodded, as though he had given her a date, and went out.

The shaft took the evening, and it took it in front of Vantic.

He had known it would. The working was the largest single spend Kaldo had made since the day the covenant lifted, and anything above the line she attended. She had come up the river to see a boat and found a hole in a hill, and she took her place at the end of the tally-house table after supper with her small book and her quill, and waited.

He laid it out. The same sketch he had made against his knee at the sealed mouth two years ago, now drawn properly, to scale, on three sheets, with costs. A crosscut from the foot of the nearest live working, the one the survey showed running less than a stone's throw off, driven through to the foot of the sealed shaft, holed through last and from the live side, lamp first. A fire at the head of the sealed shaft, in a chimney of boards, drawing its own air off the surface, with the shaft's foul air led into the chimney above the flame. Men, timber, lamps, a smith for the fire baskets. Five weeks, if the rock was what the survey said it was.

Ferra read out the cost. It was not a small figure.

"It isn't one of the year's two," she said, before anybody could ask. "It comes out of the ore line, not the plant line. Without it the foundry stops next winter, and then there isn't a plant line." She looked at the figure once more. "It's paid from revenue. All of it. This quarter and next."

Nobody asked Vantic for permission. He did not ask her, and she did not expect to be asked, and they both knew it. The line through the ledger had taken that away from her in the autumn before last, and neither of them had pretended since that it had not. She had a right to be in the room. She had a right to read every sheet on the table and to write down every number said out loud. She had no right at all to say no.

What she had was her house's question, and she asked it.

"The service," she said. "Five weeks of crosscut and fire and forty men, out of revenue, in the same two quarters. Does the next payment on the principal fall due in full and on the day?"

It was the only question that mattered to Ducatis, and he understood exactly why. The line through the ledger rested on her house's reading of one sentence, that a principal serviced in full and on the day is not outstanding in the sense the covenant means, and she had spent a year of letters making them read it that way. A missed quarter would not break anything. It would only reopen the reading. And a reading reopened would be read by men who had been waiting for her to be wrong.

Ferra began to answer. Vantic was already writing.

She had the ore line, the foundry's receipts, the tolls, the soap, the relay's running cost, and the next two payments on the principal, and she did not need Ferra's sheet for any of them. She had them in her own book, and he watched her go down them without turning a page, a line at a time, and write a figure at the foot, and then a second figure beneath it, and look at the two figures side by side for about the time it took Ferra to reach the second line of her answer.

"The service holds," Vantic said. "With a margin of a little under a fifth in the second quarter, if the ore line doesn't fall further than Madam Renner says it will." She looked at Bryda's sheet, which was lying on the table. "It won't. Her figures are conservative."

Bryda, at the far end of the table, did not react at all, which was her version of reacting.

It was faster than the ruling on the shaft had been two years ago. That ruling had been instant, but it had been a ruling on a clause, and a clause takes no time. This was arithmetic on six heads of account, and she had done it faster than Ferra could read her own sheet aloud, and she had done it as though she had been carrying the answer about with her in her head for some time, waiting for somebody to ask the question.

Emeric noticed that. He noticed it the way he noticed the weather, and let it go.

"One thing," Vantic said, and turned back in her book, a long way back, to a page he recognised by its length. It was the copy of her covering note from two years ago, the longest thing she had ever written about Kaldo, and she found the last paragraph and read it to herself, not aloud. "My note to my house said that a sealed working full of dead air with no ventilation plan was a liability and not a capital project." She laid a finger on his three sheets. "It has a plan now. I'll say so."

"You'll say so to your house."

"I'll say so in the next report, Your Highness. Which is the same thing." She wrote a line. "I'd like that sentence off my book."

Nobody talked about a loan. It hung in the room all evening without anybody touching it. A lending house that advanced money against the working would be a secured party over whatever came out of it, and a secured party over that particular hill would be a thing he could never afterward undo. He did not ask. She did not offer. When the sheets were put away she closed her book on her quill and said goodnight, and went up to the room Bryda had given her, and he did not see her again until the morning.

Ferra signed the ore contract at first light, in the tally house, with the pen, and Bryda signed under her, and Hask witnessed it with a large careful mark, and it was done.

By the middle of the morning she was on the landing with her satchel and her document case, as agreed at the table, as entered in her book with the dates. The boat had steam up. Bram was going down with it, to come back with the smith and the fire baskets and whatever else the working would need, and Vantic was going down with it because there was nothing on the hill she could not now read about.

Ferra stopped at the edge of the quay.

"Five weeks," she said.

"If the rock's what the survey says."

"It won't be." She looked up at the hill, at the head-frames, at the grassed- over mouth somewhere above them that she had never seen. "Write to me. By the relay, every day. What you've done and what you've found. Not what you meant to do." She paused. "I'll know the difference."

"You always do."

"Yes," said Ferra, and stepped aboard without a hand, and did not look back from the rail, and he stood on the quay and watched the boat go down the river on the current, faster than it had come up, until the chimney smoke was a smudge above the alders and then was nothing.

Then he turned round and walked up the hill to find Hask.
