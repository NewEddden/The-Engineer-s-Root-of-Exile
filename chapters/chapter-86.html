Chapter 86 - The Mark

Linnet Shaw watched him bend the smoke for about as long as it took to boil a kettle, and then she said, "Stop that."

It was early, in bay three, before the hammer started. He had come over the bridge at first light with a candle in his pocket because it was the one hour of the day when nobody needed him and the bay was empty, and she had been in it, sitting on an upturned bucket by the door with her shawl round her and a cup of something hot, waiting for Paskin, who was late. She had watched him light the candle, and snuff it, and bend the thin grey ribbon of smoke off the wick sideways with air, which was not his, and hold it bent for a count of nine, and let it go, and do it again.

"Stop what?"

"Pushing it like that." She put the cup down. "You're pushing it like a door. Like it's one thing, and if you lean on it hard enough it'll go. It isn't one thing. It's a crowd." She got up off the bucket. "Have you ever tried to move a crowd? In a market? You don't push the man in front of you. He's got nowhere to go. You push the back of it, and the back pushes the middle, and the middle pushes the front, and the whole lot goes."

"Where's the back of a candle's smoke?"

"Behind you." She said it as though it were obvious, which by the end of the morning it was. "You've been pushing the smoke. Push the air behind the smoke. Push the air behind that. Go back as far as you can feel and lean on the last of it, gently, and let it lean on the rest."

Abloh had hung a length of sail cloth over the beam at the far end of the bay to dry, a big square of it, stiff and grey with the night's damp. Linnet nodded at it.

"That," she said. "Not the smoke. The smoke's too easy. It lies to you."

He tried it. He reached back, as she said, past the cloth into the dead still air at the far end of the bay, where it lay against the back wall, and leaned on it. Nothing. He tried again, and the cloth stirred at one corner as if a cat had brushed it, and lay still. On the third try he pushed too hard and too near, and the air went past the cloth on both sides and set the candle on the bench guttering and did nothing else at all.

"Gently," said Linnet.

On the fourth try he went back as far as he could feel, all the way to the stone, and leaned, and kept leaning, and the cloth on the beam bellied out toward him as if a door had been opened at the far end of the bay, and swung, and hung there, full, and he held it for a count of three and let it go, and it fell back against the beam with a soft flat slap that echoed up into the roof.

He sat down on the anvil block. His head had begun, behind the eyes.

Linnet looked at the cloth, and then at him, with her head on one side.

"That's three times what it costs me," she said. "Near enough. I can see it in your face." She picked up her cup again. "Air's not yours, is it?"

"Fire."

"Fire." She drank, and considered it, as a woman considers a horse somebody else has bought. "Fire's a poor cousin to air. Fire can't do anything without air. You'll find that out." She looked at the cloth again. "Do it every morning. Not more than four. The fifth's where men hurt themselves."

He knew why it cost him three times. She did not, and he did not say, and she went back to waiting for Paskin.



He smelled it before he was through the door of the powerhouse: wool, scorched, the sharp brown smell of a blanket that has been too near a hearth.

It was past six in the evening. The yard boy who pushed Tess up the hill was sitting on the mounting block in the mill yard throwing stones at the race, waiting for her, and did not look round. Ambrose was not there. Ambrose went up to Armand's room every evening at five, with the other students, to fill the neighbors' stones under Armand's eye, and did not come back down until supper, and complained about it the whole way up the hill and the whole way back.

Tess was in her corner by the dynamo. She was sitting very straight in the chair with her hands flat on the board across its arms, and she was white. Not pale. White, the white of a sheet, to the lips, and there was sweat on her upper lip though the powerhouse was cool, and her hands on the board were shaking, both of them, with a fine fast tremor she was plainly trying to stop by pressing them down. There was a hole in the blanket over her knees the size of a palm, black at the edges, still smoking very slightly. On the board, between her hands, lay her carving point, and a chip of stone.

She looked up at him. She did not say anything.

He crossed the floor and crouched by the chair and put two fingers on her wrist before he had thought about whether she wanted them there. Her pulse was quick and thin and steady. Her skin was cold.

"Are you burned?"

"No." Her voice was all right. Quieter than usual. "The blanket. I put it out." She took a breath. "It took something. Out of me." She moved one hand off the board, to show him, and he saw the hand shake and saw her see it shake. "It's going off. It was worse."

He looked at the chip.

It was a piece of fire stone, red-brown, no bigger than the end of his thumb, worn smooth on one side. He knew where it had come from. The still crew kept a tray by the still for the fire stones that had worn too small and too thin to be worth Orrin's time to fill, and threw them on the slag heap at the end of every month, and nobody had ever stopped a boy or a girl from picking one up off the tray. On the flat face of it, cut into the stone with the point of a carving tool, small and clean and very nearly right, was a ring. And in the ring, the mark for fire.

The cut was still warm when he touched it.

He knew what she had done before she told him, and she told him anyway, in four sentences, in order, the way she told everything that mattered. She had been waiting for the boy. She had been bored. She had had the bench book open at the back, where she had been drawing the fire's glyph since the June she had sat on the floor of Bram's hearth for seven hours and watched it, and she had had the chip in her pocket for a fortnight, off the still crew's tray, because it was a nice shape, and she had cut the glyph into it, to see if she could, with the point she used for the pear-wood. And when she closed the ring, with her thumb on the cut to steady it, the stone had pulled at her, hard, from somewhere under her ribs, and lit, and the flame had jumped off it onto her knees.

"I didn't do anything," she said. "I only cut it. I didn't mean it to do anything."

"I know."

He picked the chip up and held it. It was dead now. Whatever she had put into it, it had spent in one breath of flame, and there was nothing in it, and it would not do it twice. He turned it over in his fingers and understood, in no particular order and all at once, what he was holding.

She was a commoner. There was no ring on her, never had been. She could not draw a glyph in the air; nobody in her family ever had, or ever would. She had cut one into a stone with a knife, because she was good with a knife and had a good eye, and pressed her thumb into it, and the little that every living body carries had gone out of her into the cut and done what the cut said. It had cost her. It had nearly put her on the floor. But it had worked, the first time, with a chip off a slag heap.

And nobody had ever told her it would. Nobody told anybody. In all the books he had read in Armand's case, and all of Armand's notebook, and fifteen years of the castle and the tower, he had never once seen it written down that a commoner could do this. He had never heard it said. Which meant that somebody, a long time ago and ever since, had made very sure it was not.

He knew why. It was not difficult. A mage took twenty years to make, and a family with the gift in it, and a tower. A cut stone took a knife and an afternoon. One girl in a chair with a chip of stone was a scorched blanket. A hundred men in a lower ward with a hundred stones cut in a hundred afternoons, each one good for one breath of fire, was a thing that every mage and every knight and every lord above them would have to be afraid of, for the first time, all at once.

Tess was watching him. She had been watching him work it out, and he saw, when he looked at her, that she had got there before him.

"They don't tell anybody," she said, "because if everybody knew, they wouldn't need you."

"Not me," he said. "Them."

"You're them," said Tess.

He did not have an answer to that, and did not try to find one.



She showed him the page.

It was at the back of the bench book, past the columns and the river and the lamps, a page on its own, and on it in her careful forge hand were two glyphs. The ring and the mark for fire, drawn and drawn again until it was right, a dozen of them down the side of the page getting better as they went. And beside them, on its own, drawn only once and very carefully, as if she had been afraid of getting it wrong, another ring with another mark in it, and a single stroke, that was not fire.

He knew it. He had last seen it in the air over his own hand in the dyehouse, two summers ago, at night, pulling copper through a plate, with Tess standing in the door.

"I cut that one first," Tess said. "On another chip. Last week." She did not look at him. "The other one did nothing."

"It wouldn't," he said. "Not on that stone."

She waited, with her eyes on the page.

He did not say what it was. He did not say anything else. After a while she understood that he was not going to, the same way she had understood it in the dyehouse when he said fire work twice, and she did not ask, and he watched her take what he had said, all six words of it, and put it away somewhere behind her eyes where she kept the things she meant to come back to.

"Burn it," he said. "The page. Tonight. Both of them."

Tess looked up.

"No," she said.

"Tess."

"I don't burn pages." She put her hand flat on the bench book, over the glyphs. "I've never burned a page. Bram doesn't burn pages. Ferra doesn't. You don't, I've seen your table." Her hand was still shaking, very slightly, and she pressed it down harder. "I'll cut it out. And keep it locked. Where nobody gets it but me."

He looked at her hand on the page, and the hole in the blanket, and the chip in his own fingers.

"Locked where?"

"Bram'll make me a box. With a lock. He made Doran one."

"Bram will want to know what it's for."

"I never tell Bram what things are for," said Tess. "He'd only argue."

He laughed. He had not meant to. It came out of him in the quiet of the powerhouse with the field humming beside them, and Tess looked at him with great dignity until the corner of her mouth went too.

"Not alone," he said, when he could. "Never again alone. Not here. Not anywhere a mage might walk in. If you want to know anything about cut stones, you ask me, and we find out together, behind a door, and you don't put your thumb on anything until I've looked at it."

"All right."

"And not Ambrose."

Tess considered that. "Ambrose would tell the whole tower by Thursday," she said. "Not because he's bad. Because he'd be so pleased."

"Yes."

She took the carving point off the board and put it away in its roll, and drew the bench book into her lap, and with the point of the knife she used for her pens she cut the page out close to the stitching, cleanly, in one long stroke, and folded it in four, and put it inside her shirt, where she had once put a margin with two numbers on it.

He kept the chip. He did not tell her that, and she did not ask for it back.



Armand was awake, as Armand always was now, in the chair by the fire with the blanket and the board.

The students had gone. The case of stones on the bench was shut and locked, and the room smelled of the neighbors' fire stones, warm and faintly bitter, like a forge a long way off. Emeric sat on the end of the bed among the paper and did not say anything for a while, and Armand let him, and went on writing.

"Can a commoner fire a cut sign?" Emeric said.

Armand's pen stopped.

He did not look up at once. He finished the word he was writing, and lifted the pen off the page, and wiped it, and laid it down along the edge of the board, and then he looked at Emeric over the top of the board for long enough that Emeric began to count.

"Whose?" said Armand.

"In general."

"In general." Armand nodded slowly, as if that were a very reasonable thing to have asked an old man at ten at night. "Yes. In general. Any sign cut true, on a stone of its own kind, will fire for anyone who presses into it, if there's enough in him to give. Most men have just about enough. Once. It costs them a day in bed." He watched Emeric's face. "In general, they're frightened half to death and they never try it again. Which is the best of the ways it can go."

"And the tower knows."

"The tower knows. The Church knows. Every lord who keeps a house mage knows, or his house mage does." Armand folded his hands on the board. "Nobody writes it. Nobody teaches it. When a commoner finds it by accident, as one does every few years somewhere, somebody makes very sure he forgets it, or makes very sure he has nothing to remember it with." He said it quite flatly. "A cut stone in a commoner's hand is a crossbow nobody licensed. That's how it was put to me, when I was a boy at the tower and asked the same question you've just asked. A crossbow nobody licensed." He looked at the fire. "I've thought it was wrong for forty years. I've never once said so out loud to anybody who could have done anything about it."

Emeric did not say anything. Armand did not seem to want him to.

"There's one it won't wake," Armand said, after a while, in a different voice, the teacher's voice, as if he had decided the other subject was closed and he would rather be useful. "Not properly. You'll want to know, if you're thinking about cut signs. The shard stone." He held up one long finger. "The rarest there is. Everyone knows it exists, nobody's seen one outside a treasury. They form where the sea has hammered the same rock for thousands of years, and on the known map that's the west cliffs of the Isle and nowhere else, and the old seams there were worked out before my grandfather was born. What's left is flakes. Dead ones. Grey, like old ice. Soldiers bring them home from the Isle and wear them round their necks." His hand moved on the blanket. "Cut a sign on a dead flake and press into it, and you get a plate the size of your palm that'll stop one arrow and then crumble to dust in your hand. They call it a strain shield, in the army. It'll save one man, once. It's nothing like the real thing."

"What's the real thing?"

"Live stone. In the rock. And live stone guards itself. Try to cut it, force it, and it goes to pieces under the chisel, and you've broken the most valuable thing you'll ever touch. The old quarrymen on the Isle had a way. They blew a dead zone at the face, on purpose, and cut the stone out in the hour before the mana came back, while it was asleep." Armand let his hand lie flat. "A piece the size of a fist is worth a barony. That's all I know about it. Nobody's opened a new seam in my lifetime, or my master's."

He picked up his pen again, and looked at the page, and did not write.

"Whoever it was," he said, without looking up, "tell them to keep it somewhere with a lock."

"It is."

"Of course it is." Armand wrote a word. "Go to bed."



He went home the long way, down past the north well, because he did not want to go straight to bed and lie in it.

Sela's shop was shut. The lamp was still lit in the back, and the door was open on the warm night, and she was on her step in the lamplight with a broom, sweeping the day's dust out into the lane, as she did every night, as if the lane would one day give up and stay clean. She saw him at the corner. She did not stop sweeping. She nodded, once, the way she nodded at him now, which was more than she had done the first year and less than she might one day.

He nodded back.

When she bent to the broom her hand went up to her collar without her seeming to notice, the way it always did, and in the light from the door he saw the cord, and on the cord the grey flake, no bigger than a thumbnail, the color of old ice.

He thought about it all the way up the hill. He did not say anything about it to anybody.
