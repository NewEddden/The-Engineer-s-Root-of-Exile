Chapter 170 - Harrow Bridge

The toll-house was new. That was the first thing Saban noticed, coming up the Wayford road on the fifth morning with the carts behind him, and he noticed it because nothing else on Harrow Bridge had been new in a hundred years.

The bridge was old stone, three arches over a brown stream in a steep little valley a mile south of the town, with Lisle's woods coming down to the water on both sides and the road climbing out of the valley northward toward Wayford between banks of fern. It had been there since before the Staple. It had a worn place in the parapet where carters had sat to eat their bread for three hundred years, and moss in the joints, and a mason's mark on the keystone of the middle arch that nobody could read. And at the north end of it, where there had been nothing on Saban's last trip but a milestone and a thorn bush, there was a toll-house: a hut of fresh sawn planks with a shingle roof and a window with a shutter, and a pole across the road on a post, painted in bands of green and white, which were Lisle's colours, and a board nailed to the hut with the tariff on it in black paint.

Saban stopped his horse on the bridge and read the board.

By charter of the Staple of Wayford, granted to the house of Lisle and never revoked, toll is taken at this bridge upon all that crosses it. The cart, laden, two Copper. The cart, empty, one Copper. The beast, led, a half. The man afoot, nothing. Paper, ink, and all goods of the manufacture of the barony of Kaldo, over and above, by the bale, the cask or the box, one Copper.

He read it twice. He read the last line three times.

There were four men at the pole. Two of them were Lisle's, in green coats with the white band on the sleeve, and one of those had a book and a pen and an inkhorn at his belt. The other two were not Lisle's. They stood a little back from the pole, on either side of the road, in plain brown coats without any colour on them, with swords under the coats that they did not trouble to hide, and good northern boots, and the look of men who had stood on a great many roads for a great many masters and did not care much which one this was.

There were more of them further up. Saban counted as he sat his horse. Two at the pole. Three sitting on the bank above the north end of the bridge in the fern, eating. Four at the top of the rise where the road came out of the valley, by a picket line with horses on it. And down by the water on the east side, under the first arch, where he could just see them if he leaned, three more, washing.

Twelve. He counted twice to be sure. Twelve men in plain brown coats on Lisle's bridge, on Emeric's land, keeping the peace.

He got down off his horse and walked up to the pole.

The man with the book was polite. He was young, and very nervous, and he had clearly been told what to say, and he said it well. His lord of Lisle took toll at this bridge by an ancient right of his house, which had lain unused through the goodness of his lord's father and his lord's own goodness for twenty years, and which his lord was now obliged to take up again, with regret, on account of the great increase in traffic on this road since the Kaldo works came into it, and the cost to his lord's house of the road's upkeep, which had never been any lord's duty but his.

"The road north of Wayford is Aldane's," Saban said. "Does Lisle toll that too?"

The young man looked at the board. "The toll is taken at this bridge, sir."

"On paper and ink. And everything else from Kaldo."

"On all goods of the manufacture of the barony of Kaldo, sir, over and above. As it says."

"And the gentlemen in brown?"

The young man looked at the nearer of the two in brown, who looked back at him with no expression at all, and then at Saban.

"His grace of Aldane has lent men to my lord," he said, "to keep the peace at the bridge. In case of trouble."

"Has there been any?"

"No, sir."

"Then they're doing well," Saban said.

He had six carts. He paid for all six, laden, and for the bales on each, and the young man counted the Copper into a box and wrote the carts in his book, and wrote Saban's name, which Saban gave him, in full, and spelled for him. He took the young man's receipt, which was printed, he noticed, on a coarse grey sheet that was not Kaldo paper, and folded it into his coat.

The man in brown by the pole had watched him pay. When the pole went up and the first cart rolled under it he said, pleasantly, in a northern voice, "Good day to you, master."

"Good day," said Saban. He looked at the man for a moment. He had a face like a boot. "I'll be back through in a few days. Will you?"

The man in brown smiled. "Couldn't say, master."

The relay post was on the ridge above the bridge, where it had always been, a stone hut the size of a cart shed with a platform on the roof and a mast for the lamps, one of the twenty posts on the Wayford line. Saban rode up to it at noon, off the road, by the goat track through Lisle's woods, because the road did not go near it and the goat track did.

It was shut. The door was barred on the outside with a new plank, nailed at both ends. The lamps were down off the mast and gone. On the door, nailed at the height of a man's face, there was a notice on the same coarse grey paper as the toll receipt.

This post is closed for repairs by order of the steward of the house of Lisle, upon whose land it stands. Enquiries to the steward at the house.

He stood and looked at it for a while.

The post was not Lisle's. It stood on Lisle's land, as eight of the twenty posts on the Wayford line did, because Lisle's land lay across the road for most of a day's ride; but it was Kaldo's post, built by Kaldo's masons, kept by Kaldo's keepers on Kaldo's wage, under a lease of the ground that Ferra had signed with Lisle's steward in the year the line went in, at a rent of a Silver the year the post, paid every autumn quarter, never missed. Saban knew because he had read the lease in the spring, when he read everything in Sethe's third box and Ferra's second.

He looked at the keeper's garden behind the hut. The beans were in, and staked, and had been weeded not long ago.

The keeper, he found out at the inn that night, had been told on the first morning to go down to Wayford and stay there until he was sent for, and had gone, and was sitting in the tap-room of the Crossed Keys with nothing to do. The lamps had been taken down by Lisle's men and carried off to the house. There was nothing wrong with the post at all.

The line was dark from Kaldo's side to Wayford's. A message from the keep to the counting house now went by the rider, five days, as it had before the line was built.

Pennock did not know.

He had the charter on the table in the counting house when Saban came in the next morning, the old copy out of Sethe's third box that Saban had sent north in the winter, and his own notes on it in three colours of ink, and the notice of the judgment beside it, the one printed the day after the five hanged, with its last paragraph marked. He was a thin anxious careful man who had kept Wayford's books clean for eleven years while Sethe stole round him, and he had been at the charter for a week.

"The notice voids the Staple's charter within the barony," he said. "The judgment took it. The duty on the beam, the tenth, every right the five held under it. That's certain."

"And this?"

"This." Pennock put his finger on the copy. "This is a charter to the house of Lisle. It was granted in the same year as the Staple's, under the same seal, by the same king, as part of the same settlement. It names the Staple in its first line. It gives the toll at the bridge for the upkeep of the Staple's road. The road the Staple's carts used, to the south." He took his finger off. "So it's a Staple charter, and it fell with the rest. Or it's Lisle's charter, which happens to mention the Staple, and it didn't."

"Which?"

"I don't know," said Pennock. "It turns on what the king meant three kings ago by of the Staple. I've read every paper in this building that has the word in it. I don't know."

"Who would?"

Pennock looked at him. "The Crown's man. Keller. Or someone in the chancery with his reading. It's a question of what the Crown granted. Only the Crown can say what the Crown meant."

"And if we ask Keller?"

"Then it goes to the Crown," Pennock said, "and the Crown sits on it. For a year. Two." He put the notice on top of the copy. "And the toll is taken every day while it sits."

Saban did not answer. He had known that before he asked. He had wanted to hear somebody else say it.

The letters from the capital came to him at the inn, as they always did, by the ordinary post and by two private hands, one a wool factor's clerk and one a woman who kept a glove shop under the chancery arches and had been doing Saban small kindnesses for fifteen years for reasons that were entirely her own. There were five of them waiting. He read them in his room with the door locked, in the order they had been written.

The first was the wool factor's clerk's, and it was the one he read twice.

You asked me in the winter to note anything odd in the stone trade, it said. I have been noting it. Aldane's quartermasters have been buying crystals in the capital since the first frost, and buying them steadily, a few at a time from a great many sellers, so that no one seller has noticed. I have noticed because I asked round. Not war crystals. Not the fine stones for the knights' work or the mages' work. Cheap stones. Big ones. Common crystal, the size of a fist or larger, flawed, the sort the carters use to run a lamp and the farms use for a pump. The sort nobody buys in bulk because nobody needs a hundred lamp stones at once. They have bought, by my count, some hundreds. They are paying well over the price and paying it quietly, and the stones go north in closed carts. I cannot think what for. You will know better than I.

Saban did not know better. He sat at the table in the inn with the letter in his hand and tried.

Big cheap stones. Hundreds of them. Not good enough to be worth anything, and too many for any use he could think of, and bought quietly, which meant somebody had thought about who might notice. Lamp stones. Pump stones. A man did not arm an army with pump stones.

He wrote it down in his own book, word for word, and under it Why?, and under that nothing, and went on to the next.

The second and third were ordinary. The fourth was the glove-maker's, and it was about Corbin.

The prince Corbin spoke at the spring levee, she wrote, before the whole court, of the trouble between his brother at Kaldo and his grace of Aldane over the road at Wayford. He said he grieved to see it. He said that two loyal houses of the realm should not quarrel over a toll, and that the Crown would not stand by. He offered the Crown's mediation, in the king's name, to both, openly, and said he would send a commissioner to the bridge himself within the month to hear both sides and settle it. Everyone said it was very handsome. Everyone is saying so still. His grace's man at court said at once that his grace would welcome it. It is being said that the prince at Kaldo will be glad of it too, and that if he is not he must want the quarrel.

Saban put it down.

It was handsome. It was the handsomest thing Corbin had done in public in years, and Saban admired it the way he would have admired a well-drawn contract with a clause in it he could not find. If Emeric refused the Crown's mediation offered in the king's name before the whole court, he was a prince who wanted a war with a duke over a toll on paper. If he accepted it, he was a prince who had let a duke put twelve men on his own bridge and then asked his brother to come and ask them politely to leave.

The fifth was the glove-maker's too, written three days after the fourth.

One more thing, which you will not like. The lady Maeve was at the Countess of Brede's on the night after the levee, and my friend there, whom you know, said a word to her in the prince at Kaldo's favour and asked her whether she would not say one too, in the right place. The lady Maeve said, before the whole room, that she would not; that she had never spoken for the prince at Kaldo or against him and would not begin now over a bridge. She said it gently. She said it so that everybody heard. His grace's men had it round the wine-houses by midnight, in the form that nobody who knows the prince at Kaldo will speak for him.

That was the second time. The first had been after Wayford, when she would not put her name to either of the court's letters. Saban had not blamed her for the first. He found he did not blame her for this one either, which surprised him, and which did not help at all.

He rode back to Kaldo on the sixth day, and paid the toll at the bridge on the way south, laden, and counted the men in brown. Fourteen.

He was five days on the road, because the line was dark, and he got in at dusk and went straight up to the long room without changing, and put the receipts and the letters on the table in front of Emeric and Ferra and Ward, and told them.

Emeric read Corbin's letter twice, as Saban had. He read Maeve's once. He did not say anything about either. He read the wool factor's clerk's, about the stones, and frowned, and read it again, and said "Hundreds?" and Saban said yes, and Emeric sat and looked at it for a long time and said nothing more.

"Answer Corbin," Ferra said.

"I know."

"If you don't answer, it's a no. If it's a no, it's a war you wanted."

"I know."

"And if it's a yes," said Ward, "you've let a duke's men sit on your bridge and asked your brother's leave to move them."

"I know that too," Emeric said.

He wrote to Corbin that night, at the long-room table, in his own hand, with Ferra reading every line over his shoulder. It was short. It thanked his brother for his care of the realm's peace. It accepted the Crown's mediation, in the king's name, gladly and without condition, and said that the barony would keep to the road and to the law and would lay its papers before the Crown's commissioner on the day he came, and would not lift a hand at the bridge while it waited. It was signed and sealed and copied three times: once for the book, once for Keller, once for the Crown's rider in the morning.

"He won't come," Ward said, when it was sealed. "The commissioner."

"No," said Emeric.

"Then why?"

"Because now it's written that I said yes," Emeric said. "And it'll be written when he doesn't come."

Saban went down to the strongroom in the morning, before he slept, with the toll receipt.

Marrow was kept in the old strongroom under the east tower, which had a door of oak and iron and a window the width of a hand high up in the wall where the light came in off the lake. He had a bed and a table and a chair and a lamp, and books if he asked for them, which he did not, and paper if he asked for it, which he had not since the first week. He had been there nearly two years. He was greyer at the sides than he had been in the Staple, and thinner, and his face was still the most forgettable face Saban had ever seen, and he still did not sit at the table when anyone came in. He stood by the wall under the window with his coat buttoned to the throat, and waited.

Saban did not sit either. He put the receipt on the table and stood back from it.

Marrow looked at it without coming closer. Saban watched him read the heading from where he stood, By charter of the Staple of Wayford, and the tariff, and the line about the barony of Kaldo, and saw something in his face move, and go still.

"Harrow Bridge," Marrow said.

"You know it."

"I've crossed it." His voice was the same: low, quick, hoarse, with the flat northern vowels that Saban had placed in the first week as somewhere past the Aldane border and had never been able to place nearer. "Who's on it?"

"Lisle's men. And some in brown."

"How many in brown?"

"Fourteen, on the last count."

Marrow nodded, slowly, as if it were a sum that had come out.

Saban had come down to this room eleven times since the winter. He knew the rules of it now, though nobody had written them. Marrow did not bargain, because he had nothing he was willing to sell and nothing Kaldo was willing to buy him with. He did not lie, as far as Saban had ever been able to tell. He answered what he chose to answer, which was almost nothing, and on most visits he said nothing at all, and on some he said one thing. One sentence. Never more. It was always true. Saban had not yet worked out whether that was because Marrow could not bring himself to lie to a man who had not hanged him, or because a true sentence given away cost him less than it seemed to and bought him something Saban could not see.

He waited.

Marrow looked at the receipt on the table for a long time. Then he looked up at Saban.

"He'll make you hit first," he said.

Saban waited for more. There was no more. Marrow turned his head and looked up at the window, at the strip of lake light, and did not look back.

The mediation did not come.

Saban went north again at the end of the month, because somebody had to be at Wayford and it had to be him, and he paid the toll at the bridge going north, and counted the men in brown, sixteen, and sat in Wayford through the first weeks of summer waiting for the Crown's commissioner. He had a room at the Crossed Keys with a window on the road. Every day he read the post from the capital and every day there was nothing. The court had gone to the summer palace. The prince Corbin was hunting. Nobody had been named. When Saban's friend asked, at the chancery, which clerk had the matter of the commissioner to the Wayford road, the clerk she asked did not know there was a matter, and the clerk he asked did not know either.

The toll was taken every day. Pennock wrote it in a column of its own.

On the last evening of the month Saban rode out alone to Harrow Bridge, and did not cross it, and went up the goat track through Lisle's woods to the ridge instead, and stood by the relay post as the sun went down.

The hut was as it had been. The plank on the door. The notice on the plank, gone soft and brown in a month of weather, the words running. The mast bare against the sky. The keeper's beans had not been weeded and had fallen over their stakes.

Below him in the valley the bridge was going blue in the dusk, and there was a fire at the north end of it, by the new toll-house, and men round it, and he could hear their voices come up through the trees, the flat northern vowels, and somebody laughing. On the far ridge to the north, a long way off, he could just see the next post on the line, the one outside Wayford's south gate, standing up against the last of the light. Its lamps were lit. It had nobody to talk to.

He stood there until it was dark, looking at the post beside him, which had no light in it at all.
