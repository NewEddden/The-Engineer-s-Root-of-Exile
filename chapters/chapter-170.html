Chapter 170 - Filament

Ivo would not blow the shape Emeric drew.

He looked at the drawing on the second of January in the glasshouse, with the furnace roaring behind him and his good eye screwed up against the heat, and handed it back. A pear, Emeric had drawn, about the size of a pear, with a neck at the narrow end and a short tube coming out of the neck. Simple. Ivo had blown harder things for the line room every month for two years.

"Too thin at the shoulder," said Ivo. "You've drawn it as if glass were paper. It'll crack where the neck goes in, the first time it gets hot."

"It'll get very hot."

"Then it'll crack very quickly." Ivo turned back to his furnace. "Come back tomorrow. I'll blow you the shape it wants to be, and you can draw that, after, if you need a drawing."

So Emeric came back the next day, and there were six of them on the cooling shelf at the end of the glasshouse, not pears at all but something rounder and heavier at the shoulder, like a hen's egg with the narrow end drawn out long, and Ivo would not let him pick one up until the afternoon. When he did, it was lighter than it looked. He turned it to the window. There was no seed in it, not one bubble, and the wall of it was even all the way round, as far as he could judge with his thumb and finger; and at the neck, where Ivo had sealed two short copper wires through the glass with a bead of his softest glass round each, the joins were clean.

"The wires are the trouble," said Ivo. "Copper and glass don't grow the same in the heat. One goes faster than the other. It'll hold for a day or a week, and then it'll weep." He said it with grim satisfaction, as a man forecasting a flood he has warned the town about. "I've told you."

"You've told me."

"Then I'll have the next six by Thursday."



Tess was baking thread.

She had a crucible on the bench in the line room the size of a cup, and a hank of Abloh's cotton thread, the finest he made, and a jar of charcoal dust ground as fine as flour, and her data book; and she had been at it since Christmas, and the line room smelled of scorched cloth and had done for a week. The thread went into the crucible in a little loop, bent to a horseshoe over a clay form, and the charcoal dust went in round it, packed down hard so that no air could get at it; then a lid, and a smear of clay to seal the lid, and the whole thing into the small furnace Bram had built her at the end of the bench, and left there all night.

In the morning what came out of the dust was the loop still, the same shape, but black, and brittle as a hair of burnt sugar. Carbon. All the cloth cooked out of it and nothing left but the black.

Most of them broke. She had a saucer on the bench beside the book full of the pieces. The ones that came out whole she laid on a folded cloth in a row like the bones of very small birds.

"Nineteen," she said, without looking up, when Emeric came in with the first of Ivo's envelopes in a padded box. "Out of sixty."

"That's better than last week."

"Last week was four." She wrote something. "The cotton was wrong. Abloh says the thread he spins for the netmen is twisted tighter and that's why. I asked him why he didn't say so last week and he said I hadn't asked about netmen." She looked at the envelope in the box. "Is that Ivo's?"

"He says the wires will weep."

"He says everything will weep." She held out her hand.



He had explained it to her in November, on the bench, with a pencil, and it had taken him less than a page, because the principle was simple and she knew most of it already.

A wire that carries more current than it can easily take gets hot. Everybody in the line room knew that; they had melted enough of them. Get it hot enough and it glows, red, then orange, then yellow, then white, and white is light enough to read by. The arc lamps on the wall did it the violent way, with a spark jumping between two carbons and eating them as it went. This did it quietly. One thin thread of carbon, joined between the two wires, carrying the current, glowing white; and a carbon thread that glows white in the open air burns, in the time it takes to say so, because carbon is what coal is and air is what fire wants. So you put the thread inside glass. And then you take the air out of the glass. No air, no burning. The thread glows and goes on glowing and does not burn, because there is nothing in there to burn it with.

"An electric light bulb," he had said, which had made her look at him, because he did not often use the other life's names out loud for something that did not exist yet.

"Bulb," she had said. "Like an onion."

"Like an onion."

She had written it down. Then she had written down the air out of the glass, those five words, and underlined them twice, and looked at it, and said: "How?"

And he had said that was the part he had been coming to.



They made the first one with the air still in it on purpose, so that everybody could see why the air had to go.

Tess fixed the best of her carbon loops to the inner ends of Ivo's two wires with a dab of carbon paste and a pair of tweezers, with her breath held, and Ivo, who had come up the keep stair to the line room for the first time in his life and was standing in the doorway as if the floor might crack, took the envelope from her without a word and carried it down to his furnace and sealed the neck shut in the flame and brought it back up again, still warm. Then Tess put the two wires to the cells on the bench, the powerhouse cells, a row of them in their jars, and closed the switch.

The thread lit. White, all at once, so bright in the grey January afternoon that Osric, who was in the doorway behind Ivo because Osric was in every doorway where something might happen, said something his mother would not have liked.

Then it went. Not out, exactly. It flared, and whitened past white, and there was a puff of something like smoke inside the glass, and the inside of the envelope went grey all over, as if somebody had breathed on it from within, and the thread was gone. The time it took to draw a breath. Less.

Nobody said anything.

"That's the air," said Emeric.

"That's the air," said Tess. She took the envelope off the wires and held it up to the window and looked at the grey film on the glass, and then at the two little stubs of wire where the thread had been. "All of that from what was in there." She turned it. "There isn't much in there."

"There's enough."

Ivo held out his hand for the envelope and Tess gave it to him, and he turned it in the light and looked at the seal at the neck and said, with some pride, that the glass had held, at least; and went back down the stair.



Linnet took the air out of the second one.

She came up from Armand's room on the fourth day of it with her shawl round her shoulders, because the line room was cold away from the furnace, and stood at the bench and looked at the envelope while Tess explained. Not what it was for; Linnet did not much care what things were for. Only what had to happen. All the air out, through the tube in the neck, as much as there was, and then hold it out while Ivo closed the tube in his flame behind her.

"All of it," said Linnet.

"As much as you can."

"There's no such thing as all of it." She bent over the envelope with her hands on either side of it, not touching. "Air's a crowd. You can't push a crowd out of a room by shoving at the back of it. They only push back." She was quiet. "You make a way for them. A door. And then you don't let them see you're behind them."

Emeric felt her note go out, high and clear and quick, an air mage's, and thin down to almost nothing, and go into the glass like a thread through the eye of a needle. And the air came out. He could not see it. He could feel it, faintly, the way he felt anything an air mage did: not a push but a draining, as if the inside of the envelope were very slowly being emptied by somebody who had made it, for the air, the easiest way out of the world.

The glass sang. A tiny note, high, almost too high to hear, as the outside air leaned on it and found nothing leaning back.

"Now," said Linnet, through her teeth, and Ivo put his flame to the tube.

That one lasted a minute.

It lit white and stayed white, which made Osric say it again, and Tess put her hand flat on the bench beside the cells and did not take her eyes off it, and Linnet, who had sat down on the stool by the window as if she had carried something up a hill, watched it with no expression whatever. It stayed white and steady and there was no smoke in it at all; and then, a little at a time, the white went yellow, and the thread began to thin at one end where it met the wire, and there was a fine grey bloom on the glass there, much fainter than the first; and the thread parted at the thin place, and the light went out.

Tess wrote down the time. She had been counting under her breath.

"Sixty-one," she said.

"Not enough out," said Linnet, from the window. She did not sound disappointed. She sounded like a woman reporting on a granary. "I could feel there was more. Thin, but there. I'd have needed another breath, and I didn't have it." She looked at Emeric. "I can do better than that one. I can do it with more time. But I'll tell you now, before you ask, that if you want a hundred of those I'm not going to sit at this bench and breathe into a hundred bottles."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were."

He had not been, quite. But Ferra's rule was older than the forge and it had been the rule all the way down, every time: when magic did the first one, the recipe went beside it the same week, so that somebody without magic could do the next one the slow way. The recipe for this was on the bench already, half built. Linnet's breath was the proof that the thing could be done. It was not the way it would be done.



The pump was Tess's and Ivo's, between them, and it took them nine days.

It was a glass tube as long as a man was tall, standing upright in a frame against the line room wall, with a reservoir at the top like an inverted flask and a dish at the bottom; and halfway down the tube, a branch, a smaller tube coming out sideways, which ran to the neck of whatever envelope you wanted empty. In the reservoir was quicksilver. Not much; Saban had bought a stone bottle of it on the coast in the autumn for the clinical thermometers, and Imani had grudged every drop of it that went up the keep stair instead of down to the ward.

You let the quicksilver fall. One drop at a time, down the long tube, through a nozzle Ivo had drawn so fine it took him two days and three tries. A drop of quicksilver is heavy and it fills a narrow tube from wall to wall, like a piston. Each one, falling past the mouth of the branch, took a tiny pocket of air with it from the envelope, trapped between itself and the drop below, and carried it all the way down and out into the dish at the bottom. Then the next drop. Then the next. Each one stealing a breath of air too small to see, out of the bottle and down the tube and away, until there was nothing left to steal.

"It's a bucket chain," said Tess, when she had it running for the first time, watching the bright beads go down the tube one after another, ticking. "Of very small buckets."

"It's very slow," said Ivo.

"Yes," said Tess. She had her face close to the glass. "But it doesn't get tired."

It did not. It ticked all the first afternoon and through the evening, with the quicksilver going round and round, Tess pouring it back from the dish into the reservoir every half hour with a funnel, and by night the drops coming down the long tube had begun to make a different sound. A sharp hard click, each one, like a little hammer, as they hit the column below with no air left to cushion them. Emeric had been waiting for that sound. He had not told anyone to listen for it. Tess heard it before he did.

"It's ringing," she said.

"That's how you know."

She looked at him. Then she reached out and closed the cock on the branch, and Ivo put his flame to the neck.



The third one they lit at nine at night on the twenty-seventh of January, in the line room, on the bench, with the cells.

It lit white. It stayed white.

At a quarter past nine it was still white, steady, without a flicker, and the line room was very quiet; Tess had her hand flat on the bench again and her book open under her other hand and she was not writing anything. At half past nine Osric went to fetch Declan, because it was not right, he said, that Declan should not see this. Declan came, and stood behind Osric in the doorway, and looked at it, and said nothing, and did not go.

At ten Bram came up the stair, because Tess had not come down to the forge to bank the fire, which she had done every night for seven years.

He stood at the end of the bench in his apron and looked at the little white light in its glass with his arms folded. Emeric watched him look at it. Bram had been looking at fire all his life. He knew fire by its color and its sound and its smell and the way it moved, and this had no sound and no smell and did not move, and was hotter than any fire he had ever made, at the heart of it, in a thread finer than a hair, in a glass he could have put his hand round.

"How long'll it go?" said Bram.

"I don't know," said Emeric. "That's what we're finding out."

Bram unfolded his arms and pulled the second stool out from under the bench with his foot and sat on it.

By half past ten the line room was full. Ferra had come over from the counting room, and stood by the window in her coat with her hands in her sleeves looking at it, and then sat on the window seat, because nobody had told her anything about the third bulb and she had heard it from Osric in the yard. Imani came from the ward with the lamp she carried on her night round still in her hand, a lamp stone in a wire cage, and stood in the door and looked at the stone in her hand and then at the bench; and set the lamp stone down on the floor by the door, outside, and came in. Linnet came up from Armand's room. Ivo came, and stood behind Tess's chair with his arms crossed exactly as Bram's had been, and said nothing, and his good eye did not leave the glass.

Nobody said anything much. That was the thing Emeric remembered afterward. Not a speech, not a toast. Ward came at midnight, off the wall, looked in, and went back to the wall, and came again at two. Maren came at one with a pot of something hot and a ladle and the bowls on a tray, and put it down on the end of the bench furthest from the glass, as if it might be disturbed, and ladled, and went out, and came back with more bowls when she saw how many there were. Osric fell asleep sitting up against the wall. Declan let him. Bram, on the stool, put his head on his arms on the bench at about three and slept with his face turned to the glass, and woke at four and looked at it, and slept again.

Tess did not sleep. She sat in her chair at the bench with her book and wrote the time at the bottom of a new line every half hour, nothing else, just the hour and the half hour, down the page; and at five she ran out of page and turned over and went on.

Ferra fell asleep on the window seat with her head against the shutter. Imani went back to the ward at three for a fisherman's child with a cough and came back at half past four and stood in the door again.

Emeric sat on the floor with his back against the wall by the cells. He watched the light. He had seen ten thousand bulbs in the other life and never once looked at one. He looked at this one. He looked at the thread, which was too bright to look at, so that it left a little horseshoe of dark on everything after; and at the glass round it, clean, not a breath of grey anywhere; and at the faces round the bench, lit by it, all of them, from below, white and sharp, like faces round a fire on a beach, except that there was no fire. Somebody had put the candles out at some point in the night. He did not know who. He did not remember it happening. The line room was lit by the one small thread on the bench, the whole room, the whole night, from the cells in their jars, and nobody had poured anything into anything to make it.

At seven the windows went grey. At half past seven the sun came up over the marsh, and the light came in through the line room's east window flat and yellow and laid itself across the bench, and in it the bulb looked suddenly small. Pale and small, a little white bead in a glass egg, like a candle somebody had forgotten to blow out in the morning.

It was still lit.

Osric woke, against the wall. He looked at it. He got up and came over and stood over the bench with his hair on end, and reached toward the switch.

"Don't," said Tess, without looking up.

"I only want to see if it comes back."

"Don't."

Osric took his hand back.

It went on until a little after ten, when the thread parted near the left-hand wire, at a weak place in the carbon that Tess had marked in her book the morning she took it out of the crucible, with a small x and a question mark. Thirteen hours. A little over. She wrote the minute down at the bottom of her second page and drew a line under it, and put the pencil down, and sat back in her chair, and closed her eyes.

"Again," said Bram, from the stool.

"Not today," said Tess.



He went down to the shed in the afternoon, after he had slept, with Tess, because she wanted to look.

The K Discovery stood on her legs in the gloom with frost on the shed roof over her and the doors shut, and Fletcher was in her, as Fletcher always was in her on any day when he could find a reason, in the saloon with a lantern and a plane, taking a shaving off a locker door that had swollen in the damp. He came to the head of the sealed stair when he heard the chair on the ramp.

"I heard," he said. "Osric came down at nine this morning and told every man on the slip. Twice." He came down the stair. "So the lamp stones come out of her."

"Yes."

"All of them."

"All but one."

Fletcher looked at him. Then at Tess, who was looking past both of them into the ship's open hatch, up the sealed stair, at the lamp stone in its wire cage over the saloon door, which had lit the saloon on every night flight since the first one, and which he could feel from here, faintly, a small warm note, full.

"Everything aboard her that gives light," said Emeric. "The forefoot lamp and the stern lamp. The saloon and the cabins. The dials at both helms and at Tess's station, so you can read them. The signal lamp. All of it on glass and wire. When she goes into a dead zone, every stone aboard her goes quiet at once, and if the lights are stones the lights go with them, and you're flying a dark ship in the dark with your hands." He nodded up the stair. "A wire doesn't care what the air's like."

"And the one?"

"The probe. On the cord, outside. It's not a light. It's an instrument." He looked at the cage over the saloon door. "It's how we know how thick the air is with mana. When it brightens, we're climbing into it. It stays."

Fletcher considered the cage over the saloon door. He had flown by that light forty times. "I'll miss it," he said.

"You'll get a better one."

"I've heard," said Fletcher. "Thirteen hours." He turned back up the stair with the lantern. "And then it went out, Osric says."

"And then it went out," said Tess, from the chair, still looking up at the cage. "We'll make the next one last longer." She said it the way she said a measurement. Then she turned the chair round on the ramp and rolled back up toward the door, and stopped at the top and looked back at the ship, and said, not to anybody in particular, that the batteries would have to be in the hull somewhere, low, because of the weight, and that she would need to know where by Friday.
