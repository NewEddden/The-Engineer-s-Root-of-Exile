Chapter 115 - Legs for Beaching

She went down the ways on the twenty-sixth of April, at noon, in front of half the town, and as far as half the town was concerned that was the end of the story.

Fletcher had wanted it that way, though not for Emeric's reasons. A hull that went down a slip at night, he said, went down it unlucky, and he had never launched a hull at night in forty years and was not going to start for a prince. So she went at noon, in the spring sun, with the doors at the river end of the shed pulled wide and the people of the dock quarter lining the far bank and the people of the town on the north wall walk above, and Bram's boys on the ways with mauls, knocking out the blocks that held her one at a time from the stern forward, while Fletcher walked alongside her keel with his hand on her and his head on one side, listening.

She did not look like much. That was the other reason for noon.

From the bank she was a long dark low hull, longer than anything that had come down the river in years, with a blunt bow that turned up a little at the front like the toe of a shoe and no mast and no deck house and nothing on her at all but a flat deck of planking and a few hatches. Her sides were black with Haddon's tar boiled down a third. The foils were not on her. They lay in the shed on trestles under sacking, each a broad flat blade of oak and iron as long as a man was tall. The fan was not on her either. The legs were in her, drawn up into their slots along her bottom, and from the bank nobody could see that she had any.

A hull. Long and low and strange, which everybody agreed was the prince's way, and black, and launched.

When the last block came out she did not move.

She sat on the greased ways in the sun, a hundred feet of her, and did nothing, and the crowd on the wall went quiet, and Fletcher stood by her stern with his hand flat on the planking and his eyes shut. Then there was a sound, very low, from somewhere under her, like a great deal of wood deciding something, and she began to slide.

Slowly at first. A finger's breadth. A hand. Then faster, down the long slope of the ways, the tallow smoking under her keel, and her stern went into the river with a heavy flat crash of water that threw spray up over the doors of the shed and soaked Bram's boys to the waist, and the rest of her followed, and she was off the ways and out, floating, rocking, in the brown spring river below the north wall, with a wave going out from her across the water toward the dock quarter that slapped against its piles a long moment later.

The crowd on the far bank cheered. The wall cheered. Somebody threw a hat in the river.

Fletcher opened his eyes. He did not cheer. He watched her ride, how she sat, where her waterline came on her black sides against the marks he had painted, how she rolled and how fast she stopped rolling. Then he let out his breath through his nose.

"She's light aft," he said. "She'll want a ton at the stern. I said she would."

"You did."

"You said she wouldn't."

"I said she'd have the fan at the stern."

"She hasn't got the fan," said Fletcher, "and she's light aft." But he was looking at her the way he had looked at the half hull in the reeds, the gull on her stem and the moss on her blocks, except that this one was floating, and his beard was doing the thing it had done in the reeds, at the corners, and this time it went all the way.

They warped her in to the side of the slip with lines from the shed, and Fletcher went aboard her first, alone, as was right, and walked her deck from end to end, and came back to the stern and stood there.

"Owner," he said.

Emeric went aboard.

He had the coin ready. He had carried it since January as well, in the other pocket from the pen, since Fletcher put his copper under her heel and said the owner put his down on the deck when she was launched, and had best put down silver. It was a Silver Rand, one of the last of his father's, from the pouch handed over in public at the banquet with the court watching. He put it down on the planking of her deck, at the stern, by Fletcher's boot.

Fletcher looked at it.

"That's your father," he said.

"It's what I had."

Fletcher took a nail out of his apron, and a hammer, and knelt, which took him some time, and nailed the coin to the deck through a hole Emeric had not seen him drill, with one blow, flat, so that it lay in the plank like a scale on a fish. He looked at it there. Then he got up.

"She's paid for," he said. "Both ends."



The foils went on in the next three days, in the shed, with her drawn back up into the shallows at the foot of the slip on the high water and propped. Two of them, one either side, set out from her hull below the waterline at the angle Emeric had drawn and Fletcher had changed twice. The fan went on her stern. It sat up on a frame of iron above her deck, where a mast would have been on a boat that was not her: a wheel of six broad wooden blades in a ring of iron, the height of a man, on a shaft that could tilt from side to side, and round the hub of it, in an iron housing like a lantern with no glass, the stones.

Air stones. Eight of them, out of the chest, the last of the air in it, which he had been keeping for something and had decided at Christmas that this was the something. Each was set in the housing to push, along the line of the blades, so that when they were fed and told, they pushed the air off the blades the way a wind pushes a windmill, and the fan turned.

That was Linnet's.

He had cut the stones, from inside, in the room, so that each would turn its push on and off as it came round, and the whole ring would push like one; but somebody had to feed them, and tell them how hard, and be on the deck to do it with a hand on the housing, and the somebody could not be a prince who might be needed in a capital, or a river, or a hold. Linnet Shaw had kept a lord's granaries for twenty years by moving the air through them, and she said the air was a crowd and not a wind, and you did not push a crowd, you made a way for it.

"So you want me to make a way for it," she said, standing on the deck in the shed with her hand on the housing and her shawl over her head, "round and round. Through a fan. On a boat."

"Yes."

"At night."

"Yes."

"Fast."

"As fast as you can make it go."

Linnet considered the fan. Then she looked at him, dry and weathered and brown, the face of a farm wife pricing a market.

"Armand said you'd be like this," she said. "He said, he'll ask Linnet to do something nobody's ever done and Linnet will say yes because she's never once in her life been asked to do anything interesting." She took her hand off the housing. "He was right, the old devil. I'll want a coat. It'll be cold on the river."



They took her out on the night of the twenty-ninth, with no moon.

That was the point of the trials being at night, and nobody aboard needed it explained. Fletcher, at the stern, at the long tiller he had hung on her himself. Linnet at the fan, in a borrowed garrison coat that came down to her shins. Bram, because Bram was not going to let anybody take his foils into the water without him. Ward, because Ward went where Emeric went, and stood at her bow with his hand on the rail. And Tess, in her chair, lashed to two ringbolts in the deck amidships that Bram had put in for exactly that purpose and told nobody, with a sand glass in her lap and a log line on its reel beside her: a knotted rope with a wooden float on the end, which you dropped over the stern and let run out, and counted how many knots went over the rail before the sand glass emptied, and that was your speed.

She had asked to come. She had asked in the way she asked for things, which was by sitting in the shed every day for a week in her chair watching the foils go on and saying nothing at all, until Bram had lost his temper with her and said, for the love of God, girl, if you want to come, say so, and she had said, I want to come, and he had gone away to make the ringbolts.

They took her down the river under oars, quietly, past the dark of the north wall and under the east bridge, where the lamps on the wall were lit and showed a black hull going by with men at her sweeps, which was nothing anybody would look twice at. Below the town the river widened and ran straight for two miles between the marsh on one side and the flat fields on the other, with nobody on either bank but birds. The reach. Fletcher had picked it.

They shipped the oars. The river was black and smooth and the stars were in it.

"Now," said Emeric.

Linnet put her hand on the housing.

The fan began to turn. Slowly, the blades going round one after another overhead in the dark, and a breath of air came off it down the deck and across the back of Emeric's neck, cold, and then more than a breath. The blades went faster. The sound of them came up, a low beating in the dark, a heavy soft rhythmic thump like a great bird's wings, and the air came off them in a long steady push that he could feel against his back like a hand, and the boat began to move.

She went slowly, at first, the way any boat goes. The water talked along her black sides. Fletcher at the tiller said nothing.

"Faster," said Emeric.

Linnet made a way.

The beating overhead went up into a hum and then into a roar, and the push of the air against his back became a wind, a hard steady gale off the stern that took his hair and his coat and flattened them forward, and the boat went faster, and faster, the water going past her sides in a long hiss, white at her bow in the dark, two long white wings of it peeling away behind her across the black river toward the banks.

And then she lifted.

He felt it through the soles of his boots. A change in her, all through, the way a horse changes when it goes from a trot into a gallop: the long heavy push and drag of the hull through the water went away, all at once, and she rose. The bow came up first, and then the rest of her, and the hiss along her sides stopped, because her sides were not in the water any more. She was standing on the foils. Two flat blades of oak and iron under the black water, cutting it, and the whole hundred feet of her carried up on them, her hull clear of the river by the height of a man's knee, flying along the top of it with nothing touching but the blades and the bottom of her rudder, and the river going past under her flat and black and very fast and the spray coming off the foils behind her in two long white plumes that hung in the air.

Nobody said anything.

Bram was holding the rail with both hands. Ward, at the bow, had not moved at all, but his coat was flat back against him and his face was turned into the wind. Fletcher stood at the tiller with his legs braced and his mouth open, and Emeric saw him shut it, and open it again, and shut it.

Tess dropped the log.

She dropped the float over the stern into the white plume and turned the sand glass over in her lap in the same movement, and the line went off the reel beside her so fast that it smoked against the rail and burned her fingers through Bram's old gloves and she did not let go. She counted. Her lips moved. Emeric could not hear her over the roar of the fan, but he could see her counting, knot after knot going over the rail into the dark, more and more, until the sand ran out and she clamped her hand on the line and stopped it, and looked at the reel, and then at Bram, and her face was white.

"Twenty-four," she shouted.

Bram did not seem to hear.

"Twenty-four," Tess shouted again, at the dark, at all of them. "A coaster does six. A good one does seven. The salt boat does five, down the river, with the current." She hit the arm of her chair with her burned hand. "Twenty-four."

Fletcher put the tiller over.

He did it without anyone telling him to, carefully, a little, and she answered. She leaned. On her foils, standing up out of the water, she leaned into the turn the way a bird leans, and came round in a long curve across the reach with the white plumes going out behind her in an arc, and Fletcher held her there, in the turn, leaning, until she was heading back up the river toward the town, and straightened her, and she ran on.

He laughed.

It came up out of the beard all at once and did not stop. He stood at the tiller of the fastest thing that had ever been on that river with the wind off the fan tearing at him and his feet braced and laughed out loud at the dark, a great helpless roaring laugh that Emeric would not have believed was in him, and when Linnet looked round from the housing at the noise of it she started to laugh too, and then Tess, and Bram, at the rail, let go of it with one hand and put his face in his sleeve.

Ward, at the bow, did not laugh. But when Emeric came forward along the shaking deck to stand beside him, Ward turned his head and looked at him, in the dark, with the spray on his face.

"This is a boat," Ward said.

"Yes."

Ward looked forward again at the black river coming at them.

"It is the last thing I would have called it," he said.



They ran her three nights. On the third Linnet got her to twenty-seven, and was sick over the rail afterward from the mana of it, and wiped her mouth and said it had been worth it. On the third night, too, they came down off the foils at the bottom of the reach and let her settle, and drifted, and then put her on the gravel bar below the marsh on the falling water at dawn, nose first, gently, to see what she did.

Fletcher let the legs down.

They came down out of their slots along her bottom on their iron racks, the three of them, one under the bow and two under the quarters, Bram's boys cranking at the deck winches with their shoulders to the bars, and their broad flat feet went down into the shallow water and found the gravel, and took her weight as the water went out from under her, and she stood.

Level. A hundred feet of black hull standing on three legs on a gravel bar in the grey morning with the river running out round her feet, and the foils dripping, and not a hand's breadth of list in her anywhere. Exactly as he had said she would. A milking stool.

Fletcher climbed down a rope ladder off her side into the shallows and walked round her.

He walked round her slowly, twice, in the water up to his shins, the way he had walked the slip in January, with his head on one side. He looked at the legs. He put his hand on one of them, flat, and pushed, and it did not give. He looked up at where the leg went into her bottom, at the slot and the rack and the great heavy frame it was footed on, the frames Vantic had measured with her brass rule and found twice a coaster's. He looked along her bottom from the bow leg to the quarter legs, at the other slots, the ones that were not for legs and had nothing in them, empty, cut into her bottom at intervals along her length, square, deep, lined with iron, for nothing.

He came back to the ladder, and stood at the foot of it in the water, and looked up at Emeric on the deck.

"For beaching," he said.

"For beaching."

"Aye," said Fletcher.

He climbed back up. At the top he stopped with his hands on the rail and his feet on the ladder, and looked along her deck to the fan, standing still now on its frame over her stern with the sun coming up behind it.

"I'll tell you a thing about her," he said, "since you've not told me one." He did not look at Emeric. "She doesn't want the water. I've built boats forty years, and every one of them, you put her in the river and she sits down in it like a cow in a stream, because that's where she's happy. This one doesn't. You saw her. Give her a little speed and she's up out of it on those blades as far as she can get, like a cat that's stepped in a puddle." He swung his leg over the rail. "I don't know what you built her for. I don't want to. But it's not the river."

He went aft to the tiller and stood there with his hand on it, looking at the sun come up over the marsh, and did not say anything more about it, then or afterward.



The tank was filled on the third of May.

It had been hard enough to fill for a week, Bram said, which was the same as saying that he had stood inside it every morning rapping the cement with his knuckle and could find nothing more to complain about. The water came to it in a closed main of Bram's clay pipe, laid in March in a trench up the inner ward from the aqueduct's head beyond the wall, where the channel came in off its arches from the lake. The lake stood a long way higher than the top of any tower in Kaldo. A closed pipe from it would fill anything lower than itself and go on pushing. It was the oldest trick in his books, and it worked in Kaldo exactly as it had worked for the Romans, and he had not been able to stop himself saying the word Romans out loud to Bram, who had asked who they were, and been told a people a long way off, and said he'd have liked to see their pipe.

The water went in at the top of the tower and through a bed of sand and charcoal on the top floor that Imani had made them build and then built again because they had built it wrong. Then down into a copper pan the size of a cart bed, where the fire stones lay under it in an iron grate, eight of them, Orrin's, recharged every week by Orrin himself because he said nobody else could be trusted to do it right and charged by the stone. Under the stones, in the same grate, lay coal, laid and ready and not lit. If the stones ever went dead, a boy would light the coal. The water boiled in the pan, and ran off the far side of it down a copper channel into the tank, and sat there, cooling, three floors of it, inside the tower's grey cement skin.

From the bottom of the tank a pipe went down the inside of the tower and out under the inner ward to the upper town.

The first standpipe was on the corner by the inner ward's arch, at the top of the high street, where the women of the upper town had always set their buckets down to rest on the way up from the lower standpipes with their arms aching. Bram had cast the tap. It was brass, a plain thing, with a lever handle, on a post of stone.

A woman Emeric did not know, with a bucket on each arm and a face that said she had been carrying water up the high street for thirty years and would believe this when she saw it, came up to the tap when Bram stood back from it and nodded at her.

She put one bucket under it. She looked at the lever. She pushed it down.

The water came out of the tap with a noise like a cough and then a long hard rush, white, so hard that it hit the bottom of her bucket and came straight back up out of it over the rim and over her shoes, and she jumped back and said a word that Emeric had only heard before from Haddon, and the little crowd round the tap laughed, and she laughed too, and stood there with her wet shoes looking at the water roaring into her bucket at the top of the high street, where no water had ever been, with the whole weight of the tower behind it.

Bram filled a tin cup from the tap and drank it, all of it, and then stood holding the empty cup and frowning.

"It tastes odd," he said.

"It's boiled."

"I know what boiled tastes like. Flat." Bram looked into the cup. "That's not flat." He thought about it. "It tastes like the air before a storm."

Emeric took the cup and filled it and drank. It tasted of water, cold, clean, a little of the copper. He could not taste what Bram meant. He said so.

"You've no tongue," said Bram, and filled the cup again.

The woman with the buckets had filled both and was standing with one in each hand, not going, looking back at the tap. Then she put the buckets down, and turned the lever up and down again, twice, just to see the water come, and went off down the high street with her two full buckets toward the lower ward, walking quickly, to tell somebody.
