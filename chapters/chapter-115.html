Chapter 115 - From Memory

Declan's suit had come off the forge the night before, and the sixth line in the forge's book was still empty, and that evening Emeric took it to the table.

He did it at the end, after the harbour dues and the lime kilns and the week's census, when the six had begun to push their chairs back. He said he wanted to build a suit for Rowena, one that flew, and give it to her when they came to Emberfell. He said it plainly and then stopped, because Ferra had already opened her book.

She put the cost first. She always did.

"The harness took six ounces," she said. "Hers takes twelve. You've drawn a second ring of nodes on her, so there are twice as many. At the Crown's two Platinum the pound, that is fifteen Gold Rand in stone, before the hearts, before the cells, before the steel." She did not look up. "With the five already on the forge, the winter's new stone comes to thirty-seven Gold Rand and five Silver." Then she did look up, at him. "Every room in that ship came to twenty Gold and four Silver. Oak, brass, lead, and six men's summer."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"And it goes to a foreign seat," said Armand, from the end of the table, in the voice he used for a thing everybody already knew and somebody ought to say anyway. "Worn by one of the King's queens."

"Yes."

"Then give us the reasons," Ferra said. "In the order you'd give them."

He gave them in the order he had.

"She can fly it, and she can't make another. Only the forge makes a heart. Anything that goes wrong in it comes back to us or doesn't get mended." He turned his hand over on the table. "She's outside the Crown's reach. Whatever she does with it, she does on a coast the Crown doesn't own, and she isn't a woman who hands things to people because they ask."

"That's two," said Saban, who had supplied most of the second.

"And when I was twelve," Emeric said, "she was the only grown person in that palace who thought I was worth correcting."

He gave that one because they would have seen through the other two in a week, and because he did not want to be seen through by these six. Ferra looked at him across the table for the length of time it took her to decide what it was.

"That's sentiment," she said.

"Yes."

He did not argue it, and she did not ask him to. She had written the ninth clause and had never used it, and she did not use it now. She wrote.

She wrote it in the book everybody read, the one Vantic went through line by line at every quarter day, as a single line: *equipment, diplomatic*, and the figure beside it, in full, with nothing taken off. The line was true. Everything under it, the pounds of stone and what they would become and whose shoulders they would sit on, went into the locked box she had begun in the autumn for the things Saban bought on the barony's business that the barony wanted and did not want itemised. Only Ferra read what was in the box. Everybody could see the line.

He read the figure upside down. She had written it larger than any other figure on the page, a good deal larger than the column needed, and she did not look at him while she did it.

He had drawn her suit already, over the week before, in the pattern store, with Ward at the bench and Bram at the port.

It began as the knight's pattern, because she was a knight. Two hearts and two brass levers, the lift and the push, because she could not go into the delve and never would, and a suit that could only be flown from there would be no use to her. Round the knight's pattern he laid a second ring of nodes, at her forearms and her thighs and along the line of her spine, and those had no rods to them at all. Nothing in the hearts could feed them. They would wake for one thing only, which was a caster pouring into them with her own hands, the way a caster poured into a carriage stone or a lift. They were the reason for the twelve ounces. They would give her a burst when she wanted one and a fineness no lever could, and they would run on her.

"A knight in that," said Ward, reading it, "would find half of it dead."

"Yes."

"So would the Crown."

"So would the Crown."

Ward thought about that and said nothing, which from Ward was a verdict.

She lived on a mountain that burned, so he turned the cooling round. On the knights' suits the water in the loop was the dear system and the vent was the cheap one, and warmth came first. On hers the loop and the wet skin were the main of it, running whenever she had the suit on, and the fire stone was there for the band and for nothing else. She would wear it at noon on a quay where men went about in their shirts in midwinter.

Flat sabatons. The gravity nodes at her heels, where they were on every suit. Her cape cut to her length, which was the same as the knights', with Abloh's battens down its edges and the same watchdog at her breast and the same toggle behind it; her chute in the lattice down her spine, below the heart in the yoke, and the ripcord at her left breast where the cloak's clasp would sit over it. No lions. No device at all. She had one of her own.

"And the steel," Bram said.

"Bright."

Bram looked at the black suits in the row, and at the empty frame at the end of it, and did not ask. It was Ward who answered for him.

"Black's what his men wear," Ward said. "She's not one of his men." He said it to Bram, but he was looking at Emeric. "A queen doesn't come to her own quay in somebody else's colour."

That was the reason, and Emeric gave it himself a moment later in almost the same words. It was not the whole of it. When he reached for her in memory, which he had been doing on and off for a week, she was in bright steel on the capital's yard, with the sun on it, and he could not make her be in anything else. He did not say that. He asked the glassworks for one run of glass in blue, for her panes, because blue was the colour he could find in the same memory, on a cloak, and he told nobody why that either.

Bram had one thing to raise, and it was a smith's.

"Bright won't like the salt," he said. "That's why yours are black."

"She lives on a harbour, and she's worn bright steel on it all her life. She'll have people whose whole trade is keeping it that way."

Bram considered what kind of people those would be, and nodded, the way a man nods at the idea of another man's shop.

The hearts would be her hearts. The steel would be hers. And the one thing he did not have was her.

He brought Ward into the room as soon as the table rose, and asked him to put her there.

He had tried it alone first, the night before. It had not gone well. The room took what was put into it, and what he had to put into it was a boy's memory of a tall woman in bright steel, and a face he could half see, and a voice, and very little else. What stood in the air above Kaldo when he had finished was not a person. It was a height and a colour and a feeling of being looked at, and the room had built it faithfully, and it was no use to anybody who wanted to put a breastplate on it.

"Every knight in the capital watched her on the yard," Ward said.

That was all he said about the capital. Emeric did not ask for more. There was a door in Ward he had never been through and had never been asked through, and he did not knock on it now.

"Put what you saw," he said.

Ward put what he had seen, and he put it the way a quartermaster counts a store.

He gave the shoulders first, and they were broad, broader than a man would expect before he had seen her, because she had built them on purpose over years. He gave the reach, which was long for her height, and the length of the forearm, and the hand. He gave the way she stood, with the weight on the back foot and never square to anything, not to a man, not to a wall, not to a door, and the room took it and she stood that way in the air, half turned, the way nobody stands unless they have been hit from the front often enough to stop offering it. He gave where she carried her weight, and how high her guard sat, and the line of her from the hip to the heel when she went forward. None of it was admiring of anything but her fighting. She was his king's queen. Ward would have found any other reading of what he was doing vile, and Emeric knew it without being told, and it was not in the room at all.

Then Ward stood looking at what he had made for a while, and said something more, and it was the most Emeric had ever heard him say about another fighter in ten years.

"I saw her in the field once."

He did not say which field, or which year, or whose side of it he had been standing on, or why. Every knight of his age had stood on a field. It told Emeric that Ward was a knight and nothing more.

"I never saw a woman knight that fast," Ward said. "Or a man, often. She was a demon on that field, and she had the grace of an angel doing it." He said it flatly, the way he said the number of men on a road. "It made sense she was the top of the women in Randillia. Where she sits among the men, I don't know for sure. High. If there's anybody above her, it's because there are men who are mage and knight both, the same as she is, and a man's body starts out stronger. That's all it would be."

Emeric looked at the woman in the air.

"I hope she hasn't changed," he said.

"She won't have." Ward did not seem to think it was a question. "She's a warrior. If she's still on her yard, her body's what it was. And she's a mage as well as a knight, and they wear slowly, that sort. You'll see."

So Emeric gave the face.

It came better with the body under it. The room had something to hang it on. The jaw, the set of the mouth, a way of looking at a boy from a height as though she were deciding what he was worth and had not yet made up her mind. Black hair, pulled back hard. And he gave her the height, and the room took it, and she went up in the air above Kaldo to where he remembered her, and stood there.

Ward looked at her for a moment.

"She's shorter than that," he said.

"She was taller than anybody on that yard."

"She was taller than you." Ward said it the way he would have corrected a count of arrows. "You were twelve. You were the one who was small."

Emeric looked at the figure, and at Ward, and understood that Ward was right, and that he had been building her for a week from the eye level of a boy who came up to her shoulder. He let the room have Ward's figure. The woman in the air came down, a hand and more, and stood at the height Ward gave her, and she was still tall for a woman and no longer tall for anything else.

Ward was right about the body and he was right about the height. The room built her from both of them, and where neither of them had looked, it left her vague: the hair at the back of her head, the line of her ear, the way she would stand when she was not about to hit anything. It was like Saban's demolished streets in the first room, confidently wrong, or like the streets nobody had walked, confidently nothing. Emeric did not fill them in. A breastplate did not need to know the line of her ear.

He was still reaching for her face when the yard came instead.

It was not a thing he meant to remember. He was twelve, on the capital's practice yard with a borrowed blade that was too long for him, and he was holding it the way a boy holds a thing he has been told is his, and she had come across the yard to him in bright steel with the sun on it and taken his hand in hers. She had closed his fingers round the hilt and moved his thumb, and held it there until he felt where it went.

"Not like that," she had said. "Like you mean to keep it."

That was all. She had let go and gone back across the yard and never mentioned it again, and nobody else in the palace had ever done anything like it, before or after. The boy he had been had been drunk by then, or seeming to be, for four years, and she had corrected his grip anyway.

He stood in the room above Kaldo with the memory in his hand, and he did not put it into the room. It was not a measurement.

He built adjustment into every strap of her suit regardless. A suit built from two men's memories was still built from memories, and he had just watched one of the two be wrong by a hand.

The forge took her pattern at midnight. He watched it take the first billet and lift the plate and begin, and then he went up to the long room to put the pattern's page in the forge's book, and Ferra was there, at her end of the table, with the forge's book open under the lamp and nobody else in the room.

She did not look up when he came in. She finished the line she was writing and blotted it, and then she turned the book round so that he could see the sixth line, which was still empty.

"What are you to her?" she said.

He had half expected it at the table, and she had not asked it at the table. She had waited until there was nobody else to hear the answer, which he understood only now that she had done it.

"It decides the column," Ferra said. "A prince of the house she left, bringing a present to his father's wife, is a tribute, and it goes in one place. A friend's son is a gift, and it goes in another. A barony that wants her sulphur and is paying for the first meeting is a bribe, and that goes in a third, and I'd want to know that before I wrote it anywhere." She laid the pen down beside the book. "And I'd want to know it before we land, because she'll want to know too, and she'll have decided before we're tied up."

He started to answer and she lifted one finger off the table.

"Whatever you answer," she said, "not the ledger answer."

He sat down across from her. It took him a while. He found that he had the answer, and had had it for a week, and had not once said it to himself in words.

"My mother's friend," he said. "When I was twelve she was the only one in that palace who corrected the drunk, instead of laughing at him or looking past him. She left when I was sixteen, and I never asked why." He looked at the empty line. "If she's anything to me, she's the last person who thought I was worth the trouble before I came here."

Ferra did not say anything.

He heard what he had said a moment after she did. She had been the first one here. She had put a figure in front of him in his first months in Kaldo, when nobody else in the barony expected anything of him at all, and waited to see what he would do with it. Neither of them said so. The lamp hissed. Down on the stair, Ward shifted his weight, once, and was still.

Ferra picked up the pen.

She wrote in the sixth line, in her even hand: *Rowena. Emberfell.* And across the page, in the gift column, which had seen very little use in ten years, she wrote *gift*, and nothing after it.

Then she blotted it, and closed the book, and said goodnight in the voice she said it in every night, and went down the stair.

Below him in the pattern store the forge was drawing the first plate of a breastplate out of the billet, bright, and would draw it bright all night. It would be bright steel and it would be bare. No lions, and no device, and nothing painted anywhere on it. A knight dressed her own.
