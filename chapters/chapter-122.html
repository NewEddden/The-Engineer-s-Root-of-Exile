Chapter 122 - The Matter

The court did not sit in Keller's room. It sat in the old hall of the court building, on the ground floor, which Emeric had never been in: a long high room with a timber roof gone black with three hundred years of candle smoke, and a bench at the far end raised two steps above the floor, and below it a table for the clerks, and below that the floor of the hall, bare boards, with a rail across it, and benches behind the rail for anybody who wanted to come and watch the crown do justice to its own.

A great many people wanted to. Emeric had not expected that.

The benches were full when Ward brought them in at a quarter to nine. Clerks, mostly, from the chancery and the court, who had heard about the nine sacks. Men in good coats who might have been anybody's stewards. A knot of young men near the back who had the look of the sons of the lesser houses, come to see a prince of the blood, the disgraced one, the one who had been sent south to a plague barony four years ago with fifty Gold Rand and a joke, stand up in the old hall and accuse a baron. And by the door, in a row of their own, quiet, with their hands folded, three men in the plain grey of the House of Ducatis, one of whom Ferra looked at as they came past and then did not look at again.

Sethe was already there.

He sat on the other side of the floor of the hall, on a bench at a table of his own, with a man in black beside him who was his advocate, and his steward behind him with a box of papers. Emeric had never seen him. He had looked through the window of the long room at Wayford's posting house on a Thursday in the winter and not wanted to know, and looked away. He looked now.

A man of fifty or so, in a coat of good dark blue, well cut, with silver buttons. Grey at the temples. A face that had been handsome and was still pleasant, open, the face of a man who dined in the long room on Thursdays and had travellers over to his table to ask them about the weather in the south. He was not looking at anybody. He was looking at the bench, at the empty chair on it, with his hands folded on the table in front of him, quite still.

Then he turned his head and looked across the floor of the hall at Emeric, and smiled. It was a perfectly pleasant smile, a neighbour's smile, a smile for a young man you have heard a good deal about and are glad to meet at last. And then he looked at Ferra, and at the three deal boxes Ward and Rylan were setting down on Kaldo's table, and the smile stayed exactly where it was, and something behind it did not.

The boy from the passage was not in the hall. Neither was Paxton.

At nine a door behind the bench opened and Keller came in, and the hall stood up.



He did not make a speech. Emeric had not thought he would.

He sat down in the chair on the bench, and his clerk, the thin young one with ink on his cuffs, sat down at the table below him with the register open, and Keller looked down the length of the hall at the two tables on the floor of it, Kaldo's and Wayford's, and then at the benches behind the rail, full to the door, and his face did not change.

"This court sits upon the matter numbered," he said, and gave the number. "Opened in May upon the papers of the barony of Kaldo. Concerning the crown's post on the southern road." He looked at Sethe. "The holder of the barony of Wayford attends."

"He does," said Sethe's advocate, rising.

"The holder of the barony of Kaldo attends." Keller did not look at Emeric. "With his Chancellor." He looked at his clerk. "Write it so."

Then he began to lay things down.

He laid them down himself, from the bench, one at a time. He did not let anybody else do it. His clerk brought each thing up the two steps to him and he took it and looked at it and then laid it on the bench in front of him, flat, where the hall could see it, and said what it was in one sentence and no more, and then sent for the next. It took the whole morning. Emeric understood, about an hour in, that it was meant to.

The first thing was a book.

A tall narrow ledger, old, bound in calf gone black at the corners, with the crown's arms stamped on the cover and worn nearly smooth, the way they were worn over the door of the stone house at the head of the Wayford ferry slope.

"The toll book of the crown's sorting house at Wayford," Keller said. "For the last six years. Brought to this court by the chancery's audit, upon the warrant holder's returns, in June."

He opened it. He did not read from it. He had his clerk bring up a sheet, a single sheet in the clerk's quick clean hand, and laid it beside the book.

"The sorting house weighs every bag that comes in at its front door, for the toll," Keller said, "and every bag that goes out of it north, for the same. In and out. By the day. It has done so for a hundred years. It is the crown's oldest habit on that road and the most tedious." He laid his hand on the sheet. "The chancery's clerk has set the weights of the bags that came in from the south against the weights of the same bags that went out north, for six years. For most days they agree, as they should, to within the weight of the rain. On some days they do not. On those days the bags went out north lighter than they came in from the south." He did not look at Sethe. "By the weight of a letter or two. Never more. Every one of those days is a day on which a bag from the barony of Kaldo came into the house."

There was a sound from the benches, not loud.

"Every day," said Keller. "For four years. And on no day before the barony's present holder took it."

He laid the sheet on top of the book and sent for the next thing.



The next thing was a letter.

It was one of Kaldo's. Emeric knew it before the clerk had brought it halfway up the steps, by the fold, the fold Ferra had chosen two years ago for the twin year and every letter since. It had come north in the nine sacks. It had been opened. Keller laid it on the bench in front of him and turned it so the seal was uppermost and had the clerk hold a candle beside it, though the hall was light, and Emeric understood that it was not for the hall, it was for the record.

"A letter of the barony of Kaldo to the crown," Keller said. "Of the November of last year. Delivered to the chancery in June among the held correspondence of the sorting house at Wayford." He looked at Kaldo's table. "The barony's die."

Ferra opened the second box and took out Bram's die, in its little bag, and stood, and carried it up to the foot of the bench herself, and the clerk took it from her and carried it the last two steps.

Keller put the die beside the seal, under the candle. He took a glass from his coat, a small round reading glass in a horn frame, and bent over the two.

He took a long while.

"The tower," he said at last. "On the barony's seal. At the shoulder of the tower, on the left, where the wall meets the roof, the barony's die has a nick." He did not look up. "The seal on this letter has no nick." He put the glass down. "This letter was opened, and sealed again with a die that was not the barony's. Cast, I would say, from a true impression, by a man who copied what he saw and did not see what was not there to be seen." He straightened. "This court will hear from the barony's smith, if it needs to, as to when he cut the nick and why. It does not think it needs to."

Across the floor of the hall, Emeric saw Sethe's advocate lean over and say something in Sethe's ear. Sethe did not move. He went on looking at the bench, pleasantly, with his hands folded.



The third thing was a certified extract.

It came from the House of Ducatis. One of the three men in grey by the door got up when the clerk called it and brought it to the foot of the bench himself and bowed and went back to his place, and Keller took it and read it through before he laid it down.

"An extract," he said, "certified by the House of Ducatis under its seal, from the books kept by the House's factor in the barony of Kaldo under the covenant of the barony's debt." He laid it flat. "Every cost put upon the House's security in Kaldo by the barony's neighbours since the toll at Wayford was raised against Kaldo's carts. Dated. Itemised. With the factor's own note against each of where the information came from and how she had tested it." He looked at the sheet a moment longer. "It is the most careful accounting this court has been handed in some years. The House's factor has a cold eye. The court commends it."

Ferra, beside Emeric, did not move at all. But under the table he saw her hand, which had been flat on her knee, close.



Then Keller called Paxton.

He came in by the door at the side of the hall, from a room where the witnesses had been kept, and walked down the floor of the hall to the rail in front of the bench in his plain brown coat with his hat in his hand, and he did not look at Kaldo's table, and he did not look at Wayford's. He looked at Keller, and stood there, broad and square and weather-burned, the way he had stood in the gate yard at Kaldo in August of the war year saying he was not going to write letters.

Keller asked him who he was. He said. Keller asked him whether he had received the summons. He said he had.

"You are the holder of Milford," Keller said. "Which neighbours Wayford, and Kaldo."

"I am."

"Tell the court what happened in Milford in the last week of the November of last year."

Paxton told it.

He told it the way he had written it, in the four lines sewn into the drover's coat. He did not add anything. He did not say what he thought. He said the twenty-second, two wool carts taken on the western road nine miles from the coast, the carters beaten. The twenty-third, ninety-one head driven off the western grazing toward the hills, two herdsmen hurt, one since dead. The twenty-fourth, the barn at the boundary stone burned to its walls with Kaldo's wheat and Milford's fodder in it. The twenty-fifth, Dunnel, his factor, and the boy who drove for him, dead on the coast road a dozen miles short of the coast house, the cart burned. He said it in a level heavy voice to Keller on the bench, as a steward says a stock book, and the hall was so quiet that Emeric could hear the clerk's pen.

"Why," said Keller, "do you think those four nights came when they did?"

Paxton was silent a moment.

"Because I was read," he said.

And then he turned his head, for the first time, and looked at Kaldo's table. At Emeric.

"In the April of the war," Paxton said, still looking at Emeric, "I sent the holder of Kaldo a warning. In my own hand. That Grayrock meant to come against him, and Wayford behind it, and that I'd be made to bring my levy, and that I'd stand it off at the back and not draw, if he'd understand it so. I asked him to keep that between us. I told him in his own gate yard that it was the only thing I owned that Sethe hadn't priced." He did not raise his voice. "In the November after, he wrote it into his report to the crown. All of it. To save my land from the forfeit for taking the field. He wrote and told me so himself, after. He said he'd thought the letter would most likely die unread on the road, the way all his letters died." Paxton turned back to the bench. "It didn't die unread. Somebody at Wayford read it. Seven days after he lodged it, Milford began to burn. Four nights. On the side of Milford nearest the coast and furthest from Kaldo, where my men couldn't get to it in time." He stopped. "That's why."

"The court has that report," said Keller. He had the clerk bring it up: Kaldo's own copy, from the third box, the November report with its two pages about Milford. He did not read it aloud. He held it up, so that the hall could see it, and then he had the clerk read the two pages aloud, all of them, in his quick clean voice, every word Emeric had written at his table that afternoon in November about Paxton of Milford, while Paxton stood at the rail with his hat in his hand and listened to it with no expression at all, and Sethe sat at his table and listened to it, pleasantly, with his hands folded.

When the clerk had finished, Keller looked down at Paxton.

"Do you hold the barony of Kaldo at fault," he said, "for writing it?"

The hall waited.

Paxton looked at Emeric across the floor of the hall again. A long look. The look of a man pricing a plank.

"I hold him at fault for spending a thing that was mine," Paxton said. "I've told him so. He knows it." He turned back to Keller. "I hold the man who read it at fault for the barn."

He stepped back from the rail.



The rest came quickly after that, because Keller let it.

The barn burners. Two of them, brought up from the cells under the court in irons, and a letter from the magistrate of the coast who had taken them: men from the bottom of the ferry slope at Wayford who had been paid in silver at a tavern table to ride round Milford's north on the hill road in November, and wait for dark, and who had been found in the spring in a coast town drinking the rest of the silver, because Saban had been asking up and down the coast all winter in his dry voice in every tavern that sold beer to men with money they could not account for. The coast magistrate had turned them. They said, standing in their irons in front of the bench, who had paid them, and where, and how much, and whose steward had brought the silver.

Sethe's steward, behind Sethe's table, had gone the colour of tallow.

And then Keller called the boy from the passage.

He came in between two of the watch, with his hands tied in front of him and his split lip gone dark, and stood at the rail where Paxton had stood, very small in the high black hall, and told it. The tavern at the bottom of the ferry slope. The man in the good coat with the silver on the table. Five who could use a knife. The house in the outer ring, the night before the court, the room at the window end, the young man with the gold eyes in it. That he was not to go to court in the morning. That the woman with the boxes was to be kept, because she would know where everything was.

The hall made a sound then that was not like the sound it had made before. It was low, and it went on for some time, and Keller let it go on, and then looked up from the bench, and it stopped.

"The court has the names of nine witnesses to the night of the twenty-fifth," he said. "In the lodging, the lane and the watch. Written down within the hour, and signed." He looked at Kaldo's table. At Ferra. "By the Chancellor."

He laid the summons book on the bench beside the toll book and the letter and the House's extract and Paxton's four lines.

Then he looked at Sethe.



Sethe stood up when he was told to.

His advocate had spoken for most of an hour, before, and said everything an advocate could say: that a toll book's weights were the work of a careless sorter; that a seal could be recut by anybody; that a House which had lent to Kaldo had every reason to wish Kaldo well; that Paxton of Milford was known to be a friend of Kaldo's and a debtor of Wayford's; that men in irons said what they were told to say by whoever held the key; that a boy from a tavern would swear to anything for his life. Keller had listened to all of it without interrupting. Then he had asked the advocate one question, which was whether his client could tell the court where his steward had been on the evening of the eleventh of July, and the advocate had looked at the steward, and the steward had looked at the floor, and the advocate had sat down.

Sethe stood with his hands at his sides and looked up at the bench with his pleasant open face.

Keller read the findings. He read them flat, in the order he had laid the things down, the way Ferra read a column back. That the barony of Wayford, by its holder, had caused the crown's post from the barony of Kaldo to be withheld at the crown's sorting house at Wayford for four years, and opened, and read, and sealed again with a false die. That the barony of Wayford, by its holder, had caused men to be paid to burn and rob and kill in the barony of Milford, upon what it had read. And that the holder of Wayford had caused men to be paid to kill, in the crown's own city, on the night before he was to answer for it in the crown's own court, a prince of the blood royal.

"The first two," Keller said, "are crimes against the crown's post and the crown's peace, and would forfeit any holder's land." He looked at Sethe. "The third is treason."

The hall was perfectly silent.

"Sethe, holder of Wayford," said Keller. "This court finds the matter against you upon every count. It sentences you, upon the third, to death, in the manner the law provides for treason against the blood royal. The forfeiture of your holding follows upon the sentence and will be heard by this court in its own time." He laid the paper down. "The court will rise."

Sethe did not move.

He stood at his table with his hands at his sides and his face exactly as it had been all morning, open and pleasant, and two of the court's men came round the end of the rail toward him, and he did not look at them. He looked across the floor of the hall at Emeric. He looked at him for a long breath, while the men came, with nothing in his face at all, the pleasant nothing of a man at his Thursday table asking a traveller about the weather. And then, as the first of the men took his arm, he looked away from Emeric, at Ferra, beside him, and something came into his face for the first time that morning, very briefly, and went out.

Emeric did not know what it was. He thought, afterward, on the road, that it might have been surprise.

They took him out by the door behind the bench.



Paxton was in the courtyard when they came out, by the horse trough, with his hat on, waiting for nobody in particular.

Emeric went to him. He did not know what he meant to say. He got as far as the trough and stopped, and Paxton looked at him, and then at Ferra behind him with the books, and Ward behind her with the boxes, and then back at him.

"Well," said Paxton.

"Thank you."

"Don't." Paxton looked at the trough. "I didn't do it for you. I told the man on the bench so and I'll tell you." He was quiet. "Dunnel stood up on his cart and waved his scarf at your engine. He told me about it every week after. Fourteen horses, he said. Fourteen horses out of a fire in a pot." He took his hat off and looked into it. "I'd have said it in court if you'd never written me a word."

"I know."

"You don't, but you will." Paxton put the hat back on. He looked at Emeric a while longer, the long look, the look of a man pricing a plank he has been offered and does not expect to buy. "You spent a thing that was mine," he said. "Without asking. To save my land for me." He let his breath out through his nose. "It's saved, now. Sethe's paid for it." He turned to go. "I'd still rather you'd asked."

"Next time I'll ask."

Paxton stopped. He looked back over his shoulder.

"There'll be a next time," he said. It was not quite a question. "Milford sits between Wayford and the sea, and Wayford's going to be the crown's now, or somebody's, and that somebody's going to want to know who his neighbours are." He looked at Emeric a moment longer. "Write to me," he said. "By a road you trust. I'll answer."

He went out of the courtyard gate into the street.

Ferra stood beside Emeric with the books under her arm and watched him go.

"He said write," she said.

"He did."

"In August of the war year he said he wasn't going to write letters." She shifted the books on her arm. "I wrote it down." She looked up at the sky over the court, which was hot and white and full of swifts. "I'll write this down too."
