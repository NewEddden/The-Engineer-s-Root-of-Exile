Chapter 122 - The Ground She Chose

Her knights were on the yard before the sun was over the wall.

There were more of them than there had been on the quay, and they had not been told to come. They stood along the racks and on the steps at the far end and on the top of the wall itself, in their padded jackets and their shirts, men and women both, a good many more women than any yard Emeric had ever seen, and they talked among themselves in low voices and stopped when he came in, and started again when he had gone past.

He had come in a shirt and a padded jacket from the harbour house's stores, with no plate anywhere on him, because that was her rule. Ward walked beside him to the edge of the flags and no further. The rest of Kaldo's party came behind and found places along the wall where they could see: Osric, who had already been talking to a knight on the steps for some minutes and would not say about what, and Saban, and Imani with her canvas roll, and Ferra with her book shut, and Vantic a little apart from all of them. Imani had brought the roll because Imani brought the roll to everything. Nobody had asked her to.

Rowena was in the middle of the yard with two blunt blades.

She was in a jacket like his, and her hair was bound back as it always was, and she held the blades one in each hand, point down, the way a woman holds two things she has just picked up and has not yet decided what to do with. She did not look at the crowd. She watched him cross the flags to her.

"Steel only," she said. "No casting. No armour. First to a clean touch that would have ended it."

"Yes."

She gave him one of the blades, hilt first, and kept the other, and he saw that she was going to fight him with one sword and not two, and knew what that meant, and did not mind.

He did not open the sense.

He had thought about it in the night, lying in the small room at the top of the harbour house with the mountain glowing red in the window, and had come to it by the end of the night and not before. The sense was not casting. He knew exactly where that line lay, better than anybody on this yard would ever know it. When he let the medium open out of him he put nothing into anything. He did not push, or lift, or warm, or move a grain of sand. He only listened. By her rule he could have opened it, and been within the letter of it.

She would feel it. She had felt it on the quay, go over her like a draught, and had put her hand to her sword. And she would not care where he thought the line lay. She would call it casting, in her own yard, in front of her own knights, and she would be right in every way that mattered to her, and he would have cheated his mother's friend at her own rule on her own ground.

So it stayed shut. It had been shut since the wall, the afternoon before.

He did not need it. He had put his hand on the wall and read the yard on his way through it to the harbour house, and it was in him now the way a map was in him once he had looked at it, and it would stay. The loose flag by the water butt, which rocked under a heel toward the butt and not away from it. The patch round the butt's foot that was always damp, and in the cool of the morning slick with it. The whole of the yard falling away toward the drain in the far corner by the width of half an inch, so gently that nobody who had walked it every day of their life had ever felt it.

He had never put anything into that stone and he would not now. He would never touch her ground. He only knew it.

She came at him the moment the knight on the steps dropped his hand.

He had fought Ward every morning for ten years. He had fought the garrison, and Osric, who was quick and liked to talk while he did it, and Declan, who was strong and did not. He had thought he knew what fast was.

He did not. She was not fast the way Osric was fast, a man going very quickly from one place to another. She was simply there, where the blade needed to be, without ever seeming to have been anywhere in between, and the blunt steel came at his head and his ribs and his leading knee in one long unbroken movement, and he met the first and the second and the third went past his knee by the width of a finger because his knee was not where she had sent it.

It was not where she had sent it because he had not planted.

That was the whole of what he had. He was not a knight. Nothing he had done in the delve in ten years had put a pound on him, and she had thirty years and more of a knight's body and a mage's, and more than that, she had been the best on every yard she had ever stood on since she was a girl. He could not stand in front of her and trade. He could not outreach her or outlast her or outthink her blade, which did its thinking in its own time, faster than his.

What he could do was not be where she expected him to plant.

She fought the way every good fighter fought, the way Ward had taught him to: she read the feet. She watched where a man set his weight and she struck at where it would be when he had set it. And he set it where she did not expect, over and over, not by being quicker than her but by knowing the ground under both of them better than she did. He fought on the sound stone. He gave her the rest of the yard.

He took the high side of the half-inch fall, every time, so that she was always coming a little uphill at him and never knew it, and every cut she made from below came up a hair short of where her arm said it would. He kept away from the butt. He let her have it. He gave ground toward it, and round it, and past it, and never once stood on the damp.

She drove him across the yard, and back, and across again. The blunt steel rang. He was losing, and he knew it, and every knight on the wall knew it, and he did not stop losing and he did not go down.

The first time was near the butt.

She came in fast from his left, and he gave ground backward and to the right, round the butt, so that to follow him she had to come across its foot. She came. Her back foot went down where she had put it a thousand times on this yard since she was a girl, where she knew it would be, and the damp under the butt was slick in the morning cool, and the foot went a finger's width further than she meant it to.

It was nothing. She caught it in the same breath, and was balanced again before he could have used it, if he had tried to use it, which he did not. But he saw her feel it. He saw her face do something for an instant that her face had not been given permission to do.

The second time was the flag.

It was later, after another long pass that took him the length of the yard and left his forearm numb where he had met a cut too square. He gave ground toward the loose flag. She drove him onto it, which was what he had meant her to do, and he did not stand on it. He stepped over it, backward, onto the sound stone beyond, and she came after him and put her leading foot on it as she lunged.

It rocked. It rocked the way he had known it would, toward the butt and not away, and her lunge went a hand's breadth wide of where she had sent it.

She had known that flag was loose. She must have known it for years. But she had known it the way a woman knows a stair in her own house that creaks, from one side, walking up it, and she had never in her life come onto it going forward in a lunge from the other side, because nobody on this yard had ever put her there.

At the bind she saw his hand.

It was the only time in the whole of it that they were still. Her blade came down on his and he took it near the hilt and they stood close, blade to blade, the steel grinding, and she looked down the length of the two blades at his hand on the grip.

It was her grip. It was the thumb where she had put it, on a yard in the capital, when he was twelve and holding a borrowed sword like a thing he had been told was his. He had held it that way ever since. Ward had never once corrected it, in ten years of mornings, and Emeric had never once thought about why.

Something went across her face. It was there and gone. He could not have named it and he did not try.

Then she broke the bind, and it was over in three more strokes.

She did not do anything he had not seen. She did it better than he had ever seen it done. She feinted high, and he knew it was a feint and went for it anyway, because his arm was slower than his knowledge, and her blade came round under his and turned his wrist, and his blunt steel went out of his hand and rang on the flags, and hers came to rest against the side of his throat, lightly, cold, and stayed there.

The yard was silent.

It had been won cleanly, on skill, by the better fighter, in front of her own knights, and there was not a man or a woman on that wall who could have said otherwise.

She took the blade away.

She looked at him for a long moment, breathing a little harder than she had been at the start and no harder than that, and then she looked down at the flags.

At the damp round the butt's foot. At the loose flag. At the half-inch fall toward the drain that she had never once felt in her life.

She had been moved twice on her own ground, where she had learned to fight, and she could not say how. He watched her not be able to say it. She had felt the medium go over her on the quay, the afternoon before, and she had put her hand on her sword for it. She was not a fool. She would suspect it now. But she had felt nothing on the yard today, because there had been nothing to feel, and she knew the difference between suspecting a thing and knowing it better than anybody alive.

She did not ask. He watched her decide not to ask, and put it wherever she put things she meant to come back to, and turn toward him again with her face closed over it.

"Who taught you to fight on ground?" she said.

"You did," said Emeric. "The first half."

She looked at him. Then, for the first time since the quay, she very nearly smiled.

Osric was waiting at the edge of the flags when he came off them, with a wet cloth for his face and an expression Emeric did not trust.

"There was a book on it," Osric said. "On the steps. Before you came in."

"On me?"

"On how long." Osric handed him the cloth. "The knight at the top had you going down inside a count of twenty. I had a Silver on you lasting to a hundred." He looked back at the wall, where the talk had started again, louder now, and a good deal of money was changing hands in a way that made the knight at the top of the steps look unhappy. "The book's moved," Osric said. "Considerably."

Ward, beside him, did not say anything at all. He took the blunt sword out of Emeric's hand and looked at it, and then at the flags, and then at Rowena going back across the yard to her knights, and he put the blade back in the rack himself, and that was all.
