Chapter 264 - First Glyph

Ward put him on the frost at twenty to six, by the well, with the ash blade across the backs of his knees; and held out his hand and pulled him up, and said, "Again," and put him there a second time by a different road, which he had not troubled to do since the autumn.

Emeric lay on his back on the white cobbles and looked at the sky going from black to slate over the keep's roofs, and breathed.

"You were watching the stone," said Ward.

"So were you."

"I was watching you watch it." Ward held out his hand again. "Up."

It was the twenty-eighth of February, a Monday, and the stone was a block of grey grit the size of a small cart, squared on four faces, that had lain against the north wall since the armoury's new bay went up in the autumn, because it had come out of the quarry a hand too long for the footing it was cut for and nobody wanted to move it twice. Ward had walked past it every morning since. On Saturday night he had stood in front of it in the dark with his hands behind his back for the better part of an hour; Emeric knew, because his sense had been open on the yard from his window and Ward's note had not moved.

On Sunday, at the long table, between the soup and the bread, Ward had said he would try it in the morning. Then he had eaten his bread.



The class came out at six.

They came across the yard to their lines in their shirts, with their coats left on the pegs inside the armoury door, and stood on the frost without being told, and steamed. Fifty-nine of them, and off every one a faint grey breath of it rising from the shoulders and from the forearms where the sleeves were pushed back, like horses in a cold field at dawn. A body that had been made all winter to spend what it took in, on nights in the river and days at the stones, had learned to heat itself from inside the way a knight's did; and theirs did it now as they breathed, without asking them. In November half of them could hold it the length of a drill. This morning nobody in the yard so much as looked at it.

That was what he noticed, from the well, with the frost going wet through the back of his shirt. Nobody looked.

Their arms were clean. Mira Holt's, folded across her chest at the head of the second line. Bren Tallis's beside her; he had hit the water at the Mere too hard in the autumn and been grounded four days for it, and when he was cold he still held his left side a little stiffly, as if it might have something to say. Noll Finch's, at the bar's end of the third line, thicker at the wrist than they had been in July. Clean to the elbow and past it, every one: no rings at the wrist, no silver, no welt. A farm boy's skin, a clerk's, a carter's daughter's, with a burn here from the forge and a scar there from the Isle's rocks, and nothing else written on it by anybody.

Arthur was in the back line.

He had stood there since the first week of January, at its end by the rail, where nobody stood who could stand anywhere else, and nobody had asked him why. Beside him, in a shirt that still hung off his shoulders, with his sleeves down and his hands tucked under his arms against the cold, stood Edric. He could not steam. Nobody had asked him to. He coughed into his collar now and then, low and short, the way Imani had taught him and not the way that bent him over; and he came at six because Arthur came at six, and on the first morning Ward had looked at him for the length of a breath and said, "Back line," and that had been the whole of their conversation since.

At the gate there were eleven of the freed men.

There usually were, now. A hundred and sixteen of the two hundred and thirty-nine had come up the sea lane over the winter on Rowena's word that there were rooms in Kaldo for any man who wanted to mend somewhere warmer than a barracks; rooms, and nothing else promised. Nobody had gone to them with a paper or a pen. They had walked the town. They had stood at the north well while the water ran, and under the school's window while Sela's children said their tables, and in front of the pay sheet on the works gate, reading the rates with their lips moving; and most mornings since the snow went a few of them stood at the yard gate in Kaldo coats that did not fit them yet and watched young men and women drill in their shirts on frost to a plan pinned on the board by the armoury door, where anybody could read it.

Armand was under the awning by the armoury in his chair, in two rugs and a fur, because Imani had said he could come down if he came in the fur. She stood behind the chair with her bag at her feet and a list in her hand: the garrison men who had said they would lie in the tank that week. The atlas, Ottoline called it on her cards, which Imani pretended not to like; a scan of every volunteer who would lie still in the warm water, front and side, filed by age and build in Ottoline's drawers and indexed on her cards, so that when a man came into the ward with a shadow on his lungs there would be a winter's worth of lungs on paper to say what a lung looked like with nothing wrong in it. It had been her idea, in December. She had not asked anybody whether she might.

"Holt," said Ward. "The count."

Mira took the class without a word, and Ward walked across the yard to the north wall, and the other three went with him.



He stopped two paces from the stone. He unbuttoned his cuffs and turned them back, twice each, to the elbow, which he did not do in company; and there were the lines, ring over ring of old raised silver from the wrist up, pale in the grey light. The same as Rylan's and Osric's and Declan's. The same, he had said at a fire in that yard years ago, when Emeric asked to see them and he had not moved his sleeves. The class saw them. Emeric heard them see, a small sound along the lines, under Mira's count. Then they were looking at the stone, because Ward was.

Emeric went and stood at Ward's left shoulder, half a pace back, where Ward had stood behind him since he was a boy.

Neither of them said anything about that.

"Arms first," said Emeric.

It was the only thing he had to give him, and it had taken him the winter to find the words for it. Armand had given Ward the earth core in the room, at night, in the slow time: the ring, the character, the strokes, drawn and drawn again on the room's long table until Ward could have drawn it asleep, and had, Armand said, one night, and woken with his hand still moving. But Armand had never had to take his mana out of his arms to cast, because it had never been in them. Ward had carried his in his arms since the day he got up off the floor. A knight's whole craft was keeping it there. You could not push a thing out through a hand that the arm behind it was holding on to.

Ward let his arms go.

Emeric felt it before he saw anything: Ward's note, the steadiest thing he knew in a living man, loosening along the forearms, like a hand opening slowly on a rope it had held all night. Then he saw it. The thin grey steam that had come off Ward's wrists since six stopped, all at once, and the light lay on bare cold skin.

The glyph came up before Ward's right palm, a hand's breadth off it, a ring of dull amber light no bigger than a plate.

It closed, and flashed white, and went out; and a puff of warmth came off it across the front rank like the breath of an opened oven, and the stone did not move.

Somebody's boot scraped on the cobbles and stopped.

Ward looked at his hand.

"Mirrored," said Armand, from the awning, in his lecture voice. "Every apprentice does it once. Most take a week to manage it. You've done it on your first try." He settled into the fur. "Again."

Ward did not look round. He let his arms go a second time, slower, and Emeric felt the rope open again; and the ring came up amber, and this time it shook.

It shook as it formed. The ring drew itself, and drew itself again a hair to one side, and back, the way a pen line goes in a cart on a rutted lane, and the core character inside it swam. It went on shaking for the half of a breath that every hybrid had between the arm and the hand, and that Emeric had not had since the winter of the sixth year, when he had found he could take it in the slow time instead. Half a breath. He had forgotten how long it was. He watched it go by, and did not help.

The ring closed.

He felt it close; and in the same instant, before the stone had done anything at all, before there was anything in the yard for anybody to see, he felt Imani's note under the awning jump, sharp and small, as a sleeper's does at a door slammed in the next street. His eyes went to her. Her shoulders had come up, and her hand had gone to the back of Armand's chair.

Then the stone rose.

It came up off the cobbles by a hand's width, the whole of it at once, level, with a dry whisper of grit leaving grit; and hung in the grey light with a hand's width of nothing under it, while the frost slid off its top edge in a thin glittering sheet; and dropped. The thud came up through the soles of Emeric's boots and into his knees.

Nobody laughed.

He had thought somebody would: Kit Bastow, who laughed at most things, or Osric, who laughed at all of them. Fifty-nine people and three knights and eleven men at the gate stood on the frost and looked at a stone that had moved a hand's width and gone back where it was, and the yard was so quiet he could hear the river, and down in the town under the South Gate the first tram's bell. They were being careful with him. Every one of them.

When he looked at the awning again, Imani was watching the stone with the rest of them, her face doing nothing at all, and her hand was back on her list.

Ward looked at the stone for some while.

"Hm," he said.

He put the warmth back into his arms, and the steam came off his wrists again, and he stepped back and looked at Declan.



Declan had gained more than any man in the four baronies on Armand's sheets, and had sat at the long table two summers ago looking at his own forearms as if they belonged to somebody else. He went to the trough by the well. It had a skin of ice on it, and he broke it with the side of his fist, mildly, and pushed the pieces to the far end, and stood over the black water with his right sleeve already up, because his right arm had never had anything on it to hide.

His glyph came up blue and shook for the same half breath and closed cleaner than Ward's; and a rope of water as thick as his wrist stood up out of the trough to the height of his chest and stayed there, turning slowly on itself, with the grey light going through it.

Declan looked at it the way he looked at a level.

"It wants to go back," he said, to nobody, and let it, and it went down into the trough so neatly that the floating ice barely rocked.

Rylan's would not shut.

He stood in the middle of the yard with his arms bare and his face as calm as the price of fish, and let his arms go; and the ring came up pale, nearly white, and the core came up in it, and the ring ran round toward its own tail and did not reach it. It stopped a finger short, like a horseshoe. The core sat in the open ring with nothing to hold it, and the mana bled out through the gap as a flash and a hard little gust that took the frost off the cobbles round his boots in a circle and lifted the hair on the front rank's foreheads.

"One," said Rylan.

He did it again. A finger short. The gust.

"Two."

He did it five times, counting each aloud in exactly the voice he used for a boy's figure at the rail, and on the fifth the gust took the corner of Imani's list and she put her hand flat on it without looking down. Then Ward said his name, once, and Rylan put the warmth back into his arms and folded them.

"Tomorrow, then," he said, as if it were a delivery that had not come.

Osric had been talking since Ward unbuttoned his cuffs. He had told the front rank that he had done this a hundred times in his head and his head was very good at it, and that if he set the armoury on fire they were to remember the duke had been standing right there. He let his arms go, still talking. The ring came up red, shook, closed; and a flame stood up off the ends of his fingers the size of a candle's, yellow and very pleased with itself, and leaned over in what was left of Rylan's gust, and ran up the inside of his wrist into his turned-back cuff.

He put it out against his thigh. It took three slaps.

"Well," said Osric, and held up the cuff, which was black and smoking along an inch of its edge. "It works."

Nobody laughed.

Osric looked round at the class, at the eleven men at the gate, at Ward, with the face of a man who has told his best story at a full table and been passed the salt.

"Again," said Ward. "All four. Till the bell."



Emeric went back to the awning.

From there he could see the whole of it: the four of them along the north wall with their cuffs turned back and the silver on their forearms catching the light whenever an arm came up; Ward and the stone, Declan and the trough, Rylan counting, Osric holding his scorched cuff well away from his fingers. And behind them, in their lines, the class at its own drill under Mira's count, steaming, with nothing on their arms at all.

"Holt," said Armand, without looking round. "I read her on Thursday. Do you know where she reads?"

"Tell me."

"If the rite had made her what she is, she'd have it to the elbow on both arms. Tallis the same. Your Arthur would have it to the shoulder and over." He moved his hand on the fur. "I've read thirty of them this winter, at my bench, with a cup of tea at my elbow. Not one reads below where the rite puts a man who walks out of that room. And not one has a mark on him he didn't have at Commencement."

He was quiet, watching Rylan's ring fail for the seventh time.

"Forty-one in my season," he said. "Six got up."

Imani put her hand on his shoulder, on the fur, and left it there, and said nothing. Emeric looked at her over the old man's head. She looked back at him, perfectly steadily, the way she looked at anything; and then down at her list.

In the back line Edric had stopped coughing. He was watching Ward's stone, which had just come up again by a hand's width and gone down again, and he said something to Arthur without turning his head.

"No," said Arthur, as low. "Not before."



Ward washed at the basin in his own room after the bell, with his cuffs still turned back, and did not turn them down when Emeric came in.

It was the room it had been the night of the sheaf: bare stone, the table, the bench, the rack of blades, and the shelves, fuller. Emeric did not look at the shelf at the end. He knew what was on it, tied with tape, and that the next would go on the second, because Ward had told him once, and Ward did not change a thing he had told you.

"Write it to him," said Emeric.

Ward dried his hands. He did it properly, each finger, the way he did everything. He did not ask what.

"I've been deciding," he said.

"Since Armand read his sheet."

"Since before." Ward hung the towel on its peg and squared it. "Since I got up off that floor."

Emeric waited.

"Every month since the road south," said Ward. "Your health. The wells. The baronies. The book. I've written him the war, and the pox, as small as they'd go and true." He looked at the shelf, and away. "This won't go small. There's no size of it that goes small. It's the Church's floor, and the Church's word said over every man who died on it. I write it, and he reads it, and then he has it. He sits across his table from the cardinals every day with it and he can't do a thing about it. Not with Kesrath on the coast. Not with the Church in his council." He was quiet. "I've been deciding whether that's a kindness."

"It isn't yours to decide," said Emeric. "I'm deciding it. If it's wrong, it's mine."

Ward looked at him.

"You'd take that off me."

"I'm telling you to write it. That's the whole of the difference." He had thought on the way down the stair that he would have to argue, and found he did not want to argue anything. "Don't make it smaller."

Something went across Ward's face and was put away again.

"He'll ask how I know," said Ward.

"Armand's figures. Five years of them, the still crew and the stock handlers and the ship's crew against their matched men, every number in Ottoline's box. Armand's hand on thirty arms this winter, and what it found. Four knights who cast this morning with no new line on them." He stopped. "And my name under yours, if you want it there."

"He'll believe me without it."

"He will."

"He'll believe it more with it." Ward said it slowly, the way a man reads back a figure he has been given. "From his son. From the old man, who ran that room a season and says so. From me." He did not say the rest, and did not need to: from the knight who had stood behind Amara in a passage with his sword out, and stood behind her son now.

"Why now?" said Ward.

Emeric looked at the shelf after all.

"Because it's finished," he said. "There's nothing left to prove it with that we haven't got. And because this spring there'll be a boy at the head of some captain's list who's proud of it for a week." He looked back at Ward. "Let what happened to you, Rylan, Declan and Osric never happen again. That's why. Not to anybody. He's the only man in the kingdom who can see to that one day, and he can't see to it if he doesn't know."

Ward stood by the basin with his cuffs turned back and the old silver on his forearms in the window's light, and said nothing for long enough that Emeric heard the tram's bell again, far off.

"It goes on the second," said Ward.

At the door Emeric turned round.

"The stone came up."

"A hand." Ward was turning down his cuffs at last. "Paskin's girl lifts a cart's worth before her breakfast."

"Paskin's girl cried, her first year. She told me so."

"I'll not cry," said Ward, and buttoned the second cuff, and that was the end of it.



The pair had gone over the winter to the far end of the wire shed in the old powerhouse yard, behind the dynamo house, moved by Bram from the long shop, in a bay of its own with a brick floor and a rope round it, and at nine that night it was Wes's.

Emeric had been in Bram's shop since supper over the crank axle for the Gold Rose, which the cellar's masters said was bent true to a hair and Bram did not yet believe; and on his way out across the works yard he went in at the wire shed's door instead of the gate, past Ward's man on his stool, because the lamp at the far end was lit and he knew whose it was.

It knocked. That was the first thing anybody noticed about the pair, and the last thing Bram had been able to stop noticing: a slow heavy knock inside the iron, steady as a mill wheel, where the rollers under the two weights went over the swells of the waved ring and one weight fell as the other rose. The box itself told you nothing. It stood on its brick plinth, riveted iron the height of a man's chest and lined with firebrick, with the shaft coming up out of its top through a packed collar and the belt off the shaft running down the bay to the wire bench; and over the collar the governor's two brass balls spun out on their arms, which was the only part of it a man could watch. In the side nearest the lamp was the lock: two small iron doors one behind the other with a window of thick glass between them, a brass key rod, and on the wall beside it a felt-lined box of vials, each the length of a finger and faintly blue. Low on the far side, away from everybody, one panel of the box's skin was soft lead sheet on copper rivets, and under it was a pit a yard deep, full of sand raked smooth, with its own rope round it.

Wes was on his stool by the lock with a slate on his knee.

He stood up when he saw who it was. Emeric told him to sit, and he sat, and a little later stood up again without noticing.

"Tobe's on the Hod," said Wes, before anybody asked. "All week, if it holds clear. Master Ned's book."

He said it carefully, as if the book were his. The Hod had flown again since the new year on her new bottom, and for every night run since, the book showed four aboard under Ned's line at its head; and two of the four had always come out of Arthur and Mira and Bren, turn and turn about, which Ned had not written anywhere and kept to anyway. This week it was Ned and Tobe, and Arthur and Mira. So the lock was Wes's from dusk to six, and the day man's after.

"I count her," said Wes, and turned the slate round. Strokes in fives, a column to the quarter hour. "Knocks to the minute. She does eleven when she's happy. Tobe says when the knock goes long and the balls come in, she's hungry, and you give her one." He looked at the vials. "Like the deck going."

"Like the deck," said Emeric.

"When the deck goes, you open one. If that's not enough, the next." Wes nodded at the lock, as if introducing it. "Same thing. Only she doesn't fall."

Emeric stood and listened to her knock.

There was something to say to that, and it was not long, and he did not say it. It was Tobe's lock and Tobe's lesson, and Wes held the slate the way a man holds a thing he has been trusted with, and the hands on it were perfectly still, which they had not been in the hayfield in July. Saturday, he thought; when Tobe was down. He would say it to Tobe, and Tobe would say it to Wes in his own words, which would carry further than a duke's.

"She sounds well," he said.

"Good as gold," said Wes, and made a stroke.



Imani was at her kitchen table at ten with the atlas's cards in four piles and a pot of tea gone cold. She looked up when he came in at the back door without knocking, and looked at his hair.

"Sit," she said.

"I came to..."

"You came because the front of your head's a disgrace and Ferra's too kind to tell you. Sit."

He sat on the low stool by the range where she put people whose heads she wanted, and she brought her chair up behind him, and the little jar of wax and oil off the shelf over the sink, and began at his left temple without any more discussion.

She did it the way she did a dressing, quickly and very exactly: the new growth at the root of each loc wetted under her thumb and twisted down tight against the scalp, and pinned with one of her own hairpins to hold while she did the next. Her fingers were cool. He shut his eyes. The range ticked. A cart went by in the lane, and then nothing, and then her breathing behind him, slow, and the small dry sound of the wax in her palms.

"Ward lifted a stone," he said.

"I saw."

"You jumped."

Her fingers did not stop. "Did I?"

"Before it moved."

She twisted the next one down a good deal tighter than it needed, and he made a sound, and she said, "Hold still," and put a pin in it.

She did not say anything else about the yard. She did eleven more across the front, and talked while she did them, about Armand's fur, and a garrison man who had fallen asleep in the tank on Friday and had to be woken with a spoon on the glass, and a card Ottoline had filed under the wrong build and found herself, and sworn about, in a whisper; and he sat on her stool with his eyes shut and her hands in his hair and let her not say it. He put it where he put the things he did not understand yet. It was a crowded place.

When she had done she laid both hands flat on his head, as she laid them on a chest to feel it rise, and left them there.

"Go to bed," she said. "You've Armand at nine in the long shed, and you'll sleep through him if I let you stay."

"I could stay."

"You could." She took the pins out one at a time and dropped them in the jar. "Go to bed."

He went up through the town by the north well. At the keep the stair was dark and the passage was dark, and there was a line of lamplight under Ward's door, and behind the door Ward's note, bent over a table, very still. He had felt that note bent over that table at the end of a great many months. He had never felt it so still, or for so long. He went past the door quietly, and up.



Bryda Renner's letter came up on Tuesday's morning train, and Ferra read it at breakfast in her room with her tea going cold, twice, as she read everything.

"Grayrock wants the Long Table," she said.

"Which one?" said Imani, from the window seat, with her bread.

"The custom. Not the furniture." Ferra turned the sheet. "Three of her colliers came down on the coal train in December to see their cousins, and sat at a table in the dock quarter on the twenty-fifth, the length of the lane, with the whole of the lane, and went home and told the rows. She says nobody in the four baronies ever sat down to eat in the street with a whole street before, and her rows want to know why Kaldo's allowed and they aren't." She put the letter down. "She'd like to know what it costs. In writing."

"Tell her it costs a table," said Imani.

"And a lane full of people who'll carry one out of doors in December." Ferra wrote in the margin of her book. "That isn't a thing I can send by post."

"Send her Maren," said Emeric.

They both looked at him.

"For a week. With a ladle."

"If Maren goes to Grayrock with a ladle," said Ferra, "Grayrock will keep her, and then I'll have to go to war with Bryda, and I've no column for it." She went back to the letter. "I'll send her the lane's list from the dock quarter. Who carried what. That's the custom. The food's the easy part."

Osric's sleeve had reached the counting room by eight on Monday, and the works by nine, and by noon Tess, who had not been there, had been telling it better than he did; she had him setting fire to his own beard. Imani had heard it at the ward from a stretcher bearer, and from the bearer's account Osric had very nearly burned the keep down. Ferra had written in her book only that Osric had cast fire, and the date.

"He's to come to the ward about that wrist," said Imani. "Tell him I'll not chase him."

"You'll chase him," said Ferra.

"To the door," said Imani. "Not past it."



The long shed at nine was cold enough that Orrin threw fire in his gloves, and swore about it.

Armand had come in his chair and his fur, and had himself set down by the stove at the shed's middle with a rug over his knees, and had the canvas screen put up a pace to Emeric's left: a sheet of old sail stretched on a frame, soaked in the trough until it dripped. Orrin stood at the far end by the doors.

"Ward drew his yesterday the way a man reads a word in a glass," said Armand. "Hold up your right hand."

Emeric held it up.

"Turn it over. Palm out." He turned it. "What is it now?"

"My right hand."

"Facing the other way. Still your right. Now hold up your left beside it." He did. "Your left is your right in a glass. Lay one on the other however you like, and they won't sit. Every glyph has a hand like that. Ward drew his the other hand, and the ring closed on a shape that names nothing in this world, and the mana had nowhere to go but out, as a flash and a little heat. Every apprentice in the tower does it. Mine did it so often in their first winter that I had the long room's curtains made in wool, because linen caught." He coughed into the fur, and waited for the cough to finish. "That's the hand."

Emeric thought, without meaning to, of a bench on the other side of everything and two small brown bottles of the same oil, one of which smelled of spearmint and the other of caraway, because each was the other in a glass. Nobody in the shed could have been told.

"Now," said Armand. "Orrin throws you a fire. You read it as it closes: how much, which road, which way it turns. You cast its inverse. The same fire, the same amount, the same road, turning the other way. They meet, and there's nothing. That's a reversal." He held up his own right hand, slowly, an old man's hand with the knuckles gone large, and turned it palm out. "The inverse is this. Your right hand turned over. It's still a right hand." He put it down on the rug. "The mirror is your left. Under pressure the eye gives you the left. It always has. Every man who ever tried this has had his eye give him the left with a fire coming at him, and a mirror's no counter at all. His own goes off beside him like Ward's, and the fire comes on whole." He looked up. "That's why it's one High mage in five, on a good day. Telling the turn from the flip in half a breath is the whole of the skill. The rest is arithmetic."

"And the slow time?"

"The slow time is why you'll be the best at it in the kingdom," said Armand. "It isn't why you are now. Orrin."

The first one he had.

Orrin's glyph came up red at the far end, small and quick, a ring and a core and two strokes for a bolt the size of a fist, and Emeric read it as it closed: the turn of it, sunwise; the amount, little; the road, to the canvas. His own came up the same size turning the other way, and Orrin's bolt left its ring and met his a yard short of the frame.

There was nothing. No flash, no steam, no heat; a small warm draught on the side of his face, as a door opens and shuts in another room, and the canvas hanging wet and untouched in the cold.

"Huh," said Orrin, from the doors.

"That's one," said Armand. "Again."

The second he did not have. He saw the turn, and his eye gave him the left; and his glyph closed and flashed white a yard from his face and was gone, and Orrin's bolt went on through the place where it had been, whole, and struck the canvas with a slap and a hiss, and steam came up off the frame.

He had known what he had done before the steam cleared. Armand told him anyway.

"Left hand," said Armand. "Again."

The third was the same, and worse, because he had seen the second go and was watching for the flip, and watching for it, he gave it to himself. The flash went off beside his ear. The canvas hissed. Somewhere behind the stove one of Ward's men, sent to carry the chair, made a small sound and turned it into a cough.

"One in three," said Emeric.

"One in three is a dead man twice." Armand drew the rug up. "Ward did it with nothing coming at him. You did it with something coming. That's all the difference there is, and it's the whole of it. You didn't go into your room for either of the last two. You trusted your eye." He looked at the canvas, steaming. "Your eye's a liar. Everybody's is. Again."

They went on until eleven. He had four more clean out of nineteen, and his left ear rang, and Orrin's gloves were black at the fingertips, and the canvas had to be soaked twice. When the chair went out at the doors, Armand said over his shoulder, without turning his head, that he had done worse at thirty, and better at forty, and that he expected to live to see which Emeric would be.



Ward's letter went on the Wednesday, the second, as they all had, by Kassim's line. Ward did not mention it. At the dawn drill he put Emeric on the frost twice, and the second time held out his hand a breath later than usual, and Emeric took that for what it was.

Rylan's ring did not shut on the Wednesday either. He counted to eleven and said, "Tomorrow, then," in exactly the voice he had used on Monday. Declan had the water out of the trough and over the well's rim and back. Osric had a flame that stayed on his fingers and nowhere else, and a new cuff, and a bandage on his wrist that he showed to everybody.

After the drill Ward stood the class in a half ring by the armoury and took a stone out of his coat pocket.

It was a river stone, grey, the size of a hen's egg, with a band of red paint round its middle.

"A crown knight carries one of these," said Ward. "On the belt. Left side, forward, where his right hand can find it across his body without looking. This one's paint. His is a fire stone, carved by a mage to let go of all it holds at once a few counts after it's woken. Only a knight or a mage can wake one. He wakes it with his thumb." His thumb went across the red band. "Throws it on the count. Three counts later there's a fire the size of that well, for the length of a breath. One stone, one burst. They cost what a horse costs, and he'll not throw it at one of you. He'll throw it at four."

Emeric had seen them. He had not thought of them in twenty years and saw them now: the palace guard in the long passage on the family stair, with a small leather cup on the belt, left side, forward, buttoned over. At nine he had thought it was where they kept their money.

"How do you know it's woke?" said Bren.

"It goes red under his thumb. You'll see the glow through his fingers if you're looking at his hand, and you should be looking at his hand." Ward went along the half ring with his eyes. "Three counts. Some are carved for four. Never trust one for four. If it lands at your feet and you can reach it in one, you throw it back. If you can't, you get clear. There's nothing else. Nobody stands there and looks at it."

He threw the painted stone to Mira's feet, with no warning, and said, "One."

She had it off the cobbles and back past his ear before he said two, and it went into the wall and fell in the slush. He threw the next at Noll, who was too far from it and knew it, and went sideways and flat behind the well's step on "two" and lay there; and Ward said, "Yes," which in the yard was a medal. He threw the third at Bren, who caught it on the bounce and threw it back so hard and so straight at Ward's chest that Ward had to catch it, and did, one-handed, and looked at it.

"Back at him," said Bren, red to the ears. "You said."

"I did," said Ward, and threw it at Arthur.



The boy came to the gate at the end of it, while the class was at its bread.

Emeric knew his note before he knew why: thin, a commoner's, steadier than it had been on a dispensary stool in May. A carter's shoulders, a cap held in his left hand. The right sleeve of his coat was pulled down over the right hand, and the hand was shaking inside it; Emeric could see the cuff tremble from the well. He had come in past the freed men at the gate, who made room for him without knowing why, and stood just inside it, and waited for Ward the way a man waits to be sent away.

Ward went to him. Emeric did not.

He could not hear them and did not try. The boy talked, not long, mostly to the cobbles. Once he pushed back the right sleeve and held up the arm, and there were the lines Emeric had last seen under Ivo's lamps: three rings at the wrist that had started and not closed, and above them the dark mark, faded now to the colour of an old bruise. He put the sleeve back down. Ward looked at the arm, and at the boy's face, and then past him at the armoury door, where the yard broom stood against the wall.

Ward said something short.

The boy went and got the broom. He took it in his left hand, and then, when he had looked at it, in both, with the right one shaking on the handle; and he began at the gate end, where the class's boots had trodden the frost to grey slush, and swept.

Rylan watched him from the well's step with his bread on his knee. After a while he put the bread down and went across the yard and took the broom out of the boy's hands, and turned it round, and gave it back to him the other way, with the right hand on top where it only had to rest and the left low down to do the pushing.

"Like that," said Rylan. "Let the good one work."

He went back to his bread. The boy swept, faster.

Ward came back across the yard past Emeric and said, without stopping, "He's on the count in July. I'll tell Ottoline." And then, a pace further on, without turning round: "He asked for work. I'd not have given him a place he asked for."

One of the freed men at the gate had been trying to catch Ward's eye all morning, and caught it now. He was a broad man with grey in his beard and the Isle's grey still worked into the creases of his knuckles, and he took his cap off to ask, which Ward did not like, and asked anyway. He had a wife and three children in a village two days out of Wayford, who had thought him dead eighteen years and been told otherwise in December, by a letter somebody else had written for him. Could a man with a family somewhere else bring them? Here?

"Ask the Chancellor," said Ward. "The counting room, at the keep. Tell her I sent you."

"Will she..."

"She'll ask you more questions than I would. Answer them." Ward looked at the cap in the man's hands. "And put that on. It's cold."

The man put it on, and went, and Ward watched him up the lane toward the keep.

"That's nine," he said, to nobody, "since the first."



He had gone in on the Monday, and on the Tuesday he had looked in from the door and seen Wes's back at the lock and the slate on his knee, and gone home. On the Wednesday he had been in the cellar until two and had not come down to the works at all.

On the Thursday night he came out of Bram's shop at a quarter past nine with Bram's opinion of the crank axle still in his ears, and crossed the works yard toward the gate, and went in at the wire shed's door instead.

"Fourth night," said Ward's man on the stool, who had been told nothing about anything and could count.

The bulbs were on down the length of the shed on their wire, yellow and steady. At the near end the wire bench was running: a man at the drum in a leather apron, the copper rod going into the die's mouth thick and dull and coming out the other side thin and bright and winding itself onto the drum with a small singing whine. At the far end the pair knocked. Eleven to the minute; he did not need the slate to tell him. The governor's balls spun out level over the collar.

Wes was on his stool.

"Charged her at dusk," he said, before he was asked. "On Tobe's count. She took it sweet." He held up the slate: eleven, eleven, eleven, eleven, down three columns.

"Tobe's down Saturday?"

"If the weather breaks. It's not going to break." Wes looked pleased about it, and then looked as if he thought he should not be. "Master Ned says another clear night and they'll have the racks full to Sunday."

The man at the drum swore.

It was not loud. It was the oath of a man who has felt a thing go wrong under his hand, and Emeric's head came round to it before it was finished. At the die the copper had stopped going in. A seam left along the rod from its casting, a thin fin of copper where the mould's two halves had met, had come up against the die's mouth and caught there; and the rod was not being drawn any more but held, and the drum went on pulling against it, and the whole bench took the strain. The belt groaned on its pulley. Down the bay the shaft slowed.

The knock went long.

He heard it go: one knock a hair late, and the next later and heavier, a slow labouring beat like a man climbing a stair with a sack on his back. Over the collar the governor's balls dropped in on their arms, and both stones inside the iron went heavier to push the shaft round against the bench, as Bram had told them to, and the knock went longer still.

Wes stood up.

"She's hungry," he said.

He had a vial out of the felt box and into the lock's outer door before Emeric had taken a step, and the outer door shut on it; through the thick glass the vial lay in its cradle between the two doors with its blue light on the glass.

"Wes," said Emeric. "You charged her at dusk."

"Knock's long. The balls are in." Wes had the key rod. He was not hurrying. That was what Emeric would remember afterward, longer than the rest: that he did it exactly as he had been taught, steady, with his hands perfectly still, the way he had counted the bellows in the dark over Lisle's wood. "If one's not enough, the next."

"It's the bench..."

Wes turned the key. The inner door swung, and the rod went in through it, and the neck of the vial broke inside the iron with a small clean sound, like a twig.

Emeric felt it go in.

He had never been able to feel much through the box's iron, any more than Armand could. It came to him dulled, as a fire comes through a wall: a warmth with no shape. But he felt that. The blue went up off the broken vial into a box already full, and the fullness inside the iron rose, and went on rising, and pressed outward on the iron from within, so that what had been a warmth to his sense became a pressure, like the pressure in the ears on the stair up into the Band, and climbed.

The knock came back hard. Then it doubled. Then the knocks ran together.

"Down," said Emeric.

He had Wes by the collar before the word was out. The stool went over. He took the two of them round the end of the plinth and down on the bricks behind it, on the side away from the pit, and shouted down the bay; and the man at the drum was already flat under his bench with his arms over his head, because he had worked metal twenty years and knew the sound of a thing about to let go.

Inside the box one of the stones drank.

He felt that too, through iron and brick and his own chest pressed on the floor: one note in the box going up past bright, past the brightest thing he had ever felt a stone hold, drinking the Band out of the air round it through its open neck faster than it could spend, because the bench held the shaft and there was nowhere for any of it to go; brighter, and brighter, like a lamp wick turned up and up until it is all flame and no lamp.

It let go.

The panel went out of the box's far side into the pit with a crack like a door kicked in, and the sand went up off the pit in a sheet and came down rattling on the roof of the bay. A gust of heat came round the end of the plinth over the two of them, hot as an opened kiln, and in it the Band. He knew it on his tongue before he knew it any other way: cold and metal and thin, the taste of four miles up, of the Hod's house at the top of a run, here on a brick floor at the foot of the town with frost on the roof. The stone lamp on its hook by the lock flared white, blinding, so that every shadow in the bay jumped at once. The bulbs down the length of the shed burned on, yellow and steady on their wire, and took no notice.

Then nothing.

The knock had stopped. The shaft had stopped. The belt hung slack off its pulley, and the drum at the near end turned once more under its own weight and was still. In the pit the sand ticked as it settled, and smoked, and the lead panel lay face down in it with its rivets sheared off clean and glowing at the heads.

Wes was on his side on the bricks with his right arm out in front of him and the hand still curled as it had been round the key.

"Wes."

"I counted her," said Wes. His voice was quite steady. His hand was not. It had begun to shake on the bricks all by itself, as his hands had shaken against the grating in July, and he looked at it as if it belonged to somebody else. "She went long. I counted."

"You did. Are you hurt? Anywhere?"

Wes thought about it, honestly, the way he did everything. "No."

The man under the bench was not hurt either. He came out with sand down his collar and in his eyebrows, and stood up, and looked at the rod still stuck in the die's mouth with its fin of seam folded back against the steel like a lip, and said, to the rod, what he thought of it.

Emeric stood up and put his sense into the box through the hole where the panel had been, from a habit older than anything in Kaldo, and found nothing there.

He did not mean quiet. Inside the iron the box had gone out of the world, as Lisle's wood had gone out of it in July, so that his sense went in at the hole and stopped, the way a foot stops on a stair that is not there. The other stone was in there in its weight somewhere, and the firebrick and the ring and the rollers. He could not feel any of it. A dead hole the shape of a box.



Bram came at a run with his apron on and a lamp in his hand that he did not need, and Ward's man behind him with his sword half out, and stopped at the rope.

He looked at the box. He looked at the panel lying in the sand. Then at the lock, and the felt box with two vials gone from its row, and the slate on the bricks where the stool had gone over; and down the bay at the bench, and the rod in the die.

"It did what it was for," said Bram, of the panel.

Then, of the rest, slowly: "He fed it for the bench."

"He fed it for a ship," said Emeric.

Bram looked at Wes, who was sitting on the bricks now with his back against the plinth and his hands under his arms; and at Emeric, over the lamp.

"And who was to tell him different?"

"Me."

Bram did not say anything to that. He put the lamp down on the plinth and went to get a plate and a drill for the hole, because nobody was going to leave a hole in that box overnight with a dead place in it, whatever it cost.

Emeric sat down on the bricks beside Wes.

"Tobe said..." said Wes.

"I know what Tobe said." He looked at the box. "A ship spends what you give her, on the air, every breath, all the way up and all the way down. When her deck goes she's hungry. Every time. A box spends only what the bench takes off it. It can't be hungry unless it's empty, and hers was full; you filled it at dusk. She went long because the bench caught. The balls came in because the bench caught." He stopped. "A box isn't a ship."

Wes looked at the lock for a long time.

"Nobody said," he said. It was not an excuse; he said it the way he would have read a figure off a gauge.

"No. I thought it on Monday, standing just there, listening to you tell me. I thought it in August when Tobe first took you on. I never said it, and I never wrote it down where you'd find it." He looked at Wes. "That's mine. It isn't yours."

Wes did not answer. He took his right hand out from under his arm and looked at it shaking, and put it back.

"I'll go back to the rope walk," he said. "Garrick'll have me."

"I'd rather you didn't." Emeric leaned his head back against the plinth. The brick was warm; the whole box was giving off its heat now, slowly, like a stove at night. "You're the only man in Kaldo who's heard her go long for a seam and watched what came after. I'd like that man at the lock. When Imani says."

Imani said, when she came, that it would be a week. She came at ten with her bag, because Ward's man at the door had sent the night watch running to the ward without being told; and she knelt on the bricks and took Wes's wrist, and then his hand, and held the hand in both of hers until it had stopped trying to get away from her, and looked at his eyes in Bram's lamp.

"When did you sleep?" she said.

Wes told her. She looked at Emeric over his head.

"A week," she said. "Off. Home, and in bed by midnight, and you'll eat what I send round to your mother's and not what you'd rather." She stood up. "Your hands will stop. They stopped in July. They'll stop again, and faster, if you let them."

Ferra came at half past ten in her coat over her nightgown with her book under her arm and her hair in one long plait down her back, and stood at the rope, and read the bay as she read a page: the panel, the pit, the felt box, the bench, Bram on his knees at the hole with the drill, Wes on the bricks, Imani with her bag, Emeric.

"One stone," she said. "Two charges. A panel and its rivets. A night's wire." She opened the book. "Whose say?"

"Mine," said Emeric.

She wrote it under his name, and then she looked at Wes on the bricks with his hands under his arms, and her pen stopped.



She found it at one in the morning, at her table in the counting room, with her coat still on and the pay book open under the lamp.

"He's off a week," she said. "On Imani's say."

"On full pay."

"On what?" Ferra looked up. "Under what? Show me the line." She turned the book round so he could see it. "There's a column for what a man's worked and a column for what he's paid for it, and nothing anywhere in this book for what a man gets when he's hurt at his work and can't. I looked. I looked in the works book and the yard book and the ships' books, because I thought I'd missed it. I've not missed it. There isn't one."

"He was paid in July."

"He was paid in July because Ned went on paying him out of the Hod's purse and told me to put it down to her, and I let it stand, because it was Ned." She laid her finger on the line. "That's a favour. A man shouldn't have to be liked to be paid."

He had no answer to that, so he did not try to make one.

"And this." She turned back a page. "Monday, dusk to six. Tuesday, dusk to six. Wednesday. Tonight, to half past nine. And Tuesday afternoon and Wednesday afternoon at the rope walk with Garrick, three hours each, out of Garrick's book; because he couldn't sleep, Garrick says, and asked for the work. Forty-five hours since Monday, and it's Thursday." She closed her hand on the pen. "At that rate he'd have done eighty by Sunday. Nobody asked him to. Nobody told him he couldn't. There's no line for that either."

"No."

"Every rule in this town lives in your head," said Ferra, "until somebody pays for it."

She said it without heat, as she would have read him a total, and went back to the book; and he sat on the end of the long bench by the stove where he had sat down, and looked at the side of her face in the lamp, and the one strand of copper that had come loose from the plait and lay along her cheek, and knew she was right, and that she had known it a good while before tonight and had waited for a night when he could hear it.

He got a board off the wall and a sheaf of the grey paper and her second-best pen, and put the board across his knees, and wrote at its head THE WORKS RULE, because a thing with a name got read.

Imani came in at two with her bag. She had put Wes to bed at his mother's and sat with him until his hands were still and he was asleep, and she stood in the door and looked at the two of them, Ferra at the table and Emeric on the bench with the board on his knees, and did not ask what they were doing. She came and read over his shoulder instead. Then she took her boots off and lay down along the bench with her head on his thigh, under the board, and her feet toward the stove, and said, "Read me the hard parts," and was asleep before he had found one.

So he wrote it with her head on his leg, and handed it to Ferra a sheet at a time.

Six days of eight hours: forty-eight, the base week, in every works the barony owned. One day in seven a man's own, and paid. Past forty-eight, every hour at time and a half.

"What do I call those?" said Ferra. "The hours past."

"Overtime."

She tried it under her breath, and wrote it at the head of a new column, and went on reading. "Time and a half of what?"

"Of his hour. A day's wage over eight."

"You've written over eight days." She held the sheet out to him with her thumb on the place. "As written, I'd pay a man a sixteenth of a day for every hour he stays, and he'd stay home."

He mended it. She took it back.

A man hurt at his work, to be paid his wage while he healed, on the ward's say and no one else's, out of the works' purse and not his master's goodwill. Nobody under fourteen in waged work at all. The mill had that one already, on a board by the frames; now it was everybody's.

"Barony works first," said Ferra. "Then the masters."

"Within a year."

"They'll scream."

"Let them scream in writing," said Emeric. "You'll want it for the column."

Ferra did not laugh. She looked at the sheet a moment longer than she needed to, which was how he knew.

He thought, while she read, of a country in his other life that had taken the better part of a century and a great many children dead in mills to get from a fourteen-hour day to this, and of the men who had stood up in its parliaments and said that a short day was ruin and a harvest did not read the law; and that some of them had been right about the harvest. He wrote a line for the harvest, and another for the ward's nights, because a ward did not stop at eight and nor did a fever. He did not say why he had thought of it. There was nobody awake in the room he would not have told, and he did not want to wake her.

At five the window began to go grey. He had five sheets, and Ferra had read every one twice, and had corrected two figures and one word, and had not said anything about the rest, which from Ferra was a signature.

She came round the table with the five sheets and sat down on the bench on his other side, at the end that was not Imani's, and read them through again from the top with her shoulder against his. Imani slept on. Her hand had found its way at some point in the night onto his chest, flat, over his heart, and had stayed there.

Ferra put her own hand down on top of it. Imani's fingers moved under hers, once, without waking, and settled.

The three of them sat like that while the window went from grey to white. Down in the town the first tram's bell went at the South Gate, and a cart came up the lane under the counting room window, and nobody moved.

"It's good," said Ferra at last, to the sheets. "Your hand. Then I'll copy it in mine for the gate." She did not take her hand away. "You should have written it in the autumn."

"Yes," he said.



Armand came down to the wire shed at ten on the Friday in his chair, carried by two of Ward's men, because he had asked to and Imani was asleep.

Bram had the box open by then. The plate he had bolted over the hole in the night was off again, the weight with the dead stone in it was out on the bricks on a sledge, and the stone was out of its cup: a grey crazed thing like a burned biscuit that crumbled at the edge when Bram touched it with a fingernail. He had seated a new one off the spare rack at eight, and Ferra had booked it at seven, before it was seated, from her own room, because she had known at half past ten which stone it would have to be and what it was worth.

At nine they had poured a vial through the lock into the shut box. One knock, and then nothing; the blue gone into the dead place inside the iron as water goes into dry sand.

Armand had himself wheeled up to the plinth, and laid his old hand flat on the iron, and kept it there a long while with his eyes shut.

"Nothing," he said. "As you'd expect." He opened his eyes. "A dead place heals from its edges. Lisle's wood healed from its edges in seventeen hours, because there was live air all round it to come in. This has no edges. It's iron all the way round, and a hole inside, and nothing comes in through iron that you don't put in through the door." He took his hand off the box. "It'll stay dead until you fill it from inside. With what you've got. And more than you'd like."

It took four more. The first two went the way the first had: a knock, and gone. The third gave three knocks before it went, and the fourth stayed, and the box began, slowly, to knock on its own again, heavy and uneven at first and then steadier, as a man's breath steadies after a fall.

Bram's dial was on the box's side by noon.

He had made it at his own bench between seven and nine, and had not asked anybody whether he might: a sliver of ordinary stone he had cut from the inside himself, at his own table in his own room, to push against a hairspring harder the thicker the Band round it, the wall sensors' trick turned inward; on a brass face the size of a saucer with a needle on it, set through the box's skin in a packed gland beside the lock where a man on the stool could not help but see it every time he looked up. Emeric called it a density gauge, the once, and Bram called it the dial, and the dial it stayed.

They watched the needle climb on the next three charges. Each moved it the same distance across the brass, two fingers' width, near enough, as if somebody had measured it. On the third the knock came down to eleven and stayed there, and the governor's balls stood out level, and the needle stopped, and Emeric scribed a line across the brass at its point with Bram's scriber and wrote FULL beside it, small.

Bram took the scriber off him and put a second line two fingers' width below the first.

"One charge under full," said Bram. "Pour at that, she comes up to full. Pour at that twice, and you'll be stood where you were last night." He gave the scriber back. "That's the mark."

The card went up over the lock that afternoon, in Bram's punch-cut letters on a plate of brass, because paper burned:

One to a mark. Not before the needle reaches it. Never two. Write it down.

Under it Ferra hung a new book on a chain, ruled in her own hand into four columns, and headed: Hour. Mark. Charge. Name.



Wes came in at dusk though Imani had said bed.

He stood at the rope with his hands in his coat pockets and said, before anybody could say anything to him, "I'm not working. I'm watching." His hands were still. Emeric looked at them, and Wes saw him look, and took them out of his pockets and held them out, flat, like a boy showing that he had washed.

"Imani'll have you," said Bram.

"Imani's on the ward till eight."

The needle stood at the mark at a little after seven; the box had been running the bench since four, with a new rod in the die and the seam filed off it, and a file on a nail by the drum now with a card over it of its own. Bram poured. He did it slowly, so that Wes could see every part of it: the needle first, and the card, and then the vial into the outer door, the door shut, the key, the small clean sound inside the iron; and the needle climbing its two fingers to FULL, and stopping there; and the knock steady at eleven.

Then he took the pencil off its string and held it out to Wes.

"Your hand," said Bram. "Mine's a punch."

Wes took the pencil. He stood at the book on its chain under the card, and read the card again first, all four lines, with his lips moving; and then wrote, in the careful square capitals the school had taught everybody in Kaldo who had been young enough to be taught:

Seven. On the mark. One. Bram.

He looked at it. Then he gave the pencil back, and Bram hung it on its string, and neither of them said anything.

Emeric watched from the door of the bay. He did not go in. After a while Wes picked his stool up off the bricks, where it had lain since the night before because nobody had thought to, and set it by the lock under the dial, and sat on it, not to work, he would have said, only to sit; and took his slate out of his coat, and found the chalk.

"Eleven," said Wes, to nobody, and made a stroke.
