Chapter 316 - The Claim

The gantry chain fouled at a quarter past seven in the morning, in the Forge's cellar, with a steel blank of very nearly a ton hung off it half over the build floor; and Bram, who had been running that chain since before the cellar had a floor, stood under it with his hands on his hips and looked up at it for some time without saying anything at all, which was how the cellar knew it was bad.

It was the K Amara's spare. The yard wanted a second spine casting in hand before the fleet went north, in case the first one did more than groan, and Bram would not have the Forge shape a thing that size from nothing when it could shape it from a blank of good steel poured down the hill in the works; so the blank had come down the cellar's ramp on rollers at six, and gone up on the gantry's chain at seven to be swung across onto the Forge's bed, and halfway across the chain had jumped a link on the wheel and jammed. Now the blank hung there, a long dull grey bar the size of a coffin and the weight of a cart full of stone, slowly turning on the fouled chain, a yard above the build floor and a yard short of the bed.

"It'll hold," said Bram. "The chain'll hold. It's the wheel. It's ridden up on the flange." He went to the wall for the long bar. "I'll back it off by hand. Take an hour."

The Forge's people stood back against the cellar's walls, the four of them that worked it, sworn: two of Bram's best and Ottoline's quiet nephew with the gauges and Tess, who was there for the run and sat on her high stool by the bench with her stick across her knees, watching the blank turn. Nobody went under it. Bram had taught them, as he had taught every apprentice for thirty years, that you did not go under a load, ever, for anything; that a load on a chain was a thing that wanted to be on the floor and was only waiting for you.

Bram set the long bar against the wheel's frame and leaned on it, and the chain groaned, and the blank swung, and nothing moved.

Emeric was at the bed with the shaping plans. He looked at the blank. He looked at Bram's back, bent to the bar, and at the four against the wall; and it was the Forge's cellar, sworn ground, under the castle, with no window in it and nobody in it who did not already know what he could do and had sworn on their own names never to say.

"Leave it," he said.

Bram straightened up. He looked at Emeric over the bar, and his face did not change, but his note did.

"It's near a ton," he said.

"I know."

"You said a ton. In the summer. You said a ton was the edge of it."

"I said a ton was a morning's work." Emeric put the plans down on the bed. "Two's the edge. Stand back from it."

Bram stood back from it.

Emeric did not go near the blank. He stood where he was, at the bed's end, and put his hand out toward it, open, palm up, as if he were asking somebody across a room to come to him; and took hold of it.

It was heavy. He had known it would be heavy; he had done half a ton in August on the island, with Tess timing him, and a little more than three quarters in September in the quarry at night with Ward holding a lantern and saying nothing. But he had not done this. He felt the weight of it come into his hand and then up his arm and into his shoulders and down his back, all of it at once, as if somebody had laid a cart's axle across his shoulders and stepped back; and under the weight he felt the steel's own mana, very little, dead matter's little, nothing to push back against him but its mass. That was something. A thing that was alive would have pushed back.

He lifted it.

The blank came up off the fouled chain a hand's breadth, so that the chain went slack under it and swung loose, clinking. It hung in the air a yard above the build floor with nothing holding it but him. The cellar was entirely silent. He brought it across, slowly, the yard and a half to the bed, the way a man walks with a full bucket in each hand, not thinking about the bucket, thinking about the floor; and lowered it, slowly, a finger's width at a time, onto the Forge's bed, and felt it touch, and let it go.

Then he sat down on the cellar step.

He had not decided to. His legs decided it, and he let them. He sat on the bottom step of the cellar stair with his hands open on his knees and his head down and breathed, and the breath went in and out of him like a man who has carried a sack of coal up four flights; and his arms were heavy and his shoulders were heavy and there was a fine sweat all down his back under his shirt, but there was nothing behind his eyes. No ache. No light. He had been waiting for it since August, the old ache, the one that came at the edge of a thing, and it did not come. It was only work. A morning's heavy work, done in a breath.

Bram came and stood over him. He did not say anything. He reached into the pocket of his apron, where he kept his bread for the morning because he would not stop to eat it, and broke the heel off the loaf, and put it in Emeric's open hand, and closed Emeric's fingers on it with his own big burnt ones, and went back to the bed.

Emeric ate the bread.

"A ton's a ton," said Bram, at the bed, to the blank, in the voice he had said it in to every apprentice who had ever tried to lift something on his floor that he should have used the chain for; and then, after a moment, not quite in the same voice: "Get the wheel off its flange, somebody. He's not doing that twice before dinner."



The melt was the same morning, down the hill, in the works' small furnace room; and it was Bram's, and Bram had been waiting for it since July.

Dust went into everything now. That was Tess's doing and the Dust House's and the island's mill: the spent stones that came back from the ships and the trams and the works dead and grey and good for nothing, milled down to a powder finer than flour, sorted by kind into Ferra's tins, and put into things. Into render, to keep a wall warm. Into the concrete for the winter's pours. Into Kaldo plate, layered, a face of one kind of dust and a core of another, for the knights' armour and the ships'. Each kind did a little of what its stone had done, so long as there was enough of it touching itself in the stuff to carry the instruction, and a keystone set in it to give the instruction; and the more you put in, Tess had found in the spring, the more it did, up to a third by bulk, past which it did no more.

Bram wanted it in steel.

He had wanted it in steel since the first warm wall. "If it goes in render it'll go in a melt," he had said, at the long table, in July. "It's a powder. Powders go in melts. That's what a melt's for." Nobody had argued; nobody had known enough to argue. So on the morning of the gantry he poured a crucible in the small furnace room and stood over it in his leather with his long rod, and when it was right he tipped into it a whole tin of Ferra's sorted fire dust, a pound and a half, and stirred.

The dust went in. It went in like flour into water, a grey cloud on the white-hot surface, and then it was under, and the melt swirled round the rod and took it. Bram stirred for a slow count of a hundred. Then he stopped, and stood looking at the surface, frowning.

Most of it had come back up.

It was on the top of the melt in a grey scum, with the dross, floating, the way any light thing floats on a heavy one: the dust was lighter than the steel, a good deal, and it had gone down when he stirred and come up again when he stopped. Bram skimmed it off with the dross, as he would have skimmed any scum, into the slag pot by the furnace's foot, and looked at the slag pot, and looked at the melt, and said nothing, and poured the bar.

Tess was there. She had come up from the cellar after the gantry, on her stick, and sat on a crate by the furnace room door with her bench book on her knees, because she wanted to see it fail, she said. She did not say it to Bram.

The bar came out of the mould at noon, cooled, and went under the steam hammer in the forge shop, and Bram worked it himself.

The first blow was a blow. The bar took it like any good steel, and squared a little, and rang. The second blow was a blow. On the third, the bar split.

It did not shatter. It opened, along its length, with a dull crack, as a log opens along its grain under a wedge; and lay on the anvil in two long halves, and in the opened faces of them, where the steel should have been one grey from edge to edge, there ran a line of darker grey, specks, a seam of them, as straight as if somebody had drawn it with a ruler. The dust that had stayed in. It had stayed in as specks, little separate grains of something that was not steel, and the steel had grown round them and not through them; and where there were enough of them in a line, the steel had a line in it that was not steel at all, and that was where it opened.

Bram looked at the two halves on the anvil.

"It's a crack," he said. "It's a crack I poured."

"It's an inclusion," said Tess, from the door. "That's what Emeric calls it. A thing in the steel that isn't the steel." She wrote in her book. "That's the first thing anybody's going to call this, Bram. Putting the stones in the metal. Magmetallurgy. I've written it at the top of the page."

Bram turned round with the hammer's tongs still in his hand.

"Magmetallurgy," he said. "Is that what we're calling ruining good steel?"

"It's what I'm calling it," said Tess. "You can call it what you like."

Bram looked at her, and at the two halves, and at the slag pot by the furnace's foot with a pound and a half of good fire dust in it gone to scum; and Emeric, who was in the forge shop's door behind Tess with his arms still heavy from the morning, saw him decide.

"It doesn't go in steel," said Bram. "Not like that. Not stirred. It floats, and what doesn't float cracks it." He put the tongs down. "I'll not try it again. That's a closed road."

"That's a closed road for stirring," said Tess.

"That's what I said."

"It isn't what you said." She shut her book. "Keep the slag."

Bram looked at the slag pot.

"Keep it," said Tess, getting up off the crate with her stick. "Don't throw it on the heap. I want to run it."

"It's slag."

"It's a pound and a half of fire dust in slag," said Tess. "I run everything. You know I run everything." And she went off along the furnace room on her stick, and Bram looked after her, and then at Emeric, and then, with great reluctance, he took the slag pot off its hook and put it on the shelf by the door, where the things went that Tess had said to keep.



That night, Ottoline had sent the cards up again.

They were on the end of the bed in one of her shallow trays, forty or so, laid in rows the way she laid everything, and every one of them had split. Some had gone along the fold at the corner where the horn had been bent round the paper. Some had cracked straight across the chip, so that the little stone sat in its bed with a hair-line either side of it like a crack in a pane. On top of the rows was a slip in her small hard capitals.

OCTOBER LAST. O. F.

"She's counting," said Ferra, from the pillows. She had her ledger up on her knees and a pencil in her teeth, and she took it out to say it. "That's a year on Thursday. I think she wanted you to know she'd noticed."

"She told me last October."

"She told you last October while you weren't listening. She told me that too." Ferra put the pencil back in her mouth and turned a page.

Imani did not look round. She was at her desk under the lamp with a stack of case notes and her shoes off, one foot hooked behind the chair leg, and she wrote three more words before she said, "Is that why you smell of the Motion Shop?"

He had not known that he did. He sat on the edge of the bed, which was a mistake after the morning; something in his back had an opinion about the cellar and the blank, and it gave it now. He took the card out of his coat and dropped it on Ferra's ledger.

It was the size of the old ones, a little stiffer, and it did not flex when she picked it up. Tess had laid the printed sheet between two films of her polyethylene and pressed it between two hot plates in the Motion Shop, and the film had taken the paper and the chip together and sealed them flat, so that the stone sat under a clear skin the way a pressed leaf sits under glass. Name. Number. Ward. Year. The Kaldo tree. And in the left-hand corner, the size of a thumbnail, a photograph.

Ferra looked at the photograph for some time.

"What's happened to your face," she said.

"Ivo happened to it."

"You look like you've been arrested."

"Everyone does." He took his boots off. "Everyone always did. There's no way to sit for one of those and not look like you've been arrested. It's a law."

Imani put her pen down then, and came across, and took the card out of Ferra's fingers, and looked at it under the lamp, close, the way she looked at a wound. The chip was warm in her hand only because Ferra's had been warm a moment before; it was cooling already, and it did not colour. She held it out to him without a word and he took it, and the little stone came up amber under the film.

"So it still knows you," she said. "Through that."

"Through that. The film's thin. Tess tried three."

"And the photograph's on Fennick's film." Ferra had her pencil out of her mouth again. "Ivo's using the same roll as the moving one?"

"Cut from the end. It's quick enough now that you hold still to the count of two in daylight instead of a hundred. He thinks he can do a street a morning with two boys to fetch people."

"A street a morning." He could see her doing it. "Do you know how many streets there are in Kaldo?"

"Ottoline does."

"Ottoline does," Ferra agreed, and wrote something. "And then the holdings."

"Not all at once."

"No," said Imani. "Not all at once." She had gone back to her chair, but she had turned it round, and she sat with her arms folded on its back and her chin on her arms, and she was not teasing anybody now. "Who first?"

He had meant to come to it slowly. He looked at the tray.

"The garrison," he said. "The fleet crews. The knights. Everybody who's going north."

Ferra did not say anything. Imani nodded once, as if he had told her a pulse she had expected.

"And a tag," Imani said. "Not just the card. A card goes in a coat, and a coat comes off. I want something on a cord round the neck. Metal."

"Two."

She looked at him.

"Two halves," he said. "On a cord. Stamped with the number, the blood, the name. If..." He stopped and started again, because there was no good way to say it and she would rather have the bad way quickly. "If somebody can't be brought home, one half stays with him and the other half comes back to you. So you know. So it's written down that you know."

Imani was quiet for a moment. He did not tell her what they had been called where he came from, dog tags, because it was an ugly name for a kind thing. Then she said, "Steel. Not brass. Brass goes green and you can't read it," and got up and wrote it on the top sheet of her case notes, in her capitals, and underlined it, and sat down again.

Ferra was looking at the card in his hand.

"All right," she said. "That's what it's for this winter. What's it for now? Tonight. A man in ward 4 who isn't going anywhere. What does it do for him that the horn one didn't?"

"Nothing the horn one didn't. It just doesn't crack."

"Then why are your shoulders doing that?"

He made them stop. "It gets him in the Assembly door. It puts him on the register, and the register's the only thing in the kingdom that says a commoner can't be carried off without a hearing."

"The law says that," said Ferra. "I wrote it. The card says who the law's talking about." She held out her hand, and he gave it back to her, and she turned it over to the plain back of it and then over again. "That's not nothing, at a gate in the dark, with Corbin's paper saying we're all forfeit. But it isn't why you've got that face. You've got the face you get before you say a thing you've been saving." She tapped the bottom edge of the card. "What are these?"

There was a row of small empty boxes along the foot of the face, eight of them, ruled in Ottoline's fine line.

"Licences."

"For what?"

"For whatever we'll want to know a man's allowed to do. The footplate. The trams. The powder book, the boiler ticket. Flying, when it's not only knights." He heard himself and went on anyway; there was nobody in this room he had to pretend to. "And motor carts, when there are motor carts. Where I come from you couldn't drive one without a card that said you could. Everybody had one. You'd show it to buy a drink, or get on a ship, or open an account at a bank."

"Everybody," said Imani.

"Most."

"An account," said Ferra, very differently.

He had known she would get there first. He let her.

"Tied to this." She did not need him to answer. She was looking at the chip under the film. "Not a signature. Not a seal somebody can cut a copy of in a back room in Ostrel. A note. Nobody can forge a note." She stopped, and frowned at nothing. "I had a clipped half in my hand last month, Emeric. A good one. I knew it before the scale did. The clerk had to weigh it to prove me right, and I couldn't tell him how. If the money's in the House's book against a note, nobody clips anything. There's nothing to clip."

"That's later."

"I know it's later." She was already writing; he could see the shape of a list going down the edge of the ledger. "Vantic," she said, without looking up.

"Vantic said no."

"Vantic said no to a war. She didn't say no to a bank. She'd have this out of your hand before you'd finished the sentence." Ferra wrote a word and boxed it. "Which is why she doesn't see it till after. Not till it's in the Charter who's allowed to read the House's book and who isn't."

"Who is allowed?" said Imani.

They both looked at her.

"I'm asking." She had not moved from the back of the chair. "On Earth. Everybody had a card. Who read them?"

He thought about telling her a short answer and decided she would know.

"In the end, everybody," he said. "Shops. Banks. The government. Machines, mostly. You couldn't buy bread without something writing down that you had."

"And people let that happen."

"It happened a bit at a time. Every bit of it made sense when it happened."

Imani looked at the card in Ferra's hand for a while.

"Then it's a leash," she said. "With a nice photograph on it."

"Not yet." Ferra said it before he could. "It can't tell anybody where you are. It only answers a hand. The records hall shows every man his own line, and nobody reads anybody else's without a magistrate." She paused. "That's now. That's what I can promise now."

"And when there's a machine that reads it?" said Imani. "Tess's gates. You told me they'd be doing sums by themselves one day."

"Then the rule has to be there before the machine is," he said. "That's all. That's the only answer I've got. Nothing stops a worse duke but what's written down before he gets here, and who's allowed to read it."

"That's not a very good answer."

"No."

"Then write it down tonight," said Imani.

"I'm writing it," said Ferra, who was.

Nobody said anything for a little while. Down in the yard somebody was walking a horse in from the gate, and the shoes rang twice on the cobbles under the arch and stopped.

"Why are you making them all come back in?" Imani said.

He had hoped she would not ask that one, and was glad she had.

"The chips," she went on. "The old ones. They already know the note. You could read it off the old chip and set it on the new one, couldn't you? In the delve. Nobody would need to come up the hall and sit behind a curtain again for the count of ten. Bram wouldn't have to be bored for a fortnight."

"I could."

"Have you?"

"Once. Mine." He turned his hand over on his knee. "It works. It took me an hour, and it answered me the same as the one I set by hand."

"Then why..."

"Because if I can make a card answer to somebody who never touched it," he said, "so can whoever learns how after me."

Imani looked at him for a long moment, and then nodded, slowly, the way she nodded when a symptom she disliked turned out to mean what she had thought.

Ferra had stopped writing. She drew a line under whatever it was, and then wrote a line under that, and read it out in her flat square voice, as if it were going in the Assembly's book, which he supposed it was.

"Nobody's note goes on a stone unless their own hand's on it."

"Yes."

"Good." She looked at the tray of split horn at the foot of the bed. "And them?"

"Who?"

"The ones who aren't on the register." She set the ledger down open on her knees. "Last October the inspector's clerk walked round this town on his own, the works and the printing office and the river inn, and nobody could have told you where he'd been. You could, because you felt him. Nobody else could. Saban says Corbin's been buying gate men in the capital, and there's no reason he's only buying them there. And we're about to empty this town of three thousand soldiers."

He reached into his coat again. He had one of those too.

It was nothing much. A slip of thin card, yellow, with a number at the top printed in red and a tear-line down the middle, and spaces ruled across it for a name, a home, a business, a house to sleep in, a date in and a date out. Under them, a line that said height, hair, marks, in the way a horse dealer would write up a horse.

"Ottoline drew it," he said. "Yellow this month. A different colour every month, so an old one shows across a room. The gate keeps the stub. The other half goes with the visitor. Seven days, and then you take it up the records hall and they give you another or they don't."

"No stone," said Imani.

"No stone."

"Why not?"

"Because somebody's got to set it, and there's nobody at the South Gate who can. And I'm not putting a stone at a public door that sets itself, with the Church's men walking past it every week." He gave the yellow card to Ferra. "So it's paper. It can be handed on. A good man who wants to get in will get in."

"Then what's the use of it?"

"It catches the careless," he said, "and it counts the rest."

Ferra had been reading the card. She looked up at that.

"Counting is most of catching," she said. "Saban will kiss you."

"Saban gets the gate book," said Imani, at once. "He doesn't get anything else."

"Anything else?"

Imani got up again, and took the yellow card from Ferra, and laid it on her desk under the lamp, and wrote on the back of it, quickly, in pencil, in her capitals, while they watched her.

WHERE HAVE YOU SLEPT THIS FORTNIGHT. SHOW YOUR ARM.

"The pox," she said, without turning round. "If they've come through a town with a fever in it, or there's no mark on the arm, they don't go to an inn. They come to my door first. That line's mine. It goes to the ward, and nobody in Saban's office reads it, ever. Put it in your book."

"It's in my book," said Ferra. Then, after a moment: "And mine goes on the back as well."

"What's yours?"

"If we're going to number them," Ferra said, "we owe them the same door." She looked at him over the ledger. "A man with a yellow card in date gets the hearing. Same as a man on the register. Nobody carries him off, nobody holds him, without an open court. Otherwise the card's not a card, it's a list of who to take."

He thought about the inspector's clerks, and about what Corbin's gate men would do with a list of strangers if they had one, and said, "The Assembly won't like it."

"The Assembly will argue about it for a month," said Ferra, "and then it will pass it, because somebody from ward 4 will stand up and say he'd want the same if he were in Ostrel. I'll write it up. You're not to bring it. They pass things faster when you're not in the room."

"Thanks."

"You're welcome." She picked up her pencil. "Now go away. I've got Bram's coal to do and it doesn't add up, and I'm not going to bed with it not adding up."

Imani had already turned her chair back round to the desk. She tucked her foot behind the leg again and picked up her pen and found her place on the page, and the yellow card sat by her elbow with her capitals on the back of it.

"Work first," she said, to the page.

He looked at the back of her head, and the lamp on her neck where her hair was tied up off it, and the chair.

Work first. She had said it to him a hundred times in two years, with her back to him at that desk, and he had said it to her a hundred times with his back to her at his; it was the rule, and it was a good rule, and they kept it about half the time. Tonight he had lifted a ton off a fouled chain at a quarter past seven and eaten Bram's bread on a step, and argued about cards and leashes and the Assembly, and his shoulders still had the morning in them; and she was six feet away with her foot hooked round the chair leg and her pen going.

He did not get up.

He put his hand out, from where he sat on the edge of the bed, toward the chair; and took hold of it, gently, the way he had taken hold of the blank, by the weight of it; and pulled.

The chair came. It slid back across the boards toward him with a long dry scrape, a foot, two feet, with its legs juddering on the board joints; and Imani did not come with it.

She had felt it start. She had her feet flat on the floor before the chair had moved a hand's breadth, both of them, planted, and her hand flat on the desk's edge; and the chair went out from under her, and she stood up off it as it went, laughing, and stayed exactly where she was, with her pen still in her other hand and the chair arriving at his knees empty.

"No," she said, without turning round. "Work first."

"You've done three pages."

"I've done two and a half." She wrote another word, standing, bent over the desk. "And you've just told me you can pull a cart's worth of steel across a cellar. So I planted my feet. I'm not stupid."

Ferra had put the ledger down.

He pulled Imani.

He did it gently. It was the gentlest pull he knew, the one he used to take a cup off a high shelf without getting up, the one that cost nothing; it should have brought her back across six feet of floor like a coat off a peg. He felt it take hold of her, and felt her brace against it, the way he had felt knights brace in the yard, her mana going down into her feet out of habit before she knew she was doing it; and he leaned on it a little more, the way he would have leaned on a door that stuck, and it did not give. She did not come. She stood at the desk with her feet planted and her pen in her hand, and laughed at him over her shoulder, and did not come; and he felt, through the pull, under the bracing, something that had not been there the last time he had pulled her across a room, which had been in the spring, in this room, as a joke, and she had come like a coat.

She was heavy.

Not heavy in the body. Heavy in the pull. Heavier in his hand than she had any right to be, a little, as if somebody had put a stone in her pocket; not a knight's weight, nothing like, nowhere near the way Rylan went heavy in his hand in the yard when he braced, but more than she should. More than a woman who was not a mage and not a knight and never had been should weigh, to a man who was pulling her by her own mana.

He stopped pulling.

"You're heavier," he said.

There was a short silence.

Behind him on the bed, Ferra made a sound. It was not a word. It went up at the end, and broke, and then she had the ledger over her face and was laughing into it, helplessly, with her whole body, so that the bed shook and the tray of split horn cards slid off the end of it onto the floor.

Imani turned round. She put the pen down on the desk, very carefully, and folded her arms.

"Say it again," she said.

"Not like that."

"Say it again. I want to hear exactly what you said."

"I said you're heavier. In the pull. Not in..." He could hear himself, and could not stop. "Not you. Your mana. When I pull you, it's... there's more there. It's denser. It's your mana that's heavier, not you."

"So I'm denser," said Imani.

"Yes."

"That's not better," said Ferra, from behind the ledger, and lost it again.

"It's not a bad thing," said Emeric. "It's a strange thing. It's..." He gave up. He looked at Imani standing with her arms folded and her eyes very bright and her mouth doing its best not to, and at Ferra with her face in the ledger and her shoulders going, and found that he was laughing too, at himself, at the whole of it, sitting on the edge of the bed with an empty chair against his knees. "Come here and I'll explain it."

"Explain it from there."

Ferra took the ledger off her face. Her eyes were wet. "Do me," she said.

"What?"

"Weigh me. The same way. Properly." She sat up against the pillows and held her arms out from her sides, palms up, like a woman asking to be measured for a coat. "If she's heavier I want to know if I am. I want a number. I want it in the books."

"It's not a number. It's a feel."

"Then make it a number," said Ferra. "You make everything a number. I've watched you do it for thirteen years."

But Imani had not moved, and she was not laughing any more. She was standing by the desk with her arms folded and her head a little on one side, looking at him the way she looked at a patient who had said a thing he did not know was important; and while he looked back at her she unfolded her arms, and let them fall, and let her feet go.

He felt her do it. He still had his sense on her, from the pull, and he felt the bracing go out of her all at once, the mana that had gone down into her feet come up again and loosen and spread out through her as it was meant to sit; and she was not planted any more. She was only standing there. Looking at him.

He pulled her again.

She came. Not easily; there was still that something in her, the heaviness, a little more than there ought to be, like a stone in a pocket. But she came across the six feet of floor toward him all in one smooth slide with her bare feet whispering on the boards and her eyes on his, without fighting it at all, and he put out his other arm and she landed in his lap, and he caught her.

She sat there. She did not get up. She put one hand flat on his chest, over the place she always put it, and looked at him from very close.

"It went easy," she said. "Just then. When I wanted to come."

"Yes."

"And before, when I didn't want to, I was heavy."

"Heavier than that. Twice, perhaps."

"But even when I wanted to, I was still heavier than I was. Than I used to be." She was not asking. She had already worked it out, the way she worked out a thing from a pulse before the patient had finished describing it. "Than a woman who's never been a knight or a mage ought to be. Wanting doesn't change that part. Wanting only takes off the bracing."

He looked at her.

"Yes," he said.

"Then something's happened to me," said Imani. "Not tonight. Over time." Her hand on his chest pressed, a little, the way it pressed a pulse. "I want a number for it. In the morning. A real one. Off the scanner, not off your hand."

"In the morning."

"First thing. Before the ward." She looked past him at the bed. "And hers."

"I heard," said Ferra. She had stopped laughing. She was sitting very still against the pillows with the ledger shut on her knees, looking at the two of them; and Emeric could not read her face at all, which did not happen often. Then she reached over to the lamp on the bed's far side, the little ledger lamp she read by with its light stone in a glass bell, and put her fingers on the bell's brass collar to turn it down, as she had turned it down every night for years.

The lamp went out before she turned it.

It went out under her fingers, the stone in the bell going dark from the inside all at once like an eye closing, before her hand had moved the collar at all. Ferra did not notice. She took her hand back and lay down in the dark, and pulled the blanket up, and said into the pillow, "Bed. Both of you. She's got a number to get in the morning and you've got a war," in the voice of a woman who has done the same small thing with her hand every night for years and has no reason on earth to think about it.

Emeric sat on the edge of the bed in the dark with Imani in his lap, and looked at the dark lamp.

He had not felt Ferra cast. There was nothing to feel; she could not cast. She had touched a stone and wanted it out, and it had gone out, as it would have gone out under his hand or Armand's or any mage's who had more in him than the stone did; and she had not even known she wanted it. She had just reached.

Imani had seen it too. He felt her see it, against his chest, a small sharp stillness like a breath held; and then she let it go, and put her mouth against his ear.

"Not tonight," she said. "In the morning. Both of us. With numbers."

"In the morning."

"And now you're going to carry me to bed," said Imani, "properly, with your arms, like a person, because I'm not being pulled across any more rooms tonight by a man who's just found out I'm heavy." She put her arms round his neck. "Work first. Then this."

He carried her. She was not heavy at all in his arms; held, against him, willing, she was exactly what she weighed, which was not much. He put her down on the bed beside Ferra, who made room without opening her eyes, and lay down between them in the dark, and Imani turned over at once and put her back against him and took his arm and put it round her like a blanket, and Ferra's hand came across in the dark and found his other one and held it, loosely, already half asleep.

He lay and looked at the ceiling for a long time.



Imani got her number. It took her a week, in the end, and not a morning; but that was the next week, and before it was the Isle.

The K Endeavor came home from the October run two nights later, at four in the morning, a day early and with her side pods half full, and Cade came up the castle hill himself in the dark without waiting for daylight or for anybody to send for him, and banged on the study door with his fist until Emeric opened it.

He had not shaved since Kaldo. His eyes were red. He stood in the study door in his sea coat with the salt still white on its shoulders, and did not come in.

"They found the line," he said.

They were all in the study by five: the six, Armand, the king in his dressing gown in the window seat with a cup that Hale had brought up without being asked, and Cade in the middle of the room on a hard chair, because he would not sit in a soft one, with a mug of something in his fist that he did not drink. Teague was not there. Teague was asleep in the K Endeavor's hold, where he had gone down at Kaldo and not come up, because he had been in the water three hours; Imani had been down to look at him already, at half past four, and come back up and said only that he was cold, and fine, and sleeping, and that nobody was to wake him for anything short of fire.

Cade told it the way he flew: without anything extra in it.

They had come down on the cove at one, on the dark of the moon, as always. Teague had gone down the line and coupled the hose to the riser of the first bell, as always, and the oil had come up, and the pods had begun to fill. It had been a quiet night, flat calm, no wind; the best kind for the work and the worst kind for being heard, because on a flat calm night a sound carried across water for miles. They had their pipes out. Nobody spoke.

At twenty past one, the forward lookout had seen a light.

It came round the headland to the east, low on the water, at a walk: one lamp, high on a short mast, and then a second, lower, at the bow. A boat. A Kesrath patrol boat, out of the boats' landing under the tower, under oars, very slowly, with men at her bow leaning out over the water with lanterns on poles, looking down into it.

"Not looking for us," said Cade. "Looking down. At the water. At the bottom." He turned the mug in his hands. "I've been doing this run two years. I've never once seen a boat come round that headland at night. Not once. The fishermen won't come within a mile of those cliffs in the dark. The surf's bad and the bottom's worse." He looked at Emeric. "They were looking for whatever hit them in September."

Nobody said anything. Ferra had the stone book open on her knee and had not written in it.

"I took her up," said Cade. "I had to. She was hanging a few feet off the water with her belly open and a hose down. If they'd put a lamp up they'd have seen her, black or not. So I cut the hose at our end, and shut her, and took her up, quiet, stones only, to a thousand, and held her there in the dark over the cove. And I left Teague."

He said it flatly. He did not look at anybody.

"I left Teague on the bottom with the hose coupled to the riser and the cut end of it floating up to the surface on its float, a hundred yards of it, lying on the water in the dark right where they were rowing. And I couldn't tell him. And I couldn't go back for him." He set the mug down on the floor by his chair. "So I hung there and watched."

The patrol boat had found the hose. Of course it had; it was lying on the surface like a rope across a road. They had rowed straight onto it, and a man at the bow had put his lantern pole down and caught it, and shouted, and they had all come forward and hauled on it, and found it would not come; because at its other end, thirty feet down, it was coupled to the riser of a steel bell full of oil, ballasted to the bottom.

"And Teague cut it," said Cade.

Teague had been on the bottom in his helmet when the hose went slack. He had felt it go slack, and then go taut again, wrong, from above; and he had known. He had told Cade afterward, in the hold, with his teeth going, that he had known the second it went taut that it was not the ship. So he had done what Fennick and Ward had drilled him to do on the Mere all summer, for exactly this, and hated drilling: he had shut the helmet off.

The helmet breathed off a hose from the ship, and the hose had gone when Cade cut and climbed; it had been lying slack on the bottom beside him with the sea coming up it. So he shut the helmet's cock against it, on the bottom of the sea in the dark, thirty feet down, and went over to the bag on his back, as the drill said. Ottoline's rebreather. The thing that took a man's breath back into a bag and made it fit to breathe again and let nothing out. No bubbles. Nothing to rise to the surface and burst under a lantern. And he had felt along the hose to the riser in the black dark, with his hands, by feel, and cut it, with the knife on his belt, at the coupling; and the hose had gone up off the riser and away to the surface, free, into the hands of the men in the boat.

And then he had swum.

"West," said Cade. "Along the bottom. Under the cliff. A mile. In the dark, on the bag, with the lamp off. Following the cliff's foot with one hand. Because that was the drill, and the drill said if it goes wrong you swim west under the cliff for a mile and you come up and you wait." He picked the mug up again. "He came up at a quarter to three. We found him by his note. Not by anything I could see. I can't feel notes. Old Edrin, the old air mage, was aboard for the run; it was Edrin found him. A mile west, in the water, under the cliff, holding onto a rock with one hand and the bag in his teeth, not moving." He drank, at last. "We got a line down to him and he couldn't close his hand on it. My mate went down the line and got him."

The king had not moved in the window seat. Emeric saw that his hands had gone flat on his knees.

"And the boat?" said Ward.

"The boat hauled up the hose. All of it. And then they put a grapple down where the hose had been, on a line, and dragged." Cade looked at the floor. "It took them till dawn. We watched from a thousand feet, with the stones as low as I dared and Teague in the hold in every blanket aboard. They got the bell. The first one, the one the hose was on, the nearest. They got a grapple into its lifting eye and they put four boats on it and they hauled it up off the bottom with half a month's oil in it, and towed it in to their landing, and pulled it up the beach."

"What does it look like?" said Saban.

It was the first thing he had said. He had been sitting by the fire with his hat on his knee, and his face had been perfectly quiet the whole time; but he asked it in the voice Emeric had heard him use in the wine store when he priced a thing.

"A steel bell," said Cade. "Pitch inside, for the oil. Iron ballast round the skirt. A riser on the top with a valve." He met Saban's eye. "Nobody's mark on it. Not ours. Not anybody's. Bram saw to that. No yard's stamp, no maker's, no number. We said so when we sank them. We said if one ever came up, it would be nobody's."

"It's nobody's," said Bram, from the wall.

"It's nobody's," Saban agreed. "And it's sitting on a beach under Kesrath's tower full of oil that nobody in Kesrath knew was there, in a bell that nobody in Kesrath could make." He turned his hat a quarter. "So they know there's oil under the Claim. Light oil, good oil, the kind they've never had out of the pitch lake. And they know somebody's taking it, and that whoever's taking it can sink steel bells in thirty feet of water off a cliff in a surf that their own fishermen won't go near, and come and go without a boat that anybody's seen." He looked at Emeric. "There's one house in the kingdom that's ever built a thing like that bell. There's one house that's got ships that come and go without anybody seeing. They'll not need a mark."

"They'll send to the capital," said Ward.

"They'll send to the capital," said Saban. "Today, if they're quick. To the Lieutenant." He turned the hat again. "I'll know which road their man takes inside a week."



Emeric stood at the study window when Cade had gone down the hill to his bed, and looked out at the dark yard, and said it.

He had said it to himself on the night Linus first marked the Claim's end on the survey sheet and left the rest of it unwalked, and on the night the first well came in, and on every oil night since, privately, to keep it in front of him: that the oil under the Claim was the crown's, by the Claim's own charter, and that what Kaldo took out of it every month on the dark of the moon it took from the crown. It was not lawful. It would be lawful one day, if Emeric was ever the crown and the Claim was his; until then it was theft, and he had never once pretended otherwise.

He had never once said it aloud in a room with his father in it.

"It's yours," he said, to the window. "The oil. Under the Claim. It's crown land. It's been crown land since the charter. Kaldo's been taking it for two years, a load a month, and burning it in the ships, and that bell they hauled up this morning was full of your oil."

Nobody said anything.

He turned round. His father was still in the window seat in his dressing gown, with the cup in his hands; and he was looking at Emeric across the study with an expression that Emeric could not, for a moment, place at all, because he had never in his life seen it on that face. Then he could. It was the face Bram made when an apprentice brought him a casting that had come out better than it had any right to, and the apprentice was standing there waiting to be shouted at.

"How much?" said Harrold.

Ferra told him. To the barrel. She had it in the stone book, every load, every month, since the first.

The king listened to the figure, and was quiet a moment, and then he laughed.

It was the fourth time since the crossing. It was not a big laugh; it was the short dry sound from the bench in the wine store, but it went on longer, and he put the cup down on the sill to do it.

"I'd have given it you," he said.

"You couldn't have."

"No. I couldn't have." Harrold wiped one eye with the back of his hand. "The council would have had to vote on it. Grier would have voted against, because he'd have been paid to; and the Lieutenant would have asked what a duke in the south wanted with oil, and I'd have had to tell him, and he'd have told the close." He picked the cup up again. "So you took it. Without asking. And never once wrote it down anywhere a man could find it, except in that book." He nodded at Ferra's knee. "And sank your bells with nobody's mark on them, so that the day one came up, it would be nobody's. And today one did."

"Yes."

"That's what a crown's for, Emeric. Some of the time. Taking things nobody can be seen to have given it." He drank. "I did it myself, twice, that I'll admit to. You'll do it again, if you're ever where I sat." He looked at the cup. "Just don't ever pretend to yourself it's something else. You never have. I've noticed."

Then he looked up, and across the study at Ferra, and at Bram by the wall, and at Imani, and Emeric saw him decide something.

"Is this," said the king, "the thing about the K Discovery that I'm not told?"

The study went quiet in a different way.

He had asked in August, in his way, by the study grate, the day Saban read out the red star in the north gate's book: he had said that he would not ask until he was told he might; and Emeric had said he might, and the king had said that then he would ask later. He had not asked since. He had sat on benches and in window seats for two months and listened to every table and not asked once.

"No," said Emeric. "That's a different thing."

"Ah."

"Do you want it?"

Harrold looked at him over the cup for a long moment.

"I've had the oil," he said. "I may as well have the rest before breakfast."

So Emeric told him. Not all of it. The part that was the ship's: that the K Discovery carried, in her after hull, behind a gate that was kept shut, a can of fire; that she flew silent on her stones like every other ship Kaldo had, and that when the gate was opened and the can was lit she did not fly like any ship anybody had ever seen, but went, the way a rocket went off a stand on Lantern Night, with a sound like the end of the world and a light behind her like a star. That it was the fastest thing in the world, and the loudest, and could not be hidden for a breath once it was lit; and so it was never lit, by his order, until the war. Except once.

Harrold listened to all of it with his cup in his hands and his face perfectly still.

"Once," he said.

"In May. The twenty-seventh. Coming away from Grier's house, in the small hours, because something was coming up at us out of the tower's yard with a light on it and there was no other way out."

The king looked into his cup.

"The red star," he said slowly. "Saban's slip. Low in the south-east, going the wrong way, up the sky toward the coast. Too quick to be falling." He was quiet. "The gate wrote it in the margin. A falling star."

"That was us."

"I know that now." Harrold set the cup down on the sill again, carefully, as if it were full, though it was empty. "I was in the close that night. Two rooms, and a guard on the stair, and the window shuttered from the outside. I remember the bells for the hour. I remember I couldn't sleep." He did not look at anybody. "If they'd left me a window I'd have seen it go. A mile off, over the roofs, a red light going up the wrong way. And I'd have told myself it was a falling star. Because it would have been worse to hope."

Nobody said anything.

Then the king stood up, in his dressing gown, and picked up his cup, and looked into it, and found it empty, and looked at Hale's tray on the side table, and at the window, where the yard was beginning to go grey.

"I should like to see it lit," he said. "When it's lit. Not before. Write that down, Ferra, would you, in whatever book it goes in. That the king asked to be on board."

Ferra wrote it down.
