Chapter 107 - The Shed

The shed had been up since the week before Christmas, and in a month the town had stopped looking at it, which was what Emeric had wanted.

It stood on Kaldo's own bank of the river, outside the north wall and a few hundred yards above the east bridge, where in the summer there had been nothing but a slope of mud and willow scrub that the dock quarter's goats grazed when nobody was watching them. From the wall walk it looked like a barn. A long one, longer than the tithe barn and taller, with a roof of grey slate pulled off the tithe houses and walls of rough oak boarding from the mill's new saw, standing on a footing of the same houses' rubble set in lime from the soap kiln. The end facing the river was open from the ground to the roof beam, and the end facing the town was shut, with a door in it that a cart could go through and a smaller door in that, and a lock on the smaller door that Bram had made and only four people had keys to.

Inside it was colder than outside, because the sun never got in.

The slip ran down the length of the floor from the shut end to the open one and out under the river. A long even ramp of earth, dead straight, falling a little less than a hand in every two yards, so gently that a man walking down it did not feel that he was walking downhill until he turned round and looked up at the door. Down the middle of it ran two lines of oak baulks laid end to end on cross timbers, each baulk the thickness of a man's thigh, greased along their tops with tallow that had gone hard as candle wax in the cold. The ground-ways. Whatever was built on them would one day slide down them into the water.

Paskin had made the ramp in November, in nine days.

He had walked the slope for the whole of the first morning without doing anything at all, with his hands behind his back and his grey head down, stopping every few yards to stand with his boot pressed flat on the mud. Then he had gone back to the top and knelt and put both hands on the ground, and the willow scrub had started to lean. Not to move. To lean, all of it, very slowly, toward the river, as if the ground under it were tilting, and the mud between the roots had gone dark and shining as the water was pressed up out of it and ran away downhill in a hundred little threads. By the evening of the ninth day the slope was a ramp, and the ramp was hard. Emeric had driven an iron bar into it at the top with a sledge and got it in three fingers' depth, and in the ordinary mud beside it the same bar had gone in to the hilt.

"Packed," Paskin had said, wiping his hands. "A yard down. It'll take your weight, whatever your weight is. It won't take the river if you leave it bare at the bottom. Put stone at the toe or it'll be in the sea by Easter."

Then, because it was the rule in Kaldo now and Paskin had heard it the day he got off the flour cart, he had shown the levy how to do the last of it without him.

Declan had run the levels, as he had run the levels for the wells in the first spring, with a long straight board and a dish of water on it and a boy with a marked pole at the other end. Where the level said the ramp was high, a crew of six men from the levy took it down with rammers: a log as long as a man, shod with iron at the foot and with two handles bolted through it, that two men lifted and dropped and lifted and dropped all day long, and where the level said it was low they shovelled gravel from the river bar into it and rammed that. They had done the last forty feet of the slip that way, at the bottom, by the water, while Paskin stood at the top with his hands behind his back and said nothing at all. It had taken them three weeks. It was as hard as the rest.

Bram had come down to look at it when it was done, and walked the length of it, and stamped at the bottom where the toe went under the water, and come back up.

"Cement," he said.

"In the spring."

"I'd want the toe set in cement. Under the water. And a wall along the bank either side to keep the river off it." Bram looked at the ground-ways with the expression he kept for anything made of wood that he had not made himself. "That's gravel and rubble at the bottom. It'll do. It'll not do for ever."

"It doesn't have to do for ever. It has to do till the kiln's lit." Emeric looked down the slip at the river. "The ways are timber anyway. You want them timber. You grease them and the hull goes down them, and if one splits you take it out and put another in. You can't do that with a slip that's poured."

Bram grunted, which meant he agreed and was not going to say so, and wrote cement on the board at the top of the slip where he kept his list, under lime and over glass.



Fletcher came up the river on the nineteenth of January, in his own boat, with everything he owned in it.

It was not much. The boat was a broad heavy river punt with a stubby mast and a brown sail that he had been running Kaldo's copies down to the coast house in for a year, and in the bottom of it were three sea chests with rope handles, a rolled mattress, a cooking pot, a crosscut saw wrapped in sacking, and an adze with a handle so old it had gone black and shining from his hands. He brought the punt in under the open end of the shed on the last of the light, and stood up in it, and looked at the slip running up out of the water past him into the dark, and did not get out.

Emeric came down the slip to meet him with a lamp.

"You built it," Fletcher said.

"Before the winter was out. I said."

"You said." Fletcher looked up at the roof, at the slates, at the oak beams of the trusses, at the lamp hung from the tie beam halfway along. He looked at the ground-ways. He looked for a while at the toe of the slip under the water by his punt, where the river ran over the gravel. Then he got out of the punt, onto the ways, in his boots, and stood with his weight on them, and bent his knees a little, and straightened them. "Who made the ground?"

"A mage. An earth mage. One of Master Armand's."

Fletcher did not say anything to that, which was worse than if he had.

"He did the top two hundred feet," Emeric said. "The levy did the bottom forty with rammers and a level, and he stood and watched them and didn't touch it. You can't tell where his stops and theirs starts. I can't." He held the lamp out. "Find it."

Fletcher took the lamp. He walked up the slip slowly, on the ground between the ways, with the lamp held low, stopping every few yards and pressing with his heel. At the place where the rammed ground met the pressed ground he stopped, and stood, and pressed, and moved a foot up and pressed again. He moved a foot down. He knelt, which took him some time, and put his hand flat on the gravel.

"Here," he said.

"Close."

"How close?"

"About a yard."

Fletcher got up. He looked down the slip at the punt, and up it at the door, and Emeric saw him decide something and put it away without saying what.

"What happens to your ramp," he said, "when your mage goes home?"

"It stays where it is. And the levy knows how to make another."

Fletcher grunted. He gave Emeric the lamp back. Then he went down the slip to his punt and began to hand his chests up onto the ways one at a time, and Emeric put the lamp down and took them from him, and they did not say anything else until the punt was empty.

Abloh was at the yard gate when they came out with the last chest. He was standing in the dark beyond the lamp with his hands in his coat and his collar up, as if he had been walking past and stopped, which he had not, because the yard gate was nowhere near the soap works and on the way to nowhere. He had something under his arm wrapped in paper.

Fletcher stopped.

The two of them looked at each other across the gate. Emeric looked at the chest in his arms and became very interested in its handles.

"It's soap," Abloh said, at last. He held out the paper. "It's good."

Fletcher took it. He did not open it. He held it against his apron in one big hand and looked at Abloh for a while longer, and Emeric heard him let his breath out through his nose.

"I heard," Fletcher said. "That it was good."

"Well," said Abloh. "It is."

And he went away along the bank toward the east bridge, quickly, with his collar up, and did not look back, and Fletcher stood in the gate with the soap and watched him go until the dark had him.

"Twenty years," Fletcher said, to nobody. Then he picked up the last chest, which Emeric had put down, and carried it into the shed himself.



He showed him the drawings the next morning, at the trestle by the shut end of the shed, under the lamp, because the light from the open end did not reach that far.

"You said you'd tell me what for," Fletcher said, "when I was standing on the slip."

"You're standing on it."

"I'm standing on it," Fletcher agreed, and waited.

Emeric unrolled the first sheet and weighted it with the lamp and Bram's square.

It was the hull from above, and from the side, and in a row of sections across her from the bow to the stern, the way the book in his head had shown a ship's lines, a naval architecture text with tissue-paper overlays that he had borrowed from a library and never given back. A hundred feet long. Twenty-five across at her widest, which was well aft of the middle, because she was meant to go fast and the book said that was where a fast boat wanted her width. Shallow. Much shallower than any coaster. Her bottom ran flat for most of her length and then lifted at the bow in a long easy curve, and along each side of her below the waterline, drawn faintly in a second ink, were the foils: two broad flat blades set out from her hull at an angle, like a duck's wings held out from its body.

Fletcher looked at it a good while. He did not touch it.

"That's not a coaster," he said.

"No."

"That's not anything." He moved the lamp, so that the light fell on the sections. "There's nothing in her. Where's her hold?"

"She hasn't got much of one. She's not for carrying. She's for going."

"Going where?"

"Down the coast. Up the river. Wherever Kaldo needs a thing to be quickly." Emeric put his finger on the foils. "These lift her. When she's going fast enough, the water pushing on them pushes her up, and her hull comes out of the water, and then there's nothing dragging on her but those. She stands up on them and runs."

Fletcher looked at him the way he had looked at him across the half hull in the reeds a year ago, like a plank he had been offered.

"What makes her go fast enough?"

"Air stones. A fan at the stern on a frame, and stones to turn it."

"Stones," said Fletcher.

"Yes."

"Mage stones. Like your ramp."

"Like the lamps on the wall," Emeric said. "Which work."

Fletcher did not argue with that. He went back to the sections. He put his thumb on the line of her bottom and ran it along, and stopped, and ran it back.

"And these," he said.

There were three of them. They came down out of her hull, one under the bow and two under the quarters, thick and straight and ending each in a broad flat foot, and on the side view they were drawn folded up into her, lying in slots along her bottom, and then again in the second ink let down, so that she stood on them on the bottom of the sheet like a stool on three legs.

"Legs," said Emeric.

"I can see they're legs." Fletcher did not look up. "What are they for?"

"For beaching."

It was the answer he had decided on in the autumn, lying awake under his window, and he had said it to himself enough times since that it came out easily, and he heard it come out easily, and did not like it.

"She's flat-bottomed," he said, "and she's light for her length, and there aren't harbours on half the coast she's for. So she runs up onto a beach on a falling tide, and puts her legs down, and stands, level, while the tide goes out from under her. Then you can get at her bottom. Mend her. Load her off the sand. And when the tide comes back she floats off her legs and you fold them up."

Fletcher was quiet.

"On three," he said.

"Three doesn't rock. Four rocks on uneven ground, one of them's always off it. Three sits on whatever's under it." Emeric moved his hand. "A milking stool's got three."

"I know why a milking stool's got three." Fletcher straightened up. He looked down the shed, at the slip, at the grey light at the open end, at the river going by. "Forty years I've built hulls. I've never built a hull with legs on. Nor seen one. Nor heard of one."

"No."

"And her frames." He put his finger on the sections again, on the ribs. "These are heavy. These are heavier than a coaster's of twice her size. For a boat that's for going." He looked at Emeric. "Heavy frames, and no hold, and three legs, and stones to push her."

"Yes."

Fletcher looked at him for a while longer. Then he did a thing Emeric had not expected. He laughed. Not much. It came up out of the beard and stopped, as it had stopped in the reeds, but it got further this time.

"You're lying to me about something," he said. "I don't know what. I don't much care. You built me a shed." He rolled the sheet up, carefully, from the bottom, and gave it back. "Show me the next one."



The next one was the skin of her, and that was where Fletcher took the drawing away from him.

The book had said lamination. Thin planks, a finger thick, laid over the frames in layers, each layer running across the one below it, and glued, so that the whole skin became a single sheet of wood with its grain going every way at once, stiff as a board in every direction and lighter than any hull planked the ordinary way. Emeric had read it in the same library book, in a chapter about the fast boats of the war his grandfather had been too young for. He had drawn it the way the book had drawn it.

"Glue," said Fletcher.

"Hide glue. Or cheese glue. Curd, and lime, from the dairy and the kiln. It sets hard and it's,"

"It lets go," said Fletcher. "In salt water. In a year. I've seen it. Every shipwright on the coast has seen it, some fool glues a seam and it comes away in his hand the first summer. You'll have your skin in strips." He tapped the drawing. "You're near enough. You've got the idea. You've got it the wrong way round."

He took a stub of charcoal off the trestle and drew on the corner of the sheet, quickly, without asking. Two layers of plank, crossed, not at right angles, the way Emeric had drawn them, but each running slantwise across the hull, one from the keel up and forward and the other from the keel up and aft, so that they made a lattice of diamonds. And between them a line.

"Two skins," Fletcher said. "On the bias. One each way. You don't glue them. You rivet them, copper, through both, every hand's breadth, clenched over a rove on the inside. And between them you lay cloth. Calico, old sail, anything, soaked in pitch, so the two skins bed on it and the water's got nowhere to get in." He put the charcoal down. "My grandfather built the revenue cutters that way, for the old king. They were the fastest things on the coast and they never leaked a cup. Nobody does it now. It's dear, and it's slow, and there's nothing on the coast needs to be that fast any more."

Emeric looked at the diamonds.

It was better than his. He could see at once that it was better than his: no glue to fail, the skin stiff the same way, and if a plank split you could cut the rivets on that one plank and take it out. He had not known it existed. The library book had not known; it had been about another country's boats, built in another century, with glue that a chemist had made in a factory and that did not let go in salt water.

"Pitch," he said.

"Pitch. From the north, in barrels, at a price." Fletcher scowled at the drawing. "That's where she'll cost you. Not in the timber. In pitch."

"We've got pitch," said Emeric.

Fletcher looked at him.

"Not pine. Coal. There are six ovens across the river cooking Grayrock coal, and the stuff comes out of them black and runs into a tub at a barrel a week." He thought of Ferra standing over the tub with her hands in her sleeves. "Somebody told me last week that you'd want it."

"Show me," said Fletcher.

So they went across the bridge to the ovens, in the cold, and Haddon took a ladle of the tar out of the tub and let it run back into it in a long black thread, and Fletcher put his finger in it and rubbed it against his thumb and smelled it, and put it in his mouth, which made Haddon step back, and spat.

"Thinner than pine," he said. "It'll want boiling down. A third." He wiped his thumb on his apron. "It'll do."

Ferra, when she heard, moved the tar from its line in her book to a line under the yard, at nothing, and wrote beside it the price of northern pitch by the barrel, so that anybody who looked would see what Kaldo was not paying.



The keel went down on the twenty-fourth.

It was oak from the baron's woods, three trunks, each squared on the mill saw and then scarfed end to end, the long sloping joints cut by Fletcher himself with his black-handled adze over two days, and fitted, and bolted through with iron out of the tithe houses' grates. It went onto the blocks at the top of the slip on rollers, on a morning so cold that the men's breath hung in the shed in a fog, and Fletcher went along it on his knees with a straightedge and a candle, sighting, and had it moved a finger's width to port at the stern, and then a half finger back.

When it was where he wanted it, he took a coin out of his apron and laid it on the block under the heel of the keel, at the stern, where the sternpost would go.

It was copper. An old one, worn nearly smooth.

"For her," Fletcher said, when he saw Emeric looking. "Every hull. Under the heel, where the first timber sits. So she's paid for." He did not explain who she was paid to. "Builder puts it down. Not the owner."

"I'd have given you silver."

"It's not yours to give," Fletcher said. "It's mine." He stood up. "The owner puts his down when she's launched. On the deck. And he'd best put down silver, then, for what you're asking me to build."

The sternpost went up over the coin that afternoon, and the stem at the other end, and for the rest of January and into February the shed was full of the sound of Fletcher's adze and the levy men he had chosen out of the crew that rammed the slip, four of them, the ones who had asked him questions, and the smell of steamed oak from the long box by the open end where the frames were bent.

A hundred feet. The keel ran from one end of the shed nearly to the other, a long dark line on the blocks in the cold.

Emeric went down most evenings and stood at the top of the slip and looked along it. He did not tell anybody what he saw there, because what he saw was not there yet and would not be for years. Gravity stone sold in the capital at about two Platinum a pound, when it sold at all, which was seldom, to the crown and the Circle and nobody else. He had four of it in the whole barony, and they were the carriage's springs, and he had already learned on the hill what four of them could do and what they could not. To lift a hull of this size off the water he would need a great deal more than four. He had no idea where he would find it. He had drawn the frames heavy anyway, and the legs, and the slots for things that were not in the drawings at all, because the hull was going to take years and the stone was a problem for later, and a hull built light would never carry it, whenever it came.

He had told Fletcher she was for going. That was true. It was the truest thing he had said in the shed.



Vantic came to inspect it on the twenty-eighth, four days after the keel went down, under the covenant, because new works were her business and the shed was the largest new work in Kaldo, and she had let the shed stand a month before she came, which Emeric understood was a courtesy and a warning at once.

She came with her book and a lantern of her own, in her grey coat and grey gloves, and stood just inside the cart door with the lantern held up and looked at all of it before she said anything: the slip, the ways, the keel on its blocks with the first frames standing up off it at the stern like the bones of a fish, the steam box at the open end, Fletcher's men, Fletcher.

Fletcher looked back at her, and went on with his adze.

"The timber," Vantic said.

"The baron's woods. The saw's the mill's, under running work. The slates and the rubble are the tithe houses." Emeric did not need Ferra's book for it; Ferra had made him learn it on the walk down. "The iron in the keel bolts is the tithe houses' grates, at nothing. Copper for the rivets, when it comes to the skin, out of the fifth. Wages out of the fifth. The pitch is the coke ovens' tar, at nothing."

"At nothing," Vantic said. She wrote. "That word is doing a great deal of work in this barony."

"You taught it to us."

"I ruled on it." She did not look up. "I did not expect it to be taught." She closed the book on her finger. "What is it for?"

"A fast boat. For the coast run. The copies. Salt, and the spirit, and anything that has to be somewhere quickly."

She looked down the shed at the keel.

"May I see the drawings?"

He had known she would ask, and had brought them, and had thought about it the night before, lying awake: which sheets. He gave her the lines and the sections. He did not give her the sheet with the slots.

She laid them on the trestle by the shut end, where he had laid them for Fletcher, and held her lantern over them, and he held his beside it, because the shed was dark at that end and her lantern was small. She bent over the sheets. He stood beside her with the lamp and watched her read, and it was the way she read a column, all of it once and then the parts that mattered again, and the cold had brought colour up into her face along the cheekbones, and her breath went out white over the drawing and was gone.

She stopped on the legs.

"For beaching," Emeric said, before she could ask.

Vantic looked at the legs for some time.

"Of course," she said.

She took a short rule out of her coat, a brass one, folded, and opened it, and laid it on the side view, and measured the legs, and the feet of the legs, and then the frames in the sections, one after another, and wrote each figure in her book in a column. Then she turned back several pages in the book, to a page Emeric could not see, and ran her gloved finger down it, and stopped, and wrote one more figure at the foot of her column. She drew a line under it.

She tore the page out, neatly, along a fold, and held it out to him.

He took it. It was the column, in her small upright hand: the length of the legs, the area of their feet, the weight of the frames by the timber Fletcher had drawn them in. And at the foot, under her line, the weight of the frames of a coaster of the same length, from the House's own book of hulls it had lent against on the coast. Hers were nearly twice the coaster's. She had underlined the two figures, side by side, once each.

"The House lends against ships," Vantic said. "Quite a number of them. It keeps their particulars." She folded her rule. "A coaster carries her weight in her hold. This one carries it in her frames." She put the rule back in her coat. "That is all that page says. I thought you might like to have it, since you are building her."

He looked at the page and then at her.

She did not ask him. He watched her not ask him. It was not the same as it had been on the river bank in December, the long pause the length of a question; this was quicker, and lighter, almost amused, and she had already put it down before he had finished seeing her pick it up.

"The House has no view on legs," Vantic said. "The House has a view on whether a new work is paid for out of the fifth and booked in the right column, and this one is." She picked up her lantern. "It's a handsome thing. Or it will be."

"Fletcher thinks I'm lying to him."

"Fletcher," said Vantic, "is a shipwright." She looked down the shed again, at the keel and the bones of the stern standing up from it in the cold, and Fletcher's adze going, and the steam from the box. "I like a thing that is more than it says it is."

She said it to the hull. Then he saw her hear it, after it was out, and saw something go across her face very fast and be put away.

"On a balance sheet," she said. "It's the only kind of asset worth lending against."

And she went out through the small door in the cart door with her lantern, into the grey January noon, and he heard her boots go away along the bank toward the bridge, even and unhurried, and stood at the trestle with her page in his hand.

He folded it once and put it inside the notebook, at the back, where the Kaldo pages were.

At the far end of the shed Fletcher had stopped working. He was standing with the adze hanging from his hand, looking at the small door she had gone out by.

"Who's that?" he said.

"The House's factor. The lender."

Fletcher looked at the door a little longer.

"She measured the legs," he said.

"She did."

Fletcher grunted. Then he turned back to the frame he was dressing and went on, and after a while Emeric heard him say, to the oak, not loudly, that it was a fine thing when the moneylender knew more about a boat than the man paying for her, and that he would bear it in mind.
