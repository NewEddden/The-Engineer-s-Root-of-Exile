Chapter 107 - By Night

By the back end of the summer the river had gone down over the Grayrock fords again, the way Pask had said it did, and the boat went nowhere above the lake, and Ilsa's column at the pithead had begun to grow faster again, because the carts were all there was.

There were three miles of iron by then, and a few lengths over. Bram's six had laid it through the summer as the mill made it, a few lengths at a time, and the wagon teams ran on it every day from first light, carts coming down the road to the end of the iron and unloading into wagons, the wagons going down behind their horses to the yard in strings of four. People had stopped walking out to watch them. A horse walking on iron with four wagons behind it was a thing Kaldo had now, the way it had a bridge and a foundry.

Nobody had seen the engine outside the shed.

They took her out on the last night of the summer, with a half moon and no wind. Ward had men on the road from the dusk: two at the yard gate, two at the stake at the end of the iron, and two at the farm track that crossed the road and the line near the second mile, which was the first place Ward had marked in his book in the autumn as a place where the iron could be cut. They were to turn back anybody on the road, courteously, with a reason about a wagon off the rails, and the road was nearly always empty at night anyway.

Nearly always.

There was a spur of iron from the engine shed's doors to the line, laid in the spring, forty yards of it, which the gang had been told was for bringing wagons in to be mended, and it had been. The doors opened at full dark and eight men put bars under her back wheels and walked her out along the spur on the rails with a tarpaulin over her, and nobody on the island could have told what she was.

Rudd was already aboard. There was a plate at the back of her where the men stood, behind the boiler, with the fire door at their knees and the regulator and the gauge and the water cocks all within reach, and a rail round it at waist height. Tobe drove. Wick fired. Rudd stood by the cocks with his count and his sheets inside his shirt and the lead plug in the crown of the firebox under two feet of water, where he had watched it go in with his own eyes.

Ferra came down to the yard at the same time as Emeric, in a coat buttoned to the throat, with her book in a satchel.

He looked at her.

"I signed for her," Ferra said. "Every rivet and every length of iron she stands on. If she goes wrong it goes wrong in my book." She put her hand on the rail at the back of the plate. "I'll ride in what I sign for."

Rudd looked at her, and at Emeric, and made room.

Ward did not ride. Ward sat a horse at the side of the line with a shaded lantern and looked at the engine as though she were a man he had been told was friendly and did not believe it, and when Tobe opened the regulator and the first bark went up the chimney Ward's horse went sideways across the road and Ward sat it without appearing to notice.

They went up light. There was nothing behind her, and she went up the three miles at the pace of a man walking fast, barking steadily, with her fire lighting the smoke from underneath as it went up, and the rails sliding under her in the moonlight like two lines of water. Nobody on the plate said anything. Emeric stood with one hand on the rail and felt her through the soles of his boots, the push at every stroke, the small hard knock at every joint in the iron, the heat off the fire door on his shins. He had known what this would be. He had not known it would be this loud.

At the end of the iron, where the stake was, six wagons stood loaded with ore, the day's last carts' worth, roped and waiting. Bram was sitting on the first of them. He had walked up at dusk and nobody had asked him to.

They coupled her with chains, her back to the first wagon's front, and Bram climbed down and went and stood beside Ward's horse.

"Down," said Emeric.

Tobe opened the regulator, and her wheels spun.

They spun on the iron with a sound like a grindstone and a shower of sparks off the rail, and the bark went up the chimney in a rattle so fast it was nearly one noise, and she did not move. The wagons behind her did not move. Tobe shut off at once, the way Emeric had told him to on the second sheet, and the wheels ran down and stopped, and in the sudden quiet they could hear the river.

"Dew," said Wick.

The rails were wet. Nobody had thought about it. Three miles of iron lying in a field at night at the back end of the summer with the river beside it, and every inch of it had a skin of water on it as fine as breath, and the wheels had nothing to hold.

For a moment Emeric thought about the stones. Not the lifting ones. The blank he had told to be heavy on Armand's bench in the spring, and the anchors the tower cut to press at one weight and never any other. A stone like that seated on her frame would give her the grip of an engine twice her weight. It would also give the rails the load of one, because the iron did not care whether the weight on it was iron or stone, and the rails were rolled for four tons and not eight. And it would spend, the whole of every night she ran, a thing the Crown had sent eleven men to weigh.

He did not say any of it.

"Sand," he said.

Wick had it already. He had a bucket of it on the plate by the coal, sand off the foundry floor, the same fine grey sand that Bram's men had spread at midsummer for Ward to come down on. He climbed down with it and walked ahead of her on the line and threw it on the rails in front of her wheels, two handfuls on each, and climbed back.

Tobe opened the regulator a crack. Her wheels bit on the sand with a crunch, and turned, and held, and she leaned into the chains. The chains came tight one after another down the line of wagons, bang and bang and bang, six times, and the first wagon moved, and the last, and she was going.

She went down the three miles faster than she had gone up them. Tobe let her have it. The barks came quicker and quicker until they ran together, and the fire went white behind the door, and the needle on the gauge sat just under Rudd's mark and did not move from it, and the wind of her own going came across the plate and took the smoke off the top of the chimney and laid it down flat behind them over the wagons. Ferra had a quarter-hour glass out of her satchel. She had turned it at the stake as the chains came tight, and she stood with it in her hand and watched the sand, and said nothing, while Bram's mile posts came up out of the dark one after another, white, and went by.

Near the second mile post, where the farm track crossed, there was a cart on the road.

It was on the road beside the line, coming down, loaded, with one horse, and a lamp swinging on the tailboard, and it had not been turned back because Ward's two men at the track had been watching the line and not the road. The horse heard her before the driver did. It threw its head up and went sideways and then back, into the shafts, and reared, and the driver was off the seat and at its head with both hands on the bridle before the engine was abreast of it, and held it. He held it by main force with his shoulder in its neck, talking to it, and the horse stood trembling with its eyes rolled white while she went past him, three yards off, barking, with her fire door throwing light across the road.

Emeric saw his face in the firelight for a moment as they passed. It was Garrow.

He had heard it from Saban at midsummer and put it out of his mind. Garrow had said he would cart what the iron did not reach, and then he had gone on carting all of it, the whole road, down to the yard, beside the iron, as though the iron were not there. He took the old rate and the foundry took his ore. And he drove it at night now, Saban said, so that he would not have to watch the wagons go past him.

Ward had turned his horse. Emeric saw him go back down the road toward the cart in the moonlight, not hurrying. Then the track and the cart were behind them and there was only the line and the dark.

They came into the yard at a walk with the brakes on, the six wagons knocking together behind her as she slowed, and stopped where the wagons stopped every day, and Rudd said, "Off," and Tobe shut the regulator, and Wick opened the fire door and let the heat out, and the barking stopped, and it was very quiet.

Nobody got down for a moment.

"Three miles," said Ferra. She had her book open on the rail at the back of the plate, in the light from the fire door, and was writing. "Loaded, six wagons, the weight of six carts. Less than half a glass for the three miles." She wrote. "Six carts on the road is six horses and six men and the better part of an hour for the three miles, if nothing breaks. Three men. One engine." She looked at the fire. "And coal."

"And coal."

"Eleven shovels," said Wick. "I counted."

Ferra wrote that down too. Then she closed the book and stood holding it against her chest, looking back up the line into the dark where the three miles were.

"It's a road that has to be built," she said. "Every yard of it. Fifty-five tons to the mile." She said it quietly, not to anybody. "And it goes where it goes, and not anywhere else."

"Yes."

"Good," said Ferra. "I like a thing that goes where it goes."

She got down off the plate by herself, without a hand, the way she got onto boats, and her coat had a smear of soot across the shoulder from the rail, and he did not tell her about it.

Ward came in a quarter of an hour later and got down off his horse at the shed and stood by the engine, which was ticking as it cooled.

"He saw," Ward said.

"Of course he saw."

"He didn't say anything. I told him a wagon had come off the rails and we'd been getting it back on. He looked at me the way you'd look at a man who told you it was raining when you were standing in the sun." Ward looked up at the chimney, still smoking faintly. "He said his horse would be no good for a week."

"Pay him the week."

"I did. He took it." Ward paused. "And there were lights. Four farms along the line. Every one of them had a window lit by the time we came back down, and a man at a gate at two of them."

"The lower town thinks the foundry makes noises."

"The lower town lives next to the foundry," said Ward. "The farms don't."

They put her back into the shed along the spur with the bars, and pulled the tarpaulin over her, and shut the doors. Rudd drew her fire himself and would not let anybody help him, and checked the plug with the lamp through the fire door, and wrote the night on his sheet. Tobe and Wick went home. Bram had already gone, some time in the last half-hour, without saying anything to anybody, which Emeric had expected, and which he had come to understand was how Bram said that a thing had gone well.

Emeric stayed in the yard.

About an hour after they had come in, a cart came down the road into the yard at a horse's walking pace, loaded, with a lamp swinging on its tailboard, and drew up by the ore heap. Garrow got down. He unloaded it himself, a basket at a time, onto the heap, and the yard boy weighed it and wrote the weight on the tally and gave him his chit. Garrow put the chit inside his coat. He did not look toward the engine shed. He did not look at Emeric, standing by the shed door in the dark, though Emeric was nearly sure he knew he was there.

Then he turned his horse round, walking at its head, and went back up the road toward the hill, beside the iron, at the pace of a horse.
