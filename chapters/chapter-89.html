Chapter 89 - Declared Value

The answer came up the relay before breakfast, one line from the Kaldo end in Ferra's marks, and it said: Yes. Two years. Paid from our book, not hers. She answers to me.

He had sent the question the night before, after the crowd at the chimney had gone home and the stone was in the bag. He had not needed to send it. It was his post to give and his book to pay it from. He had sent it because the girl would be answering to Ferra, and a clerk who was put under somebody without that somebody being asked started her first day with a grievance she had not earned.

He read the line twice, and folded it, and went down to the tally house, where Bryda was already at the table with the day's marks and Ilsa was beside her reading them upside down.

Ilsa Renner was twenty-one. He had first spoken to her six years ago, when she had been fifteen and had asked him what would happen to her if her mother died, and he had spent one breath afterward wondering what sort of girl asked that and the rest of the day understanding exactly what sort. She had her mother's way of standing still. She had her father's height, which she did nothing to hide, and a habit of reading any sheet of paper within reach, whichever way up it was lying.

"I told your mother I'd have an answer before I left the hill," he said. "I'm leaving the hill this morning."

Bryda put her pen down.

He laid it out plainly, because it was a plain thing. Clerk of the ore contract, on Kaldo's books and not Grayrock's. Two years, and then renewed or not, as both sides chose. The Grayrock copy of the ledger in her hand: every cart that went down the road, every tally that came up from the galleries, every rate and every price, entered by her and signed by her and sent to the Chancellor with the ore. Twelve Silver Rand a month, paid from Kaldo, on the first of the month, whatever happened on the hill.

"And I answer to Ferra," Ilsa said.

"You answer to the Chancellor."

"Not to my mother."

"Not to your mother." He did not look at Bryda. "If your mother's figures and the tally house's figures don't agree, you write both down and send both. You don't choose. The Chancellor chooses."

Ilsa considered that. She did not look at her mother either. The two of them had a way of not looking at each other that he had seen once before, at a graveside, and that he now understood was how they agreed about things in public.

"Who can dismiss me?"

"The Chancellor. In writing, for a cause, with the cause written down. Not me."

"Not you."

"I'd have to ask her."

"And if you revoke my mother's charter?"

"Then the steward changes. The clerk doesn't. The post is Kaldo's, and you hold it."

There was a short silence. Bryda picked up her pen, and looked at it, and put it down again, which was the most he had ever seen her do with an object she was not using.

"It isn't much," Ilsa said. She did not say it as a complaint. She said it the way her mother would have said it about a rate, as a fact to be set against other facts.

"No. It's a place to stand." He had thought about the phrase for most of the night and still did not like it, and used it anyway because it was the true one. "Any man who comes up this river wanting the hill looks at you and sees Renner's daughter. That's all he sees now. In two years he'll see the woman who keeps Kaldo's book on the ore, and who knows every cart and every price and every man on the rate, and who can't be bought off the post by marrying her, because the post isn't hers to bring. It's Kaldo's. She only holds it."

Ilsa looked at him for a while. Then she reached across the table and turned the Grayrock ledger round to face her, and opened it at the day's page, and took the pen out of her mother's hand without asking, and wrote.

It was the morning's first three carts, off the tally house's marks, with the weights and the hour. She wrote them in a hand that was small and upright and very clear. At the foot she wrote her name, and under it, after a moment's thought, clerk.

Bryda looked at the page.

"Her hand's better than mine," she said, to nobody, and got up, and went to see about the boat.

The boat went down on the current in a little over half the time it had taken to come up, and nobody on it said very much. Bram slept most of the way, sitting upright against the coal bunker with his arms folded. Ward did not. At the first ford Pask took her across to the deep side under the alders, as he had coming up, and looked over the rail at the gravel showing pale through the water, and said that she would not come up here again this side of the autumn rains. Nobody answered him.

The leather bag stayed on Emeric's knee from the landing at Grayrock to the bank below the outside foundry, and he did not open it once. He thought about what was in it, and about the river going down behind it, and about the road beside the river, which did not go down with the season and did not get any shorter.

Armand was waiting at the Left Bridge with a single sheet. It was a list like the one he had written the day Emeric rode north for Wayford, the things he intended to do and not do in the barony's name, and it had a mark against every line and three lines added at the foot in the same crabbed hand. He held it out. Emeric took it this time, and read it, because the barony had been Armand's for the better part of two months and a man handing a thing back is owed the courtesy of having it looked at.

"You didn't need to write any of it down," he said.

"No," said Armand. "That is why I did."

He wrote the Crown return the next morning in the long room, with the shutters open and the whole table round him, and it took all day, and most of the day went on four lines.

The rest was easy. What had been found: a body of gravity stone, black and flecked, lying below and beyond the deepest gallery of the east working on the Grayrock hill, of an extent he could not measure and would not guess. How it had been found: by a survey made before the present holder's time, which had been discovered among the late baron's papers, and confirmed by a descent into the working after it had been cleared of dead air. A sample, enclosed and sealed: a chip the size of a thumbnail, off the piece in the bag, which he struck off himself with a cold chisel on the long-room hearth while the table watched and nobody said anything about how the chip, falling, took very slightly too long to reach the stone.

The four lines were the date and the share.

"When first known to the holder," Saban read, off the draft, and looked up. "Put the day you brought it up."

"No."

"It's when you knew. Before that you had a sheet of paper in a dead man's box. Anybody can have a sheet of paper."

"I had a survey with figures in the margins and assays against every sample," Emeric said. "I had it two springs ago, and I read every line of it the same night, and I knew what it said. I couldn't open the working because the covenant said I couldn't, and I couldn't prove the stone was there without opening the working. That's the reason. It's a good reason. It's also two years."

"Two years sitting on a deposit the Crown didn't know about," said Saban, "is the kind of thing the Crown notices."

"So is a return with a false date on it, when somebody else knows the true one."

Nobody asked who somebody else was. Ferra did not look up. Saban looked at him for a moment and then let it go, the way he let go a price he had decided was not worth arguing because the other man was going to pay it anyway.

Emeric wrote it. When first known to the holder: the spring of his ninth year at Kaldo, by the discovery of a survey among the late baron's papers. Not declared until now because the working could not lawfully be opened under an existing covenant, and the deposit could not be confirmed without opening it. Confirmed on the day given below, and declared within the month.

"How found," Saban said. "By a survey. Whose survey?"

"The return asks how it was found."

"And who paid for it."

"It doesn't ask that." Emeric did not look up from the sheet. "It asks what, where, when I knew, and what I propose. I'm answering every question it asks, and every answer is true."

Saban opened his mouth, and shut it, and after a moment nodded slowly, in the manner of a merchant who has just watched another merchant leave a clause out of a contract on purpose, and has worked out why, and approves.

Armand, at the end of the table, said nothing at all. Emeric had watched him read the letters two nights ago, all three bundles, by the lamp in the long room, and had watched him come to the last one and put it down very carefully, as if it might go off. The old man had spent a long life in the tower's service and had read a great many letters in trained hands. He had not said whether he knew this one, and Emeric had not asked him.

The share was harder, and it was Ferra who found it.

There was no rate for gravity stone. Nobody had ever declared a body of it in the south, and the Crown had never had to say what part of one it wanted. Saban had heard the Circle took a third of anything its own men dug. Armand said that was a tower rule and not a Crown one, and that the tower dug nothing, it only bought. Emeric had a figure in his head he could defend and no source for it he could name.

Ferra went and got the charter.

It was the barony's own, on the old heavy paper, in a box on the shelf behind her chair where it had lived since the day she became Chancellor, and she read it the way she read everything, from the first word, without skipping. Half- way down the third sheet, in the middle of a long sentence about what the holder owed the Crown in coin and service and the upkeep of the king's road, there was a clause that nobody at the table had read since it was written.

"Saving always to the Crown the fifth part of whatsoever stone of price or metal of worth be raised within the said barony," Ferra read, "the common ores excepted."

She looked up.

"It's Kaldo's charter, not Grayrock's," she said. "Grayrock's died with Renner. There isn't a rate for the hill. There's only ours."

"Then we offer ours," Emeric said. "The rate the Crown already wrote for us. Nobody can say we chose it."

He wrote it. A fifth part, taken on what was raised, lot by lot, as it was raised, and not on any valuation of the whole: because nobody could value the whole, and a fifth of a guess was a debt nobody could pay. The Crown's fifth of each lot to be set aside at the pithead in its presence, or its agent's, and delivered in kind or sold on its behalf at its choosing.

"That's the whole of it," Saban said, when it was read back. He sounded almost disappointed. "A fifth, as you go."

"As we go," said Emeric. "It's the only way it's ever the same number twice."

Ferra had been writing on a separate sheet all afternoon, a second column beside the draft, and when he had signed the return she turned it round so that it faced him.

It said, in her hand: The Crown may answer this with a royal commission. Men sent to hold the working for the realm, at the realm's cost, taking the stone as it is raised and paying the barony what the realm thinks fit. There is nothing in the charter against it. There is nothing in the return that prevents it. I have said so. Then the date, and her initial.

"You think they will," he said.

"I think they can." She did not take the sheet back. "I want it written down that somebody said so before we sealed it, so that if it happens nobody at this table can say it was a surprise."

He read it twice. It was correct. There was nothing in the charter against it. There was nothing he could write in a return that would stop a king who decided the realm needed a mountain more than a baron did.

"Keep it," he said. "Put it in the book."

"It's already in the book."

He sealed the return with the chip inside it, in a packet of oiled linen with his seal across the fold, and it went north on the Kaldo rider the next morning, in the ordinary bag, because a special rider would have told every post on the road that something special was in it. The rider's fee for the capital run was four Silver Rand and his keep. Ferra entered it. Then, under the stone's own heading in the year's column, where Saban had already begun to write the names of the three buyers he thought would pay most for the first lot, she wrote: Sale: pending the Crown.

She underlined it once. Saban looked at the underline for some time, and put his list away.

Vantic had sat through the whole of the return without writing a word, which Emeric had noticed, and which he understood to mean that she would write all of it later from memory and that it would be accurate.

The instrument was another matter, and before they began on it she closed her book and put both hands flat on the cover.

"Before you start, Your Highness," she said. "Everything said at this table about the loan goes to my house. In full. Tonight. I'm telling you now so that nobody says anything in the next hour on the assumption I'll leave it out."

"I assumed you'd send it."

"Assume it harder." She did not smile. "And one more thing. You can ask me what a clause says, and I'll tell you what it says. Don't ask me what you should do about it. I won't advise you against my employer. Not on this."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were," said Vantic, "in about a quarter of an hour."

He did not argue, because she was very probably right.

The loan instrument lay on the table in its folder: eleven sheets, the original and the two amendments and the widening he had written himself the year before last. Ferra took it apart one clause at a time and Emeric ruled a sheet into two columns, and at the top of one he wrote hole and at the top of the other counter, and they went down it.

He had no reason to think Ducatis would reach for the stone. What he wanted was every place in eleven sheets of paper through which a house that wanted to could reach, and what stood in each one. He had been sent south in a carriage by men who were perfectly courteous, with a sentence built to travel. He was not going to leave a door open because the people on the other side of it had always been polite.

Inspection. The factor may read every book of the barony, every account and tally, for as long as she or her house cares to. Every lot of stone raised would be entered in a book, and every book was open. Emeric wrote it in the first column and then in the second, in the same words: They see. They may not touch. Nothing to counter. He did not want anything to counter. That clause was his own.

Attendance. The factor may attend any expenditure above the line.

"Attend," Ferra said, and looked down the table.

"Attend," said Vantic. "Not approve. That went in the autumn before last. My house accepted it." She paused. "Write it as I said it, please."

Ferra wrote it as she said it.

Default. If a quarter's service is missed, the house may claim against the barony's revenue until it is made good. And the barony's revenue, from the day the first lot was sold, would have stone in it.

That one took longer. The counter, when they had it, was two lines. Two quarters' service to be held in coin in the strongroom, entered as a reserve, and never spent for any other purpose, so that a missed quarter could be made good the same day from money that had nothing to do with the hill. And the stone's money to be kept in a book of its own, with its own heading, and never to pay a single coin of the loan's service, so that no clerk in any house could ever say the loan had been serviced out of the stone and therefore stood on it.

Borrowing. The house to be offered first any new loan the barony seeks. Counter: the barony borrows against the stone from nobody, ever. Ferra wrote it as a standing rule and Emeric signed it beside her line, and it took less time than any other row on the sheet.

Then assignment.

The house may assign the debt. It said nothing else. It did not say to whom, or on what notice, and it did not say that the rights of the factor went anywhere other than with the paper.

"So they could sell it," Saban said. "The debt. And the right to read every book in four baronies with it. To anybody."

"Anybody," said Ferra.

"My house has never assigned a debt," said Vantic, and then, because Ferra had turned and looked at her, "That is a statement about my house. It isn't a counter. I know."

Nobody said anything for a while. It was the only row on the sheet with a blank in the second column, and it sat there in the lamplight, and every person at the table could see what it meant. A duke who wanted to read Kaldo's books need not send a spy. He need only buy a piece of paper.

It was Saban who said it, because Saban was the one at the table who dealt in paper.

"Pay it off."

He said it simply. He had clearly been thinking it since the first row.

"First lot of stone that sells. Pay the principal down to the coin, and the service with it. Every hole on that sheet closes at once. No default, because there's nothing to default on. No assignment, because there's nothing to sell. No house." He did not look at Vantic, carefully. "It's the cleanest thing we'll ever buy."

Emeric looked at the blank in the second column.

"No," he said.

"No?"

"The rights stand when the debt's paid. I wrote that myself. It's in the widening." He put a finger on the eleventh sheet without looking for it. "Paying the principal leaves the audit where it is and takes away the reason for her being in this room."

He looked down the table at Vantic, and asked her the only thing she had said she would answer.

"If the principal were paid tomorrow. What does the clause say happens to you?"

"The clause says nothing happens to me." She said it evenly, the way she said everything. "The rights stand. My house would recall me within the year. There'd be nothing here to keep a factor for. I'd be sent to a borrower who owed us money." A very small pause. "We'd take the repayment gladly. The house would." She did not correct herself further, and nobody at the table seemed to notice that she had corrected herself at all. "And I'd exercise the right by letter. Twice a year, perhaps. From a long way off."

"Twice a year by letter," said Emeric, "isn't a brake."

He did not explain it any further than that, and he did not need to. Ferra had the stay in the ninth clause, which bound him, and which he had given her. Rudd had a sheet in his shirt that could stop the works, which bound him, and which he had written. Every brake in his life had his own hand somewhere on it, except one, and that one was sitting at the end of the table with its hands flat on a closed book, answering to a house in the north that he could not pay, flatter, frighten or dismiss. He had built her an auditor's chair the year before last and had known exactly what he was doing. He was not going to pay her to leave it now, on the first day he had something worth watching.

"The debt stays," he said. "Serviced every quarter, in full, on the day, out of the ore and the tolls. The assignment hole gets its counter." He pulled the sheet toward him and wrote in the blank. If the debt is ever assigned to any holder but the house of Ducatis, it is repaid in full from the stone's book within the year. "They can sell the paper. Whoever buys it gets a year of reading our books and then gets paid off."

Saban read it upside down, and sat back.

"That's a counter," he said, as a man says it about a move at a board.

"It's the only coin the stone's book will ever pay toward that loan," said Ferra, writing it, "and only to be rid of it."

Vantic had not moved. Emeric could not see her face clearly past the lamp. She opened her book, after a moment, and wrote in it for some time, steadily, the way she always wrote, and when she was done she closed it on her quill and looked at him.

"My house will find that an unusual reason to keep a debt, Your Highness."

"Tell them it's the only one I've got."

"I'll tell them all of it," Vantic said. "I said I would."

The pens came out of the works that week, forty of them, in a wooden tray.

The second die had held. Bram had made it harder and thicker than the first and fitted it to a heavier press, and the mill's strip had come even across its width some time in the spring without anybody marking the day, and the nibs came off the die one to a stroke, slotted and shaped and needing only the grinding. Bram costed them at two copper apiece, not counting the barrel, and said that if anybody wanted them cheaper they could buy a goose.

They went to the six first, and then to the keep's clerks, and then one in a packet up the river road to the tally house at Grayrock, addressed to the clerk of the ore contract.

Ferra took the one from the tray that had her name on its paper band, and turned it over, and looked at it for a while. It was a good pen. It was plainer than hers, and the barrel was turned on the second lathe rather than shaped by hand, and it held a little less, and it wrote exactly as well. She put it in the drawer of her table, under the spare ink, and went on writing with the other one.

Fletcher's letter came up with the lower-water carter at the end of the month.

The keel was down and the first six frames were up. And the spare section was finished, beside the keel, on its own cradle. He had built it first. He had wanted to learn the joins at the stations on a piece that was never going into the water, where nobody would drown if he had them wrong, and he had had them wrong twice. The third time was right. It was a ring of iron frames the length of a man, plated and riveted and bulkheaded at both ends, and it stood on the beach like a slice cut out of a loaf, and people came down from the village to look at it.

It's heavier at the stations than any join needs to be, Fletcher wrote. I built it as drawn.

He did not say anything else on the subject. Emeric read that sentence three times, and did not answer it.

The shaft log came down from Grayrock with the ore tallies at the end of the month, in a hand that was small and upright and very clear.

It had not been asked for. Ilsa had been told to send the Chancellor everything, and she had taken the instruction at its word. At the back of the packet, after the carts and the weights and the rate, she had copied out fair every sheet the tally house held on the east working since the seal came off, because the east working was on the hill and the hill's books were now hers to keep. Hask's daily marks for the crosscut. The fire's four days. The depth of the lamp, morning and evening. And the three dives, as Ward had given them to Hask that night and the tally-house clerk had written them down, with Hask's large careful mark beside each, because it was the rule on that hill now that what happened went into a book.

Dive one. In at the line. Called at ten, twenty, thirty. Went quiet after thirty. Hauled at sixty-one. Came round above the line. Said: I did it wrong.

Dive two. Turned at forty.

Dive three. Turned at forty-six.

Ferra read it at her table in the evening, with the lamp lit and the door open.

She read it twice. She turned back through the relay log on the shelf behind her to the night of the dives, and found his line for that day in her own copy, where she had taken it down from the marks: Gallery seventy-one paces. One fork, right, at forty. Found. Came out. She laid the two sheets side by side on the table, square to the edge, the way she laid out payments that had arrived a little late and a little light.

Then she sat for a long time with the pen in her hand, and did not write anything at all.
