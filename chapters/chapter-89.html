Chapter 89 - A Line in a Report

The masons gave the mortar four weeks, because the old master said four weeks, and on the first morning of July they opened the sluice at the Mere all the way.

Emeric was at the wall cistern when it came. He heard it before he saw it: a sound in the stone channel along the top of the aqueduct like a long breath being let out, and then the water itself, coming round the last arch from the fields at the speed of a man walking, a hand deep, then two, brown at the front with a year of dust and leaves it had picked up off the channel floor and then clear behind, clear and cold and full to the lip. It came across the new arch without a drop lost at the joints, and the old master stood on the parapet and watched it go under his boots and did not say anything at all, and then it poured into the cistern in a white rope as thick as a man's leg, and the cistern began to fill.

It filled by noon. By evening the overflow was running down the old stone gutter into the canal ring, as somebody had built it to do two hundred years before, and the canal ring, which had been a long green skin of standing water since before the plague, began very slowly to move.

They dredged it in the second week. Thirty men from the dock quarter with rakes and baskets and a borrowed punt, in the water to the waist, hauling out the black stinking mud of thirty years and spreading it on the barony's fields outside the wall, where Farris said it would do more good than anything Emeric had ever told him to put there. The smell lasted four days and the lower ward complained about it for six. On the seventh day the water in the ring ran clear from the cistern to the east sluice, cold enough to make a child yell, and the children of the lower ward were in it from dawn to dark, yelling, and nobody could get them out.



The first barges came down from Grayrock in the third week of July.

The grain had gone up first, as Bryda had said it would. Saban's hired barges, three of them, low in the water with Kaldo's old wheat, poled upriver to her landing and weighed on her scale, every sack, with Saban's man watching and hers writing. Then they came back down with ore.

Bram was on the wharf by the burned dock quarter when they tied up, and he did not wait for the barges to be made fast. He went down the ladder onto the first one and stood in the ore to his ankles and picked up a lump of it and turned it over and smelled it, and put it in his pocket, and picked up another one and did the same, and the bargeman watched him with the face of a man who has never before seen anyone put ore in his pocket.

"It's ore," Bram said, coming back up the ladder.

"Is it good?"

"It's Grayrock's." He considered that. "It'll do."

The tally came down with the third barge, in a hand Emeric did not know: tall, careful, upright letters, the weights in one column and the date and the barge's name in the next, and at the foot of it, small, two initials that were not Bryda's. With it was a letter, short, in Bryda's own hand.

Grayrock's forge, it said, rolled plate for the mine's own pumps and had done for forty years. If the prince of Kaldo required plate, Grayrock's forge would roll it to any measure he cared to send. It said measure as if it were underlined, though it was not.

He sent the measure.

Bram filed a rod for it from the copy in the tally room, a foot long, square, with the twelve lines cut into it as fine as Bram's finest point would cut, and a gauge block to go with it, and Emeric wrapped them in oiled cloth and wrote a line to go with them: that the plate should be this thick, by this gauge, and these widths, by this rod, and that if Grayrock's rod disagreed with this rod, this rod was the one that was right. He read it over and thought it sounded like a man who had never been told no, and left it as it was, because it was true.

The rod went up the river on the next barge. He stood on the wharf and watched it go and thought that it was the first time a Kaldo inch had left Kaldo.



Armand told him about the sense in the last week of July, in the room off the hall, because Emeric had asked a question and Armand could never leave a question with only one answer in it.

The question had been about Imani's column. Whether a man could feel, from outside, what a stone had done to another man. Whether Linnet's slate was the only way to read it.

"You feel it already," Armand said. "A little. You felt the boy's across the long room, the first autumn I was here. Sela's boy, with the stone in his hands. I watched you do it."

"That's only near. Across a room."

"That's only you." Armand put his pen down. "Some can do a good deal more. Not many. I used to know three in the tower who could do it properly." He paused. "Four, counting me."

Emeric looked at him.

"I was a High mage once," Armand said, as a man might say that he had once been tall. "I sat with the elders of the tower for a while, for my sins. Not long. Long enough to learn the things they don't put in the books." He folded his hands on the board. "It's a trick. That's all. A trick the very strong can do and the rest can't, and the very strong don't talk about it, because it's more use if nobody knows you can."

"Show me."

"I can't." Armand shook his head. "That isn't modesty. It's like asking a man to show you how he hears. I can tell you what I do. I can't make you feel me do it." He considered. "I can show you what it's for."

He closed his eyes.

Nothing happened that Emeric could see. Armand sat in the chair with the blanket over his knees and the board across it and his eyes shut, and breathed, a little short, as he always did now, and the fire ticked, and Emeric watched his face and felt nothing at all, or very nearly nothing: a faint pressure in the air of the room, as if a door had been opened somewhere in the house and the draught had not yet arrived.

"Two in the corridor," Armand said, without opening his eyes. "Coming this way. A man and a boy. The man has a stone in his coat, a lamp stone, about half full. The boy is carrying something hot, in both hands, and he's walking carefully, because if he spills it he'll be in trouble." He opened his eyes. "The man's walking slowly so as not to get ahead of him. That's Hale. Hale always walks behind anyone carrying anything."

There was a knock. Hale came in, with a lamp stone in his coat pocket for the stair, and behind him, with both hands round a bowl of something steaming and his tongue between his teeth, Arthur, who had been sent from the kitchen with Armand's supper because Maren's girl was down with a cold.

Arthur put the bowl down on the board without spilling it and looked extremely pleased. Hale looked at the two of them, and at Emeric's face, and asked whether he was intruding, and was told he was not, and went away again with Arthur ahead of him.

"How?" said Emeric.

"You let it out." Armand picked up the spoon. "Not a spell. Nothing told what to be. You let a little of it go out of you, very thin, the way your breath goes out of you on a cold morning and you can see it hang. Thinner than that. It goes out into the room, and it goes through the door, and down the corridor, and it touches things, and where it touches something it stops." He ate a spoonful. "And you know where it stopped. You don't see it. You know it, the way you know where your foot is in the dark. A wall stops it flat. A man stops it soft, and a little pushes back, because a man has his own. A stone stops it and hums."

Emeric sat very still.

He knew what it was. He had known as soon as Armand said it stops. On his other world there were animals that hunted in the dark by shouting, and listening for the shout to come back off whatever was in front of them, and knew from how long it took and how it came back where a moth was, and how big, and which way it was flying. Bats did it. Ships did it, with sound sent down into the sea, and a man with his ear to a box listening for the echo off a hull he could not see. Echolocation. Sonar. The words came up out of the other life as if they had been waiting.

Armand was not shouting. He was breathing out, very softly, into the whole house, and listening for the breath to come back.

"It costs," Emeric said. He could see that it did. Armand's hand had gone to his chest under the blanket, flat, the way it went now when he had climbed a stair or laughed too long, and there was a shine of sweat at his temple that had not been there before he closed his eyes.

"Everything costs." Armand ate another spoonful, slowly. "This costs more than it should, because you're spending yourself on nothing. On air. Most of what goes out never comes back." He looked at Emeric over the spoon. "And you're not to try it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to tonight, behind your shutters, with a candle and a bucket." Armand put the spoon down. "Don't. Not yet. Not without me in the room. You spread yourself thin, and you don't always come back thick. I saw a man in the tower go out to the walls of a room once and not find his way back into himself for a day and a night. He was very strong. He was strong enough to go a long way out, and it turned out that was the trouble."

Emeric said that he would not.

He meant it when he said it, which surprised him a little.



The heat came in the first days of August and sat on Kaldo for a week like a lid.

The river went flat and brown. The dogs lay in the gutters. Sela closed the school at noon because the children were falling asleep at their slates, and the still crew worked in their shirts with the doors open and drank the canal ring's water by the bucket. And on the eighth day, in the afternoon, the sky to the south-west went the colour of a bruise, and the wind got up out of nowhere and turned every leaf in Kaldo over to its white side, and Ambrose Lark went up onto the wall with a kite.

The garrison man on the stretch above the lower ward told it afterward to anybody who would stand still. The kite was paper, Kaldo's grey paper pasted on a frame of split cane, as big as a door, and the string was hemp, and Ambrose had soaked the string in a bucket all morning, on purpose, so that it would be wet, because a wet string, he told the garrison man, went where the lightning wanted to go and a dry one didn't, and he wanted to see where it wanted to go. The garrison man had said that he would rather not see that from close to. Ambrose had said that was very sensible and paid out another fifty yards of string.

The kite went up into the dark of the cloud. The rain came down in a wall. The garrison man got into the doorway of the next tower and watched, and a little after that he saw the string go stiff and stand out from the kite to Ambrose's hands as straight as a rod, and a thin blue light run down the whole length of it, and Ambrose sit down very suddenly on the wall walk with his legs out in front of him and all his hair standing up from his head.

He was not dead. He was not even badly hurt. He was carried down to the ward by two of the garrison with his hands held out in front of him like a man holding a hot dish, talking the whole way, and Imani put him on a bench and looked at his hands, which had a burn across both palms where the string had run, and at his eyebrows, which had been singed again and were now very nearly not there at all.

"You did this on purpose," she said.

"I wanted to see where it went."

"It went into you." She was dressing the left hand, not gently. "Next time, tie the string to a post and watch the post."

Ambrose stopped talking. He looked at her with an expression of tremendous respect.

"That," he said, "is a much better idea."

The second bolt came twenty minutes later and did not want Ambrose. It came down out of the cloud onto the line pole at the foot of the keep, the last one before the wire went in at the window of the line room, and Emeric, who was in the keep's hall at the time, heard it as a crack so loud that it was not a sound so much as a blow to the whole building, and smelled, a breath later, coming down the stair, hot copper and burned wax.

The coil in the line room was dead. The fine wire wound round the needle's box had gone black and run together in two places, and the wax that held it was a puddle on the bench, and the needle hung loose and would not move whatever was sent. Down at the South Gate, Ward's needle had come through it untouched, because the bolt had found the nearer end.

Emeric knew what was wrong as soon as he saw the coil. He had known what was missing from that line since the day they strung it, and had put it on a list of things to do, and then not done it, because there had been other things. He stood in the line room with the burnt coil in his hand and the rain coming in at the window and knew exactly what to build.

He took the coil down to the powerhouse the next morning and put it on Tess's board, and told her what had happened, and nothing else.

Ambrose was there already, with both hands bandaged to the wrist, sitting on the short-legged stool. He had been there since first light. He looked at the burnt coil with his head on one side, like a dog that has heard its name.

"It took the wire," he said, "because the wire was the easiest road down. That's all it ever wants. Down. The ground. It doesn't want the coil. It doesn't want the kite or me or the tree. It wants the ground, and it takes the easiest way to get there, and anything that's in the way of the easiest way, it goes through." He held up his bandaged hands. "Like me."

Tess looked at the coil. Then at the drawing of the line on the wall of the powerhouse, where she had it pinned, every pole and every plate, down to the two copper plates buried in the wet ground at each end.

"Then give it an easier one," she said.

They did it between them, without him, in two days. He watched from the door and said nothing, which was harder than he had expected. Tess drew it and Ambrose argued with the drawing and Tess drew it again, and on the second afternoon Bram made it: a little iron box at the foot of the line where the wire came in at the keep, and inside the box two iron points facing each other, a finger's width apart and no more. One point on the line. The other on its own wire, thick, going straight down the wall and into the ground to a copper plate buried in the wet below the keep's well. The telegraph's own current was far too weak to jump a finger's width of air. It went past the gap as if the gap were not there. Lightning, which had jumped a mile of air to reach the pole, would not think twice about a finger.

When they showed it to him he said, because he could not help it, that he had heard a thing like that called a lightning arrester. Tess wrote it down. Ambrose said it aloud four times, to see how it sounded, and liked it better each time.

The next storm came nine days later. Emeric was in the line room when it hit the same pole, and he was looking at the iron box when it did, and he saw the gap fill with a crack of blue light like a struck match the size of a fist, and go dark, and smelled iron. The needle on the board in front of him jumped, once, and settled, and when he pressed the key it answered from the South Gate as if nothing had happened at all.

Down at the powerhouse, he heard later, Ambrose had been standing in the door in the rain watching the keep, and had seen the flash at the foot of the line, and had sat down in the mud of the mill yard in his good coat and laughed until Tess sent the yard boy out to fetch him in.



Ferra asked her question at Vantic's weekly review on the Friday after.

It was put as procedure, the way Ferra put everything that mattered. The House's factor reported on the security every week to her partners, in writing. The security was the barony's revenue. The barony's revenue was, at present, very largely spirit, sitting in casks in a cooper's shop, which the coast would not buy, because the coast had heard about the war and did not buy casks from a town that might not be standing in the spring. A buyer was in the lender's interest. Might the House's standing report on the security therefore be shown to a buyer?

Vantic read the question twice.

"It may," she said.

She wrote it on the House's form, in her small grey hand, under the House's line and the date. One line. Accounts sound. Security in good condition. And her initials, and nothing else, because nothing else had been asked for.

Saban took it down the coast on the Monday, folded inside the cover of his own book where he kept the things that were worth more than they looked. He came back in twelve days. The same buyers who had sent three casks out of four back up the river at the end of June last year had read the House's line, and read it again, and asked who the House's factor was, and been told, and taken thirty casks on trial at the coast price.

"Thirty," said Ferra, and wrote it, and drew a line under it, and did not move on to the next line straight away.

Emeric went up the east tower stair that evening to say what had to be said. Vantic was at her table with the lamp lit. She looked up when he came to the door.

"Thank you," he said.

"It isn't a favour." She did not put her pen down. "It's accurate."

"I know."

"Then there's nothing to thank."

He went down. On the stair he passed Hale coming up with the week's sealed copies for her case, and stood aside on the landing to let him by, and Hale stopped, as Hale did when he had something to say that he had decided was not gossip.

"The granary," Hale said. "Your highness. I thought you'd know." He shifted the copies under his arm. "The factor was there on Thursday, at first light, before the granary man had his door open. With a lantern. She had him open it, and she went down every row of sacks herself, and counted, and put her hand into six of them, and asked what had been turned and when. She was there two hours." He considered. "The granary man asked me afterward whether the barony was in trouble."

Emeric looked up the stair at the light under her door.

Accounts sound. Security in good condition. She had written it on the Friday. She had made sure it was true on the Thursday, at dawn, on her knees in a granary with a lantern, and had not said a word about it to anybody, and would not have if the granary man had not been frightened.

"Thank you, Hale," he said, and went on down, and found at the bottom of the stair that he was smiling, and did not entirely know at whom.
