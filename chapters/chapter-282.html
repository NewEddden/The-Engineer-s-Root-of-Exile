Chapter 282 - The Works

Ferra brought him the stone on the Thursday morning, at seven, in its box.

She had not slept much and neither had he. She had got up at six, from the warm floor, without waking Imani, and dressed in the dark, and gone down; and when he came down after her at a quarter to seven she was at the table in the study with the box open in front of her and her stone book open beside it, and her pen in her hand, and her face washed and her hair pinned so hard that the pins showed. She did not look up when he came in. She turned the book round on the table so that it faced him, and put her finger on the line she had written.

It said: 26 November. One. The bench arm. E.'s hand.

Under it, the count. Three.

"You'll want it today," she said. "You'll want to write the first lines this morning, before you think about it too much. So you'll do it the way you said at the table and not some cleverer way you think of at noon." She took the stone out of its felt and put it on the blotter in front of him, grey, unlit, the size of a long small egg, one of the four left from the pod. "I'm going to the hospital. He'll want to know the pin went again. He's forgotten that he asked." She stood. "Imani says he'll forget it four or five times. The head does that. It isn't anything."

"Ferra."

"I'm all right." She looked at him then, once, and her face did something small, and stopped doing it. "I'm better than all right. I had a night." She picked up her book and put it under her arm. "Write the lines. I'll be back at ten to read them."

She went out. He heard her on the stair, and then in the ward below, quick, and then not.

He sat and looked at the stone a while. Then he put his hand on it, and went in.



The room in a new stone was always the same: bare, and quiet, and waiting, like a house with the plaster still wet. He had written in perhaps a dozen. He had never written in one that was going to drive a body.

He did not write the arm. He wrote the door first.

Before anything. Before the joints, or the motions, or the register, or the thing that would tell the arm to close its hand or open it. At the very top, the first lines any reader would come to, in his own hand, plain: that this stone would take a change in one hand only, his, and after his in the hands of six others, and he wrote the six notes in one after another, Ferra's and Ward's and Armand's and Bram's and Saban's and Imani's, which he knew better than his own; and that any other hand that cut into it, anywhere, in any line, would lock the brakes and stop the arm where it stood, and the stone would do nothing at all, ever again, until one of the seven came and opened it.

He wrote it so that it would fail dead. He went over it four times to be sure it failed dead. The old makers' guard had seen a stranger's hand and gone for his throat; this one would see a stranger's hand and lie down.

And then he wrote the stop. A word. Any of the seven notes, by touch or through the door at a hair's width, could say it, and the stone would stop whatever it was doing, whatever it had been told, and lock, and wait.

It took him an hour and a half. It was the shortest piece of writing he had ever done in a stone and the slowest. When he came out his head ached behind the eyes, and his tea was cold, and Ferra was in the door with her book.

"Read it," he said.

She came and put her hand on the stone beside his, and went in. She was gone a long time. She read slowly in a stone, the way she read a contract, every word twice; and when she came out she sat back in her chair and looked at the window for a while.

"It doesn't say what happens if all seven of us are dead," she said.

"No."

"Then it stands where it is forever."

"Yes. Locked. On its brakes." He rubbed his eyes. "It's an arm on a bench. If all seven of us are dead, nobody's going to mind."

"It's an arm on a bench today," said Ferra. She wrote something in her book. "Write that down somewhere. That you thought about it. For the day it's not."

He wrote it down.



The arm had stood on its bench at Tess's end of bay three since the spring, without a mind.

It was a great thing, iron, the length of a man from shoulder to fingertip and three times as thick; bolted at the shoulder to an iron stand that Bram had made for it, with a waist that turned on Ottoline's bearings and an elbow and a wrist and a hand. Wren had lain under it all summer holding the lamp. It had cables in it, and Isa's looms, and a battery box in what would have been its chest if it had a chest; and at the shoulder and the elbow, the two big joints, it had the two things Dov had been cutting since September and swearing at since October, which were the reason the whole works now said the word cycloidal as if it were a kind of weather.

A cycloidal drive. Emeric had drawn one in the spring of the year before, at the elbow of Abel's arm, and left the drawing on Tess's bench, and said nothing. It was a gearbox you could hold in two hands. A flat disc with a wavy rim, like the edge of a pie crust, that rolled round inside a ring of pins, pushed by a shaft that was off its centre; so that every turn of the shaft walked the disc back round the ring by one bump of its rim. A motor that spun fast, through it, came out the other side turning slowly and very strongly, in a box no thicker than a loaf. Dov had cut the turbine's turning gear with one in July, and it had worked first time, and he had refused to admit it. He had cut these two off the Forge's masters and then spent five weeks making a jig to grind them without the Forge, because Emeric had asked him to and because Dov did not trust anything he could not make twice.

"It wobbles," he had said, at the time, of the first one. "On purpose. A gear that wobbles on purpose."

"Two discs," Emeric had said. "Half a turn apart. They cancel."

Dov had made it with two discs. It had stopped walking across the bench. He had not said anything about that either.

So the arm had its mundane muscles: two electric motors in its big joints through Dov's drives, and the battery, and in its hand, under a glove of Abloh's grey leather, five bundles of the fiber, a bundle to a finger, like Abel's hand but bigger. And it had its stone muscles, too: pairs of shaped gravity stones at every joint, set either side like a man's biceps and triceps, that would make it strong for its weight and fast, much faster than the motors. Either could move it. Both together were what Emeric meant it to be.

And now it had a mind.

He set the stone in its seat in the arm's chest on the Thursday afternoon, with Tess and Dov and Merrin and Isa watching, and Wren, who had been told she could watch from the doorway and had come in as far as the end of Isa's bench; and he put his hand on it, and went in, and told it to close its hand.

It closed its hand on the bench.

It did not close it on the air above the bench, where Emeric had meant it to. It closed it on the bench, the whole oak top, because its stone pairs had been written as he had written the cave golem's in his head, for a thing that was a ton of stone and had to move a ton of stone; and the hand came down on the oak and closed, and the oak split from end to end with a noise like a shot, and the two halves of the bench went over in opposite directions, and everything on them went with them. Isa's tray of wire. Merrin's truth tables. Tess's tea.

Nobody moved for a moment.

"That was my bench," said Dov.

"It was a strong bench," said Merrin, from the floor, where she had sat down very suddenly.

The hand opened. Emeric had told it to. It opened, quite gently, the way he had meant it to close, and let go of the two halves of the oak, and hung there over the wreck in the air above the floor with its fingers spread, waiting.

"Too much," said Tess.

"Too much," said Emeric.

"You wrote it for the thing on the hill."

"I wrote it for the thing on the hill."

"Write it for a bench," said Tess, and turned her chair round, and went to get a broom.

He wrote it for a bench. It took him the rest of the day and half the next, and a new bench from the carpenters with a note on it from Ward's quartermaster asking what had happened to the last one; and on the Friday afternoon the arm closed its hand on the air above the new bench, and opened it, and closed it, on a thought, as softly as Abel's, and Wren in the doorway let out a breath she had been holding for a whole minute and said, "Oh," and nobody laughed at her.



Rylan came to the Motion Shop on the Saturday morning.

He had said he would, at the gallery gate, at midnight, with his lantern. Emeric had thought he would come the next morning, after his hour of shouting; but the next morning Rylan had been at the hospital with Ward, and the morning after that at the base with the class, and he had not come; and Emeric understood, by the Saturday, that Rylan had been putting it off because he did not know what he was going to say.

He came in at the bay's end door at half past nine, in his garrison coat, with his hands in his pockets, and stood there. Wren was not there. Wren had been at the shop every afternoon after school since the spring, and every Saturday since the summer, and she was not there; she was at home, Emeric found out later, because her father had told her to stay at home until he had been and come back, and she had stayed.

Tess was at her bench with the arm's relay board open in front of her. She looked up, and saw him, and put down her iron, and turned her chair round to face him, and waited.

Emeric was at the arm's shoulder with Dov. He thought about going out, and did not, because Rylan had seen him and had not asked him to.

"What are you teaching her," said Rylan.

It came out flat. Not angry. He had got past angry at the gallery gate.

Tess thought about it. She thought about it the way she did, with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes on a place a little to the side of the person she was talking to, a long time; and Rylan stood in the door and let her.

"To look first," said Tess, at last. "Then to think. Then to do the thing she thought, and not the thing she was going to do before she looked." She paused. "That's the whole of what this shop is. Anybody can do a thing. Dov can do anything. The trick's in the looking. And she's better at it than anybody I've had. Better than Merrin. Better than me at her age." She looked at Rylan then. "She looked on the hill. She looked, and she saw he couldn't see, and she thought where the thing would go when the light came on, and she put the light where it would be safe for it to go, and she didn't stay with it." She was quiet. "I didn't teach her that. You did. I asked her who did, and she said nobody, and then she went red, and I knew it was you."

Rylan said nothing for a while.

"She could have been killed," he said.

"Yes," said Tess.

"She's fifteen."

"I was eleven when I did this," said Tess, and held up her thumb, with its old pale scar across the ball of it. "Nobody asked me if I was old enough to be near a fire. I could have been killed in the dynamo house. I nearly was. I was seventeen and I stood in the door because I knew the switches and nobody else did." She put her hand down. "She'd have gone up that hill whether she'd ever met me or not. She went up it because she knew what a battery lamp does in a dead zone, which is go on being a lamp. She knew that because of me." She looked at him steadily. "So. If you want her out of here, take her out. I won't argue. I'll miss her. Dov'll miss her more than me and he'll never say so." Dov, at the arm's shoulder, said nothing very loudly. "But if you want her to go on going up hills, and she will, Rylan, she's yours, she'll go up every hill there is, then I'd sooner she went up them knowing what's at the top."

Rylan looked at her.

"What would that be," he said. "If she stays."

"An apprentice," said Tess. "A real one. Papers. Ferra's office. A wage, a small one, out of my budget, which Bram signs. She'd learn what Merrin learns and what Isa learns and what Dov learns, which is more than any of them know, because she'd learn all three. And the rule." She did not take her eyes off him. "The rule's the same as the shop's rule for everybody. Nobody goes near anything that's alive, or might wake, or might go off, without me or Bram or him" (she tipped her head at Emeric) "saying so first. Nobody. She broke that on Wednesday, and I'll tell her so myself, and she'll be told what it cost. And she'll keep it."

"She broke it to save his life," said Rylan.

"Yes," said Tess. "That's why she's not out on her ear."

Rylan stood in the door a long time.

Then he took one hand out of his pocket, and looked at it, as if he had forgotten what was in it; and there was nothing in it. He put it back.

"I'll ask her mother," he said.

He went. He came back at noon, with Nessa, and with Wren between them in her school coat, scrubbed, with the cut on her forehead closed up under one of Imani's strips; and Nessa, who had never been inside the works in her life, stood in bay three and looked round her at the arm and the benches and the relay boards and the coils, and at Tess in her chair, for a long time, without a word.

Then she said to Tess, "She talks about you at supper," in the voice of a woman giving evidence.

"I'm sorry," said Tess.

"Don't be." Nessa looked at the arm. "She talked about him first." She tipped her head at Emeric. "When she was four. A prince with no crown." She looked at her daughter. "Then you. Then that." She looked at the arm again. "I don't understand a word of it and I never will." She turned to Tess. "If she's going to be in it, she's going to be in it properly. With papers. And you keep her off that hill."

"Off every hill," said Tess. "Until I say."

"Until you say," said Nessa, and looked at Rylan, and Rylan nodded, once; and Wren, between them, who had not said a single word since she came in, looked at the floor of bay three as if it were the most interesting floor in the four baronies, and went slowly scarlet to the ears.

Ferra wrote the papers on the Monday. Wren signed them with the shop's own pen. Under her name, where the trade went, Ferra had written, after a long time with her pen over the line: Motion. And then, because the word was not a trade yet in anybody's book in the kingdom, she had underlined it.



They took the arm into the hill on the second of December, for the dead zone.

The proving gallery was the hill mine's lower working, where Ward had given back his key in July: a long low tunnel cut into the rock under the castle hill a hundred years ago, for a seam that had run out, with a heavy door at its mouth and a bend in it after forty yards and solid rock all round. Fennick tested in it. The golem's pieces had lain in its far bay from July to the end of November, and lay now in a side bay by the door, well back from the face, where Ward and Emeric had carried them down from the quarry spoil under canvas the night after. It was the one place in Kaldo where a stone could burst and nothing would care but the rock, and where a dead zone, when it was made, filled again in minutes instead of hours, because there was nothing round it on every side but a hill of dense stone breathing the mana back in.

That was the point of it. Emeric wanted to know what the arm did when its stones stopped, and then he wanted the stones back quickly, so that he could find out again.

There were two arms on the bench at the gallery's bend, side by side, on a trestle of railway sleepers.

The first was the stone arm. Dov had built it in a week from the spare parts of the real one, because Emeric had asked for it: the same iron, the same stone pairs at every joint, and nothing else. No motors. No drives. No fiber in its hand, only the stones. It was what the cave golem had been, an arm made only of what magic could do. The second was the arm from bay three, with its motors and its drives and its five bundles of fiber, and its stone pairs as well. And on the sleepers under each of them, in its hand, each arm held a weight: one of the base's iron plates, fifty pounds, hung from a hook in its fingers a foot above the stone floor.

Ward stood at the gallery's bend with the dead-zone stone in his fist, as he had stood on the hill. He had not wanted to. He had said so at the table, in front of everybody, and then come.

"Stones first," said Emeric.

Both arms held their plates on their stone pairs alone, the motors off, the violet showing faint at the joints. Tess, in her chair at the bench's end with her book on her knee, wrote down the time.

"Now," said Emeric.

Ward squeezed.

The mana went out of the gallery like a breath out of a man. Emeric's sense went; his lightness; the faint violet at the joints went dark on both arms at once. And the stone arm dropped its plate.

It did not drop it the way a hand drops a thing. It dropped it the way a man drops who has been shot: everything at once, the whole arm, from the shoulder; the elbow folding, the wrist folding, the fingers opening, all of it collapsing down onto the sleepers under its own weight with the plate going out of its hand and onto the stone floor of the gallery with a crash that rang down the working and back. It lay there across the sleepers, a dead thing, folded, with its fingers open on the stone.

Tess wrote it down.

Nobody said anything. They had known it would. That was why there was a stone arm. Emeric had wanted every one of them to see, once, with their own eyes, what the cave golem had been on the quarry floor when Ward's stone burst: nothing, with nothing under it. That was the first thing.

The second arm sagged.

It sagged, in the dark, the fifty pounds dragging its elbow down and its wrist down; and then the motors, which Dov had switched on at the battery the instant Ward squeezed, came up under it with a whine, and the drives took the weight. The sag stopped. The elbow began to come back up, slowly, very slowly, the drive's disc walking round its ring of pins one bump at a time; and the fiber in the hand, on the battery's own small coil, held the plate. Not well. The fingers had given half an inch. But they held.

"Hold it there," said Emeric.

It held. The gallery was full of the small sound of the motors and the drive. In ninety breaths, by Tess's count, Emeric felt the mana come back into the working from the rock, faint and then less faint, his sense coming back to him round the edges like a tide coming in over flats; and at two hundred, the violet came up again at both arms' joints, and the stone arm on the sleepers twitched, and tried to rise, and could not, because it had nothing left in its seat but what it had been told before the zone, which was to hold a plate it no longer had.

"Again," said Ward, at the bend, before anybody else could say it.

They did it again. The same. The stone arm dropped, and the geared arm sagged and held.

"Brakes," said Tess.



Bram drew the brakes in bed.

He had been moved home on the Monday, to his own bed in the house by the forge, because he would not stay in the hospital and Imani had decided that a man who would not stay was a worse patient in a ward than at home; and he lay in it with his left arm strapped across his chest and his head bound and his face a colour Emeric did not like, and on the Wednesday evening he made Ferra bring him a board and paper and the stub of a carpenter's pencil, and drew.

He reached for the board with his strapped arm three times in the first ten minutes, because he forgot, and swore each time; and then he drew, slowly, with Ferra holding the board.

A sprung brake. It was the band cuff's latch, the one he had made years before for the knights' cuffs so that a dead zone could not snap every cuff shut at once: a spring that wanted to close and a stone that held it open. When the stone stopped, the spring won. He drew it bigger, for a joint, a brake shoe against a drum on the joint's axle, a spring like a coach's behind it, and a single small stone in a cradle holding the spring back off the shoe; so that the moment the stone in the cradle stopped, for any reason, a dead zone or a fault or a hand pulling it out, the spring went home and the shoe bit the drum and the joint locked where it stood. Not slowly. At once.

"Like the golem fell," he said, from the pillow, to the ceiling. "It fell because its stone came out and nothing was holding it. This holds it." He shut his eyes. "Nothing falls on anybody. It just stands there like a fool till you come and let it off."

"It'll stand there like a fool with its arm up," said Ferra.

"With its arm up. Better than with its arm down on your head." He opened his eyes. "Give it to the girl. Tess. And the other one."

"Wren," said Ferra.

"The other one," said Bram, who knew perfectly well what Wren was called; and shut his eyes again; and was asleep in a breath, with the pencil still in his fingers.

Tess and Wren fitted them on the Thursday and the Friday: four brakes on the stone arm and four on the geared one, on every joint that could bear weight, Wren on her back under the shoulders with the lamp in her teeth and a spanner in each hand, Tess at the bench's end in her chair calling the springs' numbers off Bram's drawing, Dov pretending not to watch. On the Saturday they took the two arms back into the hill.

The stone arm, this time, did not drop.

Ward squeezed, and the mana went, and the violet went out at the joints, and every brake on the stone arm went home in the same instant with a sound like four doors slamming in four different rooms; and the arm stopped. Exactly where it was, the plate in its hand, its elbow bent, its fingers closed; stiff, stupid, a fool with its arm up, as Bram had said. It did not fall. Nothing it held fell. It stood there in the dark of the gallery on its brakes, and waited.

And the geared arm did not need them.

Its brakes went home too; that was their nature, they knew nothing about what else was in the arm. But Emeric had written the stone, the day before, to know: that when the stone pairs stopped and the brakes bit, the motors were to come up and take the weight off the brakes, and then the brakes were to be let off, one joint at a time, by the battery's own solenoids, and the arm was to do the one thing a sensible arm does when it finds itself weak in a dark place holding something heavy. It was to put it down.

It put it down.

Slowly. So slowly that Wren, crouched by the trestle with her lamp, said afterward that she had counted to forty between the first inch and the last. The motors whined. The drives walked round their pins. The elbow came down, and the wrist, and the plate went down onto the sleepers as gently as Maren putting a dish on a table, and the fingers opened, and let go, and the arm straightened, slowly, and lay itself down along the trestle beside the plate, and stopped, and was quiet.

"It set it down," said Dov.

He did not say anything else. He went and stood by it, in the dark, with his hand on the cold iron of its shoulder; and Emeric, whose sense was coming back round the edges, felt Dov's note do a thing he had never felt it do in a year, which was let go of something.

"That's the rule," said Emeric. "That's the whole of it. You can walk. Then you put the frame on, and you can run, and lift, and jump. Take it off, and you can still walk." He looked at the two arms on the trestle. "That one couldn't walk."

"That one," said Tess, writing, "could carry the shop's tea from here to the river and not spill it."

"Not in a dead zone."

"Not in a dead zone," Tess agreed, and underlined something.



The others took the arms up on the handcart at noon, Dov walking beside it with one hand on the stone arm's shoulder as if it might climb off. Emeric stayed. So did Ward, who had not been asked, and Tess, who could not be got up the gallery's slope without two men and a plank and had said she was perfectly happy where she was.

"I'm going to look at the left arm," said Emeric.

"Look," said Ward.

It lay in the side bay under its canvas, with the rest. A week ago, on Bram's drawing and with no reason given, Emeric had asked Dov to pin up one piece of it on a frame of sleepers the way it had stood on the quarry floor: the chest block, and the left shoulder, upper arm, forearm and hand hung off it. The tank held a cupful from Bram's canister. Ward had said a cupful, and had meant it. The chest stone sat in its seat with the hatch plate off, grey, unlit; Emeric had put it there himself that morning with the forge tongs, not his fingers, and had not liked how long his heart had gone on beating in his ears afterward.

He sat down on a crate with his back to the chest block, three feet from the stone, and did not touch it.

He went into his own. The room was where he had left it, and the door in it, and he sent the door out as he had learned to, a thread of it, a hair's breadth, across the three feet of gallery air; and it found the other stone's note, old and quiet and unlike any living thing's, and he did what he had done all autumn with his hand under it in the study. He read.

The guard was where he had left it too. Bright, coiled, every knot of it lit the way it had been in the quarry, waiting for a hand with a pen. It did not stir. He stood in the doorway of the stone with nothing in his hands, and it let him stand. It had been written as a lock on the cutting. Nobody had thought to lock the asking, because nobody had thought there would be someone who asked without cutting; or somebody had, a thousand years ago, and left the door off the latch on purpose, and he did not like not knowing which.

The seat in the head was empty. The line that would have listened to it ran on into the dark and stopped. He stood where the voice would have been, and meant: lift.

Nothing, for the length of a breath. Then the violet came up at the shoulder, and the elbow, and the left arm rose off its sleeper with a long grinding noise like a cart going over gravel, and stopped at the height of his chest, shaking.

It was like trying to thread a needle in a pair of oven gloves cut for somebody else. Every joint answered a beat late, and with far too much; its stone pairs had been written for a ton of stone and took his lift for the order of a man hauling a gate. He let it down. He told the fingers to close, and they closed on the plate Dov had left on the floor and he lifted that, fifty pounds, as easily as a loaf, and set it down again with a clang, and thought: that part is easy. It is only a big hand.

"Tess," he said aloud. "The cup."

Tess looked at her tin cup of tea on the bench's end, and at him, and slowly held it out at the length of her arm.

The fingers closed on it the way they had closed on the oak. There was a bang like a small drum, and a hot brown fountain, and the arm opened and let go a flat tin disc that spun on the stone floor and settled.

"That was the shop's tea," said Tess.

"Give me another."

"There isn't another. That was the other." She put her book on her knee, away from the splash, and wrote down the time. "Ward has a flask."

Ward, who had been standing at the bend all this while with the dead-zone stone shut in his fist and his face like a shut door, looked at the flask on his belt and, with great reluctance, unhooked it.

It took Emeric three tries. He stopped trying to say close, which was a command to a hand, and began to mean the cup: its weight, its rim, how much of it there was to hold. The third time the great grey fingers came down on the flask, and lifted it, an inch at a time, with the cork rattling in it, and set it on the sleepers by Tess's elbow without a drop gone, and opened, and let it be.

When he came out of the stone the crate was cold under him. Tess had her watch open on the book.

"A hundred and ten breaths," she said. "From the lift."

It had felt like an afternoon. He sat looking at the arm lying quiet along its sleepers, and he knew, in the way he knew the length of his own stride, what the rest of it would be. The arm had only been the first piece. Every other piece in the canvas had its rails running to this one stone, its pairs set to the same hand, and the hard part, which was the door, he had now found; a body was only more of the same, to be pinned together, filled and stood up. Not a year. A month, perhaps, with Dov and Bram and no one asking him anything else. Less if he could get it standing in the first week.

He did not say any of that. He picked the tin disc up off the floor instead and turned it over.

"Well?" said Ward.

"It works."

"How well?"

"It flattened Tess's tea."

"That is not an answer."

"No." Emeric put the disc down on the bench. "With all of it stood up, I could have it walking inside a month. Maybe less." He looked at Ward. "I'm not going to. Not yet. I wanted you to hear it from me."

Ward said nothing for some time. The dead-zone stone, Emeric saw, was still in his hand, and the knuckles round it had gone pale.

"It tried to take Bram's head off," said Ward.

"It was doing what it was told. Nobody was telling it anything else." Emeric glanced at the arm. "Somebody is now."

"That is what I'm afraid of," said Ward, quite mildly, and hooked the flask back on his belt and went to get the plank.



Ward found him in the keep yard at a quarter past five on the third, in the dark, hitting a sack.

It was a grain sack, filled from the masons' sand heap and hung by a rope from the beam of the cart shed, where the frost had not got onto the cobbles; and he was hitting it with his left hand, because his right was strapped to the wrist with the last two fingers bound together over Imani's splint, and Imani had said a month and meant it.

Ward stood in the shed's mouth with his lantern and watched three. Then he hung the lantern on its nail.

"It's sand," he said.

"I know."

"You'll not crack anything on sand."

"That's why it's sand." Emeric caught the sack with his good hand and stood holding it. "I'm not trying to hit it hard. I'm trying to hit it right."

Ward waited. He had been waiting in this yard for eleven years and he could do it longer than anybody Emeric knew, Ferra included.

"I had the shield on," Emeric said. "Over the hand, over all of me. It kept the stone off. There isn't a mark on the skin; Imani looked." He held up the strapped hand. "They cracked anyway. Because the shield stops the stone, and it doesn't stop me. Everything I put behind that fist went into the shoulder, and the shoulder broke, and then the whole of it came back down my arm, and the first thing it met was two bones about this long." He measured them on his good thumb. "The stone didn't break them. The stop did. How fast I stopped."

"Every knight knows that."

"Does he?"

"He knows not to hit stone." Ward came in off the cobbles and stood by the sack. "A knight takes a blow. You know how; I showed you." He put his forearm out, level, the way he had five years ago, on the night of the bad landing. "Not more. Everywhere at once. Wrist, elbow, shoulder, back, legs, ground. You don't take it in one place. You hand it on." He took the arm back. "And he doesn't throw his hand at a wall. That's what the sword's for. It's why we carry the thing."

"Everywhere at once makes you a wall."

"Yes."

"So it's a wall hitting a wall." Emeric let the sack go. "I don't want to be harder. Hard is what I was. I want the hit to take longer getting to me."

On the other side of everything, they had built the front of a car to crush. On purpose, at great expense, with men paid to drive them into walls and measure the wreck, so that the people inside stopped over a yard and not over an inch. He had walked past a hundred of those wrecks in photographs and never once thought of them as something a hand could do.

So: inside, not over. Not a skin on the hand, which a dead zone would take off him with the shield, but his own mana pressed down into the bone of the knuckles and the flesh on top, packed, in rings like an onion's, and every ring set to give in its turn: the outermost first, the next a hair after, and the next a hair after that, so that the blow came down through them the way a man comes down a stair and not off a roof. Only there. Only for the blink of the contact. Packed everywhere, the hand would not close. Packed a moment early, it was a stone; a moment late, it was nothing at all.

He hit the sack.

The jolt went up to his elbow and stopped there, and he stood and looked at his fist, which would not open. He had packed the joints along with the knuckles. It stayed a fist on the end of his arm for the length of a slow breath, a small grey stone of a thing, and then let go all at once, and the fingers sprang out like a dropped bundle of sticks.

"That's a club," said Ward.

The second time he was late, and the rings folded after the knuckles had already met the sand, which was the same as having no rings. The third time he was early. The ninth time something happened that he had not felt before: the sack swung away from him exactly as hard as before, and his hand felt as if it had hit the sack through a folded blanket.

He stood and looked at it.

"Again," said Ward.

It did not come again for thirty-one more. Ward counted them. Then Ward took the lantern off its nail and held it up to the shed's corner post, which was oak, a foot square, black with eighty winters, and said, "Now that."

Emeric hit the post eleven times with his left hand. One of them was right. The sting of it came late and small and stayed in the skin. The other ten went up into his wrist and made his eyes water, and on the tenth he said something in no language anybody in the four baronies spoke.

"And you'll think all that," said Ward, "between seeing a man and hitting him."

"Not yet."

"Then don't hit stone yet." Ward looked at the post, and at the hand. "Has it a name?"

"A crumple."

"You've a word for everything."

"Somebody has to."

Imani came across the yard from the keep door with her bag over her shoulder, on her way down to the forge house and Bram, and stopped at the edge of the lantern's light, and looked at the post, and at Emeric's left hand, which he had not managed to put behind him in time.

"I strapped one hand," she said.

"This is the other one."

"I can count to two." She put her bag down on the cobbles and took the hand and turned it over to the light and pressed each knuckle in turn with her thumb, watching his face, not the hand. Red. Hot. Nothing moving that should not. She let go of it and took her book out of her coat. "How many?"

"Ten," said Emeric.

"Eleven," said Ward.

She wrote eleven. "And how many did what you wanted?"

"One."

"One in eleven." She wrote that too. "That isn't a technique, Emeric. That's a fracture you haven't had yet." She shut the book. "Sand until January. The post when the strap comes off and not a day before. If you break that one as well you'll be eating with your feet, and I'll write that down."

She picked up her bag and went. At the yard's gate she said, without turning round, "Ward, you'll count them," and Ward said, "I always count them."

At night he aimed the mending at the two knuckles, as he had aimed it at his legs all year: thin, into the one place, at the pace the flesh would take it and no faster, while Imani's splint held the bones where they belonged. Mana set nothing and knitted nothing; it fed the body that did, and the body knew the shape it was meant to come back to, which was the whole of why it left no mark. A knight's frame mended quicker than a carter's whatever he did about it. This was the same mana with somewhere to go. It could not be hurried, because hurry was what put silver in a joint, and Imani, who had counted his legs, had priced her month on that. She would not cut the strap before the new year, and he did not argue; nothing in her chart from the year before had been cleared to take a load until it had been loaded in stages.

He hit the sand every morning that winter, left-handed, and then with both once she cut the strap off on the ninth of January: three hundred a morning, counted aloud in the dark by Ward, who could not watch a man do anything without counting it. Most mornings he got one in ten. The arm in the gallery had its brakes now, and fell safe. His hand did not, yet.



The table made the shop formal on the Sunday.

It did not take long. Bram's chair was empty, and Ferra had his answer written out on a slip of paper from the bedside, in his right hand, large and crooked, and read it out when Armand asked who would run the thing: She does. I sign.

"He's sore about losing the end of bay three," said Ferra.

"He said that?" said Saban.

"He said that twice. And then he said he'd been losing it for a year anyway and he might as well be paid for it." Ferra wrote. "The Motion Shop. Under Works. Its own budget out of Works', its own people, its own head. Tess Kerrow. Bram signs." She looked up. "Its own key to the gallery, with Ward's. Its own line in my book." She paused. "Its own name, in fact, which none of us chose. Merrin says the hands have been calling it that since the summer and Bram's been pretending not to hear."

"The Motion Shop," said Armand. He said it slowly, as if tasting it. "What is it for? When you write it down. For the people who read your book after us."

Emeric thought about it.

"Things that move on their own," he said. "Safely. Arms. Cranes. Hands for men who've lost theirs. Switches that think." He looked at the table. "Robotics. It's not a good word. It's the one I've got."

"Robotics," said Ferra, and wrote it down, and looked at it, and wrote beside it in smaller letters: E.'s word. "And the other thing it's for. The thing in the gallery's side bay. The thing that isn't on anybody's paper."

"That's under," said Ward.

"That's under," Ferra agreed, and did not write it down at all.



Fennick had dynamite by the end of the week.

He had been at it since August. He had got his key back from Ward in July, after his month, with a new set of rules about ice and the shed's distance from everything and his own presence outside the shed at all times while the stuff was being washed; and he had kept the rules, every one, and had gone on making nitroglycerin in very small amounts in his new shed four hundred yards out in the scrub, and had gone on being, as he said himself, frightened of it every day, which he said was the right amount to be frightened of it.

And he had been trying to tame it. That was Emeric's word, in March, at the table in the shed with the ice melting in its tray: that the stuff was the best thing he had ever made and the worst thing to carry, because it went off if you looked at it, and that what he wanted was the same stuff, as strong, that did not.

"Soak it up," Emeric had said. "Into something. Something fine and dry and dead, that takes it in like a sponge and holds it. Then it's a paste, and then it's a stick, and you can carry a stick."

"Into what?"

"I don't know. Try things."

Fennick had tried things for four months. He had tried sawdust, which worked and then didn't; and flour, which worked and then went mouldy; and chalk, and brick dust, and ash, and the grey clay off the riverbank, and fuller's earth from Abloh's dye works. And in the third week of November he had found it, in a sack at the back of the glassworks' store: a pale fine earth that the glassmen used for polishing, light as flour and dry as bone, the crushed remains of something very small that had lived and died in a lake a very long time ago. It drank the oil the way a cloth drinks wine. It held it. It made a grey putty that you could roll between your palms; and then a stick, wrapped in waxed paper, that you could drop.

He dropped it in the gallery, on the first Wednesday of December, in front of Ward and Emeric, from the height of his own shoulder, onto the stone floor, at the gallery's far end where the rock went in to nothing.

It bounced.

It did not do anything else. It lay on the stone where it had landed, a grey stick in its waxed paper with one end a little flattened, and Fennick looked at it with an expression that Emeric had seen on Bram's face once, when Ferra was very small and had added a column correctly for the first time.

Ward, who had flinched, very slightly, for the first time Emeric could remember, said nothing.

"Dynamite," said Emeric.

Fennick looked at him.

"That's what it's called. Where I read of it." He picked up the stick, which took some doing, because his hand wanted to be careful and his head knew there was no need. "It's called dynamite."

"Dynamite," said Fennick. He said it twice more, under his breath, the way he had said nitroglycerin on the black earth in June, as if he liked the sound. Then he took the stick back out of Emeric's hand, quite firmly, and put it in his coat.

He set four of them off in the gallery the next morning, in holes drilled in the far face, with fuses, while everybody else stood behind the bend: and the far face came down in a great heap of broken rock that filled the working to the roof for ten yards and took Bram's apprentices a week to clear. And on the Friday he took six of them up the hill, to the old quarry, and set them in the north face.

Emeric went up with him.

He had not been back since the Wednesday. He had thought he would not want to; and then he stood on the quarry floor in the cold bright morning with Fennick drilling holes in the north face forty paces away, and found that he did not mind it, much. The floor was a floor. There was a black smear of burnt oil on it where Bram's lamp had gone. There was a furrow in the broken stone a yard long, ending in a hollow, where his heel had gone back under the golem's first blow; he stood and looked at that for a while. And at the top of the spoil bank on the open side there was a great block of broken stone smashed into three, with chips and grit all round it, white in the sun, and a place at its foot where something had lain on the spoil and pressed a shape into it.

He looked at that for longer.

Then Fennick shouted, and they went behind the spoil, and the north face came down.

It came down beautifully. Fennick had set the charges not all at once but in a row, each fuse a little shorter than the last, so that they went off one after another along the face from left to right, crack, crack, crack, crack, crack, crack, quickly; and the face peeled away from the hill in a long rolling sheet, from left to right, as if somebody were turning a page, and lay on the quarry floor in a heap of stone broken small enough for a man to lift. Fennick stood up behind the spoil with his ears ringing and his new eyebrows full of dust and looked at it, and said it was a disgrace, and that the third hole had been a hair too shallow, and that he could see it from here.

"Earth mages," he said, on the way down, "would have had that face down in an afternoon and charged you a gold rand an hour for it. And it'd be in big blocks, because they push, they don't break. And you'd need four of them, because the first three would be tired." He was walking very fast, the way he did when he was pleased. "Six sticks. A morning. Me and two lads and a drill." He looked at Emeric sideways. "And it works when there's a dead zone. And it works when the mages have gone to the capital. And it works when the mages are dead."

"That's why," said Emeric.

"That's why," said Fennick, satisfied, as if he had thought of it himself; and then, after a while, walking: "There was one in the middle. The fourth. Did you see it? It went flat."

"I saw it."

"It went flat one way. Out. Not up. I'd packed the hole a bit odd, with a cup of clay at the back of it, because I'd run out of tamping, and it went out flat, like a hand, like it was pushing." He waved a hand flat in the air, pushing. "I'd like to know why."

"So would I," said Emeric; who knew exactly why, from a book he had read at twenty in a library on the other side of everything, and was not going to say so yet, because a man who could make charges push flat one way could make them do a great many things, and Fennick was the last man in the four baronies who needed to be given a great many things at once.

Fennick looked at him, shrewd under the dust.

"You know," he said.

"Some."

"You'll tell me."

"When you've made it push flat on purpose three times in a row," said Emeric, "and told Ward first."

Fennick thought about that all the way down the hill. At the bottom he said, "Done," and went off toward his shed with his hands in his pockets, whistling.

The stone from the north face went to Linus's road. Linus Vance had had the survey drawn since August: the first paved road out of Kaldo, from the South Gate to the brickfield and the station and on along the Grayrock line's cutting, broken stone rolled into a bed, and on the bed, hot, the black from the refinery's bottom and the Isle's pitch lake mixed with sand and grit and laid and rolled again. Asphalt. Emeric had said the word at the table in the summer, and the road gangs had been saying it since as though it were a swear. They laid the first hundred yards outside the South Gate in the second week of December, in a fog, and the whole of the lower town came out to stand at the edge of it and smell it and put a boot on it, and an old woman from the north well stood in the middle of it for a long time with her basket, looking down at her own feet, and said it was like walking on a table.



The truck died on the hill on the Thursday.

It had been built in the vehicle works by the west wall since the autumn: a long brick shed in the light-industry park on the inside of the wall, on the town's new electric drive, with its own forge and its own little foundry and its own gang of smiths who had come in from all four baronies to learn engines, because the shop was open to any smith who wanted to learn, and that had been the point of it. Any smith could mend an engine, once he had seen one. An engine worked in a dead zone. It did not need a mage to charge it, or a delver to write it, or a duke to say what it might do. It needed a smith, and gasoline, and a crank.

The engine was four cylinders of cast iron, with Ottoline's bearings in it and a spark from a magneto off its own crank, so that it made its own spark as it turned and needed no battery: the dynamo in the river, made small enough to hold in two hands. The wheels were Odo's, iron-spoked and heavy, because Odo said a wheel that was going to carry coal should look like a wheel that could. And the tyres were Tess's: solid, black, Isle rubber cooked with Emberfell sulphur in the press in bay three, which she had been perfecting all summer for the cranes' wheels, until it neither melted in the heat nor cracked in the frost, and smelled like nothing else in the world.

It had a flatbed behind, and a box in front for the driver with a roof and no sides, and it was painted dark green because Odo had a great deal of dark green paint.

It went out of the vehicle works' door on the first Thursday of December under its own power, with a smith called Tull at the wheel and half the works running beside it, and went up the hill toward the station at a brisk walk, and two thirds of the way up, on the steepest part, made a noise like a hen being stepped on, and stopped, and began to roll backward.

Tull stood on the brake. It held. They chocked the wheels and looked at it.

The clutch. The plates that let the engine's turning into the wheels or let it go had been faced with leather, because leather was what Kaldo had; and on the hill, under load, the leather had grown hot, and then hotter, and had glazed, and slipped, and burned, and the smell of it came up off the truck in a blue cloud that the smiths stood in and argued about for an hour.

They faced the plates with something else. They tried three somethings; Emeric suggested one of them and kept quiet about which, and it was not his that worked. It was a smith from Wayford called Mabb, a big quiet man who had made brake blocks for the Wayford line's wagons all his life, who said at the end of the second day, with his arms folded, that a thing that had to bite under heat ought to be made of what brake blocks were made of, and went back to Wayford on the train and came back the next morning with a sack of his own stuff, a cooked mess of fibre and resin and a little brass dust that he would not tell anybody the recipe for. They faced the plates with it. It bit.

On the Saturday the truck went up the hill again and got to the top and over, and went on down the far side toward the station, and a mile beyond it, on the long flat road past the brickfield, began to steam out of its front like a kettle, and stopped.

The cooling. The water that went round the engine's iron to keep it from cooking had boiled, because the radiator in front, a nest of copper tubes that Isa had soldered so beautifully that Ottoline had come down from her room to look at it, was too small. Not by much. By enough.

They made it bigger. They put a fan behind it, on a belt off the crank, which Dov drew on the back of a coal chit in the road with Tull holding the chit flat on the bonnet; and on the Monday the truck went out of the vehicle works' door, and up the hill, and over, and down, and past the brickfield, and on to the rail yard by the station; and stopped at the coal stage, and was loaded with two tons of Grayrock coal by four men with shovels in a quarter of an hour; and came back, laden, the whole way, up the long road from the rail yard to the station's coal bunker, and tipped its load into the bunker's mouth, and drove away again to fetch another.

It did that eleven times that day.

The station's coal had come from the rail yard in horse carts since the station was built: four carts, eight horses, sixteen men, all day. On the Monday night Ferra wrote in her book that the truck had done the work of three of the four carts, with two men, and that the fourth cart had spent the afternoon following the truck up and down the road with its driver staring at it.

Fennick had been watching it all day too.

He had come down from his shed in the morning and stood at the top of the hill by the station, with his coat buttoned up and his hands under his armpits, and watched it go by eleven times; and at four o'clock, when it went past for the last time, empty, with the light going, he fell into step beside Emeric, who was walking home, and said, "Gasoline."

"Yes."

"It's the best thing in the world. Isn't it." He was quite serious. "I've been watching that engine all day, and I've been thinking. It's the best thing in the world. A cup of it does more than a cart of hay does for a horse. And you burn it in a box, a little at a time, and it goes, bang, bang, bang, four times over and over, and the wheels go round." He looked at Emeric. "Why don't the ships burn it?"

"Never," said Emeric. "Not aboard an airship. Not a drop. Not ever."

"Why?"

"Because a cup of it does more than a cart of hay," said Emeric, "and the fumes off the cup do it all at once, if they find a spark, and a ship is a box full of sparks two miles up. The K Discovery has a diesel in her. Diesel's the heavy cut. You can drop a lit match in a bucket of it and the match goes out. That's the only kind of fuel I'll have in the air." He walked. "Gasoline stays on the ground."

Fennick thought about that for a while.

"You could make a ship go fast with it," he said, regretfully.

"You could make a ship go fast for about a minute," said Emeric, "and then very fast in every direction at once."

Fennick laughed, delighted, and turned off toward his shed.

Emeric walked on home alone, along the new hundred yards of asphalt outside the South Gate, in the dusk, with the truck's smell still in his coat. He thought about engines. What an engine was, under all the iron. Air, and heat, and push. You took air, and you made it hot, and hot air wanted to be bigger than cold air, and it pushed. Everything else, the cylinders, the cranks, the plugs and the magneto and Mabb's secret clutch, was only a way of catching the push.

And he thought, without meaning to, about the K Discovery. The unarmed ship; the one that went first, and came down lowest, into the places where the others could not go. Who came down low over a thing, and hung there, and looked; and when something came up at her from below, climbed out of it as fast as her matrix would lift her, which was fast enough until the day it was not.

He found the notebook that night.

It was in the chest at the foot of his bed, under the others: Armand's notebook, the one he had carried in his coat the winter of the dynamo, its spine cracked, its pages soft. He turned to the page he wanted without looking for it. He had known where it was for eleven years. In the back, under a night's counts, in his own hand, younger, quick, after a night on the floor with a candle flame bent away from his palm: Fire for thrust?

He had pushed a flame, that night, and felt it push back, about as well as blowing on a boat. And under the question, a winter later, after a night hanging in the air with the candle bent beside him, two more words in the same hand: Not yet.

He looked at it for a long time.

A flame weighed nothing. That was the answer, and he had had it all along, and had not seen it. Throwing a flame was throwing nothing; you could throw it as hard as you liked, and nothing came back at you, because there was nothing in it to come back. But air had weight. Air was a cart full of something, every cubic yard of it; and if you threw air, it pushed back; and if you threw air and then made it hot, so that it wanted to be twice the size it was, it left you twice as fast as you threw it, and pushed back twice as hard.

Not fire for thrust. Fire behind thrust. Fire as the thing that made the air leave faster.

He took a pencil, and under Fire for thrust? he wrote, in his own hand, older, slower:

Air first. Fire behind.

He told Fennick in the morning, at the shed, because he had said he would tell Fennick things when Fennick had earned them, and Fennick had been the one to ask. Fennick listened to all of it standing in the shed's door in his apron, with his arms folded; and at the end of it, when Emeric had said that there was no fuel in it at all, none, only air and the fire stones that the ship already had, he looked deeply offended.

"No fuel," he said.

"None."

"Then where's the bang?"

"There isn't one. It's a roar. A very long one."

Fennick thought about that, and was visibly consoled.

"I want to be there," he said, "when it's lit."



Two things in the works that week were nobody's but their own.

One was on Bram's floor, in the corner of bay two where the mill's new frames had been put together in the summer: a narrow iron machine on a stand, no wider than a church pew, with a ring of fine hooked needles in its middle that went up and down in turn when you turned a handle. An apprentice girl of Bram's had built it, out of the mill's drawings of a stocking frame, which nobody had asked her to build, because she had wanted a machine of her own and had picked the one nobody else wanted. It made a tube. Not a flat cloth, like the looms. A tube, round and round, of knitted wool; which was what a stocking was, she said, and a sleeve, and a lot of other things. The first thing she made on it was a pair of socks for Tess, whose feet were cold in her chair all winter; and they were badly shaped, the heels in the wrong place and one longer than the other, and Tess wore them every day and showed them to everybody.

Bram, told about it in bed, said it was a toy.

The other was in ward three, under the new wash house on the corner of the second avenue: a cellar, roofed with a vault. There was nothing strange in a vaulted cellar. Kaldo had a hundred of them. What was strange was that this one had been printed, the whole curve of it, by the house printer's gantry, bead by bead, out from both walls toward the middle, with nothing under it but the air of the cellar and the arch cradle, a rig of stones in a frame that held each new bead's weight while it set, and handed it on to the bead before; and that when the two halves met at the crown and the cradle's stones were lifted away, it stood. No timber centring. No formwork. A curve of grey stone over an empty cellar, holding itself up.

Fletcher, who had come up from the yard to see it because he did not believe it, would not stand under it. He said so. He said he would stand under it when it had cured a week and not before, printed or no printed, cradle or no cradle; and a week later to the hour he came back, and went down the cellar steps, and stood in the middle of the floor under the crown of the vault with his hat in his hands and looked up at it for a long time, and came up again, and went back to his yard, and told nobody, as far as anybody knew, what he had thought.



Ferra read Bram the charter on the Sunday evening, at his bedside.

It was a short charter. She had written it on one sheet. The Motion Shop: under Works; its head, Tess Kerrow; its hands and their wages; its budget; its key to the gallery; its rule, which was Tess's rule, that nobody went near anything that was alive or might wake or might go off without leave; and its first apprentice, under that, Wren, daughter of Rylan, papers lodged with the Chancellor's office, trade, Motion, underlined. Bram had asked to hear it. He had said he wanted to hear what he was signing, which was the first time anybody in Kaldo had heard Bram say that about anything with Ferra's writing on it.

So she sat on the chair by his bed in the house by the forge, in the lamplight, in her grey wool with her book on her knee, and read it to him, slowly; and Emeric stood in the doorway, because the room was small and Bram was large and there was not room for another chair; and Imani, who had come at six to change the dressing on his head, sat on the other side of the bed with the old dressing in a basin and the new one ready, and worked while Ferra read, and did not look up.

Bram listened to all of it with his eyes shut.

"Good," he said, at the end. "Give it here."

Ferra held the board, and he signed it, right-handed, large and crooked; and then he lay back on the pillow and looked at the ceiling, and Ferra put the charter in her book, and closed the book, and sat looking at her father in the lamplight with an expression Emeric had never seen on her face before. Not the column face. Something younger.

"You knew," she said.

Bram did not move.

"About us. Him. Imani." She did not look round at either of them. "You knew. You've known."

Bram turned his head on the pillow, slowly, because of the bandage, and looked at her.

"Yeah," he said. "Do you think I'm stupid?"

Ferra opened her mouth.

"You seemed happy," said Bram. He looked back at the ceiling. "Happiest I've seen you since you were small. So I let you get on with it." He was quiet for a moment. "I was young once, you know. I remember how your mother and I were..."

He did not finish it. He did not need to. He lay there on his pillow with his eyes on the ceiling and the faintest possible expression at the corner of his mouth, of a man remembering something a very long way off and very pleasant, and said nothing else at all.

Imani laughed.

She did not mean to. It came out of her all at once, over the basin, and she put the back of her wrist to her mouth to stop it and could not; and in the doorway Emeric, who had been trying very hard to be a doorframe, was laughing too, helplessly, silently, with his shoulder against the jamb and his eyes shut.

Ferra went red.

Not pink. Red. It went up from the collar of her grey wool, buttoned to the throat, in one long tide, over her chin and her cheeks and her freckles and up into her hair until there was no telling where the one stopped and the other began; and she sat on the chair by her father's bed with her book clutched on her knee and her mouth still a little open, scarlet to the roots, and looked at Bram as though he had grown a second head.

"Father," she said.

"What," said Bram, to the ceiling.

"You can't..." She stopped. "You don't..." She stopped again. "That's..."

"I'm in bed," said Bram. "I've a cracked head. I'll say what I like." He shut his eyes. "Imani. Is that dressing done?"

"Nearly," said Imani, in a voice that was not steady at all.

"Then do it," said Bram. "And then all of you go away. I want to sleep." He opened one eye, briefly, and looked at Emeric in the doorway, and shut it again. "And mind the fourth step going down. It wants mending."

Imani finished the dressing. She did it well, and quickly, with her lips pressed together and her shoulders shaking. Then she picked up her basin and went out past Emeric in the doorway without looking at him, because if she had looked at him they would both have been done for; and Emeric stood aside to let Ferra by, and Ferra went past him with her book against her chest and her face still the colour of the forge, and did not look at him either.

They went down the stair, the three of them, in the dark, one after another. Ferra first. On the fourth step she stopped.

It creaked. It always had.

She stood on it for a moment in the dark, quite still. Then she went on down, and out into the yard, and across the yard to the forge's cold wall, and put her forehead against it, and stood there; and Imani, at the bottom of the stair, sat down on the last step with her basin in her lap and laughed until she cried, very quietly, so as not to wake him.

Emeric went across the yard to Ferra.

"Don't," she said, into the wall, before he could say anything.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were." She did not lift her head. "You were going to say something kind." She was quiet. "How long?"

"How long what?"

"How long has he known." She turned her head on the brick and looked at him sideways in the dark, one grey-green eye, still scarlet round it. "You know. You knew he knew. I saw your face. In the door."

Emeric thought about the fourth step, and the casings, and a big man not looking up from a sleeve, two years ago, saying four words.

"A while," he said.

Ferra looked at him for a long moment.

"I'll find out," she said. "I'll find out exactly. I'll find the day."

"I know you will."

"And then I'll put it in a column," said Ferra; and took her forehead off the forge wall, and put her book under her arm, and walked off across the yard toward the keep with her back very straight; and at the yard's gate, without turning round, put out her hand behind her, and he took it, and they went up the hill together in the dark like that, with Imani coming on behind them, a long way behind, still laughing, with her basin.
