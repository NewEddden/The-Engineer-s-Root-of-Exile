Chapter 254 - Dead Air

Wes counted the rails because nobody had asked him to.

Sixty lengths, thirty feet each, in four tiers down the middle of hull two's hold, chained every six feet; and they did not talk, the way Ned said barrels of nails talked. They lay in the dark under the hatch grating and were heavy, and that was all they did. He had counted them at the dock and again over the river. At a quarter to twelve, with the wood coming, he knelt at the grating and counted them a third time, and Ned, at the helm beside Garrick with his arms folded, watched him do it and said nothing at all.

There were four of them aboard, which was new.

Garrick had the helm. Wes and Cade had the racks. And Ned stood at Garrick's shoulder with no lever under his hand, because the Hod was in the secret dock with a new bottom going in; and because a fortnight ago he had written a line in her crew book in his own slow capitals, NEVER FEWER THAN FOUR ABOARD, and a man did not write that and then watch another man's ship go up with three. Garrick had copied the line into hull two's book under Ned's, and signed it.

The house was warm, since April, with the iron skin behind its planking and the pump breathing the air in at Imani's mark. The head tank stood at the stone-keeper's station amidships, a glass body the size of a cask in an iron jacket with a slit to see the blue through, and a horn that went out through the iron into the gap where the stones were. Beside it, on its shelf, the latch: the leather bellows the size of a loaf, full and tight, and the catch it held shut, and the spring under the catch, cocked.

Watch the latch, Garrick had told him. If anything goes, watch the latch. It does the thinking. You just watch it do it.

And at the helm, under Garrick's left hand, the newest thing of all: a stone the size of a walnut in a brass cup on the helm post, cut to stay warm as long as the lift bank had more than its reserve in it and to go cold when it hadn't. Garrick kept his palm on it like a hand on a dog in a strange yard. Everybody called it the watchdog.

The card had been sealed until the dock with Saban's thumb in the wax, which was Saban's rule this summer, and Garrick had read it to the three of them and nobody else: the lamp at Harrow Bridge at midnight, the turn over it, a bearing across Lisle's wood, low, three hundred feet, because she was heavy; down at the cut field past the wood's north edge, to wait for the ganger's carts; the rails out, and home before light.

The lamp had been where the card said. A single lantern on a pole at the south end of Harrow Bridge, on the stone footing where the toll-house had stood before it came down, steady and yellow over the black river. Garrick had brought her round over it slowly, the way a loaded barge comes round a bend, and laid her on the bearing, and the wood had come up under them black and soft and close, the beech tops like a dark sea you could have stepped down into off the rail.

Wes stood up from the grating.

"Sixty," he said.

"It was sixty at ten," said Cade, without looking up.

"Leave him," said Ned.

They were over the middle of the wood when Ned turned his head.

He turned it sharply, the way a man turns when somebody he did not know was there says his name behind him; and Garrick's hand jumped on the watchdog; and Wes, looking at both of them, felt nothing for the space of a breath, and then felt it: a cold that went over his face and through him and out the other side, quick, like the door of an ice house opened in the next street. It did not move anything. The lamp flame by the door did not lean.

The lamp flame went out.

All the stone lamps went out together, the lamps in the house and the hold lamp under the grating and the small green one over the gauge, and the dark came down so fast that Wes saw them on in his eyes after they had gone. All but one. Over the helm, in its wire cage, the battery bulb Bram had put in every hull for exactly this burned on by itself, yellow and mean and no bigger than a candle, and gave Garrick's hands and the gauge faces back to him and left the rest of the house black. Under the dark a sound he had long since stopped hearing stopped: the pump. The silence it left was louder than the hum had been.

Then the floor went.

Not much, at first. A softness under his boots, and his stomach coming up, and his hand going out by itself for the grating's edge and finding it.

Watch the latch.

He could not see the latch. He could not see his own hand. But he knew where it was, three steps aft and one to the right, and in the dark, holding the grating, he listened for it.

He heard the bellows sigh.

"One," said Wes, out loud. He did not know he was going to.

The leather went on sighing, a long breath going out of it with nothing to put it back.

"Two."

Somebody said something short in a lighterman's voice. The floor went on going.

"Three."

The catch went with a clack like a bolt shot back, and the spring thumped, and something glassy broke, no louder than a cup on a stone floor; and then, on the far side of the iron, all along the ship's middle, a hiss, long and fierce and getting louder, like a kettle boiling over on the other side of a wall.

Over their heads, on the deck amidships, a bang, and canvas, a great long ripping run of it going up away from them in the dark; and a jerk through the whole ship that took Wes's knees out from under him and put him on the floor on his hands.

And she hung.

She hung under the drogue, level, the right way up, falling still but falling flat, the way a plate goes down through deep water; and Wes on his hands on the boards did not know how far there was to go, and thought of the beeches, and thought, quite clearly and for no reason, of his mother's kitchen.

Then the stones woke.

He felt them through his palms, through the iron under the boards: a thrum coming up out of the ship's middle, out of the gap where the hiss had gone, the way you feel a cart on the road through your feet before you hear it. Garrick said, quite quietly, "There," and the softness went out of the floor.

The lamps came on one at a time, as if each had been asleep and was not sure yet what it had been woken for. Cade on his knees by the racks with both hands on the frame. Ned at the helm post with one fist round it, his face grey in the green light and perfectly calm. Garrick at the levers with his teeth shut, flying her.

The pump coughed, and caught.

"I've got her," said Garrick.

"Then put her down," said Ned.

"Where?"

"Anywhere that isn't trees."

Garrick put her down in a hayfield at the wood's east edge, a mile short of the cut field, in the light of a moon three days off full, with the hay down in long silver rows and two stacks half built at the far hedge. He had no time to be gentle and was not. She came down on her slant with the drogue still out above her like a sail on a dead calm, and hit, the whole ship ringing once like a dropped pan, and the port leg folded with a crack and she lay over on it a hand's breadth, and stopped.

The drogue came down out of the sky behind her and settled over the deck in long slack folds.

Wes was sitting with his back against the grating and did not remember sitting down, and his hands in his lap were shaking and would not stop when he told them to. He put them under his arms.

Cade laughed, once, with his forehead against the rack frame, the way a man laughs when a horse he was sure would kick him walks past instead.

Ned let go of the post. He went three steps aft and one to the right and put his eye to the slit in the head tank's jacket, a long time. Then he looked at the latch: the bellows flat as a burst bladder, the catch open, the spike standing through the hole where the glass had been.

"Well," said Ned. "It works."

"Once," said Garrick, from the floor, where he had sat down.

"Once is what it's for." Ned touched the spare disc clipped in its brass ring to the tank's leg. "And she's empty. Every drop out of the horn. She'll not go up again with that tank dry. Not on my line."

"Not on mine," said Garrick.

"Then we sit." Ned looked at Wes against the grating, a breath longer than at the others, and said nothing about the hands. "Somebody's got to tell them up the hill we're sat."

"The railhead's two miles on," said Garrick. "There's a sounder in the ganger's hut."

"I'll go," said Wes, and got up, and kept his hands under his arms.

"Cade goes with you," said Ned. "With a lamp. You walk, and you don't go near that wood." He looked out of the thick panes at the black line of the beeches across the hay. "There's something in that wood that put the lights out."



The sounder in the gate tower began at twenty to two, and the clerk who read it took it down twice before he would believe it, and then ran.

Emeric was not asleep. He was at his table with the shutter open on a warm night and the falls dam's sluice gates drawn out under his hands, which he had told Ferra he would not look at until August; and he heard the clerk's note on the keep stair, running, and was at the door before the boy reached the landing.

It was in the clerk's round school hand, every letter separate, the way the railhead had clicked it: HULL TWO DOWN HAYFIELD EAST EDGE LISLES WOOD MIDNIGHT ALL FOUR WELL STONES DIED OVER WOOD LATCH FIRED SHE CAME OUT LEG BROKE TANK EMPTY WILL NOT LIFT. And after a gap, as if somebody at the sender's elbow had said something: WES.

He read it twice. The second time he read only the four words in the middle.

Stones died over wood.

"Ward," he said, not loudly, and the door at the end of the passage was already opening. "And Imani. And Tobe at the dock, with two cartridges from the niche and a spare disc. The K Discovery. Now."

They were crossing the inner ward with a lamp when Saban came in under the gate arch from the town. He had not been sent for. He was in his day coat, buttoned, with his hat on, as if he had been sitting up dressed waiting for exactly this, and he stopped in front of Emeric in the lamplight.

"Which hour," said Saban. "The railhead's on the same wire as my shop. I heard where. I didn't hear when."

"Midnight. Over the wood. On the bearing from the bridge lamp."

Saban's face did nothing. His note, under Emeric's sense, went down flat and still, as a man's does when he turns over the card he was hoping not to see.

"Then I'll want to come," he said. "I know which man had midnight. I'd sooner tell you after I've seen the field, in case I'm wrong." He was quiet. "I'm not often wrong about a man. I'd like to be, tonight."



He told it at three, over the river, standing on the top rung of the ladder into the K Discovery's cockpit with his hat in his hands, while Emeric flew.

"Three men on the last stretch need to know when a ship comes," said Saban. "The ganger, for his carts. Aster's yardman at Wayford, for the tally. And the watchman, for his lamp. I gave each a different field and a different hour."

"Like the crossing inn," said Emeric.

Saban looked up. "Like the crossing inn. Only the crossing inn was news, and I could wait a month for it to come back to me. You can't wait a month on a ship. So I salted the ship."

"I did it to grain buyers for twenty years. Each got a figure a little off, his own, and when the fair's price moved early I knew whose figure it moved on." He looked at the floor. "The watchman had to have the hour and the line; you can't give a lie to the man holding the lantern. So the ganger had the right field and the wrong hour, half past one, which did no harm. Aster's yardman had the twelfth milestone at two. And the watchman had the truth: midnight, his lamp, the line across the wood." He was quiet. "If anybody had been waiting at the cut field at half past one, I'd know the ganger. At the milestone at two, the yardman. It was over the wood. At midnight."

Nobody said anything. The ship went north through the dark with her irises shut and her floor warm.

"I hired him," said Saban. "Myself. Three years ago. I put him on that bridge."

"You don't know yet," said Ward, from the cockpit's other seat.

"No," said Saban. "That's why I wanted to see the field."



Emeric felt the wood before he saw it.

It was a little after five, with the sky going grey in the east, and he had his sense out ahead of the ship the way he flew at night: a farm with its dogs asleep, sheep on a slope like small warm coals, and then Lisle's woods, a great dim spread of beech and oak full of quick small lives and the slow hum of the trees.

And in the middle of it, nothing.

A round hole a quarter of a mile across, as clean at its edge as a coin cut out of paper, where his sense went in and did not come back. It was like putting his hand out in the dark for a wall he knew was there and finding the edge of a cliff.

He had felt it before, from the inside: on the bank above the meadow, the night after the dead field, in the frost, with the world gone out of him and coming back a note at a time. A dead zone stopped what went out of a man. His sense was what went out of him.

"There," he said to Ward, and nodded down through the glass at a place in the trees that looked, from a mile up, like every other place in the trees. He took her round it, wide, to the east side, to the hay.

Hull two lay in the hayfield where the telegram had said, over on her broken leg with the drogue across her like a sheet over furniture in a shut house. Garrick and Ned sat against a half-built stack. Wes and Cade, back from the railhead at four, were asleep in its lee.

Imani was out of the hatch before the legs had settled, going across the hay toward the four of them at a walk that was very nearly not a walk.

Garrick stood up to tell it, with hay in his hair, plainly, the way he would have told Fletcher, from the bridge lamp to the fold of the leg.

"Nobody's hurt," said Garrick. "She is. Not us. And the rails are in her, all sixty." He looked at the stack, where Imani crouched by Wes with two fingers on his wrist, not waking him. "It was him counted the bellows, in the dark. I'd have thought it was ten. You think everything's ten, in the dark."

"It was three," said Ned, from the stack, without getting up.

Garrick jerked his head at the trees. "There's a ride goes in by the oak with the split crown. Wes and Cade crossed the mouth of it at two, going for the wire, and there were ruts going in off it, fresh, a heavy cart, at that hour of night. Cade said leave it. I'd look there."



They went into the wood at six, the three of them, Ward first and Saban last, by Garrick's ride, in at the oak with the split crown and on under the beeches along the ruts.

Emeric felt the edge come. The wood round him was full: birds waking in the beech tops, a fox off to the left going home, Ward's note ahead of him broad and steady and banked like a fire for the night, Saban's behind, dry and tight. Then, between one step and the next, the birds on his left went out, the way a lamp goes out when a door shuts on its room. Then the fox. Then, ten paces ahead, as he walked on into it, Ward.

He stopped on the ride.

Ward was still there; he could see him and hear his boots in last year's mast. But in the place where Ward had been for twenty-five years, in the sense Emeric had kept open every waking hour for so long that he no longer knew he did it, there was nothing. A man made of light and sound and nothing else.

He took the next step, and the rest of the world went out of him at the edges like a tide over a flat shore, until there was nothing in his sense anywhere and he was walking in a beech wood on a July morning like any man.

He had forgotten what that was.

Ward had turned round and was looking at him.

"You're walking like a man in the dark," said Ward.

"I am in the dark. You've gone. I can see you. I can't hear you." He made himself walk on. "It's very rude of you."

Ward came back down the ride and walked beside him, close, on his left, where he had walked when Emeric was seven, and said nothing else about it.



The clearing was an old charcoal burner's hearth, a round of black earth thirty paces across with the bracken grown back waist high, and a woods road coming into it from the north: the road a man with two horses and his plate in a sack had taken two summers ago, an hour after a house shook.

In the middle of the clearing the bracken had been trodden flat in a ring, the way men stand round a fire. A heavy cart had come in by the ruts from the north and gone out lighter and faster, its wheels cutting the grass on the turn.

And in the middle of the ring, on a flat stone somebody had dragged out of the bracken for it, glass.

Grey-white pieces, from a fingernail to a fist, on the stone and round it in the trodden bracken, glinting in the low light.

Emeric crouched and picked up the largest.

It was heavy and cold and dead; and with his sense gone he could not have told dead from spent, and had to read it the way any man would. One face was a crystal's old outside, clouded, with a long crack running into it like a flaw in ice. The other faces were new, bright and sharp-edged, broken that night.

"It burst," he said.

Ward crouched beside him.

"A stone that's drained goes to dust. Grey dust, like flour. You pull the last out of it and it falls through your fingers." He turned the shard over. "This didn't go to dust. It broke, outward, from the inside." He looked at the ring of pieces, lying farther from the stone the smaller they were, and at two places in the trodden bracken on opposite sides of it, nearer than anywhere else, where the black earth was dented by knees. "Two of them, kneeling there. Pushing into it until it wouldn't take any more. And then pushing again."

Saban held out his hand for the shard and turned it in the light the way he turned everything.

"I know this," said Saban. "Cheap stone off the edges of the old workings in the east. Won't hold a charge even, bursts if it's pushed. His Grace of Aldane's quartermasters bought it by the hundred two autumns ago, at a third over the price, and sent it north in closed carts." He gave it back. "I sat at your table and told you I didn't know what anybody wanted bad stone for. I knew at Harrow. I didn't know there was any left over."

Ward was not looking at the shard. He was looking across the clearing at the bracken on the far side of the ring, the untrodden side, and at a place near its edge where it did not stand.

He got up and went across.

The man lay on his back a dozen paces from the stone. Two shallow furrows ran a yard behind his heels and stopped, as if whoever was dragging him had thought about it and let go. A thin man of thirty or so in a plain brown coat, his hands open on his chest; and on his right hand a grey stone in a silver band worn thin at the back, the kind of ring a mage got from a tower and went on wearing after the tower had stopped wanting him.

He was breathing, slowly, with a gap between breaths that made Emeric hold his own. His face was the colour of the shards.

Emeric knelt and reached for him the way he always reached, the sense first, as a hand goes out to a falling cup, and there was nothing to reach with. So he put his palm flat on the man's chest instead, as Imani would have, and felt the slow heart under it, and the cold coming up through the coat.

"He did it," he said. "Him and the other one. A stone that size wants two; one would run dry before it went over. He gave it everything he had and then more, and when it went, he went with it. Not dead. Drained flat." He looked at the furrows. "His friends dragged him a yard and thought about how far it was to the cart, and how heavy he was, and how far it was to the north."

"Every mage Aldane can hire can break a stone," said Ward, very quietly, as if he were reading it back. "By a man who doesn't care what it costs him." He looked at the ring on the man's hand. "I said that in the quarry. I didn't think about the man after."

He got his arms under the man's shoulders and knees and stood up with him as if he weighed nothing.

Saban had not come across. He was standing by the flat stone with his hands in his pockets, looking north along the woods road, toward the Wayford road and the bridge and the lamp on its footing.

"Midnight," he said, to nobody. Then he stooped and picked a small shard off the stone, the size of a coin, and put it in his pocket, and came after them.



The world came back at the edge of the trees, all at once: one step under the beeches and nothing, the next out into the hay and everything, the sheep and the birds and Imani's note across the field like a struck bell, clear and quick and very close to angry. He stood still in the hay until it had all come back.

Imani met them halfway. She put her hand flat on the man's chest, as Emeric had, then two fingers at his throat, then lifted an eyelid with her thumb.

"Drained," she said. "Down by the stack, Ward, in the sun, on his side. He'll want warming slowly, and water when he wakes, and watching, because a man emptied like that sometimes doesn't take a breath when he's meant to." She looked at Emeric for the first time since the ship had landed. "What's he done?"

"Broken a stone under them."

Imani looked across the hay at hull two lying over on her leg, and at Wes asleep in the lee of the stack, and back at the grey man in Ward's arms. Her face did something and then stopped doing it.

"He's my patient," she said. "Till he wakes. After that he's yours."



Tobe reset the latch at eight, on his knees on the house floor: the broken glass picked out of the throat grain by grain, a new disc seated, the spring wound back until the catch took it. Then the tank, from the two cartridges, the blue going into the glass body with a sound like a far-off kettle. The bellows' stone drew from it, and the leather swelled on its shelf and held the catch shut, as if nothing had ever happened.

Wes watched from the hatch. He had woken at seven under Imani's hand and drunk the hot sweet stuff she made him drink, and said nothing to anybody since; and when the bellows was full he let out a breath he seemed to have been holding since midnight.

"Will it do it again?" he said.

"Every time," said Tobe. "As long as somebody fills it after."

"I counted it. Three."

"It's three on the drawing," said Emeric, from the stone-keeper's station. "It was three on the bench when Bram timed it. But nobody had heard it go three in a ship with the lights out and the floor going. You did." He looked at him. "That's worth more to me than the drawing."

Wes looked down at the tank and did not say anything, and some of the grey went out of his face.

Ned came and stood by Emeric at the jacket, and looked into the slit, and spoke low.

"He'll not want to go up again for a while. He'll say he does, and go, and count every breath that bellows takes from the dock to the field, and not sleep after." He did not look at the hatch. "Send him to Tobe. At the lock, for the winter, pouring off the needle on the ground, where a thing can go wrong and nobody falls. He's got the head for it. He counts."

"Ask him," said Emeric. "Don't send him."

Ned thought about that. "In a week," he said. "When he's sore at himself for being glad."



The man in the hay woke at eleven, and looked at the sky, and at Imani with her fingers on his wrist, and at Ward standing over them both; and shut his eyes.

He drank when she put the cup to his mouth. He let her listen to his chest. He answered nothing: not his name, not where from, not who had knelt at the stone with him, not who had paid. When Saban crouched by his head and said pleasantly, as if they had met at a fair, that his friends had left him a yard from the cart, the man's eyes opened, and looked at Saban, and shut.

Emeric stood over him a long time.

He could have had it. The man's note was back under his sense now, thin and flat, a commoner's trickle where a mage's fire had been, and everything the man knew was in there behind a door Emeric had been through twice. The faces. The rooms. The hand that had counted out the coin. One push, and the door in, and the man's whole winter laid out on the floor.

And the man had not said yes.

He had forced a door once, in the Staple's strongroom, and he and the man behind it had bled on the floor together after. He had gone through a door by accident once, into Imani's, onto a pier in October, and she had told him in her dispensary, flat, what she thought of it, and what he owed. He asked, now. He had asked Wren's parents in April before he so much as looked inside their daughter with the thing that only looked.

He was not going to kick in a drained man's skull in a hayfield because the man would not talk to him.

"No," said Imani, very low, without looking up from the man's wrist.

"I wasn't going to."

"I know you weren't. You've been standing there deciding not to for a quarter of an hour. I could see it from the stack." She wrote a number on her card. "Go and do something useful. Garrick wants to know where the leg goes."



The leg could not be mended in a field. Garrick had the pin out of the broken knee by noon, and said it could be strapped, and she could be flown home on two and a half legs and set down gently at the lake, and Fletcher would say what Fletcher would say.

The ganger's carts came across the hay at one. The ganger was a big quiet man with a face like a split log, who had laid every yard of the Wayford line north of the old railhead and met hull two in the dark in a dozen fields, and never once seen her by daylight, nor asked to. He walked round her lying over in the hay, slowly, with his hands behind his back. Then he walked round her again.

"She's a barge," he said to Garrick.

"She's a barge," said Garrick.

"Right," said the ganger, and turned round and shouted for his men.

They had the rails out of her by four, and Wes stood at the hatch with the ganger's tally board and counted every length aloud to sixty and signed the board at the foot. The ganger looked at the signature, and at Wes, and put the board under his arm and went off across the hay without a word, and at the gate turned round and nodded to him once.

At five Emeric felt the hole close.

He was sitting against the stack in the sun, because Imani had told him to, with his sense out over the wood; and the round dead hole in it, clean-edged all day, went soft at its rim. From the outside in, as Armand had said a dead place healed, the living wood leaning into the gap the way water comes back into a footprint in wet sand: the birds at the rim, then the beeches, then, last, a faint dead glimmer of broken stone on a flat stone, which was all there was left to feel.

"It's gone," he said.

"Seventeen hours," said Ned, beside him, who had not needed to ask what. "From the lights to that." He looked at the K Discovery's dark nose over the hedge. "And a ship can't sit in a field seventeen hours with nobody knowing she's down but a lad who walked two miles in the dark to a ganger's hut. You'll want something better than walking, Highness."

"Grace."

"I know what you are," said Ned. "I'm saying you'll want something better than walking."

They flew home that night an hour apart: hull two first on her strapped leg, with Garrick at the levers and Ned at his shoulder; the K Discovery behind her, with the grey man asleep under a rug in the saloon and Ward on the bench opposite, awake.

Saban sat in the cockpit the whole way and said nothing, and turned the shard over in his pocket until it had worn a hole in the lining.



Imani saw hull two's crew at the ward the next day, all four, one after another: their eyes and chests and hands, and their sleep, which she asked about and did not believe. She saw the grey man too, in the old strongroom under the east tower two doors along from Marrow's, and sat with him an hour and wrote down nothing, because he gave her nothing to write.

Then she came up to the counting room at seven, where Emeric was writing the night down in his own hand for Ferra's book, and stood in the door with her bag.

"You," she said. "Bath."

He looked up.

"You've been up since two yesterday morning. You walked into a dead field with nothing in your head for the first time in years and came out the colour of that man, and you've sat at this table since noon. Look at your hand."

He looked at it. It was not shaking, exactly. It was doing something it should not have been.

"The dock quarter's," said Imani. "The council gives me the end room after eight for anybody I send. I'm sending you." She looked at Ferra at the head of the table, who had not looked up. "He'll be back by the bell."

"He'll be back when he's back," said Ferra, and turned a page. "Go on. I've his hand for tonight."



The end room had been the fish store's salting room, and kept a smell under the carbolic that the council said was gone and was not. But it had the best of the twelve baths: a long copper riveted from a boiler's end, big enough for a lighterman to lie down in, with the steam line coming up through the floor to a brass cock at its head.

The woman from the council took Imani's copper at the door and looked at Emeric behind her, and put the copper in her box.

"Bell's at ten," she said.

"I know when the bell is," said Imani.

"I'm saying it," said the woman, and went back to her stool.

Imani filled the copper herself, with her own arm in it to the elbow to judge it, and turned round and looked at him standing on the stone floor with his coat still on.

"Do I have to do that as well?"

He took his coat off.

The water was nearly too hot. He lay back in it to the chin with his locs going heavy and dark round his shoulders and his knees up, and shut his eyes, and felt the heat go into him from the outside in, the way the wood had come back at its edges. His hands first.

He heard her shoes. Then nothing, long enough that he opened his eyes.

She was standing at the copper's head in her shift, with her hair coming down out of its tie.

"Move up," said Imani.

"There's no up."

"Then make some."

He sat forward, and she stepped in behind him with a hand on his shoulder for the slick floor, and sat down in the water at his back with her knees either side of him, and the water went over the rim on both sides at once and across the stone floor in a sheet toward the drain; and she said a word about it that she would not have said in her ward, and pulled him back against her.

The water closed over them both to the shoulders. Her shift went dark and weightless in it. She put her arms round him across his chest and her chin on his shoulder in the wet of his hair, and they stayed like that a long time.

He could feel her heart against his back, with his body and his sense together, and he lay and listened to it, because for a whole morning in a beech wood he had not been able to hear anybody's, and he had not known until he walked out into the hay how much he had wanted to.

"You were frightened," said Imani, into his ear. "In the wood."

"I was."

"There was nothing in it. Ward said. A stone in bits and a man asleep."

"That was it. Nothing in it. I couldn't hear Ward, ten paces off. I could see him. I couldn't hear anybody." He was quiet. "You're all I've got, in there. The noise of you. I've had it so long I'd forgotten it, like the pump. And it stopped."

Her arms tightened a little across his chest. Then she took her right hand away, and found his left wrist under the water, and turned it, and laid two fingertips on the inside of it, where the pulse was, as she had laid them at the back of the crowd on the day this bath house opened.

"There," she said. "Now you've got mine and I've got yours."

He laughed. It came out of him without his meaning it, a short surprised sound in the wet stone room, and he felt it go into her through his back and felt her smile against his ear.

"Seventy," she said. "Eight. Nine. You're coming down. Lie still."

He did not lie still.

He turned in her arms in the copper, which the copper had not been built for, and the water went over the rim again in a wave; and she said "Emeric," in the voice she used on nurses, and then nothing else, because he had his mouth on hers.

She tasted of the hot sweet stuff she had made Wes drink, and of salt. Her shift under his hands was nothing in the water, and very nearly not there at all. She pushed her wet hair back with both hands and held his face in them and kissed him back, hard and slow, with the copper's rim at her back and her knees come up round him; and then laughed into his mouth, because his knee had gone out from under him on the slick floor and nearly put them both under.

"You'll drown us."

"You said lie still."

"I said it to your pulse." Her fingers were still on his wrist, somehow, under all of it. "It's not listening either."

"What is it now?"

She kissed him instead of answering, and he felt her fingers on his wrist press, and lose the place, and look for it, and give it up; and she made a small sound against his mouth that he knew, that was about nothing at all except that she had lost count.

The water went cool round them and neither of them noticed. Outside the window a lighter went down the dark river with somebody on her deck singing badly. Long enough that she shivered, once, all down her body against his, and he spread his palms on her back under the wet shift and let a little of the heat he kept inside himself go out into her, and felt the shiver stop and her breath go out long and slow against his neck.

"Cheat," said Imani.

"Hybrid."

"Cheat." She did not move away. She moved closer, and her hands went down his back under the water, unhurried, as if the bath house were hers and so was the night; and he thought, with what was left of his thinking, that perhaps they were.

The bell went.

It went in the passage outside the door, the council woman's handbell swung at arm's length by a woman who meant every stroke, ten of them; and then her voice through the door, perfectly level.

"Ten. Closing."

Imani put her forehead down on his shoulder and laughed, without any sound, until the water shook.

"She did say," said Emeric. "At the door. She was very clear."

"She was." Imani lifted her head. Her face was flushed and her hair was down, all of it, in long wet curls on her shoulders and his, and her eyes were doing a thing he did not see often. "I told her I knew."

"Ten," said the woman through the door. "I'm saying it again."

Getting out was not dignified. His shirt had been where the wave went, and he put it on wet. Imani tied her hair up anyhow and put her dress on over the damp shift and buttoned it wrong, and saw it at the door, and did it again right with her back to him while he watched.

The council woman looked at the two of them coming out, and at the thin bright line of water going out under the end room's door into the passage, and said nothing at all. Then, as they reached the outer door, she said, to the box at her feet: "There's a mop."

Imani went back for it.

He waited on the quay in his wet shirt with the river going by. She came out after a quarter of an hour, and the bolt went across behind her, and she stood in front of him and put her two hands together, flat, one on the other, under her cheek, and tilted her head onto them, and looked at him over them with her eyebrows up.

"Sleep," he said. "Till you say."

She told Ferra at breakfast, across the toast, before he was down: the end room, the copper, the bell, the mop. Ferra listened with her cup in both hands and did not interrupt once. Then she put the cup down.

"Ten strokes?"

"Ten. And then she said it again."

Ferra opened the small book she kept in her pocket that was not Kaldo's, and wrote something, and turned it round on the cloth so that Imani could read it; and Imani read it, and put her face in her hands.

When Emeric came in at eight, wrecked from eleven hours of sleep that had not felt like enough, the book was shut, and neither of them would tell him what was in it.



Saban came back from Harrow Bridge on the fourteenth, a Wednesday, on the evening work train from the railhead, and walked up the hill to the counting room without going home first, with a stone jar under his arm.

He had gone up alone on the Monday, which Ward had not liked, and sat two nights in the watchman's hut on the footing with the watchman and his wife and their two children, and talked.

He put the jar on Ferra's table. Ferra moved her inkwell away from it.

"It was him," said Saban, and sat down without being asked, which he never did at Ferra's table, and told it the way he priced a cargo: what it was, what it had cost, what it was worth now.

The watchman had been Saban's for three years: a carter with a leg off below the knee, found at a posting inn with a wife and a baby and nothing, put on the bridge to count the north road for a Silver a month and, when the line came, to light the lamp for two. Some of the best of what Saban knew about the north road had come out of that hut.

"And in May," said Saban, "a man came down the woods road and drank his beer and asked what the lamp was for. He didn't know, which was true; I'd never told him. The man said he dealt in timber and wanted to know when Kaldo's barges came over, so as to be elsewhere with his carts. And paid him for the hours. A Silver an hour."

"And he told you," said Emeric.

"He told me. In his weekly sheet. A timber dealer from the north asking after barges, paying for news." Saban almost smiled. "He sold me the man and the man me. Both ways. He thought a timber dealer was nothing, and a Silver an hour was a Silver an hour, and he had a second baby coming." He was quiet. "I read that sheet in May. I priced it at nothing too."

He took the lid off the jar and tipped it out on the blotter: silver, mostly, a few gold, all of it the wrong shape. Every piece had been clipped, the rim taken off with shears a thin crescent at a time, so that the king's face on each was cut off at the forehead.

"Northern," said Saban. "They clip it past the border and pass it back as full weight. It's what you pay a man in when you don't want to have paid him." He picked up a coin. "In the spring Keller's clerk sent me what the magistrate took off the man with the knife at the feast. A blade, a comb, a clean shirt, and sewn in his hem, eleven silver pieces, clipped. Like these." He put it down. "I took one down to Marrow this morning and put it on his table by the beer. He weighed it on his finger and said: they still pay short."

Ferra wrote that down.

"So," said Saban. "The same purse. The knife at the feast, Marrow's men at the Staple, the men at the stone in the wood. The northern hire network, the road calls it, as if it were a guild with a hall and a sign over the door. It isn't. It's a purse in the north: you bring it money and a thing you want done, and it finds the men and pays them short, and nobody sees anybody's face." He looked at the coins. "Who goes to it, I don't know. Aldane did, in the war; those were his stones. But a man who's lost a war doesn't spend his last bad stone on a barge in July. Somebody else brought it out. It's a long way north, and they're careful, and I'm a grocer."

"You're not a grocer," said Ferra.

"I was," said Saban. "I'd like that written down somewhere."



"The crossing inn, and now this," said Emeric. "The trick's got a name."

Saban looked at him.

"I read it, long ago. The same trick with letters, one small thing changed in each copy." He turned a clipped coin over with a fingertip. "A canary trap."

"Why a canary?"

"Because the copy sings."

Saban considered this for some time.

"That's a stupid name for it," he said. "A canary sings because it's in a cage. It doesn't tell you anything. In the trade it's salting the well." He looked at Ferra. "Write it as salting."

Ferra wrote it as salting. Under it, small, in brackets, she wrote something else, and did not show either of them.

"What will you do with him?" said Emeric.

It was the question Saban had come up the hill to be asked. His note went still and then settled, the way a man's does when he made up his mind on a long road and only has to say it now.

"Keep him," said Saban. "As I kept the crossing inn. Only not cheap, this time. On the bridge, lighting the lamp, counting the road. At four Silver a month instead of two, from the first of July. Because the timber dealer was paying him a Silver an hour, six hours a month, and I was paying him two, and a man with a leg off and a second baby coming goes to the better price, and that's not his fault. That's my book. I priced him cheap and somebody else priced him dear." He turned the hat. "I'll not do that twice."

"He nearly killed four men."

"He sold six hours of a lamp to a timber dealer. He didn't know what came over the bridge, because I never told him." Saban's voice did not rise. "I've told him now, and their names. Garrick's and Ned's and Cade's and the boy's. He cried. His wife didn't. She'd known for a month. She said if I put him off the bridge they'd starve, and if I gave him to Keller they'd starve faster, and I could please myself which." He picked up his hat. "So he stays. And when the timber dealer comes back for the hours, he'll get them. Wrong ones, from me."

"And if he sells both ways again?"

"Then I'll know that too. I'll be salting every card he ever gets." Saban stood up. "I can't trust him. I can trust what he does for four Silver with his wife watching him. That's as far as anybody trusts anybody, on a road."

Emeric did not like it. He wanted the man off the bridge and the hut empty and one of Ward's on the footing with the lamp who had never sat with a timber dealer in his life. He opened his mouth to say so.

"It's his man," said Ferra, without looking up.

He shut it.

"He hired him. He priced him. He sat in the hut. You didn't." She turned a page. "Put a garrison soldier on that bridge tomorrow, and the timber dealer comes down in a month and sees him, and knows, and goes and finds another bridge." She dipped her pen. "Saban's man. Saban's decision. I've written it as his, with the date, so that if it goes wrong it's in his column and not yours, and you can't tell yourself afterward you'd have done it better."

Saban looked at her from the door. Something went across his face that on another man might have been a smile.

"Thank you, Chancellor."

"It's not a kindness. It's a column. Take your coins off my blotter, they're leaving marks."

He swept the clipped silver back into the jar with the side of his hand. Then he stood at the end of the table with the jar under his arm and did not go.

"One more thing, since I'm here. It's been in my pocket since May." He put a folded paper on the table. "The gorse bend. The pay cart, three years ago. Wat's second stone, the one somebody picked up on the way past."

Emeric went still.

"I've had a man asking after it in the north for three years, the way you ask after a lost horse. In May he found where it went. Sold three times, dearer each time, the last to a priory north of the capital, off the road, in the hills. Thirty lay brothers and a prior; they keep bees." Saban touched the paper. "My man took a room at the inn across the valley from the priory wall and stayed three nights. Every morning before light he heard them in the yard. All thirty, in ranks, drilling." He looked at Emeric. "His words were: like a garrison."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't know what it means," said Saban. "A house of men who keep bees, drilling at dawn, who bought a stone a boy could throw fire out of with his thumb. I'm filing it. That's all. I'd like it filed where I can find it."

Ferra unfolded the paper and read it and put it in the cabinet behind her, in a folder of its own, and wrote a word on the folder's spine that Emeric could not see from where he sat, and locked the cabinet. Saban went.

Emeric sat a long time looking at the blotter. There were marks on it where the silver had lain, faint and grey: small crescents, a great many of them.

He was thinking of a carter's son of seventeen standing in the capital road with his hand out and the gorse burning on both sides of him; who had cut practice blanks until the autumn quarter day, as Armand had told him to, and had cut the straightest lines in the etching room every day since, and in three years had never once asked to carry a stone past the gate.

Down at the works, on the floor of the erecting shop, there was a small tank engine half built on its own rails, with no name on it yet, that the crews had already decided to call Wat. Not for that one. For the other: the grey-headed hedger from the Ridings, thirty-one, who had died in the end room of Imani's ward the week of Harrow Bridge, laying a hedge on his blanket with his hands, with Emeric on the stool by the bed. Kaldo had had two Wats in four years, and one of them was still cutting stone.



Ferra found the two lines the next afternoon.

She had been reading Grayrock's war-year ledger with Ilsa, because Ilsa's ten clerks were to rule Grayrock's books over in Kaldo's columns from the first of August, and Ferra would not let a clerk rule over a book she had not read herself, every line, with the woman who had kept it beside her. At a little after four her finger stopped on a page near the end of October, and stayed.

Emeric heard it at seven, in his own room, with the door shut and the ledger under her arm. She opened it on his table and turned it round and put her finger on the place: two lines in Ilsa's square black hand at the foot of a page of carts and powder and war pay, with no figure against either of them in the money column.

28 Oct. Received of His Grace of Aldane, by his man, a purse, as a debt of honour to the widow of Grayrock. Not opened.

2 Nov. Returned, the same, by the same man. Not opened.

He read them twice.

"After the bridge," said Ferra, "Aldane wrote to all the widows. It was in the gazette; he made sure it was. He wrote to Bryda as well. His old partner's widow, from the mine they hid together. With a letter, and a purse." She sat down on the end of his bed. "Bryda burned the letter in the kitchen grate before Ilsa could see what was in it, and sent the purse back the same week, unopened, and told Ilsa to leave both out of the book."

"And Ilsa didn't."

"Ilsa said a book that lies is worse than an angry mother." Something went across Ferra's face that was very nearly approval and very nearly pain. "So she wrote it in, with no business against it, so that it would be in the book and not in the money. Bryda found it when she read the quarter over. They didn't speak for a week, in the same house." She looked at her hands. "Ilsa said she'd been waiting two years for somebody to ask. She said she was glad it was me and not you."

He sat looking at the two lines.

In February he had sat at the foot of Ferra's table with Bryda Renner's hand flat on it, and told her that he could not trust them. That nobody had moved, even in Aldane's war: all three of them sitting tight in their holdings while Kaldo fought, waiting to see which way it went. He had said it to her face, and thought it was the honest thing to say, and it had been.

"I said nobody moved," he said.

"You did."

"She moved."

"Into her own kitchen grate," said Ferra. "You didn't see it because it was the kind of moving that leaves no mark on your side of the table. Nothing came to Kaldo. Nothing went to Aldane. A purse went up a road and came back down it, and you'd have had to be standing in her kitchen to know." She was watching him. "You were right to be wary of her. You'd killed her husband, and Aldane had been his partner. You'd have been a fool not to be."

"And wrong about her."

"Both. In the same breath. It happens."

He put his hand flat on the ledger. "I'll write to her tonight. She ought to know that I know, and that I..."

"No," said Ferra.

He looked at her.

"She didn't tell you because telling you meant saying out loud that Aldane thought she'd turn. That he looked at her after the bridge and thought: a grudge and a dead husband and a duke's boot on her neck, she'll take my money. She'd sooner burn his letter than have anybody know he'd thought it." Ferra did not look away. "Write to her, and you make her say it. To you, in her own hand, because she'll answer; she always answers. You'll make her thank you for knowing a thing she burned so you wouldn't."

He did not say anything.

"Saban found a man who sold you this week for a Silver an hour, who didn't know what he was selling," said Ferra, more quietly. "I found a woman who wouldn't sell you for a purse she never opened, who knew exactly what she was refusing. Same week. Same book, if you like." She closed the ledger. "Let her keep it. She's earned keeping it."

"Then what do I do?"

"Nothing. I've done it." She took out the small book that was not Kaldo's and opened it near the back and showed him: a page with a heading in her square capitals that he had not seen before, and only a few names under it, and newest, in fresh ink, B. RENNER, the twenty-eighth of October of year eight, and one word. Kept.

"What is that page?"

"Mine," said Ferra, and put it away. "You'll see it when there's a table to see it at."



She priced Vantic's figure the same night, at his table, because he asked her to and because she had already done it in her head on the stair.

He had owed it since March, when Vantic had asked what the thing saved. It had fired over Lisle's wood a few minutes past midnight on the ninth of July, with the lights out and a boy counting the bellows aloud in the dark.

"Hull two," said Ferra, writing down the margin of his paper. "Built again from the keel. Her stones, and the season's charge in her racks. Sixty lengths of rail, and the week of the Wayford line we'd have lost, which this summer is a great deal." She drew a line. "Against it: two cartridges, refilled in a week from the Band. One glass disc. Tobe's hour with a spring." She did the sum. "Three hundred and sixty to one. Near enough."

"And the four men."

"I don't price men. You know I don't, and I'll not start for a House." She put the pen down. "Tell her the Chancellor gives her the ship and the rails and the week, and declines the rest. She'll understand. She'd decline it herself, if it were her book."

He wrote it short, because Vantic's had been short.

You asked what it saves. On the night of the eighth of July, over a wood I will not name: one hull, sixty lengths of rail, a week of a railway, and four men, whom the Chancellor declines to price. Against it, two cartridges, one glass disc, and a spring wound back by hand. Without the men she makes it three hundred and sixty to one. With them she will not make it at all. You were right; I did not know the figure until it fired. I would still not have priced it first.

He signed it. Then he sat with the pen over the page a breath longer, and added a line under the signature, after a space, the way she had:

You may tell the House this one. E.

Ferra read it over his shoulder with her chin very nearly on it, as she read everything he wrote now.

"Good," she said, and went to bed.



He did not go to bed. He sat at the window with the shutter open on the warm dark and the letter folded on the sill for the morning post, and let his sense go out over Kaldo, as he did every night, and had done for so long that he had stopped knowing he did it.

He knew it tonight.

The ward on the hill, its night nurse walking her round. Hull two on the slip with her leg off, and Garrick, who could not sleep either, sitting on the slip's edge with his feet over the black water. The dock quarter, a hundred notes asleep along the quay, and over the bath house the woman with the bell, asleep at last. Two notes under the east tower, two doors apart, neither of them asleep. Ferra's across the passage, going down into sleep as she always did, all at once, like a book shut. And Imani's, down the hill in her low house by the ward: very faint, a long way down, steady.

He sat at the window and listened to all of them until it began to be light.
