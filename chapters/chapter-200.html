Chapter 200 - Sethe's Boxes

He told her at the railhead.

He had not meant to. He had meant to wait for the long room, and the table, and everybody at it, and say it once, properly, in order. But the train came in at the railhead at dusk on the twenty-second of April with the empty cart on its flat wagon and the horse in its box complaining, and Ferra was there, which she never was; on the platform of sleepers by Paskin's hut, in her coat, with a lantern, because Aster's line on the wire the night they left Wayford had said only: The Highness will want the strongroom key. A.

She did not ask how the road had been. She held the lantern up to his face and looked at it.

"Aster's never sent a line on the wire that wasn't about barns," she said. "What's in the strongroom?"

He told her. Standing on the sleepers in the dusk with the engine ticking as it cooled and Paskin's men unhitching the flat wagon, and Ward behind him with the horses, and the field round them going blue; the chain, and the slip, and the shed with the iron beam, and Ottoline's twice, and Aster at the posting house fire with his hat in his hands. The Staple. Five houses and a charter. And four boxes of Sethe's private papers, under the crown's seal, that had come south with the grant.

Ferra stood with the lantern and did not move while he told it.

"The ninth of October," she said, when he had finished. "Four years ago. The grey men's cart. Four boxes, iron-bound, the crown's seal on every hasp, and a paper with them from the judgment room releasing them to the holder of Wayford as the attainted baron's goods." Her voice was perfectly level. "I signed for them. I had them carried to the strongroom. I wrote them in the book: Sethe, private, four boxes, to be read." She looked at the lantern. "To be read."

"There was the flux at Wayford that winter. And the hospital, and the forge, and the debt, and..."

"I know what there was," said Ferra. "I was there for all of it." She turned toward the horses. "Give me the key."



She read all night.

He knew she had, because he came down to the counting room at six, after Rowena's stair, with his thighs shaking, and the lamp was still lit on her desk and turned down low, and she was still at it.

The four boxes stood open on the floor round her chair like the walls of a little fort. Iron-bound, oak, with their hasps hanging and the crown's red seal broken on every one of them; and the papers out of them were on every flat surface in the room, in piles, in an order he could not read and she could. Letters, most of it. Bundles of letters tied with tape, by year, and by sender. Three ledgers bound in calf, thin, with no titles. A tin of seals. A packet of receipts on a skewer. And her book, open, in the middle of the desk, with her pen beside it.

She looked up when he came in. Her face was grey with the night and perfectly composed.

"Don't," she said.

He had not said anything.

"You were going to." She turned the book round on the desk so that it faced him, and put her finger beside a line near the foot of the page. "Read that first."

It was in her own hand, small and exact, under the night's date.

Sethe's four boxes. Received 9 October, year 4. Read 22-23 April, year 8. Not read for three years, six months and thirteen days. The delay is mine. F.

She had initialed it. The ink was dry.

"Ferra," he said. "It isn't..."

"It is." She did not raise her voice. "It's the chancellor's to read what comes into the strongroom. Not yours. Not Hale's. Mine. I wrote it in the book myself, to be read, in my own hand, on the day it came, and I never read it, and I never put it on a list, and I never once in three and a half years opened that book to that page and looked at it again." She did not look away from him. "If I'd read it that winter, you'd have known about the Staple that winter. You'd not have paid at the chain. You'd have known about..." She stopped, and put her hand flat on the nearest pile. "About the rest of this. Four years ago."

"We were burying people that winter. You were."

"Yes. And I had time to read the mill's quarter every week that winter, and Wayford's barns, and the price of lamp oil in Grayrock." Her hand did not move on the pile. "I didn't read these because they were the attainted man's private letters and I thought they'd be sordid and dull and not ours. I decided that. Without reading them." She looked at the line in the book. "That's what's written there."

He stood across the desk from her and looked at the line, at the F under it, at her hand on the pile of letters.

He wanted to take the pen and strike it through. He wanted it with his whole body; to reach across and draw a line through the delay is mine and write under it, in his own hand, no it isn't, E., and close the book. It was the easiest thing in the room to want. He had her book in front of him and her pen beside it and she was grey with a night's reading and she was wrong, he was nearly certain she was wrong; nobody alive could have done more that winter than she had done.

And she had not written it so that he would strike it through. She had written it because it was true in the way her book was true, every line, to the Copper; and if he struck it, he would be telling her that her book was hers to keep true about everybody in four baronies except herself.

He did not touch the pen.

"Then it's written," he said.

Ferra looked at him for a long breath across the desk. Something in her face that had been held very tight since the railhead let go, a little, at the corners of her mouth; not much. She took her hand off the pile.

"Sit down," she said. "It's worse than the Staple."



She took him through it the way she took him through a quarter, from the beginning, in order, with her finger on each pile as she came to it.

The letters were from the five. All of them; a bundle for each house, tied with tape, by year, going back to before Sethe was baron, when his father held Wayford and the five had written to his father. Dinner letters, most of them. Thanks for a dinner, the promise of a dinner, the weather. Placit's hand was square and old-fashioned, Hollin's sloped and showy, Dray's barely a hand at all, Vennor's very small, Strade's very neat and never more than six lines. They wrote to Sethe the way men write to a man they have been paying for a long time and expect to go on paying.

"The tenth," said Ferra.

She opened the first of the calf ledgers. It was thin, and old, and in Sethe's hand all the way through, one line a quarter: a date, and a sum, and W.

"The Staple pays the crown a tenth," she said. "On the quarter days, at the crown's account at Wayford. Aster says so; it's in the crown's own book at Wayford, I've seen the totals." She put her finger on the first line in Sethe's ledger. "And here's a second tenth. Every quarter. Paid to the baron of Wayford, in coin, by the five, privately, to Sethe's own hand." She turned the pages, slowly. "For twenty-two years. For nothing. No charter, no service, no line in Wayford's books, which is why Aster never saw it. It's a tenth for keeping his hands off. A baron of Wayford can't touch the Staple, but he can make its life a misery in a hundred small ways a charter doesn't mention: the road, the carts, the inns, the watch. They paid him not to." She closed the ledger. "That's the tenth owed. Sethe called it that, on the cover, inside." She showed him: a single line on the endpaper in Sethe's hand. The tenth owed.

"And the second ledger?"

"Riders." She opened it. Names, and dates, and small sums, and places. "Men he paid to carry things. Or to not carry them. You'll know some of the names." She ran her finger down to one, and stopped on it, and did not need to say it. The man at the Wayford crossing. The one who had held Kaldo's crown post back in his back room for four years, every bag of it, until a chancery form asking for his returns had frightened him into sending all nine sacks north at once. His name was there, eight times, each with a sum beside it and a date; and beside the first of them, in the margin, in Sethe's hand: the matter.

Emeric sat very still.

"We knew that," he said. "We proved that. At the trial. Sethe paid him."

"Sethe paid him," said Ferra. "With whose money?"

She reached across to the bundle of Placit's letters and untied it, and took out one from the middle, which she had marked with a slip of paper, and laid it open in front of him on the ledger.

It was dated in the autumn of his first year at Kaldo. A month after the first sheet of paper had come off Tess's pear-wood blocks in the inn's old cheese press: forty sheets of an alphabet, by noon, for a school that had opened that week. Placit's square old hand. Six lines more than Strade ever wrote, and he read them twice.

A prince with a press is a prince who will one day ask what a staple is. My colleagues and I would think it a kindness if the crown heard as little from Kaldo as possible, for as long as possible, until he has drunk himself back to where he belongs. We will each bear a fifth of whatever the matter costs you. It is cheaper than a lawsuit, and quieter.

He read it a third time.

The cheese press. The forty sheets of the alphabet. He remembered Tess lifting the first one off the block in the inn yard in her scarred hand, and holding it up; and somebody in a stone house at the top of a hill four hundred miles away hearing about it, in a dinner letter, and writing to Sethe within the month.

"Each a fifth," said Ferra. "Here." She turned the riders' ledger round. Beside every payment to the crossing man, in the margin, very small: five marks, a fifth each. P. H. D. V. S. "They paid for every bag he held. Four years of it. Every report you sent the crown, every petition, every letter to your father that never came." She was quiet. "They didn't want Wayford, or Kaldo, or you. They wanted you to stay a drunk in a ruin a long way from the capital, and never become a man with a press and a road who might come north one day and look at the chain."

"And I did."

"And you did," said Ferra. "A week ago. And paid it." She took a breath. "There's the second matter."



She had kept it till last. He understood why when she opened the third ledger, because it was not a ledger at all; it was half a dozen loose sheets folded inside a calf cover, in Sethe's hand, written later than everything else in the boxes, in the last year before the mail broke, and much less carefully.

The second matter. That was all it said at the top.

A date, in the autumn. A sum, received, from the five, a fifth each again, the marks in the margin. And then a list of payments going out, against it: horses, a boat, a hire for men by the week, a sum to a man in the capital for a name; and at the foot, the largest single figure in all four boxes, by itself, on its own line, underlined twice:

Forty Gold. To M.

Ferra did not say anything. She did not have to. She had put the date at the top of the page beside the date on a page of her own book, which lay open beside it, from that winter: the December Rylan's son was taken, and Emeric ended in the hold of a boat in the dark.

"A boat," said Ferra. "Hired by the week. In the December of year three. A sum to a man in the capital for a name." She put her finger on the line. "Forty Gold. That's what they paid for you. Less the horses."

He sat at the desk with the sheet in front of him and was, for a little while, not in the counting room at all.

He was in the hold. On his back on wet boards, in the dark, with the smell of bilge and tar and the boat moving under him on the river and his hands tied, listening; and two men on the deck above him talking, low, the way men talk over a job. A quick voice and a slow one. The quick one had said a name, once. He could hear it, the shape of it, on the deck boards over his face. Something says it reads better than a ransom.

He did not let himself finish it. It went past him in the dark like a lamp on a far bank, and he let it go, and was in the counting room again, with Ferra watching him, and the M on its own line, underlined twice.

"Who's M?" said Ferra.

"I don't know."

She looked at him a breath longer than the answer needed. Then she wrote it in the book, under the night's date, under her own line about the delay: M. Not known. And did not underline it.



The long table sat at ten. Everybody; Ferra had sent to the works for Bram and to the ward for Imani and to the gate tower for Ward, and to Armand's room for Armand, who came down in his chair with the blanket over his knees and Osric pushing, and to Vantic's rooms for Vantic, who was in Kaldo for the quarter and came down in her grey with her own book under her arm. Saban. Hale with the minutes. Rylan, at the end, because Ward had sent for him without being asked.

Ferra laid it out. The Staple, and the chain, and the five. The tenth owed. The matter, with the five marks in the margin beside every bag the crossing man burned. And the second matter, which she read aloud from Sethe's sheet in a voice so level that Hale stopped writing in the middle of it and had to be told to go on.

Rylan, at the end of the table, sat with his hands on the wood and did not move at all while she read it. When she came to the forty Gold he looked down at his own hands, and at the knuckles of the right one, and Emeric watched him remember the boat burning on the river in the dark, and his son in his own arms and Emeric on his back, and Emeric's face in the firelight telling him to come back in seven days.

Nobody spoke when she finished.

Then Vantic, at Ferra's left, reached across without asking for the Staple's receipt from the junction, which lay on the table where Saban had put it, the printed slip with the bale at its head; and for the sheet Saban had copied at the roadside, in pencil, of every figure on the Staple's posted board. She laid them side by side in front of her and read them, and opened her own book, and wrote down three figures, and added them, and looked at the sum.

She did it the way she did everything at that table, without hurry and without being asked, and nobody interrupted her, because in all her years at that table nobody had ever known Vantic to write a figure that turned out to be wrong.

"The posted price," she said at last. "What they pay at the junction for a ream, and what the capital pays them for it, less the carriage, less the crown's tenth, less Sethe's tenth while he lived." She put her pen down. "It's a good trade. A comfortable one. Five old houses could live on it very well, in the country, with a carriage each and a daughter married well every ten years." She looked at the sum. "It isn't enough."

"Enough for what?" said Saban.

"For what they spend." Vantic closed her book. She did not say how she knew what the five houses spent. She did not say which of them kept accounts with the House, or how many, or what she had seen in them over the years on quarter days in a dark green carriage with no arms on its door. She did not say anything at all, for a breath; and Emeric, watching her, saw her decide exactly how much she would say, and say that much and no more, which was the most Vantic thing he had ever seen her do.

"The numbers don't balance," said Vantic. "Unless something isn't on the books."

She put her hands on her closed book, and that was all she said about it, then or afterward.



Ward spoke first, after.

"They paid to keep your letters from your father," he said, flatly, to the middle of the table. "And they paid to have you taken off a street in your own town. Through Sethe. Who is dead." He looked at Emeric. "And they are not."

"No."

"The mail is closed," said Hale, carefully, from his minutes. "The crown closed it. Sethe was tried for it and hanged for it, and the barony was forfeit for it, and the crown's judgment is entered. A matter closed by judgment..."

"Was closed against Sethe," said Ferra, without looking up. "Not against the men who paid him. They aren't named in the judgment. Nobody knew to name them." She turned a page. "They are now. In his hand, with their own marks."

"Under a crown charter," said Saban. "All five. Houses older than the barony. With friends at court who dine with them every quarter, and a crown that takes a tenth of their trade and likes it." He spread his hands on the table. "You can't take a letter of Sethe's to the capital and say, these five men paid a hanged man to burn my post. They'll say Sethe wrote what he liked in his own ledgers, and he isn't here to ask, and they've never heard of any matter. And the crown's tenth will go on arriving on the quarter days, and the crown will want it to."

"Then I'll not take a letter," said Emeric.

They looked at him.

He was looking at the sheet. At the second matter. At the forty Gold on its own line, underlined twice, and the five marks in the margin, a fifth each. He had read it four times now and it said the same thing each time, as a ledger does; and the thing it said was that five men he had never met, in a stone house at the top of a hill, had sat over a dinner in the autumn of his first year and decided between the soup and the meat that he was not to be allowed to become anything, and had paid, in fifths, for every month of it. And then, when he became something anyway, had paid again, in a larger sum, to have him taken out of his own town in the dark.

He was not angry. He had expected to be. He sat at the long table and waited for it to come, the way it had come in the hold, and in the yard at Emberfell, hot and hard and quick; and it did not come. Something else did, slower, colder, that he did not have a name for and did not much like.

"It's a debt," he said. "That's all it is. They incurred it. Sethe kept the book." He put his hand flat on Sethe's sheet. "It's owed."

Ferra looked up from her book.

She looked at him down the length of the table, in front of everybody, with her pen in her hand and the night's greyness still in her face; and he could not read what was in her own. Then she looked down, and wrote, under M. Not known, one more line, and blotted it, and turned the book round on the table so that he could see it.

The matter and the second matter. Paid for by five. Owed. F.

"Then we'll collect it," said Ferra. "Properly." She turned the book back. "In order. And I'll keep the book."
