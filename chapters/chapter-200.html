Chapter 200 - The Lift

The engine came west on a Tuesday, on a flat wagon at the back of the morning train, with a tarpaulin over it and a works man sitting on the tarpaulin eating bread.

Colm had been at the siding since first light, waiting for it, which he would have denied. He had the kiln firing its fourth load a mile down the leg and nine men at the pit and three at the drying racks and no business at the siding at all, and he had come up anyway, and stood on the cinder platform with his arms folded, and watched the train come in off the flat out of the east with its smoke laid back along the wagons.

Everything had come that way. That was the thing he had not got used to, in three months of it. The boiler plate, in the second week, already cut and rolled and punched at the works so that it only wanted riveting. The pump barrels, two of them, bored true on the works' mill. The pipe he had not made himself yet, while the kiln was building. The brick for the kiln. Lime. Iron for the tower's bands. The first drill, off the pattern of the old spreader at Kaldo, with no stone in it anywhere. Every piece of it had come the same road, in the same closed wagons, in a morning, and not one cart had gone over the Left Bridge for any of it. He had been at the foundry with Bram's men the winter night the lane burned, at the water barrels, and watched the carts from Milford jammed nose to tail on the bridge so that the barrels could not get over. He thought about that every time the train came in. He did not say so to anybody.

The works man got down off the tarpaulin and brushed the crumbs off it and pulled it back, and there it was: an engine off the rail works' pattern, the small one, black and new, with its flywheel chocked and its cylinder packed in straw.

"That's her," the works man said. "Bram says you're not to let anybody drive her who hasn't been taught, and you're not to let the man who drives her watch her." He said it like a lesson, which it was. "He said you'd know."

"Aye," said Colm. "I know."

The engine house stood on the leg under the first bench, where the cut came nearest the bench's foot. They had built it in the month before, Bram's two masons and six Milford men, in stone off the bench's own face, with a slate roof off the train. The intake went out from its riverward wall on piles driven into the leg's bed, a timber box on legs with a grating on its mouth, standing in water to the depth of a man's thigh, and the pump would sit on its bed inside the wall with its barrel down to the intake and its rising main going up the bench's face beside the path.

At the top of the path, on the bench's high corner, stood the tower. Colm had built that himself, and he was prouder of it than of anything he had built since the pipe. A stone base, square, up to a man's shoulder. On it a timber tank, banded in iron, the staves pitched inside, big enough to hold what the bench would want in a day. On the tank's edge, a mast with a wind pump on it, its vane turning over the flats. And at the base, beside the rising main, a hand pump with a beam. It was the tower on the western rise at Kaldo, near enough, that he had helped raise in the third year with a great deal of rope. He had asked the prince, the night of the plan, why the same, and the prince had said: because it worked, and because every man who had ever stood a shift under that one would know how this one went.

From the tower, the main ran down the bench. He had laid that in his own pipe, the kiln's first loads, socket and spigot with the collar packed in tow and clay and driven home with an iron, the joint he had given a prince on his knees in wet clay a long time ago. He had laid the better pipe. The harder fired, with the deeper collar. The one he had made in the winter of the covenant and stood against the kiln wall for a whole spring because a woman with a small book had read a clause and said it was an improvement and not a repair. That had been Vantic. She had been right about the clause. He had not forgiven the clause.

This was his pipe, and it was the better one, and it went down the first bench in a trench to the far end, a thousand yards, and nobody read a clause over it.

Off the main, at every field's head, a channel lined in tile, and at every channel's head a sluice. The water would come down the main and out into the channels and down the furrows, along the ground, the way the old men had watered a field since there had been fields, only with a tower at the top of it. The prince had put sprinkler heads on the seed beds and the kitchen gardens and nowhere else. Colm had asked why not the fields. The prince had said, how many miles of your pipe would it take to put a head over every rod of a bench, and Colm had worked it out on the back of his hand with his thumbnail and said, aye, well.

Farris would not let him turn the water on.

It was the second week of the high summer and everything was ready. The engine was on its bed and piped, the boiler set and fired and proved, the tower full to the brim on the wind pump alone, the main laid and tested, the channels cut and lined, the sluices hung. The first bench lay broken under the sun in long dark strips where the oxen had turned the sod in the spring, and Aske's one paddock stood fenced out on the flats beyond, with his cattle in it, and Aske sitting on the fence most mornings watching them eat, saying nothing to anybody. All that was wanting was the water.

And Farris stood at the low corner of the bench, the south-east corner, where the bench dipped a little toward the leg in a shallow dish nobody would have noticed who had not seen it on the prince's sheet, and said: tile first.

"It's summer," said Colm. "It's dry as a bone."

"Tile first," said Farris.

"There's no water in that corner. There's never been water in that corner. I'll put tile in the bottoms where it's sour, where it's wanted. This isn't wanted. It's waste. It's a month of my kiln and a week of men in a trench for a corner that's never been wet."

"It's never been watered," said Farris.

He did not say it sharply. Farris did not say things sharply. He had a slow flat way of talking, like a man who had spent his life being right about weather and had never once been thanked for it. He stood in the dip at the bench's corner with the prince's sheet rolled in his hand, and looked at the ground, and scuffed it with his boot, and looked at Colm.

"I've seen this," he said. "At Kaldo. The fourth division. The prince built a tower and a main and a post, a good one, better than this, and the main went in the third year and put the whole tank into the fourth division at four in the morning and made a bog of it. That was the second bog. There'd been one before, the first year, from the ditches. Same corner. The low corner of the fourth, where the water always pooled." He unrolled the sheet enough to show the corner of it, and the little dish drawn in the shading. "That's this. The water comes down the furrows and it doesn't stop at the bottom of the field because you tell it to. It goes where the ground goes. And the ground goes here." He rolled the sheet up. "We were watering the ground on the way to the ground, the first year. I said it then. I'm saying it now. Tile first."

Colm looked at the dish. He could see it now that he had been shown it: the way the stubble of last year's grass stood a little greener in the bottom of it, even now, in the high summer, as if something under there had remembered a wet spring.

He did not like being told. He had never liked being told, by Bram or the prince or anybody. He especially did not like being told by a farmer about where water went, when he had spent his life making the things water went through. He stood there for some time with his arms folded not liking it.

"Aye, well," he said. "Tile first."

It took eleven days. His own tile, from the kiln, through the gauge ring on its nail over the bench in the siding shed, every one of them. A trench across the dish at the depth of a man's knee, and another, and a third, running down the fall to a ditch at the bench's edge that went down the face to the leg. The tile laid end to end in the bottom of each trench on a bed of gravel, butted, not joined, with a gap between each so that water could get in, and gravel over the gaps, and turf over the gravel. When it was done the dish looked exactly as it had before, and there was a month of the kiln under it that nobody would ever see again.

Farris walked it when it was done, from end to end, and nodded, and went back to his steading.

They lit the engine on a Thursday, and it was Ennis who stood the gauge.

He was the young one of the three debtors, the one who had looked at the gauge ring on its nail on the first day and asked who made the first one. He was twenty and had owed his lord four years of a bad harvest rolled forward, and he had come to the siding with his hat in his hands, and he had been at the kiln since, and he had been the only one of the thirteen who asked Colm, when the boiler came west on the wagons in plates, what it was going to do if it went wrong. So Colm had sent him to Kaldo on the morning train to stand a week beside the watcher on the rail works engine, a young man called Kester who had seen what a boiler did when it went wrong and did not talk about it. Ennis had come back quiet. He had come back with a sheet in his shirt, copied out in his own bad hand from Kester's copy of the watchers' rule, that he did not show anybody, and Colm did not ask to see.

The driver was a man called Hadley, from the rail works, whom Bram had lent for the summer to teach two Milford men the engine. Hadley was fifty and had driven engines since there had been engines in Kaldo, and he looked at Ennis at the gauge, a debtor boy of twenty who had never seen a boiler until a month ago, and said nothing about it at all, which was the rule. The man who watches is not the man who drives. The driver makes it go. The watcher stops it. Nobody overrides the watcher.

The fire was straw.

Last year's straw, from Paxton's own ricks, off the flats, which had been going to be burned in a heap in the autumn anyway because Milford had never had a use for it that the cattle did not already have. It came down to the engine house on a cart in bales, and a man stood at the firebox door with a long iron fork and fed it in, a bale at a time, into a box built wide in the throat and long in the grate the way the prince had drawn it, because straw burned fast and hot and light and wanted feeding all the time. It went up the stack in a great white smoke that smelled of harvest. The fireman was a Milford man who had pitched straw all his life and said, the first morning, that he had never thought he would be paid a wage to burn it.

By the second hour the boiler was in steam and the needle on Ennis's gauge was where it ought to be, and Ennis stood with his hand on the lever and his eyes on the glass and did not look at anything else, and Hadley eased the engine over and let it take, and the flywheel went round, and the pump began to stroke.

The water came up out of the leg through the intake's grating, and up the barrel, and into the rising main, and went up the face of the bench, and Colm, standing at the foot of the tower with his hand flat on the main, felt it come: a knock in the pipe, and a surge, and then the steady pulse of it under his palm, going up.

It was in the afternoon. There was hardly any wind. Up on the mast the wind pump's vane turned slowly, a quarter round and back, and the tank filled anyway.

At the fourth hour, with the tank full, Farris walked along the main to the first field's head and opened the sluice.

The water went down the channel in the tile, clear, quick, with a little sound like a stream over stones. It went to the field's head and out into the furrows, one after another as the channel filled, and ran down them between the ridges of the broken sod, shining in the sun, dark where it soaked, and went on and on down the long field to the bottom of it. Colm walked beside it. He found he was walking faster than he meant to, and made himself slow down.

At the bottom of the field the furrows ran out, and the water, what was left of it, gathered, and went on across the headland toward the south-east corner and the dish. It did not stop at the bottom of the field because anybody told it to. It went where the ground went.

Colm stood at the lip of the dish and watched it go in.

It spread in the bottom of the dish, a skin of water on the turf, shining. For a while it stood there. And then it was not standing there. It went down. It went into the turf and through the gravel under it and into the gaps between his tile, and away down the tile in the dark under the ground to the ditch at the bench's edge, and out of the ditch's mouth down the face, a thin brown trickle, into the leg. The dish was damp. It was not wet. By dusk it was only darker than the ground round it.

Farris came and stood beside him and looked at it, and did not say anything. That was worse than anything he could have said, and Colm was fairly sure he knew it.

The river dropped in the last week of the summer.

Farris had said it would, as a thing everybody on the lake farms knew, the way they knew frost came in the tenth month. The snow off the northern hills was long gone and the rain had not come, and every stream in four baronies went down a foot and showed its stones. At Kaldo, a man on the Saturday train said, the sawmill wheel was turning so slow in the race that the white lights at the quarry had gone yellow and the night gang had been sent home. Colm heard it and did not think about it. He did not think the leg was a race.

The leg went down in the night.

He found out at dawn, because Ennis came to the kiln for him, running, which Ennis never did. He had been at the boiler gauge all night and the boiler had been sweet all night, he said, and the needle where it should be, and that was his, and he had watched it. But the pump had changed its note in the dark, a long while ago, he did not know how long, he had not been listening to the pump, and when the light came up he had gone to the door and looked at the intake.

Colm went and looked at it.

The intake's mouth stood in a foot of water where it had stood in two and a half. The leg had gone down in the night and left the box standing on its piles with its grating half out of the stream, and the water going past the grating had a skin of air drawn down into it, in a whirl, and the pump was sucking the air. It had been sucking it for hours. It had been running with next to nothing in the barrel, and a pump running dry does to its barrel what a file does. When Hadley stopped the engine and they drew the bucket, the barrel's bore had a groove round it you could put your fingernail in, bright, scored deep, and the leather was in rags.

Hadley swore for some time. Ennis stood by the boiler with his face white and said it was his fault. Colm told him it was not. The rule said the watcher watched the fire and the gauge and stopped the engine, and he had watched the fire and the gauge; nobody had told him to watch the pump, because nobody had thought the pump would want watching, because nobody had thought the leg would go down under it. Colm had not thought so. He had set the intake himself, in the spring, when the leg was full of spring.

"Write it down," he said to Ennis. "That's what it's for. Pump changed its note. Hour you noticed. What you saw at the door. Write it all down."

Ennis wrote it down. It took him a long time. His hand was not much better than Colm's.

The spare barrel was at the works, still on the mill, a day short of bored. Then the train, with the barrel on a flat wagon and a works fitter to set it. Two days.

And the tower did not go dry.

Colm had not thought about that either, not in the first hour, with the barrel's groove under his thumb. He thought about it when he climbed the path at noon, sore and angry and with a new intake half drawn in his head, and stood at the foot of the tower and looked up. The wind pump was turning. It had been turning all night, on a light wind off the flats that had got up after midnight and not gone down. It could not keep up with the engine's pump, not a quarter of it, but the engine's pump was not running, and the bench was not being watered, and the tank was not being drawn down except for the kitchen garden and the troughs to Aske's paddock. The float on its chain stood at a little under half. On the second day, when the wind dropped at dusk, Farris put four men on the hand pump for the night, turn and turn about, and the tank held.

Nobody went thirsty. No beast stood at a dry trough. The fields went a day and a half without their water in the high summer, and did not die of it. When the barrel came back on the Thursday train and the fitter set it and Hadley eased the engine over, the pump stroked, and the water went up the face of the bench, and that was all.

He found out afterward, from a letter of Farris's that had a line in the prince's hand copied at the bottom of it, that there was a rule for that too, written at a table in Kaldo that summer in the middle of a still week. What must not stop goes on what cannot stop. He read it twice. He had not known there was a rule. The wind pump and the hand pump had been on the plan because the prince had put them there, and Colm had built them because they were on the plan, and they had done what the rule said a thing like that did, an hour down the line from the table where it was written, with nobody from that table anywhere near.

"Aye, well," he said, to nobody, and went to see about the intake.

He dug a sump. He dug it in the leg's own bed, below the intake, with four men in the water to the waist and the leg low enough now that they could stand in it: a pit in the bed a yard and a half deep, lined with stone, so that however low the leg went there would be a hole in the bottom of it the water had to fill first. He took the intake box off its piles and set it lower, on new piles, with its mouth down in the sump where the river could not leave it. And he drew, on the back of a tile sheet, for next year, a settling pit on the bank beside the engine house: a stone tank the water would come through before the pump took it, so that whatever the leg carried in the autumn floods would lie on the pit's floor and not go up the barrel.

He sent the drawing to Kaldo on the Saturday wagon, to Bram. It came back the next week with one word on it in Bram's big square hand. Yes.

The first green was turnips.

They had gone in at midsummer, drilled into the broken sod of the first fallow off the new drill with a horse in a collar before it, in rows a man could walk between, which Aske had said, on his fence, was a waste of good ground and the kind of thing a man from a grain barony would do. And in the field beside them, peas, and beans, in rows. Nobody at Milford had ever grown a turnip for a beast. Paxton's own herdsmen had laughed.

Then the last month of summer came, and it was dry. It was the driest the old men could remember, they said, which the old men said every year; but it was dry. The flats went brown. The grass on the drove roads went to straw. On the far range, beyond Aske's fence, the grass stood in pale tufts with the dust between them, and the cattle walked further for less every day, and stood at the wells under the wind pumps in the afternoons with their heads down.

And the first bench was green.

Not all of it. The first bench only, one bench of three, and not all of that; the channels reached as far as the main reached, and past the main's end the bench was the same brown as the flats. But where the water went, the turnips stood in their long rows, green and thick-leaved and heavy, and the peas had climbed their sticks and flowered and podded, and the beans stood waist high, and the whole of it lay on the bench's top in a long bright strip with a hard edge to it, like a green cloth laid on a brown table. You could see the edge from the flats. You could see it from the siding. You could see it, Dunmore said, from the road into the market town, if you knew where to look, and everybody in the market town knew where to look by the second week.

Paxton rode out on the last day of the summer.

Colm saw him from the tower, where he had gone up to read the float. He came along the drove road from the town on a big grey horse, alone, without Dunmore, at a walk, and turned off the road onto the flats, and rode across the brown grass toward the bench, and stopped.

He stopped on the line. There was a line, on the ground, where the water had reached and not gone further: green on one side of it and brown on the other, the turnip rows on his left hand and the dead grass of the flats on his right, and a hand's breadth between them. Paxton sat his horse on it.

He sat there a long time. Colm, on the tower, watched him sit there, and did not go down, because it was not a thing a man went down to. Paxton did not look at the tower. He looked at the green, and then at the brown, and then at the green again, and the grey horse put its head down and pulled at the dead grass on the brown side, because it was a horse, and Paxton let it.

After a while he turned the horse's head and rode back along the drove road to the town, at a walk, the way he had come. He had not spoken to anybody. He had not got down.

Colm stayed on the tower till dusk.

The float gauge was a thing he had made himself, off the one Bram had put on the second tower at Kaldo in the spring: a float of pitched wood in the tank on a chain, and the chain over a pulley on the tank's lip and down the outside to a board with the marks cut in it, so that a man on the ground could read the water without climbing. But he always climbed. He liked to see it.

He stood on the tank's edge with the wind pump turning slowly over his head, and read the float, and wrote it in the farm book, which lived in a tin box at the tower's foot and went to Ferra every Saturday. The date. The hour. The water at dusk. The engine's hours, which Hadley gave him, and the bales, which the fireman gave him, and the needle, which Ennis gave him. The sluices open, by field. And at the bottom of the day's line, in the column the prince had ruled for remarks that did not go in any other column:

P. rode out. Sat on the line. Went home.

He blotted it with his sleeve, and shut the book, and climbed down.
