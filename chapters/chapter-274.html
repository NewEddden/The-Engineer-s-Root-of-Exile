Chapter 274 - The Second Half

They read the second half the way they had read the first: at the long table in Emeric's study, with the shutters open on the August heat and a jug of Maren's barley water sweating on a tray, and Zale at one end with the book and Ferra at the other with her own, and Emeric between them with the drawings.

It was not the same book. The first half had been Vey's fair copy, written at a table in a port, in a clean brown hand, by a man who expected to be read. This was the thing he had carried down the stair himself: his working notebook, cracked oilcloth over boards, the pages cockled with damp and gone the colour of weak tea at their edges, and the hand inside it smaller and faster and worse as it went on, until in the last third it was a man writing by a lamp that was running out, sitting on stone, in a hall nobody had walked in for a thousand years, with nobody to read it but whoever came down after him.

Zale turned the leaves as if they might come apart in his fingers. Some of them did. Ferra had Ottoline bring up two sheets of glass from the bulb works, and they read the worst of the pages under glass, flattened, like pressed flowers.

"Numbers first," said Ferra, on the first morning, exactly as she had said it in the winter; and nobody argued with her, because in the winter she had been right.

So they counted. Vey had done what he had done on the island's wall: copied the carving on the hall's walls down in rows, sign by sign, with his own small notes running under them. The signs were the same thirty-four. The dot marks were the same, and Ferra had those already, to twenty-eight, and found that in the hall they went higher; she had them to forty by the Tuesday and to a hundred by the Wednesday, because the carvers had a mark for ten and a mark for a hundred that the island wall had never needed, and once she saw the mark for a hundred she saw it everywhere, and sat back in her chair and said, "Oh. They're counting people."

Emeric looked up from the drawings.

"Here." She turned her book round to him. "And here, and here. A sign, and a number after it. The same eleven signs, over and over, each with a number, in the same order every time. Eleven of something, counted, the way a parish book counts a parish." She ran her finger down the column. "Eleven peoples. Or eleven towns. And then the list again, further along, and the numbers have gone up. And again. Vey says the lists are carved one under another down the length of the wall, with a sign between them he thinks is a year."

"A census," said Emeric.

"If you like." She didn't look up. "A count kept every so often by somebody who wanted to know how many of everybody there were."

Zale had stopped turning leaves. "There's a lot of those," he said. "In here. He copied all of them. He says he didn't know why, he just thought he ought to." He found a page near the back, under glass, where the hand was very small. "He says the wall goes on past where the picture stops. Into the next hall. And it's only lists there. No pictures. He says it's very plain carving, plainer than the rest, as if somebody was in a hurry."

Ferra held out her hand for the page, and Zale gave it to her, glass and all.

She read it the way she read a ledger she didn't trust: once fast, all the way down, and then again slowly with her finger, saying nothing. Emeric watched her face. He had watched her read a great many ledgers. He knew the moment she found the thing that was wrong in one; she went very still, and her pen stopped turning.

Her pen stopped turning.

"They go down," she said.

Nobody said anything.

"The numbers. In the plain hall. Every list is smaller than the one before. All eleven. Not one of them goes up, not once, not in any year." She turned back a leaf and forward again. "Some of them go down slowly. Some of them go down by half between one list and the next. And then the sign's gone. The sign for that people, or that town, whichever it is. It doesn't get a nought. It just isn't there any more in the next list, as if it had never been." She put her finger on the last list Vey had copied. It had two signs in it. "Two left. Small numbers. And then the carving stops." She looked at Zale. "Does he say if the wall stops? Or if he stopped copying?"

Zale found the line. He read it to himself first, and Emeric watched his mouth move.

"The wall stops," he said. "Half a row in. He says the last row isn't finished. He says the stone's been cut ready for the next sign, the mark's scratched on to show where it would go, and it was never carved." He read on. "And then he says..." He stopped.

"Zale," said Ferra.

"He says: I have read every book in every library I was ever let into, at home and on this coast, and every book anybody would sell me, and every book anybody would let me read and not sell me, and none of this is in any of them. Not the peoples, not the city, not the winged ones. Not a word, not a picture, not a children's story." Zale's voice had gone flat, the way a man reads aloud when he doesn't want the words to touch him on the way past. "Somebody has taken this out of the world. I do not think it was the people who carved it."

He shut the notebook on his finger, carefully, and sat with it in his lap.

The barley water sweated on its tray. Outside, down in the inner ward, a cart went over the cobbles, and somebody laughed, and a dog barked twice at it and gave up.

Ferra wrote something in her book. Then she sat with her pen over the page and didn't write anything else.



The drawings were in the last third, where the hand was worst.

Vey had drawn what he had seen on the walls, as the carvers had cut it, in brown ink with a fine nib, with no names. He had not known any names. There weren't any to know. He had drawn them carefully, the way a man draws a plant he has never seen before for a botanist at home, from the front and the side, with the hands done separately at the edge of the page: the short broad people at their forges and their tunnel mouths, with their beards and their great hands, and beside them the figures from the doors, twice their breadth; the tall slender people among their trees, long-faced, long-handed, and the trees braided and tiered and hung with pods; and others, page after page of others. A people with something about their faces he had drawn three times and crossed out three times, and written beside it: I can't get it. The light's going. A people very small, drawn life-size, the height of his thumb. A people half in water, with the lower half of them drawn and rubbed out, and drawn again, and rubbed out, and left as a question.

One page in the middle of the others he nearly turned past, because it had no people on it: two trees, side by side, smaller than the rest and drawn with more care, their canopies apart and their roots under them knotted into each other like the fingers of two hands; the one on the left hung with pods, and the one on the right with none, only small cupped flowers along its lowest vines. And between them, very small, a tall figure with its arm out and its hand held flat over one of the flowers, palm down, not touching it. Vey had written beside it: Not a harvest. Nothing in the hand. I don't know what he's doing. Blessing it. Emeric looked at the hand for a long time. Zale, at the end of the desk, was looking at the wings on the page after, and didn't see; and Ferra, who saw, said nothing in front of Zale, and turned her own book back a dozen pages to a column headed not drunk, and laid her pen in it, and left it there.

He had tried to draw the one from the middle of the hall, too. There were four attempts on two pages, none of them finished: the robe, the pressed hands, the ruff at the throat done in fine strokes like feathers, and every time the face left blank, an oval with nothing in it. Beside the last: Easier from memory than from looking. I couldn't stand in front of it long. I don't know why. And under the band along the foot of the wall, which he had copied in a long strip across the bottom of three pages, the rows of beds and the tall ones bending over them: They're planting the peoples. That's what it says, I'm nearly sure. The old priests at home say the first man was dug out of a riverbank. Everybody tells it some way.

"A creation story," said Ferra, reading over his arm. "Everybody has one." She went back to her columns.

And on the last page but one, alone, across both leaves, the wings.

He had drawn one whole. Long, ridged, the neck and the tail and the great sail of the wing, with a man drawn small under it for the size, as a surveyor puts a man under a tower; and hanging from its claws, carved to the life and drawn to the life, a long pod, its shell plates done one by one.

Under it, in the smallest hand in the book: They carried the seed. That's what they're for, on the wall. Every one of them that's carrying anything is carrying a pod. I think they planted the groves. I think the trees could not walk and these were their legs.

And under that, last: Where did they all go?

Emeric sat with the page in front of him and looked at it.

He did not say the word. He had not said it in the hall under the Isle, and he did not say it now, in his own study in the August light with the barley water on its tray; but it was in his head, the word and every picture that went with it, from every book he had read on the other side of everything, every film, every cheap painted box of toy soldiers on a shelf in a toy shop in a mall, every map at the front of every fat paperback with a dragon on its cover. And the short broad ones at their forges. And the tall ones in their trees with their long faces. He knew all of them. He had known all of them since he was six. He had never once in his life, on that side, met anybody who thought they were real.

He looked up and Ferra was watching him.

She was not watching the drawing. She was watching him; and he knew from her face that she had been watching him for a while, since before he looked up, since he had turned the page and seen the wings; and that she had seen whatever his face had done.

She didn't say anything. She looked down at her book again, and wrote something in it, and turned the page.



Zale went back to the inn that evening with the notebook under his arm, because Vey was his; and Emeric went down to the castle's yard in the last of the light and found Ward, and asked him, and Ward said yes before he had finished asking.

Rylan said yes too, the next morning, in the base's yard, and then said, "What's it like?"

"Like being asked a question," said Emeric, "and remembering the answer, while somebody stands behind you and watches you remember it. You'll feel me there. You can stop me any time."

Rylan thought about it. "And you'll see the cave."

"Only the hall. If you think about the hall, that's where I'll go. It follows what you follow."

"Then I'd better not think about anything else," said Rylan, who had a wife and a son of seventeen and a daughter of fifteen and a great deal else to think about; and he sat down on the mounting block in the yard with his hands on his knees and shut his eyes, and Emeric put two fingers on the back of his wrist, and went in.

It was the hall. Rylan's hall: lower and darker than his own, because Rylan had held his lamp lower, and narrower, because Rylan had walked down the middle of it watching the doors and not the walls. The wall came in pieces, where Rylan had looked at it, very sharp in some places and gone to fog in others; the wings very sharp, all of them, because a soldier looks up; the short broad people sharp at their forges, because one of them had a hammer like Rylan's father's; the tall people in their grove a grey blur, because Rylan had not cared for them. Emeric walked it once, slowly, the way you walk a house you have been lent, touching nothing, and came out.

Rylan opened his eyes. "You didn't look at the trees much."

"Neither did you."

"No." Rylan rubbed his face. "I was watching that door at the end. The whole time." He stood up. "It was like you said. Like somebody behind you." He went to see to his horse, and then came back. "The short ones. One of them had my father's hammer."

"I saw."

"I'd forgotten I thought that." Rylan went off to his horse again, and did not come back.



The six went down that night, to the old wine store under the hall, to the long table and the chairs and the dark, with Ferra's number pinned on the plaster by the door where everybody had to pass it; and went under; and in the room, at the long table that was the same table and was not, Emeric built them the wall.

He had thought about how. The room had its rules, and they were stricter than people liked: he could not show them a thing, only what somebody remembered of it, and he could not make anybody remember more than they had seen. So he did not show them his own hall. He laid the three halls over one another, his and Ward's and Rylan's, along the length of the room's far wall, as a draughtsman lays three tracings over one another on a lit window; and where the three agreed, the carving stood up sharp out of the dark, and where only one of them had seen a thing it came faint, and where none of them had looked there was nothing, only a long grey blank like weather.

It was enough. It was more than enough. The wings across the top of it, very sharp, three soldiers' eyes on them. The trees, faint, in Emeric's memory only, with their pods. The tall people faint among them. The short broad people at their forges and their tunnels, sharp, with the door figures standing beside them twice their breadth. And at the far end, where Emeric had stood with his lamp up longest, the tree alone, very large, its canopy filling the wall; and the shape of a man sitting at its foot with his hands in his lap, which was not carving, and which Emeric had not meant to bring, and let fade.

And in the middle of the wall, sharper than anything else in the room, because all three of them had stopped in front of it: the tall one with its hands pressed together and its eyes shut. Nobody walked toward it. Saban, who had not been under the Isle, looked at it once and then looked at the table and kept his eyes on the table, and said to nobody, "Well. That's a horrible thing," in the light voice he kept for things that were not light at all.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Armand got up from his chair and walked down the room to the wall, slowly, with his hands behind his back, the way he walked down a gallery in the tower; and stood under the wings, looking up.

Imani went to the short broad people. She stood in front of one of them a long time, a smith at a forge, and put her hand up near its forearm, not touching. "The tendons," she said. "Look at the tendons. That's somebody who worked with their hands for two hundred years." She looked back at the table. "Whoever cut this had stood next to them."

Bram had not got up. Bram sat at the table with his big arms folded and looked down the room at the forge, and the smith at it. Emeric watched him look.

The smith in his own memory had Bram's face.

He had known it would. He had known it in the hall under the Isle, with his lamp up: the broad head, the heavy brow, the beard, the set of the shoulders, the way the figure held its weight back on its heels at the anvil, which was the way Bram stood at his own anvil and had stood every day for the eleven years Emeric had known him. And he had told himself in the hall that it was the lamp and the hour and his own tiredness, and that if it was anything else it was nothing a man could trust, because recall rewrites and he had been looking at Bram's face across a table for eleven years; and he had come here tonight half hoping the other two would give the carving some other face, and they had not given it any face at all. Ward had seen the hammer. Rylan had seen the hammer. Neither of them had looked at the smith's face. So the face on the wall was only his; faint, as the rule made it, and Bram's.

He did not say so. He could not trust it, and he did not say things he could not trust.

Bram got up at last and walked down the room to the forge.

He did not look at the face. He looked at the smith's hands, and at what was in them; and he stood there a long time with his own hands hanging at his sides, and then he put one hand up, slowly, and closed it in the air round the tool in the smith's left hand as if he could take it.

"That's a swage," he said.

"Is it?" said Saban, from the table.

"A top swage. For drawing a round. You hold it on the work and somebody strikes it." Bram turned his hand over in the air. "But the haft's wrong. Look at it. It's bent. Not bent by accident. It's set off, so you can hold it at the work and keep your hand out of the way of the blow." He was quiet. "I've seen a picture of a swage like that once. In a book in Grayrock my old master had, older than he was. I never saw one held." He let his hand drop. "Nobody's held a swage like that in my lifetime. Nobody in Grayrock. Nobody I ever heard of."

"Could you make one?" said Emeric.

Bram looked at the tool a while longer.

"I could make one," he said. "I couldn't use it. Look at his wrist." He went back to the table and sat down. "You'd need his wrist."



The questions came after, at the table, in the dark of the room, the way they always came; one person at a time, while the wall stood faint at the far end and none of them looked at it directly.

"How did we win," said Saban.

He said it to the table, flatly, as a man prices a cargo he does not want. "That's the first question, isn't it. That's what Vey's lists are. They're the score." He turned his cup on the table that was not a table. "People who built that hall, and that tree, and those walking things at the doors. And us. People like us, with our bits of stone and our lamps. And we won. How?"

"Did we," said Armand, from under the wings. "Win."

"The numbers say somebody did."

"The numbers say somebody lost." Armand did not turn round. "That isn't always the same thing as a war. A war has two sides. A war is what you call it afterward when you were the side that's still writing." He was quiet. "The tower's records go back further than anybody's in the kingdom. Before that there is a gap. Every apprentice in the tower is taught where the gap is, in his first year, the way you're taught where the stair's rotten. Nobody is taught why it's there. I was taught not to wonder." He looked up at the wings. "I was a very good student."

"They face out," said Ward.

Everybody looked at him.

Ward was sitting at the end of the table where he always sat, by the door, with his back to the wall. He had been looking at the door figures on the carving, the ones that stood beside the smiths, the whole time.

"In the city. The ones at the doors. I stood next to six of them." He did not move his hands from the table. "A statue at a door faces in. To greet you. Or it faces the street, for show. Those face out. Away from the doors, toward the stair, toward the water, toward whoever might come up out of it. I've stood at doors all my life. I know what facing out is." He looked down the room at them. "They weren't decorations. They were the guard. And something got past them anyway."

Ferra said, very quietly, "Where are they."

She had her book open in front of her in the room, as she always did, though there was no book there; she had built it out of habit, the first time she ever went under, and never stopped. She was not looking at it.

"That city held thousands. I did the sum in the boat." She did it again now, aloud, flatly: the streets, the houses cut in the rock, the rooms in each, the width of the stair, the size of the hall; the lists. "Thousands. In one city. And that was one sign out of eleven on one wall." She looked down the table. "There are no graves. There were no bones in that whole city but Vey's. Not one. A city that size, swept, and no dead in it." She stopped. "So either they took their dead with them when they went, all of them, every one, which nobody does who is leaving in a hurry. Or somebody took them away after. Or they're somewhere else." She looked at Emeric. "Linus has been walking the four baronies for three years with a rod and a level, and he has said to me, more than once, that there's old fill under half the towns he's walked. Under the old part of Grayrock. Under Milford's church. Under us." She let that sit. "I am asking what everybody at this table is thinking, so that somebody has asked it. Are we living on top of them?"

Nobody answered her. Nobody could.

Imani came back from the wall and sat down. "What did it," she said. "If it was a killing. A sickness doesn't go down that evenly." She had seen Ferra's page. "Sickness goes through a people like fire through a field, and burns out, and they come back. Those numbers don't come back. Something did that to all eleven, for years, on purpose." She stopped. "Somebody did."

The room was quiet.

"Nobody at this table can answer any of that," said Emeric at last. "Not tonight. Maybe not for years." He looked at Ferra. "Write them down. All of them. Exactly as they were asked."

She had already started.



They came up, and the six of them sat on in the wine store in the lamplight with their heads aching, as they always did after a long one, round the real table, with Bram's drawings of the eleven pieces of the figure spread out on it because nobody had wanted to go to bed; and Ward, looking at the drawing of the chest plate and the shaped stones at the shoulder, said the thing nobody else had wanted to say.

"If they were still here," said Ward, "they'd be a threat."

He said it the way he said everything, plainly, to the table. "That's what I'd write. If I was writing to a king about this. People who built those, and could build them again, and had a reason to hate us." He touched the drawing. "That's a threat. That's the first thing anybody in a uniform would say."

"That's the Church's thing," said Imani.

"I know whose thing it is." Ward did not look up. "I don't like hearing it in my own mouth either. It's still the first thing anybody would say."

"Who's to say we couldn't live with them," said Emeric.

Ward looked at him.

"Who's to say," said Emeric again. "They lived together. Look at that wall. Eleven kinds of people on one wall, and the trees, and the winged ones. Somebody carved that, and it wasn't a war picture." He looked round the table. "If they came back. If any of them are anywhere. Who says it has to be a threat? Who says it can't be a neighbour?"

"Nobody says it can't," said Saban. "You're right. Nobody says it can't." He turned the drawing of the shoulder toward himself and looked at the stone muscles in it, the rods and the plate stacks, drawn very neatly by Bram. "But I've sat across a table from a great many people, Your Grace, and I'll tell you what everybody does first. Before they're friendly or not friendly. They weigh what's on the other side of the table." He put the drawing down. "And the weaker side doesn't make demands. It makes requests. It asks. And sometimes it's told yes."

"There's a saying," said Emeric.

Ferra, beside him, did not look up from her book; but her pen stopped.

"Two men walk into a room with nothing in their hands," said Emeric, "and one of them makes a demand. That's a negotiation." He looked at the drawing of the figure. "The same man walks into the same room with a weapon, and makes the same demand. That's an order."

Saban looked at him for a while. "Whose saying is that?"

"Nobody's. It's just a saying." Emeric put his hand flat on the drawings. "You're right, Saban. You're both right. So Kaldo had better be the side that has both. The thing in its hand, and the wish not to use it. So that if anybody ever comes back, from anywhere, we can sit down across from them as equals." He looked at Ward. "And never need to give the order."

Ward was quiet for a long moment.

"That's a lot of building," he said at last.

"Yes," said Emeric.

Bram unfolded his arms and pulled the drawing of the hip joint toward him, the one with the coils in it, and looked at it with his head on one side, the way he looked at anything he meant to make.

"Then we'd better get on," said Bram.



He went to see Doran on the Friday, about something else, and asked him on the way out.

Doran was in the vestry with his death book open, ruling the next year's pages, which he did every August whether the year wanted them or not; and he looked up and said, "Tales?"

"Old ones. For children. Anything about things under the ground."

"Oh, those." Doran put his ruler down and smiled. "My grandmother had a dozen. Every grandmother in the kingdom has a dozen." He thought. "The proud ones under the hill. That's the one you mean. Once there were proud ones who walked on the land and wouldn't kneel, and they built themselves high houses and shut the Light out of them, and the Light came down and put them under the hills, and they're there still, and if you're bad they'll come up through the cellar floor." He laughed. "I was frightened of my grandmother's cellar till I was twelve. There's a hundred versions. In some of them they're giants. In some they're little. In the one from the west coast they had wings, and the Light cut them off, and that's why there's thunder." He picked up the ruler again. "Why?"

"A book," said Emeric.

"A book." Doran looked at him over the ruler, kindly, and with a great deal more in his face than he let into his voice. "Then I hope it's a better book than my grandmother's."

"So do I," said Emeric, and went out into the heat.



The pieces of the figure lay in the proving gallery under the hill, where Ward had put them: the hill's lower working, pumped dry, shored, lamps on wire with oil lamps beside them, and at its far end a long bay cut back into the rock where Fennick kept nothing any more, and where now eleven shapes lay in a row on trestles under canvas, like men laid out after a fall. Ward had put a lock on the bay. There were two keys. Ward had one, and Emeric the other.

He went down there at night, alone, at the end of August, when the town was asleep.

He took the canvas off the chest. It lay on its back on its trestle, with its back plate unpinned and set aside, as he had left it in July: the dry clouded tank, the small glass port, the rails coming in to the seat from where the shoulders had been and going out again to where the hips had been; and in the seat, grey and unlit, the size of a long small egg, the stone.

He sat down on the trestle's end and put his hand flat on the chest, beside the seat, where the shell was thinnest; and opened his sense into it, slowly, the way you open a door into a room where somebody is asleep.

It was not asleep. It was not anything. A stone that has been bonded to somebody, and kept, has a note; this had no note at all. It had been made so long ago that whoever's note had been in it was gone out of it, worn away to nothing, as a voice wears off a wax cylinder. But it was a control stone. He knew control stones. And inside it, the way the inside of every control stone he had ever held was written, it was written. Thick with it. Written all through, in lines and knots and long runs, finer than anything he had ever cut in his own band in all his years of cutting, finer than anything he knew how to cut; a whole library in a stone the size of an egg, packed so close that his sense went in among it like a hand among threads and could not find an end to pull.

He could read the marks. Any delver could read another delver's marks; you could not copy them, but you could see them, as you can see the strokes of a letter in a language you don't know. He could see every stroke. He could not read one word.

It was code. He knew that much the moment he was inside it, the way you know a program is a program when you open it in a language you never learned: there were the loops, there, the same shape going round and coming back; there were the places where one thing waited on another; there were the long flat runs that could only be lists. It was some kind of coding language, and he did not get it. He did not get any of it.

He took his hand away and sat on the trestle with his hand in his lap.

Then he put it back, and shut his eyes, and went under, into the room, and from the room down the stair he had built himself in the first year, that went down into the place where he kept what he knew.

He did not go far. He knew where he was going. A book, read at nineteen, on the other side, in a long summer when he had been supposed to be doing something else: a fat book with a stone on its cover, about the men who had read dead scripts nobody living could read, and how. He found the summer, and the room, and the chair by the window, and the book on his knee, and opened it; and it was sharp, every page of it, because he had read it the way he read everything at nineteen, as if it might be on a test. Count the signs. Count how many different ones there are; that tells you whether it's letters or syllables or words. Find the ones that repeat. Find the ones that only ever come at the start, or only at the end. Find a name you already know, in a place you already know it must be, and hang everything else from it. Find a bilingual. Always, always look for the bilingual.

And from the book, sideways, the way the room let you go sideways, to a hall with tiered seats and a man at the bottom of it talking about the same thing, a lecture he had gone to because a girl he liked had been going; and that was fog. He could see the hall. He could see the back of the girl's head. He could hear the man at the bottom talking and could not hear one word he said. Every shortcut that man had known was in that fog, and Emeric had been looking at the back of a girl's head.

He came back up, slowly, the stair, the room, the gallery, and opened his eyes, with the beginning of the headache sitting behind them like a thumb.

So. He had the method and not the shortcuts. It would be slow.

He put his hand back on the stone, and began, as the book said, with the register: the part of any control stone that held the notes of the people it knew. He knew where that sat in his own band. He found where it sat in this one, by its shape, because a list is a list in any language; and it was full. Notes, dozens of them, written into the stone a very long time ago; and every one of them denser than any human note he had ever met in his life. Denser than Armand's. Denser than his own, since the pod. Not one of them was a note like anybody's in Kaldo.

He found that he had been looking, among them, for Bram's.

He sat there with his hand on the stone in the dark and felt his face go hot, alone, in a gallery under a hill, at night, with nobody to see. It was not there. Of course it was not there. Nothing in here was anything like anybody he knew.

"Where do you go," said Ferra, "when your eyes stop moving?"



She was in the gallery's mouth, by the last of the wired lamps, with a shawl round her and a lantern in her hand that she had not needed because the wire was on. He had not felt her come. He had been too far down.

"You've been sitting there an hour," she said. "Ward told me where. I said I'd come and get you because it was my turn to be the one who comes and gets you." She came down the gallery to him and stood by the trestle, and looked at the thing on it, and away. "Your eyes were open for a while. Then they stopped moving. Like a man reading who's stopped reading but hasn't looked up." She sat down on the trestle's other end. "Where do you go?"

He thought about not telling her, for as long as it took to know he was going to.

"A memory palace," he said.

"A what?"

"It's a trick. From the other side. You learn things by putting them in rooms, in a house in your head, and when you want them back you walk through the house." He looked at the stone. "In the delve it isn't a trick. The room's built out of memory. So my memory's a place. I can walk it. I went down to find a book I read when I was nineteen."

"Did you find it?"

"Every page."

"And?"

"And there was a lecture I went to after, about the same thing, that I didn't listen to. Because of a girl." He looked at her. "So I have the method and not the shortcuts."

Ferra looked at him for a while in the lamplight.

"What was she called," she said.

"I don't remember."

"Good," said Ferra, and pulled her shawl round her, and looked at the stone in the golem's chest again, longer this time. "And that's what you were doing in the study. On Monday. With the wings." She did not look at him. "You weren't reading. You'd gone somewhere. And you came back, and your face..." She stopped. "What did you recognize?"

He told her.

He had not decided to. He found that he had started, and that it was easier to go on than to stop. He told her that on his side of everything there had been stories, very old ones, older than any book, about the short broad people who lived in the mountains and worked metal better than men, and the tall people who lived in the woods and lived for ever, and the winged ones; and about others: people who lived in the sea, and people who went into the hills at night, and little people, and terrible ones. He told her the names. He said them aloud for the first time in his life in this world, in a gallery under a hill in the middle of the night, to the one woman on this side of everything apart from one other who knew where he had come from; and she sat on the trestle and listened to each one, and did not say any of them back.

"And they weren't real," she said, when he stopped.

"No. Never. Not there. Nobody ever saw one. Nobody ever found a bone." He looked at the stone. "And every child knew what they looked like. Every child. You could ask any six-year-old in my whole country to draw you one and they'd draw you the ears, and the beard, and the wings, and they'd get them right. I could have drawn you that smith's swage at six." He stopped. "And here they were real. Here there was a city of them. And nobody knows. Not one person. Not even a children's story, except the one about the cellar floor." He told her about Doran's grandmother. "Somebody took it out of the world, Vey says. And on my side, where it never happened, everybody knew."

Ferra was quiet a long time.

"Your side," she said at last. "You said once it was like here, but further along."

"Further than you know." He had not meant to say that either. "Further than the side I've told you about, even. I told you about the cars and the lights and the machines. I didn't tell you how far." He tried to find a way to say it. "There were things I used every day that I never once opened. That I couldn't have made if you'd given me ten years and Bram. A thing you spoke to and it answered. A car that drove itself home while you sat in it with your hands in your lap. The trial they put me through, that put all this in my head." He touched his temple. "That was new, there. It wasn't new by much."

"So it was the future," said Ferra.

He looked at her.

"Your side. It wasn't only further along than here. It was further along than it had ever been. You keep saying 'on my side' as if it's a place over the hills. It isn't." She had her pen out, the way she did when she was thinking, though she had no book, and was turning it in her fingers. "You lived in the future of a world. Some of what you know, nobody on your side had known for very long either. And some of it you used and never looked inside, so you can't make it, and that's why you're always so cross about things you can't make." She stopped turning the pen. "That's it, isn't it."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"You might have said."

"I'm saying."

"You're saying in a cellar at one in the morning because I caught you." She put the pen away. "Tomorrow you'll say it to Imani, in daylight, with your face washed, because that's the rule, and I'm not having her hear it from me first. Term two."

"Term two," he said.

She sat on the trestle with her shawl round her and looked at the chest of the thing, and the grey stone in it.

"Will they come back?" she said. "Wherever they are. If they're anywhere."

"I don't know." He thought about Vey's drawing of the people half in water, drawn and rubbed out and drawn again, and left as a question. "I don't know if there's anybody to come. But if there is." He looked at the stone. "If there is, and they come back, then somebody in Kaldo is going to open a door one morning and see one of them standing there. And I'd rather that somebody was a child who already knew what they were. Who'd heard of them. Who'd heard a story about them that was kind." He was quiet. "I'd rather they'd heard of them before they met them. Than meet them first, and have nothing in their heads to put them in but the cellar floor."

Ferra looked at him.

"On my side," he said, "there was a night for it. One night a year. The night the strange things and the dead got one night, the old stories said. And the children dressed up as them. Every kind. The winged ones and the ones from the woods and the ones from under the hill, and terrible ones, and silly ones, made of sheets. And went door to door in the dark, in their costumes, and people gave them sweets." He smiled, a little, without meaning to. "It was the best night of the year when you were seven."

"Sweets," said Ferra. "For dressing up as dead people."

"For dressing up as anything."

She was quiet. He could see her turning it, the way she turned a column she had not seen before: what it would cost, and who would object, and what it was really for.

"You're going to do it," she said. "Aren't you."

"I haven't decided."

"You decided while you were saying it. Your voice went like it does." She stood up, and pulled the shawl tight, and held out her hand to him. "Come up. You've got a head on you; I can see it from here. It'll keep." She looked at the stone a last time. "All of it'll keep. It's kept a thousand years."

He put the canvas back over the chest, and took her hand, and went up.



On the last day of August Tess's room held without him.

It happened in the Motion Shop, in the middle of the afternoon, with the doors open on the heat and Dov at his bench and Wren under the bench arm with a lamp, where she was not supposed to be; and Emeric did not know about it until Tess came to the castle at supper in her chair, very fast, and came up the ramp into the hall and stopped at the table, and said to him across it, in front of everybody:

"I went in."

He put his fork down.

"On my own. At the bench. I put my hand on it" (the band at her wrist, the stone under the leather) "the way you showed me, and I thought about the relay fault, and it was there. The room. My room. The drawers and the window and the river. And you weren't." She was very pink. "You weren't holding it up. It stood up by itself. I stayed in it for..." She looked at Bram. "How long was I?"

"Long enough that Dov came and asked me if you'd died," said Bram. "I said she's thinking. He said he knew what that looked like and that wasn't it." He reached across the table and put his big hand on top of her head, briefly, the way he had when she was twelve and new in his house and would not cry, and took it away again. "Eat your supper."

Armand, at the head of the table, put down his cup.

"Six weeks," he said.

"Yes," said Emeric.

"You took months. With your mother's stone. In the first year." Armand looked down the table at Tess with his old pale eyes, interested, as he was interested in very few things. "Months. I remember. You used to come down from your room looking as if you'd been in a fight." He turned his cup. "And it's six weeks. For her."

"Why?" said Tess.

"Because you are sitting in Kaldo," said Armand. "And I was sitting in it with him, that first year, and it was thin. It isn't thin now. You've drunk this town's water for years, and it's dosed. You've eaten what comes off the Milford trains, and some of that is dosed too, a little, whatever anybody tells you. You've breathed the air in the works, which has had stones being charged in it every day since you were a girl, and you sleep over it." He drank. "The stone learns you faster here. Because there's more of you for it to learn."

Tess thought about that. "So I didn't do it."

"You did it," said Armand. "The town helped."

She looked at him a moment longer, and then she nodded, as if that was fair, and turned her chair to the table and began to eat; and halfway through her supper she stopped, with her fork in the air, and said to nobody in particular:

"Wren knew. When I came out. She was holding the lamp, and she looked at me, and she said, You were away." She went back to her supper. "I don't know how she knew."

Ferra, across the table, wrote something in her book.

After supper Emeric went back down to the gallery under the hill, and took the canvas off the chest, and sat down on the trestle's end with his hand on the shell beside the grey stone, and began again with the register: counting.



It was past one when he came up from the gallery, and the winter garden was dark behind its screens; and there was a lantern on the step of the pass-box, turned low, and Ferra sitting beside it in her shawl with her book on her knees, because she slept with one ear on the stair and had heard him go down, and had known, she said afterward, where he would go when he came up.

"You saw it," he said.

"I saw you see it." She stood up. "Is that what it's waiting for?"

"I think so."

They went in one at a time through the box, and stood inside the inner door in the thick warm dark with the lantern, at the ring of white stones; and he went up Bram's short ladder to the lowest tier and put his mask round his neck and stood among the flowers, with the tree's full slow note all round him like standing inside a bell.

He held his hand over the nearest flower, flat, the way the figure in Vey's book held it, and did not touch it; and he let out a little of his own mana, the way he pushed ink, a thread of it, not much, into the cup of the flower and no further.

Nothing happened. Then the flower closed. It closed slowly, like a hand, round nothing, and hung there shut on its vine; and the tree's note did something he had not felt it do since the pod, which was move. A small thing. A shift of weight, the way a sleeper turns over.

He did the next one. And the next. Ferra held the lantern up to the tier from the ladder's foot and counted them aloud under her breath, so he would know where he'd been, and he went along the lowest vines in the dark with his hand out, flower after flower, a thread for each; and it took him till after three, and the tips of his fingers were cold and numb by the end of it, and when he came down the ladder his knees were not entirely his.

"A hundred and twelve," said Ferra.

He sat down on Bram's crate. Above him the tree stood in the lantern's light with every flower on its lowest tier shut, and its note was not sitting any more. It was pulling. Gently, at the room, the way it had pulled every day before the pod: a drinking note.

Ferra went out with him through the box, and sat down again on the step, and opened the gauge book.

The next evening the day's cartridge went in at eight, at the line, and at nine the needle came back down to the line, and a hair below it; and Bram, who had come to watch, put his hand flat on the glass of the gauge as if it were a horse that had done well, and went off to bed without a word. Ferra closed the second column. She ruled it off across the bottom with a double line, the way she closed an account, and wrote under it in ink, small: Pollinated, by hand, 1 to 3 in the morning. 112 flowers. E. And then, because it was her book and she had kept it honestly since April, in pencil under that: Vey's figure. Not blessing.
