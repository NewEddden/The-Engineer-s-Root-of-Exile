Chapter 257 - The Wayford Line

The last footing on the Wayford line went in on Kaldo's own ground, three days before the line opened, a quarter of a mile below the works yard, and Emeric did part of it himself and did it badly.

It was the footing for Tess's box. The three lines met there, or would on Thursday: the Grayrock line along the river, the Milford line in off the flats, and the Wayford line coming down from the north round the long curve under the brickfield, three hundred and seventy miles of it. Where they met there was a fan of points on gravel, and a hut, and in the hut a lever frame and a pointsman with a slate. For years the pointsman had been enough. Then it had been Wick on the ore and Dunnel on the grain and Harl on the Wayford work trains, and since last week Tam on the coal and Wat shunting the yard; and Odo Pike had said, every time he came down from Grayrock, that a junction with one man and a slate in it was a collision with the date not yet written in.

The box that replaced the hut would stand in the angle between the Wayford and the Milford lines, two storeys of brick, the levers upstairs where the man could see every point and Tess's relays in the room under them. A lever frame could stand on gravel. A cabinet of relays could not, not with ore trains going by four feet away all day; the contacts would chatter, Tess said, and a chattering contact was a lie. So the box would stand on rock, and the rock was four feet down.

"Nobody's counting here," said Emeric.

"Somebody's always counting," said Paskin, who had heard it from Ward and liked it. But he took his pipe out of his mouth, which meant yes, and put it in his pocket, and called the girl.

She was not a girl any more. She had chosen Paskin at the third Commencement and gone white to the lips on the ridge at the first cutting, and she had cut a good part of the line since then on Kaldo's ground and Milford's, where nobody stood on the verge with a book; and beyond them, where the Church's men did, she had stood back with Paskin and watched the levy do it with picks, and Fennick's powder do what picks couldn't, for three hundred miles. She had a mason's way of standing now. She looked at the pegged square, and at Emeric, who had taken his coat off.

"He wants a turn," said Paskin.

"On rock," said the girl.

"On rock."

She did not say anything to that. Paskin had watched him take a turn at the dune drum in February, where the sand had moved under his hands like water and the rock under it had not moved at all; Paskin had been kind enough not to mention it on the flight home, and had plainly mentioned it to her since.

He did the gravel first. Earth was not his; it cost him the way a borrowed tool costs, everything taking a little longer and a little more grip than it should. But gravel wanted to move. He leaned on it, slow and heavy, the way he had watched Paskin lean on a hill, and it slid out of the square in two low banks, quietly, like sand in a glass, while the girl dressed the sides behind him. In a quarter of an hour there was a hole four feet deep with a floor of grey rock, and two of the levy at its edge with shovels and nothing to do.

Then the rock.

He put his hands flat on it, because it was the only way he knew yet to get his note into stone; Paskin did it standing up with his hands in his pockets. It was a grey grit, old and cold and very sure of itself. He leaned. It did not lean back. It was like putting his shoulder to the keep.

He leaned harder. A crumb of grit came away under his right palm and rolled into the angle of his thumb.

"That's a start," said Paskin, from the edge. "You're pushing at the stone."

"It's stone."

"It's stone." Paskin took his pipe out of his pocket and looked at it and put it back. "Not at the stone. Under it. The stone isn't where it's going."

It took him most of the morning to understand that, and he did not understand it by being told. He understood it at about eleven, with his shirt black at the back and his head beginning to ache, when he stopped trying to move the rock up and let his note go down past it, into a seam a foot below the floor where the grit had cracked once, long ago, and lay on itself with a skin of clay between; and leaned there, on the place where it was already willing to part.

The floor of the hole lifted along the seam by the width of a finger and lay down again, with a long low sound in the ground like a door closing two houses away.

The girl, sitting on the gravel bank with her arms round her knees, sat up.

He did it six more times, and at noon there was a slab of grit the size of a table lying loose in the hole, and the levy got bars under it and hauled it out in pieces. Then the girl did the rest of the floor in twenty minutes, flat and true, standing with her hands at her sides, and did not once look at him while she did it.

"How long would that have taken you?" said Emeric. "Alone."

"The whole of it?" She thought. "Since breakfast."

"It took me since breakfast to do a table."

"Yes, Your Grace. But you've the seam now. You'd not have had that from me." She brushed off her hands, though she had touched nothing. "Master Paskin told me the same my first year. Not at the stone. I cried."

"I nearly did."

Paskin laughed round the pipe, which he had lit at last, and went to tell the bricklayers they could have the hole.



Tess tried the box that afternoon, on her bench in bay three, because the room under the levers was not built yet and because Tess tried everything on her bench before she let it out of her sight.

It filled the end of the bay: a long oak frame on trestles with a painted board along its front, and on the board the junction as a man would see it from the box's window, every point and every signal numbered in her square white capitals, each with a brass lever no longer than a finger and a little lamp beside it. Behind the board, on a sloped shelf, seventy relays in rows, each a coil on an iron core with a hinged tongue over it and a pair of contacts on the tongue, and the wires running off them in looms bound with waxed thread. Isa Brann had wound every coil and laid every loom. She sat at the far end of the bench with her spool and said nothing, which was what Isa did.

Merrin Sallis was at the board, with a bandage round two fingers of her left hand from the first coil she had wound in April and run a current through before she had finished winding it. Ferra's counting house had sent her back in the spring because she would keep asking why a sum came out instead of what it came to.

"Route one," said Tess. "Wayford down to the platform road."

"Points four, reverse. Six, normal. Eleven, normal." Click, click, click, behind the board, as the relays pulled in and their lamps went white. "Signal two." The signal's lamp went white.

"Route two. Milford up, across four."

Merrin threw the levers for four and six back. Nothing clicked. The lamps stayed where they were.

"It won't let them go," said Merrin, pleased, though she had known it wouldn't. "Two's clear over four, so four's locked."

She put signal two back, and the relays behind four and six let go, click, click, and she set the Milford route and cleared its signal, and then tried signal two again out of mischief, and nothing happened at all.

"That's Osric's sentry," said Tess, to Emeric, behind her chair. "Two clears if four's set AND six's set AND eleven's set AND nothing that crosses them is clear. Seventy times over. Every one a question somebody would have had to ask out loud with a slate in his hand while the ore train was coming."

"It doesn't ask," said Emeric. "It knows."

"It doesn't know anything. It's a box of wire." She looked up at him. "It just can't be talked into it."

He had given her the name for it in the spring, and she had used it since without fuss, the way she used his names once they stopped sounding like his. Relay interlocking. It was in the board's top corner in white paint. Odo had come down from Grayrock to see the first drawing and stood in front of it a long time with his stick and his bent back, and said only that it was a thing he had wanted for forty years and had not known how to want.

Dov Kettle had not said anything at all.

He stood at the bench's other end with his arms crossed: a broad, slow man in his thirties in a millwright's apron, with sawdust in the hair on his forearms. He was Grayrock's, the mills' best fitter, lent down the line for a month to make the junction's lever frame and the rods that would run from it to every point, because nobody in Kaldo's works fitted a crank as true as he did, and Bram had said so to his face, which Bram did not do. He did not like the box.

"Boxes that think," he had said that morning, not loudly. "I'll fit your rods, mistress. I'll not trust a box that thinks."

"It doesn't think," Tess had said.

"Then I'll not trust a box that says it doesn't."

He uncrossed his arms now and went round the back of the frame without asking, and took a fold of card out of his apron, the kind fitters used to shim a bearing, and slid it between the tongue and the core of the relay that said whether points four lay for Wayford.

The tongue stayed down on the card. As if four were set.

"Your Wayford signal," said Dov, from behind the board. "With four lying for Milford."

Merrin looked at Tess. Four's lamp said Milford. The Milford signal said clear.

"Throw it," said Tess.

Merrin threw signal two, and its lamp went white.

Nobody in the end of bay three said anything. The board said, in two white lamps side by side, that the Wayford train had a clear road down the platform line and the Milford train a clear road up across it, over the same points, at the same moment. Dov came round the frame and looked at the two lamps, and then at Tess.

"A relay sticks," he said. "I've seen them stick. Wet morning, a bit of dirt in the hinge. A wasp." He nodded at the board. "And your box tells my driver the road's clear because a wasp said so."

Emeric had seen it the moment Dov's hand went into his apron, and had very nearly said it, and had not, because it was not his to say. He stood behind Tess's chair with his arms folded and watched her look at the two lamps.

She did not look at him at all.

"Take the card out," she said.

Dov took it out. Signal two went red on its own.

"So it's right when it works," said Tess, to the board, "and lies when it doesn't. That's worse than the slate. A man with a slate knows when he doesn't know." She turned her chair a quarter, so that she was facing Dov. "Thank you."

Dov looked at her as if she had handed him something hot.

"I'll have it by the morning," she said.



She had it by the morning, and it was simpler than the first one, which was how Emeric knew it was right.

He came down at seven and found her where he had left her at ten, with the lamp still lit and Osric asleep in a chair against the wall under a blanket somebody had put over him, which Emeric suspected had been Tess. Twenty relays stood on the bench with their covers off.

"It believed them," she said, without saying good morning. "That's the fault. A relay's a man telling you he's looked. So don't take its word. Every point gets a contact out at the rail, on Dov's rod, that only closes when the blade's lying home, and nothing else can close it. The signal's current has to come through the relay and the blade both, and through every route that crosses it lying at danger, all the way along, and the last of it holds the signal's arm off with a magnet. The arm's weighted. Lose the current anywhere, for any reason, and it falls."

"To danger."

"By its own weight. Like the portcullis on the South Gate. Cut the chain and it comes down. It never goes up." She tapped the nearest tongue. "And these don't get to stick. Every contact goes grey, the buttons from the telegraph boxes that a spark can't eat, and the tongue hangs, so it drops by weight when the coil lets go. A spring tires. Weight doesn't."

"And the card?"

"The card holds the relay in. The blade's still lying for Milford. Its contact's open. The current stops at the rail." She sat back. "I tried it at four. With the card. And with a wasp. Osric caught me a wasp."

Osric said something into his blanket about wasps.

"It fails quiet," said Tess. "Like the steady. If it doesn't know, it does nothing, and nothing's danger."

"Fail-safe," said Emeric.

She thought about it.

"Quiet's better," she said, and wrote fail-safe on the board's corner anyway, under relay interlocking, because she wrote his names down even when she didn't like them, the way Ferra wrote down being overruled.

Dov came at half past seven. Tess gave him the card herself. He put it in the same place, and then in three others, slowly, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth, and each time came round the front and looked, and each time the lamp said red. The fourth time he took his own snips and cut a wire, without asking, and came round and looked.

Red.

"I'll still not trust a box that thinks," said Dov at last.

"It doesn't think," said Tess.

"Aye. You said." He put his snips away. "But I'd trust that one to stop."

Bram came down at nine and stood in the bay's door and looked at the end of his own shop, which every soul who worked there had called Tess's end for a year, except Bram.

"From the second pillar," he said, and pointed at it. "To the wall. Yours." He looked at the pillar as if it had let him down. "The door stays where it is. I'm not walking round the back to get to my own yard. And two hands; I can't spare a man off the floor." He nodded at Merrin, and at Isa with her spool. "On my books. Your say." He looked at Isa. "She doesn't talk."

"She doesn't need to," said Tess. "She's never wound me a bad coil."

Isa did not look up. But she stopped winding for the length of a breath, and then went on.

Dov was at the door with his apron on, watching, the way he watched a shaft turn to see if it was true.

"The rods'll be done the end of August," he said, to nobody. "Grayrock'll want me back for the mill's new wheel." He looked at the board, and at fail-safe in white paint in its corner. "After that I've no place I have to be."

He came back at the end of August with his tools in a box on his shoulder and a letter from the mill's master saying he had been impossible all month; and Bram, who had wanted him on his own floor since the day the frame came off the bench, gave him to Tess and was sore about it for a week.



The through train left the yard at seven on the Thursday evening, the twenty-ninth of July, and before it left, Ferra named its engine.

There were two. They had come off the works floor a fortnight apart, the first engines Bram's shop had built to pull people and not ore: taller in the wheel than Wick and her sisters, with a long boiler and a cab with a roof on it, because twelve hours was a long time to stand in the rain. One had gone up the line light on the Tuesday and stood tonight at Wayford's new platform with eight coaches behind it, waiting for the same hour. The other stood at Kaldo's, at the head of the other eight, hissing at the crowd.

The crowd was most of the town. It had come down to the yard below the works as it had come for Wick five years ago, and stood packed along the fence and up the spoil bank and on the roofs of the wagon sheds, eating, because Maren had set up a table; and on the platform, in the space the garrison kept clear, Ferra stood with her book.

The crews had asked her for names in June. She had said she would give them two, and had not, and had gone home that night with the book; and Emeric, who asked why, had been told she was writing two letters first.

She opened the book now at a page near the back, a column ruled in her own hand and headed in her own capitals. He knew the page. He had sat at the long table in the October of the eighth year while she matched it against every priest's answer in the north, and watched Imani put her hand flat on it.

SERVED.

"The crews name an engine for one of Kaldo's dead," said Ferra. She had never in her life raised her voice, and the platform went quiet to hear her anyway. "Wick. Dunnel. Harl. Tam, and Wat. These two will run the Wayford line every day, both ways, through Wayford's parishes. So they take Wayford's names." She looked past the crowd at the line going north under the brickfield. "Wayford's ours. It was ours the day Keller signed it over. We hadn't read its book yet." She put her finger on the column. "Two hundred and twenty men went to the Staple in a bad year to work the rag, and came home with a figure in this column. A hundred and thirty-one of them were dead inside five years, and their wives were told it was the rag."

She turned a page.

"I wrote to two kitchens," she said. "I asked. They said yes. Haskell, of Coldwell parish. A drover." She looked up at the engine. "She's at Wayford tonight. She leaves at this hour and they pass in the dark at the halfway house. And Kemp. Of Wayford town. He mended carts at the posting house."

The fireman on the footplate had a lump of chalk. He leaned out of the cab and wrote it on the cab side, KEMP, a foot high and crooked, while Ferra waited; and when he had done it he took his cap off, and so did the driver, and so, raggedly, all along the fence, did the men in the crowd who had caps, and a good many who didn't took off what they had.

"The painter has them on Monday," said Ferra. "Properly." She shut the book and stepped back.

There was an old woman at the very end of the platform in a black shawl, with a younger one holding her arm, who had come down on the people's train from somewhere and been let through by the garrison. Emeric had not known she was there until Ferra looked at her. He watched Ferra not go to her, because she had not come to be gone to; and watched the old woman look at the chalk on the cab a long time, and nod once to nobody, and let the younger one take her back to the coaches.

He did not know whether she was a Kemp. He did not ask.



Odo Pike had a ticket, because Ferra had made everybody buy one.

"The people's fare is the fare," she had said at the booking window, putting down five copper of her own and five of Imani's and holding out her hand to Emeric for his and Ward's. "If the duke rides free on the first night, it's a duke's train and everybody else is on it by his leave." The clerk put four cards one at a time into the little press on his counter, which bit the date into each with a sound like a knuckle on a door, and Ferra put them in her book and read his tally upside down through the glass and told him he had sold three hundred and ninety-eight, and the clerk looked at her the way people did.

Odo was on the first bench of the first coach with his stick between his knees and his bad back against the coach side, and when Emeric came along to see him he said, "It's a plank."

"It's oak. With a back."

"It's a plank with a back. I've sat on planks forty-four years. I know one." Odo looked along the coach, which was full: carters and their wives, two of Ilsa's clerks, a priest, a man with a crate of hens, a dozen of the levy going home to their villages on their first leave in a year, children at every window, battery lamps in brass cages along the roof, and the evening coming in long and gold through Ivo's glass. "It's a good coach," he said grudgingly. "Twelve hours on a plank."

"It was a fortnight by cart."

"It was. And I've done it by cart, and I'll tell you at the other end which was worse." He put both hands on his stick. "Go on, then."

At seven exactly the guard, who was the first guard Kaldo had ever had and had been chosen because he could read a watch and would not be argued with, blew his whistle; and Kemp's first beat came up the chimney, and the second, and the train went out under the brickfield with the crowd running beside it, children first and then the men, the way they had run beside Wick.

The last boy dropped back at the bend.



The compartment was at the back of the last coach, a small room with two benches facing, where the guard was meant to keep the mail and himself; and the guard had his seat in the van behind it now, by Ferra's asking and against his judgment, and the compartment had the duke's party in it.

Ward was in the passage outside, a short passage between the compartment's door and the van's, with a window on the north side. He stood in it with his back to the door and his arms folded and watched the country go by. He would stand there, Emeric knew without asking, until Wayford.

"He could sit with us," said Imani.

"He could," said Ferra.

Neither of them got up to tell him so.

For the first hour they did nothing at all, and that was the best of it, afterward, though nobody would have written it down. The river flats and the hay in cocks and the steadings' lamps coming on, and the beat of the engine coming back along the whole train into the floor and the bench and the glass. Ferra reading back through the line's pages from the first cutting, not writing. Imani with her shoes off and her feet up on the bench beside him, crossed at the ankle, her head back against the wall and her eyes half shut. Himself in the middle of the other bench with nothing in his hands, which did not happen often.

He looked at the two of them, and the dark coming on, and Ward's shoulder in the passage; and did not say anything at all.

Ferra looked up.

"What," she said.

"Nothing."

"You've been looking at me for a page and a half."

"You turned two pages."

"I turned one. I read it twice."

"He's been looking at both of us," said Imani, without opening her eyes. "Turn and turn about. Nine breaths each. I counted."

"You were asleep."

"I was resting my eyes," said Imani, "and counting." She took her feet off the bench, leaned across the space between them, took his face in both hands, and kissed him, slowly, as if she had been meaning to since the yard and had only waited for the town to be out of sight. When she let him go, Ferra had put her book down and moved across to his other side, and put her hand flat on his chest, over the place Imani counted, and turned his face to her with two fingers under his jaw, and did the same.

It went on for some time.

There was nothing in it that needed to be got to. That was the difference, five years on: they were not hurrying toward anything. Ferra's hand stayed on his chest and moved now and then, a little, as if she were checking a figure she already knew. Imani had his hair loose from its tie in the first minute and her fingers in it at the back, and was in no hurry to take them out. At one point, in the swaying dark between two lamps, he was not entirely sure which of them he was kissing, and Ferra laughed against his mouth and said so, and Imani said it didn't count if he couldn't tell, and Ferra said everything counted. The train went over the river crossing with a long hollow roar under the floor, and none of them looked out to see it.

Somebody knocked on the door: low, twice, with a knuckle. Ward. Then a second voice in the passage.

"Tickets," said the guard.

"They're the duke's party," said Ward.

"Everybody shows a ticket between the river and the halfway house. Mistress Ferra's orders."

There was a short silence in the passage, of the kind that happened round Ward. Then the guard's boots went back toward the van, rather quicker than they had come, and the van door shut, and Ward knocked again, once, by himself.

Emeric, with Imani's hand in his hair and Ferra's on his chest and his collar a good deal further open than it had been at the yard, raised his voice just enough to carry through the door and no further.

"The duke's party has its tickets. Ask the duchesses."

There was a different kind of silence in the passage.

Ferra, without moving her hand from his chest, reached behind her for her book with the other, opened it one-handed, took out the four cards and passed them over his shoulder; and Imani, without moving at all, hooked the door handle down with her toes and let it open a hand's width. Ward's hand came through the gap and took the cards and came no further. Through the gap Emeric could see a hand's width of Ward's back, turned to the door as squarely as it had been at the yard, which had somehow, without moving, turned itself a little further toward the window.

The hand went away down the passage with the cards, and the van door opened and shut, and after a while the hand came back with them clipped, four times, and put them through the gap, and the door shut.

"He'll not say anything," said Imani, with her eyes shut.

"He never does," said Ferra, and put the cards back in her book in order, and went back to what she had been doing.

Outside in the passage, Ward did not answer, at some length.



The halfway house was where the railhead had stood the whole of the last winter, while the bridge over the second river went in: a field once, with a water tower and a hut and Paskin's painted sign, and now, at a little before midnight, a long low building of Grayrock brick lit along the platform, with the engine drinking under the tower's arm and a smell of soup coming out of the door that went the length of the train.

Maren had trained its cook. She had kept him in her kitchen a month in the spring and sent him back up the line with a list of eleven things he was never to do again and a pot of her stock to start his from, and he was frightened of her still at a hundred and eighty miles. The train stopped twenty minutes, and four hundred people got off it, and most of them ate.

Odo ate standing at the end of the long counter with his stick hooked over its edge, and told Emeric the soup was better than Maren's, and then looked round very quickly, as if she might have come up the line on the tender.

"Five hours," he said, and blew on his soup. "And seven to come." He looked out at Kemp under the water tower, her chalked name white in the lamp from the house, and he looked at her the way he had looked at Wick coming round the hill at Grayrock: not as a man looks at a machine. "It's a wonder. I drew it on a tavern wall forty years ago and they painted it over, and here I am drinking soup halfway to Wayford on it." He drank. "And a man going to the capital on the king's business still gets off at Wayford and takes a horse." He looked at Emeric over the bowl. "It's not fast, Your Grace. It's faster. That's not the same."

"Then I'll need your grades."

"You've had my grades for five years. Make it fast." He unhooked his stick. "And make it soft."

Haskell came through from the north while they were boarding, on the other line, as Ferra had said she would: a long whistle out of the dark beyond the tower, then her lamp, then her, running slow past the platform with her own eight coaches lit behind her and her own name chalked crooked on her cab in a different hand. Her driver and Kemp's lifted a hand to each other across the two lines as she went by.

Ferra wrote the minute in her book.



They were asleep before the second river.

Imani first, he thought: her head came down on his left shoulder somewhere past the halfway house, the whole weight of it, the way she let her head go nowhere else, and then her arm round his middle and her knees drawn up on the bench and her breathing going long against his neck. Ferra fought it. She had the book open on her knee by the turned-down lamp, and every few minutes her head came down on his right shoulder and she lifted it again and turned a page she hadn't read, until at last she didn't lift it, and the book slid off her knee onto the floor, and he did not reach for it, because reaching would have woken them both.

So he sat.

The coach beyond the door's glass was dark, full of people asleep on planks, and somebody's child far up it was singing to itself in its sleep, the same four notes over and over. The country outside had gone black, with a star now and then low over a ridge. The beat of the engine came up through the bench into him, and their two bodies lay on him, one each side, with their weight and their warmth and the whole of their trust, and he could not move.

He did not want to move. He wanted, very much, something else; and that was the trouble, and had been every night like this since the first. Imani's breath on the side of his throat, warm and slow, with a small catch at the top of each one. Ferra's hair against his jaw, the smell of her soap and the ink that was always somewhere on her. Her hand gone loose on his chest in her sleep, lying over his heart and rising with it. Imani's knee against his thigh. The heat of the two of them in a small room on a summer night, which his own body had begun in the last hour to answer with its own, because he could not stop it doing that, so that the three of them lay like coals in a pan.

He could wake them. He knew exactly how. A hand on Ferra's waist; his mouth on Imani's temple. Either would wake and look at him and know, and both would say yes, and Ward would stand in the passage until the sun came up if he had to.

He did not.

He could not have said why, entirely. They were tired: Ferra had not slept five hours a night since the coaches came off the wagon shop's floor, and Imani had been in the ward till three the night before with a boy who had put his hand in a coupling. The first train was the line's night and not theirs. He wanted it so plainly that the wanting itself felt like a reason to wait, which was the kind of thing he would have laughed at on Earth and did not laugh at now. So he sat, and wanted it, and kept his hands where they were, and it did not get any easier toward morning.

He thought about Tess's portcullis. He thought about the old woman in the black shawl, and Odo saying it was not fast. He thought about the falls, and the dam that would go across them in the autumn, and how many men it would take. He thought about Imani's mouth, and stopped, and thought about the dam again, and found he had come back round to her mouth by way of a cofferdam, which was not a road he had expected the dam to take.

Some time after two the train slowed, and stopped.

Outside there was a dark field and a darker hill rising beyond it; and up the line, round the long curve, the red lamp of a signal, and beyond it, far up the hill, small and orange, another train's lamps.

He knew where they were. He had walked it in his head from Linus's maps a dozen times, and Linus had flown it in the spring with Ivo's camera and pasted the prints into the survey book: the place where the line left the river flats on Lord Lisle's side and climbed into the ridge country, the one ridge in three hundred and seventy miles too broad to cut, a long slow grade three miles to the top. The crews had a name for it already, as crews did. Fallow Bank.

The first grain train that would ever carry Milford's harvest all the way to Wayford without a cart had gone out at four that afternoon, thirty wagons, an hour ahead of them all night. It had stalled on the bank.

He watched them do it.

He could not get up and did not try. He watched through the window over Ferra's head with his sense out across the field and up the grade, where the crew moved round the stalled train as small warm notes against the cold of the hill, walking down the wagons with lamps. They split her in the middle. He felt the coupling go, a hard small jolt in the iron that reached him a breath later as a sound. Then the engine took the front fifteen up the hill, slowly, its beat coming down the grade hard and short and far apart, like a man climbing stairs with a load on his back, while the rear fifteen stood with their brakes pinned and two men with lamps beside them.

It took her forty minutes to reach the top. He counted it on Imani's breathing. Then the walk back down, and the coupling, and the brakes, and the waiting, which took longer than the climb.

Then her lamp came back down the hill alone, running light, and coupled onto the fifteen that had waited, and took them up; and a third of the way up, at the steepest, he felt her labour exactly where she had laboured with the first half. So he watched it done twice, once with each half, and the second time he knew where she would labour before she did. At the top a lamp swung, and the signal ahead of them went white.

They went up Fallow Bank behind her without any trouble, because eight coaches of people asleep on planks weighed a fraction of thirty wagons of grain. And at the steepest of it Emeric took his notebook out of his coat with his right hand, very slowly, without moving his shoulder, and opened it on his knee, and wrote one line in pencil in the dark without being able to see the page.

He did not show it to anybody. He put the notebook back, and watched the black hill go by, and did not sleep.



They came into Wayford at five to nine in the morning, an hour and fifty minutes late, every minute of it Fallow Bank's.

Ferra woke at the first sight of the town and sat up and looked at the book on the floor, and at him, and at the window, in that order; and picked up the book and found her place and wrote the time, and only then said good morning. She looked like a woman who had slept eight hours in a good bed. She put her hair back up with four pins out of her pocket, without a glass.

Imani woke slowly and better, with a long stretch that went all the way down to her toes, against him, and then sat up and looked at him for a long time.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"A little."

"Not at all." She took his wrist, from habit. "Sixty-eight. Dry eyes. Your hair's a bird's nest and you've a crease down your face from Ferra's pins." She let go. "You sat up all night with us asleep on you, and didn't move."

"It was a good night."

"It was," said Ferra, and kissed him on the cheek, briskly, as a woman does who has slept well and has a station to see. "Do your collar."

Wayford's platform was new brick under a roof on Grayrock's iron columns, and it had been full, the stationmaster said, since four. When Kemp came in under the roof with her whistle going and stood steaming at the buffers, the town did not cheer at first. It looked at her, and at the people coming down out of the coaches stiff from fourteen hours of plank, from Kaldo, in a night.

Then it cheered.

Aster was waiting at the end of the platform, exactly where the van would stop, because he had measured it: in his mended brown coat, with his hat in one hand and a paper in the other.

"Your Grace. Chancellor. Mistress Imani." He held the paper out to Ferra, not to Emeric. "The first freight bill. Forty bales of wool for Kaldo's mill, from the Wayford wool-merchants' hall, on the first train south, at the posted rate. Paid." He paused. "I wrote it last Thursday. In case."

Ferra read it standing on the platform with the crowd going by her on both sides, all of it, to the copper; and initialled its corner, and gave it back.

"It's right," she said.

Aster put it inside his coat as if it might break. Then he looked at Emeric properly, and his face, which had learned over fifteen years at another man's books to show nothing it could not prove, showed something.

"Your Grace looks," said Aster carefully, "as if he had walked."

"He sat," said Imani. "All night. Upright, with two women asleep on him, and didn't move once, and didn't sleep. It's a known complaint." She took her bag from Ward. "There's no cure for it."

Ward said nothing whatever, at considerable length.



Aster had the new book depot to show them, a room at the end of the platform with shelves to the ceiling and a lamp in its window and every book Kaldo's presses made for sale stacked in bales: the primers, Imani's handbook, Linus's road maps with every posting house and milestone on the capital road, the readers and the arithmetics. Every traveller north to the capital came through Wayford, and from today every one of them came through this room, Aster said, because the coach stand was outside its door, and he had moved it there himself.

The first broadsheet was on the counter in twenty bundles, come up in the van in the night: one sheet, KALDO across its head, the prices of grain and wool and coal at all four baronies, the timetable, and three columns of work wanted, masons and plumbers and clerks and nurses, with the wage beside every line. Against the back wall stood a tier of canvas sacks, tagged.

"Lessons by post," said Aster. "Your Chancellor's office sets the bookkeeping, and yours, mistress, the nursing. They go out up the line to whoever's paid for the course, and come back written in, and go down to be marked." He touched a tag. "Thirty-one for the nursing. I have the names."

Imani read the tags one after another, all down the tier, and did not say anything; and Emeric, watching her do it, did not either.

Aster's clerk came down the platform with a leather bag, and Aster opened it on the counter.

"The first mail south. It goes down on Kemp this evening. There's one in it for Your Grace, by the capital road. It came up by rider on Tuesday with orders to wait for the first train, and I thought you'd rather have it in your hand than read it in Kaldo tomorrow."

A soldier's fold, plain grey wax with a thumbprint in it and no seal. Emeric knew the hand before he had it: square and careful, the hand that had written three things on the back of a toll ticket in a posting-house yard ten years ago with a stub of pencil while a farrier nailed on a shoe.

He read it at the counter with Ferra on one side and Imani on the other and Ward at the door.

It was short. Varek's letters would be. His men had taken a deserter in the spring, a Duvrenne man, a guard, who had walked away from a post he would not name, and who swore every day in the same words that there were Randillian men alive on the Isle of Shards, taken in the old war and kept, that he had seen them. He could not say where, not well enough to send anybody; he had been taken in and out in the dark, and he was not a clever man, and he was afraid. Varek held him at his fort on the strait, and would hold him until somebody told him what to do with him, and nobody in the capital had.

He still had the ticket.

Come if you like, Varek had written at the foot, a little larger. Don't promise her.

V.

Emeric folded it along its folds.

He had already begun. He knew it afterward; he had begun before he reached the foot of the page. Sela's counter. What he would say first. Whether to say a deserter or only a man. Whether it would be kinder in her kitchen with the door shut, the way Imani had made him learn to tell a woman a thing, in the eighth year.

"No," said Imani.

She had not read the letter. She had read his face, while he read it.

"Not Sela," she said. "You're working out how to tell her. Don't."

"Not on a deserter's word," said Ferra, on his other side. She had not read it either. "Not until you've a name and a place and a boat. You'd give her a year of waiting, and if it's nothing, you'll have killed her son twice."

Ward, at the door, did not turn round.

Emeric put the letter in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left, where Imani counted.

"A man who can't say where," he said, "may still know where."

Imani looked at him a breath longer, the way she looked at a wound that was not telling her all of it. Then she nodded once, and took a broadsheet off the counter and folded it into her bag for the ward's wall.

They went home that evening on Kemp. He slept from the halfway house to the yard with his head against the window and both of them awake on either side of him, and was told about it in detail at breakfast.

On the thirty-first he walked up through the town to the keep past the north well, and past Sela's shop with its door open on the street and Sela behind her counter weighing out salt for a girl with a basket; and did not go in.



Odo's complaint reached the long table that afternoon in Odo's own person, with his stick and a canvas bag, and by then it had stopped being about the benches.

"Two days by the grain train," said Odo, standing, because he had sat for twelve hours each way and would not sit again before Michaelmas. "Twelve by the people's. A man on the king's business can be at the capital by horse in the time it takes him to be at Wayford by me." He put the bag on the table. "I've been thinking about it since the halfway house."

There were six things in the bag: six engines in pine, each as long as a forearm, the same engine from the frame down and every one different at the front. Kemp as she was, her round smokebox door standing square to the wind. A blunt cone. An egg. A long flat nose like a shovel. A wedge, sharp, its edge running up the middle from the buffers to the chimney. And a thing like a fish's head, which Odo said was his grandson's and he had only brought so the boy would stop asking.

"Since the halfway house," said Ferra.

"I'd a knife," said Odo. "And seven hours."

Emeric had drawn the engine already, at his own table that morning, as soon as he was off the train. He opened it beside Odo's row.

A long engine, longer than Kemp by a third and lower. Three cylinders, the third between the frames driving a cranked axle, so that the beats came six to a turn of the wheels instead of four and she ran smoother and did not hammer the rail. A boiler pressed to two hundred and fifty pounds to the inch, a figure that made Bram put his cup down; and in it a nest of small tubes threaded back through the big fire tubes, so that the steam went back through the fire a second time and came out dry and hotter than water alone could make it. The superheater, he called it, and wrote the name beside it. Twin blast pipes into a chimney with two mouths, a double chimney, so the fire drew harder at speed without choking. Driving wheels six feet eight inches across. Air brakes on every coach, off a pump on the engine, so a driver at speed could stop the whole train at once and not hope the guard was awake. And over everything, from the buffers to the cab, a casing of sheet steel sloping back in one long line, the chimney sunk into it, so that she would go through the air the way a fish went through water and not the way a barn did.

He had drawn the front as a wedge. Odo's fifth.

"Streamlined," he said. "Shaped to the air. At the speeds Odo wants, the air's most of what she's pushing."

"Lined to the stream," said Odo, trying it, and then, sourly, "It's a good word."

"Which front?" said Bram.

Emeric had his guess, and a book in his head from another world that told him it was very nearly right; and he did not know why it was right, not in any way he could have shown to this table. The person who would know why was not at it.

He sent for Sorrel.



She came down from the leads at four with her cape-wing furled on her back and her bad leg cocked, listened to Odo for about as long as it took, and carried the six engines down to the works' long shed, empty on a Saturday with its doors open at both ends; and the table, having nothing better to do, followed her.

She stood them in a row on a trestle in the middle of the shed, their fronts to the north doors, and walked slowly to the north doors and turned in them with her back to the yard, and shut her eyes.

The wind came down the shed. Not a gale: a long steady push of air, smooth as a river, the kind Emeric could not have held for longer than a shout. It took the sawdust off the floor in a low drifting sheet and lifted the hair off Ferra's neck and went out of the south doors. Sorrel held it with her hands loose at her sides, as easily as a woman holds a cup.

Then she limped down the shed into her own wind and put her hand flat in front of each engine in turn, an inch from its nose.

At Kemp she made a face. "Like standing behind a door in a gale. Everything piles up and goes round the sides fighting." The cone: "Better." The egg: "Better again." The shovel she held a long time, and moved her hand up over the top of it, and frowned. "It goes up off the top and pulls. Like a wing. You'll lift her front wheels at speed." Odo wrote that down with his mouth open.

At the wedge she said nothing for a while. She ran her hand along the ridge an inch off the wood, and down each flank, and then put her face in front of it instead, close, chin lifted, eyes shut, the way Emeric had seen her stand on the keep's leads in a west wind deciding whether to go off them.

"This one. It goes past. It doesn't fight." She put her thumb hard on the very top of the ridge where the wedge met the chimney. "Except here. Too sharp. It catches and spills off both sides at once and leaves a hole behind. Round it, just there, the width of my thumb. And the sides want to run back further before they turn in." She drew it in the air. "Longer. Like that."

Odo took out his knife.

He did it on the trestle, in her wind, which she held for him without being asked; she said "There," and "No, back," and "There," and twice put her own thumb on the pine to show him, and when he had blown the shavings off she put her face in front of it again with her eyes shut for a long time.

Then she let the wind go, and the sawdust settled.

"Nobody touch it," said Sorrel.

Odo's fifth was still under it. The top had gone soft where her thumb had been, and the flanks ran back longer and turned in later, and from the side it had a long, low, almost sorrowful look, like the head of some large patient animal put down to drink. He had seen it before: its grandfather, in a photograph, in a book, in a library on the other side of everything, standing at a platform in steam.

He had guessed a wedge. She had felt the shape the wedge wanted to be.

"Draw that," he said to Bram.

"I've drawn it," said Bram, who had.



Tess came in at the south doors in her chair at half past four, with Osric beside her, because somebody had told her Sorrel had made a wind in the long shed. She read the drawing on the bench the whole way through, the way she read a circuit, and her eyebrows went up at the boiler's figure.

"Why isn't it electric?" she said.

Bram groaned.

"I'm asking. You can lay a motor flat now; I did it last week. A motor on every axle and no engine at all, and she'd start like a tram and never smoke out a cutting again."

"Three hundred and seventy miles of copper," said Emeric. "Over two rivers, up Fallow Bank, every mile of it to be watched and mended. And at the end of all that copper, one station. If the station stops, every train on the line stops where it is, with four hundred people on each in some cutting in the dark." He put his finger on the firebox. "She carries her own fire. Whatever happens anywhere else, she's got her fire and her water and her coal, and she goes."

He did not say the rest, which was that coal burned in a dead zone, because Tess knew it.

"Not on the long line, then," she said. "Not yet."

"Not yet."

"Round the wall. One day. A short line, a station at each end."

"One day."

She wrote it on the page in her book she kept for what wasn't yet, small, the ring, and drew a box round it.

Ferra had been at the end of the bench all this while counting the superheater's tubes off the drawing, which nobody had told her. She looked up.

"It wants a name."

"It'll get one off the crews," said Bram. "Same as the others."

"It's the one that isn't the same as the others." Ferra put her pencil to the top of the drawing, over the long nose. "The others carry our dead, up and down the lines every day, so nobody forgets them. That's what they're for. This one goes as near the capital as a train can. Every man on the king's business who gets off it at Wayford and buys a book at Aster's counter is going to look at it on the way north, and say what he saw." She wrote. "So it carries ours."

GOLD ROSE.

"It's the duchy's device," said Ferra, as if somebody had argued. "It's on the gate. It's on the seal." She did not look at Emeric. "It's what your mother wore."

"I'd have put a hammer," said Bram.

"You'd have put a hammer on the gate," said Ferra. "It's a rose."

Bram looked at the drawing for a long time, at the patient nose Sorrel had felt out of a wedge with her thumb and the name over it in his daughter's capitals; then found a pencil, and on the casing's flank, where the crews would one day paint it, drew a small rose in outline, very carefully, the way he had beaten them onto a sword's guard in the spring of the investiture.

"The crank axle," he said while he drew, quietly enough that only Emeric heard. "And the wheel centres. Three on one axle wants it bent true to a hair, or she'll shake herself to bits."

"The cellar does the masters," said Emeric, as quietly. "Your floor does the rest."

"My floor does the rest," said Bram, and finished the rose.



On the Monday Ferra wrote that the line's gangs were done. They had laid the Wayford line a stage a season since the first cutting, with the levy's picks and Fennick's powder and the spoil wagons, and Paskin's students on Kaldo's ground. On the Monday they were paid off the line, and on the Tuesday taken on again, the same men at the same rate, and the spoil wagons and the light track and the cranes went with them to the falls, where by the autumn there would be a dam.

Ottoline's tabulator counted the Wayford line's first four days on the Sunday night, and under its figure Ferra wrote a second: the tickets sold at Wayford and every halt north of the halfway house that had been bought one way only, south, to Kaldo.

Sixty-four.

"They read the broadsheet," said Imani, over her shoulder. "The nurses' line." She put her finger on the figure. "I want those houses."

"They're in Bram's book. Gate Avenue. The next block."

"I want them in the ground."

"So does Bram," said Ferra, and wrote down that Imani had said so, which Imani watched her do with her arms folded.

Milford's grain went to Wayford again on the Monday, so that it reached all four baronies now by rail; and the second through grain train stalled on Fallow Bank at the same place, a third of the way up, and was taken up in two halves the same way. Nobody told Emeric, and he had not asked anybody to. He had his line in his notebook, written in the dark with his left shoulder full of Imani and his right full of Ferra, on a page by itself, and it said only what he had watched.

That night he took Varek's letter out of his coat, where it had been for three days, and read it again at his table by the lamp.

Come if you like.

He put it in the back of the drawer, under the drawings of a lens Ivo was still grinding, and shut the drawer, and sat a while with his hand on it.

Then he opened his notebook to a clean page and wrote at its head, in pencil: Varek. The strait. And under it, after a while, the two words that had been his rule since a girl's belly in Imani's end room in April, and long before that, since a pier he had walked onto by accident in another woman's head.

Ask first.
