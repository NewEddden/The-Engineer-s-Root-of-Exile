Chapter 234 - Two Hulls

The first thing Zale asked about was the hose.

Emeric had thought about it for a week, how to do it. He had thought about telling him at the table, with the others there, in words, the way he told the table everything, in order. He had thought about the delve, the grey place, the island on the trestles; he could put the ship on the trestles beside it and let Zale walk round her. He had thought about writing it down, which was what Ferra would have done, and was the worst of the three. In the end he did what he had done with the flying, because it had worked: he took his brother down to the lower water after dark, without saying why, and let him look.

They went in by the small gate in the wall of Fletcher's yard, the one on the lane that the yard men used, with Ward ahead of them and a lamp stone in Ward's hand shut down to a slit. Zale had walked past that wall every day since he came home. He had sat on a bollard on the quay on his first morning and watched it with an interest he had taken some trouble to make look idle. He said, going through the gate, "At last," under his breath, and Emeric did not answer.

The yard was dark, and full of shapes. The dock in the middle of it, a black trench as long as a street with the ribs of hull four standing up out of it against the stars like the bones of something that had died there. The sheds. The long grassed mound in the far corner that everybody in Kaldo who had ever looked over the wall took for the spoil from the dock, with its timber gate and its row of sand buckets on a rail and the painted board at the gate that said, in Ward's kind of letters, NO FLAME. And along the quay on the yard's river side, lying in the black water under the wall, two ships.

They did not look like much, in the dark. That was the first thing. They looked like what the yard men had been told they were: a coaster, long and low and sweet-lined, lying quiet at her moorings, and beyond her, at the quay by the grassed mound, a great ugly high-sided thing like a barge that had been given ideas, blunt at both ends, broad as a house, with a deckhouse aft and a rail all round her and some sort of long bulge along her bottom. Zale looked at them both for a while from the gate, and then walked down to the quay, to the big one, because she was nearer, and stood on the edge of the stone and looked at her.

Under her, in the bulge along her keel, a hatch stood open, and in the hatch was a drum, a great wooden drum on an iron axle, as broad as a cart and wound thick with something black and heavy that shone a little in Ward's lamp. A hose. A great deal of hose, fat as a man's arm, of canvas and rubber, with an iron coupling on its end hanging a foot out of the hatch like a snake's head.

"What's that for?" Zale said.

"Oil."

"In a barge." Zale put his head on one side and looked along the drum, and up at the ship's high side, and down again. "That's a hundred feet of hose if it's a foot. What does a barge want with a hundred feet of hose? You'd tie up alongside and put a plank across." He looked at the drum for a moment longer, the way a sailor looks at gear he does not understand, which is the way a dog looks at a closed door. "Unless you weren't alongside."

Emeric said nothing. Behind him Ward had stopped a little way off, and was standing with his lamp down and his back to the wall, and said nothing either.

Zale turned and looked at the other ship, the coaster, the long sweet low one. He walked along the quay to her, slowly. He stopped at her bow and looked at it, and then walked the whole length of her, stern to bow, looking at her, the way he had walked along the suits in the fitting shop. At her waist, where her rail was cut for a door, he stopped. There were three great iron legs folded up under her, into wells in her bottom, on rams, that nobody would have noticed who was not looking at a ship the way Zale was looking at her. He looked at those for a long time. Then he walked on to her stern, and stood under it on the quay, and looked up.

Emeric came and stood beside him.

For a while neither of them said anything. The river went by under the quay. A long way off, on the island, a dog was barking at something and then stopped.

"I saw you," Zale said.

He said it quite quietly, looking up at her stern.

"At the island. On the dawn boat. I told you. I stood at the back and looked at the ridge till I couldn't see it." He did not take his eyes off the ship. "I didn't tell you the rest, because it was the kind of thing I tell in taverns and nobody believes. There was something in the sky. Out west, over the island, where it was still dark. Long. Black. It came up off the rock, low, and it hung there a moment, and it went up, and it was gone. Up. Not away. Up." He was quiet. "I thought it was a cloud. There weren't any clouds. I thought it was a bird, a big one, close. It wasn't close. I thought about it on every boat from there to Merrow. I thought I'd dreamed it on no sleep and bad water." He put his hand out and laid it flat on the side of the ship, on the black pitched planks under her stern, the way a man lays his hand on a horse. "It was this."

"It was this."

"You were on it."

"I was a hundred feet under it on a line, going up. I saw a boat pull out of the anchorage under the east fort. Supply boat, on the tenth day. There was a man at the stern rail who wasn't rowing. He was looking at the sky." Emeric looked at his brother's hand on the ship. "I thought he was a rower resting. I didn't think about him again for a year and a half. Ferra wrote him in the log. The hour, and the heading, and a man standing at the stern, facing the sky."

Zale turned his head and looked at him.

"You knew," he said. "When I told you. On the gallery. You asked me when. You went and looked."

"I went and looked."

"And you said nothing."

"I couldn't say anything without this." Emeric put his own hand on the ship, beside his brother's. "So I said nothing."

Zale looked at the two hands on the planks for a while. Then he laughed, very softly, not the easy laugh or the other one; a third one Emeric had never heard, which seemed to surprise Zale as much as anybody.

"I went round the world," he said. "Twice. The long way. Past the edge of both maps. And the strangest thing I ever saw in my life was my little brother going up a rope into the sky off a rock I'd been told I'd be killed for climbing."

"The world is such a small place," Emeric said.

"It's not. I've been round it." Zale took his hand off the ship. "You're the small place."

They had it out in the deckhouse of the big ship, because it was the nearest room with a door, and Ward stood outside it on the deck in the dark and let them.

It was not loud. Zale was not a loud man, when it mattered, and Emeric had never been one. It was two men sitting on the benches either side of a small table in a ship's cabin with a lamp stone on it, with a great deal of room between them that neither of them could get across.

"How long," Zale said.

"The ship? Years." He meant the Discovery, lying at the next berth. "Longer than you've been home, both times. This one, the big one, three summers. There are two more. One's on the river. One's the bones in the dock."

"Years." Zale was looking at the lamp. "When I came home the first time. Two years ago. Six weeks in the small room reading you the margins. You had two of these lying in this yard the whole time."

"Yes."

"And the flying. The rope off the jetty. You'd been doing that for years too. And I sat in the small room and you told me about the delving, you took me into the grey place and you told me that, and you didn't tell me this."

"No."

"Mother." Zale stopped. "She's been up in one. Hasn't she."

"She flew home to Emberfell in that one." Emeric nodded at the wall, and the Discovery beyond it.

"She never wrote it." Zale said it to the lamp. "Two years of letters to the ports, and she never wrote it. She wrote about the sulphur." He looked up. "Why?"

Emeric had known it would come to that, and had known for a week what he would say, and it did not help, now it was here.

"Because you weren't here," he said. "Because you'd been gone half your life and you were going again, in six weeks, by sea, along coasts where anybody could buy a man a drink. I told you about the delve because I needed you to read the margins faster and I couldn't do it without. I didn't need you to know about the ship, so I didn't tell you." He made himself look at his brother. "And because a secret held by more people is a weaker secret. That's not about you. That's arithmetic. Every man who knows it is another door it can go out of. There are more than forty men in this barony who know these ships fly, and I know every one of their names, and every name is a door."

"I'm not a name." Zale said it very quietly. "I'm not forty men. I'm your brother."

"I know."

"Do you know why I came home? The first time?" Zale was still looking at the lamp. "Not the book. The book was the excuse. I heard in a port on the other side of the western sea that my little brother had been sent south to a barony full of plague and dead men, for saying the wrong thing at a banquet. And I laughed, I told you, I laughed. That's Emeric, he always did what he was told." He put his hand flat on the table. "And then I heard about the mines. And the war. Some lord's coalition, three baronies against one, and the one was you. And I thought: he's down there on his own. The one of us who never could do anything. The one Father kept by him because he couldn't be trusted out of the house. The one who was sick on the barge." He looked up. "I came home to look after you. That's why I came home. To be the one of us who knows where the door is, for the one who never did. And I got to the gate, and Mother said you'd tell me what you wanted me to know."

Emeric did not say anything.

"And now I find out," Zale said, "that you've got a flying ship. Two. Four. That you've had them for years. That you fly yourself. That you went to my island, that I sat under for ten days and couldn't climb, and went up the rock in the dark and off it into the sky while I stood on a boat and thought I'd dreamed you. That you don't need looking after by anybody. That you never did." He took his hand off the table. "So what did I come home for?"

"I don't know," Emeric said. It was the truth.

They sat there. The lamp stone burned on the table between them without a flicker, as lamp stones did.

"I'm glad you did," Emeric said, after a while.

"That doesn't help."

"I know it doesn't."

Zale got up. He stood for a moment in the low cabin with his head bent under the beams, and then he went to the door, and stopped there, the way he stopped at every door, and looked back.

"It's still my book," he said.

"It's still your book."

"Then I'm still coming." He went out onto the deck, past Ward, who did not move, and down the gangway, and along the quay, and Emeric sat in the cabin and listened to his footsteps go, and did not follow, because there was nothing he could say that he had not already said, and none of it had helped.

When he came out on deck, Ward was still standing at the rail.

"He'll come round," Ward said.

"Will he?"

"No." Ward looked out at the dark river. "But he'll come."

She got her name at the long table three nights later, with everybody there, and it was Osric's fault.

They had got to her in the plan. The long room in the east range was full, fuller than it had been since the palace took the government: the six at the table, and Zale, who had come to it and sat by the door opposite Ward and said nothing about the quay, and Osric and Ned Tallis and Fletcher at the far end, and the six men who were going to fly to the island standing along the wall because there were not enough chairs. Ferra had the plan on the table in front of her, in the book, and had got as far as the second ship, and her pen had stopped over the line, because the line in the book said hull two, and had said it for three years, and Ferra did not like a line that said a thing's number where its name should be.

"She'll want a name," Ferra said. "Two ships, six nights. If Ned calls for the Discovery on the lamp, and the Discovery is in the band and hull two is on the flats, somebody's going the wrong way."

"Call her the Barn," said Osric, from the end of the table.

Everybody looked at him.

"It's in the record," Osric said. He had a sheet in front of him, one of Ferra's copies, from the box; he had asked for it that morning, and Emeric had wondered why. He picked it up now and read from it, in the high carrying voice of a clerk at the assize, with a great deal of relish. "Garret Oakes, scout of the duke's household horse, being posted on the hill north of the stream, saw a thing pass between him and the stars. He swears that it was longer than a tithe barn. That it made no sound. That it went lower than a hawk flies at the end of its stoop." He put the sheet down. "Sworn in court. Longer than a tithe barn. That's her, that is. That's the only true thing anybody outside this room has ever said about her. Put it in the book."

Some of the men along the wall laughed. One of them, Garrick, who had been on the Hod with Armand, said, "The Tithe Barn," trying it, and two more laughed.

Ned Tallis did not laugh. Ned had flown her more nights than anybody, and had been at her rail over the hill north of the stream on the night Garret Oakes saw her, with his heart in his mouth. He looked at Osric down the table with the long patient look of a man who has been flying the same ship too long to find her funny.

"She's not a barn," he said. "A barn stands in a field. She's never stood still in her life." He put his big hands flat on the table. "She carries. She goes out empty and comes home full and goes out again. She's a wain." He said it as if that were the end of it. "A barn's where you put the harvest. A wain's what brings it in."

There was a small silence.

"The Wain," said Ferra, and wrote it.

"There's a wain in the stars," Fletcher said, from the end. He said it to nobody, the way Fletcher said things, looking at his hands. "The seven. The ones that go round the pole. Old men on the coast call it the Wain. It goes round all night and never sets." He looked up. "Good name for a thing that works at night."

Ferra had written it. In the book, on the line that had said hull two, with hull two struck through once, neatly, so it could still be read. And beside it, in the margin, in the smaller hand she kept for the things that were true and lawful and nobody's business: NKC-2. The ledger line for the kept gravity stone, as the Crown's own first-purchase right required every kept stone to have. That was the only place those letters would ever be written.

The men along the wall called her the Tithe Barn for the whole of the six nights. Ferra never wrote that down.

Then the plan. Emeric gave it himself, standing, with his hand on the book.

"Six nights," he said. "Dark of the moon, or near it. Two ships. The Discovery goes into the band over the sea at night and stays there. Six days and six nights. She never comes down below the band while the island's under her, not once, not for anything. She's the house. Imani's on her, and the vial rig, and the galley and the bunks, and every suit heart on this campaign is filled on her every dawn." He looked at Osric. "Osric flies her. When I'm not aboard, what Osric says aboard her goes, except about anybody's body. About anybody's body, what Imani says goes."

"The Wain," he said, and nodded down the table at Ned, who did not quite smile, "is the one that comes down. She lives in the band by day with the Discovery, and she comes down at night, low, over the dunes, over the outcrops. Ned flies her. Fletcher's aboard. Tobe's aboard to push charge into a rail by hand if a rail goes. She hangs over the cave mouth on the first night to put us down. She comes down to the dunes to load every night, low, on the hose. She lands on her legs twice, on the third night and the sixth, on the flats, for the bales. On the sixth night she takes us off." He turned a page in the book. "Nobody's on the surface by day. Nobody. Not a man, not a lamp, not a footprint. Everybody who's on the island at night is in the band by dawn or under the ground."

He looked along the men against the wall.

"Extraction," he said. "Two teams of three. You go down off the Discovery's boat door after full dark and fly west to the dunes. You know the dune. You know the hatch. Dig to it, run the fan, send the bird down first, and no flame, ever; you know why. When the Wain's over you, one of you takes the hose's end off her winch and couples it to the pump at the hatch, and one of you runs the pump, and one of you stands on the dune and watches the ridge. When she's full, the hose drains back down into the drum before it's uncoupled, so there's nothing on the sand in the morning. Then you close the hatch and dress the dune, every night, so that it looks in the morning the way it did the morning before. After the oil, the bales, off the flats, up on slings. Before first light you're back on the boat door." He looked at the six. "You'll be doing it in the Skins. No plate. No rebreather. You don't go under anything, so you don't need one. Rylan goes down with you in his suit and stands on the high ground, and if Kesrath comes west, which they never have, it's Rylan's job to see them first, and it's yours to leave. Not fight. Leave."

"The cave mouth." He turned to Ward. "Ward, in his suit, and Wes at the line. Every night, down after dark, up before dawn. Ward holds the mouth. He's got a lamp for the ship and a line into the cave, and the line's the only thing that talks to us." He looked at Ward. "Ward delves now. Everybody here knows that, or does now. It doesn't reach through rock. It reaches a few paces, in air, if he's lucky. So he talks to the ship on the lamp and to us on the four pulls, the same as if he didn't."

Ward nodded once. He had said the same thing himself, at this table, a week ago, before anybody could say it for him.

"And the cave," Emeric said. "Five of us. Me, Ferra, Zale. Sir Aled and Sir Gavin, who'll hold the dry place past the first water and run the hand lines and haul. We go down the first night off the Wain on a line, and cross the first water, and make camp on the far side of it. And we stay there." He looked round the table, at all of them, one face at a time, the way he did when he wanted a thing argued with and did not expect it to be. "Every one of us. For all six nights. We don't come up to sleep in the ship. We don't come up at all. Anybody who goes into the water doesn't go into the band after. Not for a day and a night. Not after five nights of it. Fliers don't dive. Divers don't fly."

"So we don't come up," said Zale, from the door.

It was the first thing he had said at the table.

"Not till it's over," Emeric said.

Zale looked at him across the length of the room. Then he nodded, once, the way Ward nodded, and folded his arms, and looked at the ceiling, and did not say anything else.

It was one of the six along the wall who asked the other thing, and Emeric had known one of them would, and had not known which.

It was Jibari. He was the youngest of the six, a rigger off the Discovery's first crew, who had come to Kaldo off a merchant's boat years ago and stayed, and gone up the Discovery's shrouds in a gale with nobody holding a rope, and had been picked, like the other five, for loyalty, and strength, and the kind of patience that does a dull thing well every night. He had been quiet all evening. When Emeric had finished, and the room was breaking up into talk, he said from the wall, not loudly:

"Highness. Can I ask a thing?"

The room went quiet again, because he had said it into a gap.

"Go on."

"When Tobe pushed the rail on the Hod," Jibari said. "When she was going down, and he put his hands on it and pushed, and she held. I was there. I saw him. His hands were white after, for a day." He looked at his own hands. "And Cade can do it. And Garrick. And me. You told us, on the ship, what the band does to a man. That it puts something in him. That we had a right to know it. You said that." He looked up. "But I could do it before I ever went up in her. A bit. Not much. The first time I held a stone in the rigging, it was warm in my hand before anybody told me how to make it warm." He looked at Emeric steadily. "It's not the band. Not all of it. What's the water in the towers been doing to us?"

Nobody at the table moved.

Emeric looked at Imani. She was looking at Jibari, and her face had gone very still. She did not look at Emeric. She did not need to.

He had said, on the ship, a year and more ago, to the whole crew in the Discovery's long cabin in the band: you have a right to know what's in you. He had meant it. He had meant it about the band. He had not said it about the towers, because the towers were not his to say alone, and because nobody had asked.

Somebody had asked.

"It's been making you what you are," he said.

He told them. Standing, at the head of the table, with the book under his hand and the six men along the wall and Zale by the door and Osric and Ned and Fletcher at the end, who had not known either. He told them what was in the cage under the hatch in every tower over every well on the island, and in the first quarter's tower in New Kaldo, and why. That the water carried a little of the medium, a very little, every day, the way the air in the band carried a great deal of it. That Imani set it, and signed for it, and read a jug of it off every tap on the island on the first of every month, and had since the towers went up. That everybody in Kaldo who drank from the towers had been drinking it for years, and was the better for it, as far as anybody could tell, which was as far as Imani's books went, and Imani's books went a long way. That it was why a rigger could warm a stone in the shrouds before anybody showed him how. That it was why Tobe and Cade could hold a rail. That it was why he had picked these six and not six others: because they had drunk it longest, and had the most in them to push with, if a heart died over the sea at night and there was nothing else.

He did not soften it. He did not say that it was harmless, because he did not know that; he said what Imani's books said, and that they went back years and not lifetimes. He did not say that he was sorry, because he was not, entirely, and Jibari would have known.

When he had finished the room was very quiet.

"Everybody," Jibari said. "In the town."

"Everybody who drinks from the towers."

"And they don't know."

"They know it's good water. They know it's the barony's water, and it's treated. That's true. They don't know what it's treated with." Emeric did not look away from him. "The six at this table knew. Now you do. It doesn't leave this room. Not because it's wrong, but because it's the one thing in Kaldo that, if the Church heard it in the wrong words, would hang the woman who signs for it."

Jibari was quiet for a long moment.

Then Imani said, from her chair, level: "Ask me. Anything you want to know about it. What it does, what I've seen, what I haven't. I set it. I'll tell you." She looked along the wall at all six of them. "Any of you. Tonight, or on the ship, or when you're home. I've got the books. They're yours to read."

Jibari looked at her. Then he looked at his hands again, the rigger's hands, the ones that had warmed a stone in the shrouds before anybody had told him how.

"I'll read them," he said. "When we're home." And then, after a moment: "Thank you for telling me, Highness. On the ship, you said we had a right. I didn't know if it was true."

"It was true," said Emeric. "I was late."

Across the room, by the door, Zale was looking at his brother with an expression Emeric could not read at all, and did not try to.

They went down the river the next night on the tide, both ships, under tow behind the Marsh Hen as far as the bar, as the Discovery had always gone: two hulls in the dark behind a coaster's lamp, a long low one and a high ugly one, that nobody on the banks had ever looked at twice. Past the bar the coaster cast them off and turned for home, and the two ships lay on the black sea a mile apart with their lamps out, rolling a little, and the Discovery's legs came down out of her bottom on their rams with a long hiss, and the boards on their feet took the water.

Zale was at her rail. He had come aboard her with the rest and gone straight to the rail by the boat door, the nearest place to the ladder, and stood there, and had not moved since the tow. Emeric came and stood beside him.

"Hold the rail," he said.

"I always hold the rail," said Zale. "I've been holding rails since I was twelve."

"Hold it anyway."

He did not go to the helm. Osric had the levers, with his hands on them, and Emeric stood at the rail beside his brother with his own hand on the rail and his mind on the stones, all of them, the whole bank of them in her keel and her ribs, and told them what they had been told a hundred times.

She ran. The engine took her forward on the black water, faster, and the boards on her legs began to lift her, and she came up onto them with a shudder and a hiss of water, and the hull was out of the sea and riding over it on the legs, smooth, fast, the waves going by under her with a long tearing sound like cloth. Zale's hand went white on the rail. Then Osric put her lift up, and the stones in her keel took her weight, all of it, and the legs came up out of the water and the sound stopped. All at once. There was only the wind.

Zale made the sound he had made on the jetty. Then he stopped making it.

They went up. Not fast. The sea went away under them, black, with the Wain's shape on it a mile off, and then the Wain was coming up too, a darker darkness against the dark water, rising, as Ned brought her lift up; and then the sea was a long way down and there was nothing below them but a faint grey where the swell caught the starlight, and nothing above them but stars, more stars than there had ever been, and the cold was coming.

The cold came first, as it always did. It came up through the deck and down the rail and into the hands, a clean hard cold that had nothing to do with the sea's. Zale put his free hand inside his coat. Somebody brought him a second coat and he put it on without looking at who. His breath smoked. Then it began to come short, a little, as the air thinned, and he drew it deeper without noticing he was doing it, and looked round at Emeric, and Emeric nodded: yes, that too.

And then they were in the band.

Emeric felt it come, as he always did: the medium thickening round the ship like water round a diver going down, until it lay on everything, heavy and slow and sweet, and every stone in her keel drank it and eased, the way a man eases in a warm bath. He had felt it a hundred times and it still did the same thing to him every time. He watched his brother feel it.

Zale could not know what it was. He had no word for it, and nobody had given him one yet. He stood at the rail in two coats with the stars all round him and his breath short and his hand white on the wood, and something came into his face that Emeric had last seen on it on the jetty, hanging on Ward's rope over the black lake with his arms out: not fear. Something before fear, or after it.

"What is that?" Zale said. His voice was very low. "Emeric. What's that? It's like, it's like the air's gone thick. Like honey. Like standing in the sea." He took his hand off the rail and held it out in front of him in the dark and turned it over, slowly, looking at it, as if the air on his palm had a weight. "Like a warm hand on the back of your neck."

"It's the band."

"What's the band?"

"It's where we live for the next six days." Emeric looked out at the dark. Far off to the east, on the edge of the world, there was a line of grey where the dawn was going to be. "It's why any of this works. The ships. The suits. The lamp in your hand on the jetty. It's up here. It's always been up here, over everybody's heads, three miles up, as long as there's been a sky. And nobody knew." He looked at his brother. "I'll tell you all of it. Tonight, if you want. We've got a day in the band before the first night's dark, and a long cabin with a table in it."

Zale did not answer. He was looking at the grey line in the east, going pale, and at the sea three miles under it, and at the other ship off the Discovery's beam, a black shape against the grey, riding the band with them.

"A small place," he said, at last, very quietly. Not to Emeric. To the dawn.

The sun came up out of the sea while he was still saying it, and lit the underside of the Wain gold.
