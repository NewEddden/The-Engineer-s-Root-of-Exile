Chapter 234 - Caulked

Ned Tallis came down the reed bank on the nineteenth with his left knee going stiff under him, as it did when the lake fog came in at night, and took the Hod's plank one board at a time and pretended he was not.

Nobody said anything about it. Cade, behind him with the lamp, slowed to his pace without seeming to, and Tobe, at the top of the plank, put a hand out for the lamp and not for Ned; and Ned reached the deck and stood on it a breath with his weight on the right leg, looking at the hatch cover as if he had come aboard to inspect it, and then went aft into the house and took his place at the helm post under the cupola and put his hand on the lever. His hand did not care about the fog.

It was the first Saturday back. Armand had been up on the Wednesday in the ship; this was the Hod, and the Hod was his, the chair in her after corner with the arms and the strap and the window Fletcher had cut two fingers lower than it was cut the first time. He came down the bank on Ward's arm in Wexler's coat, and at the foot of the plank he let go of the arm, and took the plank on his stick, slowly, with the old dignity, and nobody helped him either.

Imani went up behind him with her bag and her clock. She settled him, as she settled him every time: the strap across his lap, the coat over his knees, the mask on its hook by his ear with the candle lit in its cup; then the clock on the shelf at his elbow, face out, where he could see it and she could see it and neither of them could pretend about it.

Ward stayed on the bank with Ferra and the book. Three and one more; Imani was the one more, and the house only took six.

"Lever," said Emeric, from the trap.



She went up as she always went up now, without fuss, the reeds sliding off her bottom and the lake falling away, Ned's hand easy on the lever and the box at his feet humming to itself. At a mile the cloud took the panes and gave them back wet, and Ned said "Seal," and Tobe went round the door's dogs with his palm, and the pump began under the floor. At three miles the frost came into the corners of the glass. At four the Band came.

It came in through the house as it had come in every flight since the launch: through the oak and the felt and the pitch and the oak again, through the seams of the door and the leather of its seal, as warmth comes through a wall in a summer kitchen, everywhere at once and from nowhere in particular. Emeric had drawn it to do that. He had drawn iron on the first sheet in June and oak on the second, and written two words in the margin beside the oak, and Fletcher had built what the margin said; and the Band came in, and filled the house, and filled everybody in it, and in the after corner an old man sat with his right hand quiet on the brass rail, as it had been quiet since Wednesday's flight, and would be now for days more.

Armand looked out of his window at the dark. He did not say anything. He never did, at the top; he had said, the first time, that it was quiet, and after that he had not wasted the quiet on saying so.

The clock went round.

Emeric sat on the floor by the trap with his back to the wall and his sense out through the whole of her, the lift stones drinking and the air stones holding her nose into the westerly and the racks asleep under the iron deck; and the house full of the Band, and the five people in it full of it too, each a note he knew. Tobe's, steady and low. Cade's, quick, a little tight. Imani's, grown so much in five years that he no longer thought of it as a commoner's at all. Armand's, the great still lake, very nearly full, for an hour. And Ned's, at the helm, the smallest in the house by a long way, a lighterman's, who had never carried a stone in his life until the summer.

At an hour and ten minutes Ned put the back of his wrist to his upper lip, and took it away, and looked at it.

There was a red line on the skin. Not much. He looked at it without alarm, as at a fraying rope he had been told to expect; and then he wiped the wrist on his coat and put his hand back on the air lever and went on holding her into the wind.

Imani had seen it. She was across the house in a breath, with a cloth out of her bag, and she put it into his hand and held his wrist with her other hand, two fingers on the inside, without asking.

"It's the cold," said Ned.

"It's the Band." She did not take her fingers away. "You hold the least in this house. You bleed first. It's in my book." She looked over her shoulder at the clock on Armand's shelf. "An hour and a quarter I've had in my head since July. You're five minutes early. It's the knee."

"What's my knee to do with my nose?"

"You've been standing on one leg since the reeds to spare it. It costs." She let go of his wrist. "Pinch it. Not back, forward. Hold it." She went back across the house and stood by Armand's chair and looked at the clock, and at the hour and a quarter, to the minute, she said: "Down."

Ned put the lever back with the cloth still pinched to his nose. The Hod went down.

Armand did not say anything on the way down. Usually, when the Band fell away above them, he said something dry about the time, or the cold, or Cade's singing, or Imani's tyranny, which he called her tyranny to her face; it was how he took the hour away from himself, by making a joke of the person who ended it. This time he sat with his hands on the rail and looked out of his lowered window at the cloud coming up to meet them, and said nothing at all: an hour and a quarter, cut to the minute for a lighterman's nose, and he would not make a joke of it.

That was worse. Emeric would rather have had the joke.

On the reed bank, at one in the morning, Ferra wrote it in the crew book and then in her own: under A. ALOFT, the date and the hour; under THE HOD, the same, and then, after it, small, N. bled. 1 h 10. She looked at Ned's cloth, which he had folded into his coat as if it were a thing he might want again, and wrote nothing about that.



Fletcher had the Hod up on the old slipway on the Sunday morning, as he had her up every Sunday since the launch, to look at her bottom and her rivets and the seals of her legs. He was under her when Emeric came down the shore track, on his back on a board on rollers with a lamp beside his head, tapping rivets with a little hammer and listening to each one as a doctor listens to a chest.

He came out from under her when he saw Emeric's boots. He did not get up. He lay on his board on the slipway looking up at the house on her stern, the low oak house with its bellied walls and its curved roof and the cupola like a glass bell over the helm.

"Ned bled," he said.

"A little."

"He came past my door at two with a rag in his coat and his knee gone. My wife gave him a cup of something." Fletcher kept looking at the house. "And the old man would stay up there till the sun came if the healer let him. Ned says he sat looking at his own hand all the way down."

"Yes."

Fletcher was quiet. He was a man who thought lying down, Emeric had learned, as other men thought walking; the best of the K Discovery's legs had been drawn in Fletcher's head on his back under her keel.

"You put iron round the racks," he said. "In July. Or I did, and you let me. And Orrin put a stone in the iron and it went dead to him, a pace off, as if I'd put it behind a wall." He turned his head on the board and looked at Emeric. "Iron keeps it out of a stone."

"Iron keeps it out of a stone."

"Then why haven't you put iron round the men?"

Emeric opened his mouth, and found nothing in it.

Ned had come down the slipway behind him while they talked, with his hands in his pockets and his cap on the back of his bald head, walking carefully on the left leg; and he stood looking up at the house beside Fletcher's board, at the bellied oak walls, and nodded slowly, as if Fletcher had said a thing about a boat that he had been waiting forty years to hear somebody say.

"Caulk her," said Ned.

Fletcher looked up at him.

"It's what you do," said Ned. "You've a boat that takes water. You can bail her. Every hour, all your life, a man on the bucket, and you'll get where you're going wet and tired and the man on the bucket will hate you." He took a hand out of his pocket and laid it flat on the Hod's iron flank. "Or you caulk her. You don't bail a boat you could caulk." He looked at Emeric. "That's a bucket, that clock of the healer's. It's a good bucket. I'd not go up without it. But it's a bucket."

Emeric stood on the slipway between the shipwright on his board and the lighterman with his hand on the hull, and saw it.

All of it at once; and it cost him, because he saw at the same time how long it had been there to see.

He had built the house as he remembered the airliners of the other life being built: a pump, a seal, a pressure held inside against the thin cold sky. And no airliner he had ever sat in let its cabin go to the outside air, or held it at the ground either. They held it somewhere between, on purpose, a number a man set on a dial. He had known that. He had built that half. And the other air, the one that was not air, he had treated as the old climbers treated weather: something that came in through the walls whether you liked it or not, that you could only meet with a clock. Oak or iron. Open or shut. He had given the mana two settings and called it done, and never once thought of a valve.

A man from a world of valves.

"Yes," he said. "Caulk her."

"With what?" said Fletcher, from the board.

"With iron. Between the oaks, where the pitch is." He looked at the house. "Not shut. If you shut her, Armand gets nothing, and he goes up for the Band, not the view. Iron keeps it out, all but a seep at the door. Then you let some back in. A little. On purpose." He was already drawing it in his head. "The pump's drawing through the crown. Put something in the pipe between the crown and the house that takes the mana out of the air on its way through and leaves the air. And a way round it, with a valve on it, so you can choose how much goes round."

"Something that takes mana out of air," said Fletcher.

"A stone."

Fletcher thought about that, on his back.

"A stone takes it till it's full," he said slowly. "Then it stops." He had learned that at the racks too; he had watched them fill. "Then it's a full stone."

"Then it's a full stone," said Emeric. "And you take it out and put in an empty one, and the full one goes down the trap into the racks, and it's cargo. Call it a scrubber. A mana scrubber."

Ned laughed. It was a short laugh, through his nose, which hurt him; he put a hand to it.

"The bucket," he said, "and the water in it's worth money." He shook his head. "Caulk her and sell the bilge." He looked at Fletcher. "How long?"

Fletcher sat up on his board at last and looked at the house, pricing it in days.

"Six," he said. "Five, if Bram doesn't argue."



Bram argued.

He argued about the case, which he said would crack the first time Cade dropped a spanner on it, until Ivo said it would not if it was enamelled and kept warm and not dropped on by Cade, and Bram said that was three ifs. Then he argued about the cartridge. Emeric had drawn one tray of spent stones in a frame that slid in and out of the case like a drawer; Bram, at his bench in the long shop on the Monday morning with the drawing flat under both hands, said it would want changing at four miles by a man in a mask with gloves on in the dark, and so must be two.

"Two drawers," he said. "One in, one out. A man pulls the full one and the empty's already working. He doesn't stand at four miles with the pipe open to the sky waiting for his mate to find the other tray in a locker." He drew it, with a stub of pencil, a second drawer under the first, and a lever between them like a railway's points. "Throw it, the air goes through the bottom one. Throw it back, the top."

"Two," said Emeric.

"And a rail."

"What rail?"

Bram looked at him over his spectacles as at a man who had built a furnace without a flue.

"You're putting iron round the house," he said. "Round the heat stones under the floor. Round Tess's little spinning thing and its eight trim stones. Round the pump's stone and the lamp and the stone in the latch that keeps the bellows full." He tapped the drawing. "What fills them, up there? Now? The Band, through the oak. You've had them living off the sky since August and never once had to charge them." He put the pencil down. "You put iron round them, they starve. In a month the house is cold and the pump's slow and the latch drops on a full ship for no reason but its bellows went thirsty."

Emeric looked at the drawing. Bram was right. Bram was usually right about the second thing, the thing that came after the clever thing.

"So you run a rail," said Bram, "from the drawer that's working to the house's own stones. Short. Sleeved. The stones in the drawer catch it out of the air, and the stones in the house drink it off the drawer, and the house eats what the crew would have breathed." He drew the rail, a short line from the drawer to a box marked STONES. "It'll want watching. If the house drinks more than the drawer catches, the drawer never fills, and if the drawer never fills it never wants changing, and nobody'll know which is which." He thought. "A gauge. On the drawer."

"A gauge on the drawer," said Emeric, and Bram grunted and went to the stores for the brass.

So there were six days, and they were full.

Fletcher's men took the inner oak off the house on the Monday, board by board, numbered in chalk; and under it was the felt, and under the felt the pitch that had been poured hot between the walls in July. They scraped it out with knives and wire brushes, two days of it, and burned it in a brazier on the shore, and the reed cutters on the far bank complained to their wives. Then the iron: sheet iron from the mill, thin as a good knife's blade, cut on Bram's shears and bent to the belly of the walls on Fletcher's own former, every sheet laid edge over edge and soldered along every seam by two of Tess's lamp shop men who could run a seam as fine as a hair. Up the walls, over the roof, round the frames of the windows and the cupola's foot. The glass needed nothing; glass held mana, and had been holding it back all along. The door was the one place the iron could not go all the way. It had its leather seal, and the seal would seep a little, and Emeric let it.

"You want it to seep?" said Fletcher, on the Tuesday, with the soldering iron's smoke going up past his face.

"I don't want it to be nothing. Nothing would mean I'd got the drawing wrong somewhere and didn't know where."

Fletcher looked at the leather seal for a while. Then he went back to his seam, and Emeric heard him say to the lamp shop man, quite quietly, that the duke wanted a leak in it on purpose so that he could find the other leaks, and that it was the most sensible thing the duke had said all month.

Ivo enamelled the case, three coats, and Abloh made it a felt coat with a pocket for a walnut of fire stone and said nothing about it, which from Abloh was a comment. Bram fitted the drawers on brass runners, one above the other, each a shallow tray of the works' spent stones laid on edge like books on a shelf, sixty to a drawer, the grey empties the split days had been draining into all summer. His lever sat between them, and his gauge on the front of each, a needle on a dial, coded by Emeric over two nights to read how full the drawer was and not any one stone, which had cost him a headache and one dial thrown at the wall.

The bypass was brass. A pipe the thickness of a thumb that left the main pipe above the case and came back into it below, so that a little of the raw air from the crown could go round the drawers and into the house untouched; and in the middle of the bypass a valve with a screw stem and a square head, and over the square head a brass cap that locked.

"One key," said Emeric, and gave it to Imani.

She looked at it in her palm. Then she put it on the chain round her neck with the key of the dispensary press, and did not say anything, which from Imani meant she had understood exactly what he was giving her and would have refused it if he had given it to anybody else.

And the probes: two spent lamp stones out of the charging room's bucket, each in a little cage of wire, the kind the K Discovery hung below her cutwater, grey at the ground and glowing in the Band. One went on the house wall beside Armand's window, inside the iron. The other went on a pole through the roof beside the cupola, where the man at the helm could look up at it through the glass.

"And between them?" said Imani.

"Something to say how far apart they are."

She made that herself. She went back through her own book on the Tuesday night, through every flight on the ship since July where she had called the probe's shade every half mile up and every half mile down; and she sat in the dispensary with Tess's watercolours and a strip of card from Ferra's stock and painted the shades in a row, ten of them, from the grey of the lake at night to the glow of the Band's foot; and in the morning she held the card up beside the probe on the house wall, in the shed's dimness and then outside in the light, and frowned, and took it back, and painted two of them again.

"A shade card," said Emeric, when she brought it the second time.

"It's a card with colours on it," said Imani. "Call it what you like."



On the Wednesday the Hod lay in pieces on the slipway, and Armand flew in the ship.

Emeric took him, with Imani and Ward; the hour and a quarter, the iris, the hands still on the brass. On the bank afterward Armand stood a while looking along the shore toward the old slipway in the dark, where the Hod sat on her cradle with her house open to the sky and her iron in a stack beside her.

"What are you doing to my chair?" he said.

"Nothing to the chair. To the walls."

"I liked the walls."

"You'll like these better."

Armand looked at him sideways. "I have been told that by you about a great many things," he said, "and I have usually ended by liking them, and been very cross about it for a month first." He turned his stick on the bank. "Imani tells me I'm to have a pipe."

"A mask on a pipe of your own. Off the crown, before the drawers. The raw Band. A valve at your elbow, under her hand."

"And the rest of them get what?"

"A fraction. Whatever she sets."

"And I get the whole."

"For as long as she lets you."

Armand thought about that.

"Then I shall be a very old man breathing out of a pipe in a corner," he said, "while four young ones sit about in a comfortable fraction and look at me." He did not sound as if he minded. "How long will she let me?"

"Ask her."

"I did," said Armand. "She said three hours, and that the number was for my heart and not for the Band, and that if I asked her again she would make it two." He began to walk up the bank. "I've not asked her again."



On the Thursday Emeric went up in the lift hull with Linus, because the Hod was in pieces and the lift hull was the one ship in Kaldo allowed to be seen.

Old Edrin flew her from the casting seat with his eyes half shut and his hand flat on the plate, as calm as a man warming his hands. Linus stood at the forward window with a drawing board strapped to his chest and a pencil in his teeth, and did not speak the few miles from the town to the cut; at the cut he said "Lower," and then "Hold," and for an hour they hung two hundred feet over the gorge above the falls while he drew.

Below them the river went over the lip in a single sheet. On the shelf of rock above it, small as beetles, Fennick's bore crew stood round a tripod waiting for the core to come up, and Fennick himself walked the very edge of the gorge with his hands behind his back, closer to the drop than any man had a right to who had already left two fingers to a fuse that was shorter than it said it was.

"He'll be over," said Old Edrin, without opening his eyes.

"He's never over," said Linus, round the pencil. "He's been at the edge of something since he was born." He drew a line. "That's the cut. A wall from that knob to that one, and the whole valley behind it's a lake. Two years."

"More," said Emeric.

"Two years once there's cement," said Linus, and wrote a figure in the corner of the sheet and drew a box round it.

On the way back they came over the head of the bay and the new granaries: four long roofs of new slate on the slope above the water, with a line of carts going in full at one end and coming out empty at the other, the year's harvest going under cover a twentieth at a time, as Ferra had said in the counting room a year ago with her finger on the word.



On the Saturday, the sixth day, with the Hod on the slip on a flying night for the first time since the launch, Imani set the mark.

She set it on the slipway, in the house, with the iron in and the oak back over it and the seams all done, and the drawers in their case, and the bypass in its brass with the cap off and her key on its chain; and she could not set it on the ground, of course, because on the ground there was nothing to set. She could only decide where it would go, on her card. So she sat on the bench along the forward wall with the card on her knee and the drawing of the bypass beside it, and Emeric sat on the floor by the trap with the K Discovery's whole flight log open on his knees, and they argued over what he called the cabin density and she called the dose.

"The top of what's safe," said Emeric.

"Why the top?"

"Because they'll be up there three hours. Four, some nights. Ned's knee. Cade's back, which he doesn't tell anybody about. Tobe's hands, from twenty years of the foundry." He turned a page of the log. "Up there, at the Band's foot, Armand's hands went still. Every ache he had went quiet. If the house gives them a part of that, they come down with nothing hurting and they're not tired at the end of a run. Comfort."

Imani looked at him across the house for a while with the card on her knee.

"You want them grown," she said.

He did not answer at once.

"Say so." She did not raise her voice. "I've read Armand's sheets the same as you. The still crew went up a third and more in five years carrying stones in their hands. Three hours a night in a fraction of the Band is more than a year in the still. You've done that sum. I watched you do it in the long room in June." She put her finger on the card. "You want Ned and Tobe and Cade to come down from four miles twice a week a little bigger than they went up. Comfort's true. That's true as well. Say both."

"Both," said Emeric.

"Thank you." She looked down at the card. "Then I'll grow them slowly." She put her finger on a shade near the dull end, the third of ten, the colour the ship's probe had been at about a mile and a half on the way up, in the cloud, where nobody had ever bled and nobody had ever felt anything. "There."

"That's low."

"It's for Ned." She did not look up. "He holds the least in the house, and he bleeds first, and it's one room with one pipe in it, so it's one mark, and the mark's his. If it's right for Ned it's right for everybody, and the ones who hold more can take more at the seat, on Armand's pipe, under my clock." She looked up then. "You'd have set it for yourself. Or for Armand. You'd have looked at the strongest man in the house and asked what he could stand. That's how the rite's done." She tapped the card. "I set it for the weakest."

Ned, at the helm post with his arms folded, who had been listening to all of it without appearing to, said: "I'll take that as a kindness, Mistress."

"Take it as a dose," said Imani. "It's the same thing up there."



The trial was the last day of the month, the Wednesday, Armand's night.

They went up at ten from the reeds, six of them, with Ward and Ferra on the bank and the lamp. The house smelled of new solder and old oak and Abloh's felt, and it looked exactly as it had before, which unsettled everybody a little going in: the same chair and bench and cupola and windows and the same oak walls, and nothing different but a brass case under the crown's pipe with two drawers and a lever and two dials, a brass pipe round it with a locked cap, and a caged stone on the wall by Armand's window and one on a pole outside.

And Armand's mask, on its hook by his ear, which had grown a pipe of its own, a thin brass one, that ran up the wall beside the case and joined the crown's pipe above it.

"Lever," said Emeric.

At a mile the seal, the dogs, the pump. At two the frost. Emeric sat on the floor by the trap with his back to the wall, where he always sat, and let his sense out through her, and found at once that it was harder.

It went out through the house easily enough, through the people and the bench and the stones in the drawers, sixty and sixty, flat and grey and asleep. Then it came to the wall, and went through, but slowly, and it cost, like putting a hand through cold water to reach the bottom of a tank. Beyond the iron the lift stones and the air stones and the racks were all there, but he had to reach for them, on purpose, and every reach was a small pull behind his eyes, like a headache that had not yet decided. He took it back inside the iron and watched the dials instead. Ned flew by the dials. Ned had always flown by the dials.

At four the Band came.

He saw it come before he felt it. The probe on the pole outside the cupola, which had been grey since the cloud, brightened, all at once, as it had on the ship every time, to the clear hard light of a lamp; Ned saw it through the glass over his head and said "Hm," and his hand was already easing the air lever as the air stones outside felt the Band and pushed. And inside, on the wall by Armand's window, the house probe stayed where it was. Grey. The lake at night.

And he felt the Band, then, and it was not in the house.

It was outside, pressing on the iron as weather presses on a window, the whole thick warm sky on every side and as close as the wall, and not coming in. A thread of it at the door, where the leather was. And in the case under the crown, the top drawer, beginning to wake.

He felt the stones wake one row at a time from the top, as the air came through them, a slow warming like a row of coals catching from one end; and the air that came out of the bottom of the drawer into the house was the air of four miles up, thin and pumped and tasting of copper, and nothing else. The drawer took the Band out of every breath and kept it, and the house got the air.

"It's working," said Tobe, quite quietly, as if he were afraid of waking it.

"Not yet," said Imani.

She had the card on her knee. She had her key in her hand. She knelt at the bypass, took the cap off its square head, and fitted a small brass key-spanner to it, and turned it, a hair, the width of a fingernail, and looked up at the house probe on the wall.

It brightened. Very slightly. From the grey of the lake toward a first faint whiteness, like the inside of a shell.

She turned it another hair, and looked again, and held the card up beside the probe at arm's length, and lowered it, and turned the spanner back a little, less than a hair, and held the card up again, and was still for a breath; and then she put the cap back on the square head and locked it, and put the key back on its chain, and sat back on her heels.

"There," she said.

The house probe glowed on the wall, steady, the colour of the third shade on the card. And Emeric, on the floor by the trap, felt it: the house, round him, with a little of the Band in it. Not the flood of the oak nights. A thin, even warmth to the sense, everywhere at once, as a room is warm after the fire has been banked, the kind of warmth a man stops noticing in a quarter of an hour.

"Masks," said Imani, to Armand. "Yours."

Armand took his mask off its hook and put it to his face, and Imani reached past him and opened the small brass valve at his elbow, a quarter turn; and the raw Band came down the thin pipe from above the case, untouched by any drawer, and into the mask, and into him. Emeric felt it go. He watched the old man's hand on the rail, the right one, which had been moving again since the weekend, and saw it go still, as it went still in the ship and in the oak house, the same; and Armand closed his eyes behind the mask and breathed, long and slow, and did not say anything.

Imani looked at the clock on his shelf and wrote the time in her book.

Then she did the thing Emeric had not known she would do. She took her own mask off its hook by the bench, the mask she had worn at every opening of the door for a summer, and looked at it, and hung it back on its hook, empty; and sat down on the bench with her book on one knee and the card on the other and her fingers on her own wrist, the inside, under her cuff, and counted her own pulse against the clock.

He understood. She had eaten a corner of an oatcake every morning for nine days before she would let a child near one. She was not going to put Ned in a fraction of the Band she had not sat in first, with nothing in her mask and her own heart under her own fingers.

He watched her. He had meant to watch the dials, and did, with half an eye; but what he looked at for the first hour was Imani on the bench, her lips moving very slightly over the count, her eyes going from the clock to the card to the probe and back, frowning when a number did not please her and writing it down anyway. One curl had come out of the strip of cloth over her ear and she had not noticed. She looked as she had looked at the trough with the jar of water held up to the light in the first summer, the morning she had decided to believe him: a woman checking a thing she meant to stake people's lives on, and not trusting anybody else to check it.

At the end of the first hour she looked up from her wrist, and found him looking at her and not at the gauge, and her face said, quite clearly, the gauge.

He looked at the gauge.



Nobody bled.

At an hour and ten minutes, when Ned's nose had started on the Saturday, Ned put the back of his wrist to his lip without thinking, and took it away, and looked at it. Nothing. He looked at it a while longer as if it might change its mind, and then wiped it on his coat out of habit and put his hand back on the lever.

At an hour and a half, Cade, who had been watching the probe on the wall and the brass cap on the bypass in turn for a quarter of an hour, said: "Mistress."

Imani looked up.

"That valve," said Cade. "If a bit's good." He nodded at the locked cap. "I feel grand. I've not felt this grand since I was nineteen. My back's not said a word since we came through the cloud. If a bit does that, a bit more..."

"No," said Imani.

"Just a hair."

"No." She did not raise her voice. "A bit's good. More's my ward, with a basin by the bed. I've had men in it who thought a bit more would do them good." She looked at him. "You'll feel grand for three hours. Then you'll go home and sleep and wake up with your back as it was, because that's what it does, and on Saturday you'll come up and feel grand again. That's what you get. That's all you get." She went back to her book. "And nobody touches that cap but me. Not you, not Ned, not the duke. It's on my chain."

Cade looked at Emeric. Emeric looked at the gauge.

"It's on her chain," he said.

At two hours the top drawer's needle had gone round to the red, and Tobe, who had been watching it like a man watching a pot, threw Bram's lever. The air went through the bottom drawer, and Emeric felt it begin to wake as the first had. Tobe pulled the top drawer out on its runners, sixty stones warm in their tray and every one of them full, as full as anything Orrin's bench turned out. Ned said "Masks," because Ward was not there to say it; Tobe knocked the trap's dogs off, and Cade went down the ladder with the tray into the cold black hold and put the sixty into the racks in their straw, and came back up with an empty tray and slid it into the top runner, and the trap shut, and the masks came off.

"Sixty," said Tobe. "Free."

"Not free," said Emeric. "Bought with the air. You breathed through it and it kept what you'd have got." He looked at the full racks under his feet, beyond the iron, which he could feel only by reaching. "But the works will have them in two days."

Ned said nothing. He was standing at the helm post with his weight on both legs.

Emeric noticed it the way one notices a clock has stopped: by the absence of a thing he had not known he was hearing. Ned had stood at that post for a summer with his weight a little on the right, taking it off the left, a lighterman's lean that looked like habit and was not. He was standing square now. He had been, Emeric thought, for some time.

Ned looked down at his own left leg. Then he bent it. Slowly, all the way, as a man does in the morning to find out how bad it is; and straightened it; and bent it again, quicker, and straightened it, and stood there.

"Hm," he said.

Imani looked up from her book.

"When?" she said.

"I don't know." Ned bent the knee again, as if it belonged to somebody else and he were testing it for them. "After the drawer, maybe. Before." He frowned at it. "It's been talking to me since the fog came in. Three weeks. It's stopped talking."

Imani wrote it down.

"It'll start again," she said. "Tomorrow. On the reeds, in the wet. It's relief, not a mending. You've a knee that's forty-six and has stood on wet decks for thirty-four years, and four miles up won't make it thirty."

"I know that," said Ned. He put his hand back on the lever. He stood square on both legs. "I'll take the relief, Mistress. Wednesdays and Saturdays."



Cade had the long pole out at the hatch at the end of the second hour, on his line, in his mask and his fur, with the net on the end of it that Bram had made him in August after the first three; this one was twice the length of the first, jointed in brass so that it folded, with a hoop on it like a shrimper's and a bag of fine mesh.

He had told nobody he was going to do it. He came in through the door twenty minutes later with the frost on his eyebrows and the pole folded under his arm and the bag at the end of it hanging heavy, and he stood just inside the door with the pump thumping and held it up.

"Seven," he said, through the mask.

They were small, the size of a thumb's end, one the size of a walnut, grey and rough and nothing to look at. To Emeric's sense, inside the iron, they were seven small bright points, warm and full. Wild gravity stones, like the first three in August, that nobody had dug for and nobody had died for.

"There's more," said Cade. "Dozens. I could see the light come off them, with the moon up. They drift. You can't chase them. You wait for one to come past and you sweep." He took the mask off. "I want a longer pole."

"You've a longer pole," said Tobe.

"I want a longer one than this."

Emeric put them in the pocket of his coat, inside the iron, and did not say what they were for. He did not know yet himself.



At three hours Imani shut the valve at Armand's elbow.

She did not say "Down." She looked at the clock, and reached past him, and turned the small brass valve back its quarter turn, and the raw Band stopped coming down the thin pipe into his mask; and she waited while he breathed what was left in it, two breaths, three; and then she took the mask out of his hand, gently, and hung it on its hook.

"Three," she said.

Armand opened his eyes. He looked at her for a breath, and then at the clock, and then out of his lowered window at the dark and the stars and the Band, which he could not see and had never been able to see, and which was there, close as the glass, on the other side of the iron, pressing.

"Three," he said. "Thank you."

He said it the way he said it to a student who had got a hard proof right, dryly, as if it surprised him a little. Emeric, on the floor, let out a breath he had been holding since the Saturday.

"Down," said Imani, to Ned.

Ned put the lever back. And Armand, on the way down, at three miles, when the probe on the pole outside began to dull, said to nobody in particular that Cade's singing at the winch was flat on every third note and that somebody ought to tell him; and Cade, at the winch, said that somebody had been telling him for six years; and Armand said then they had not been telling him properly; and the house laughed, and the old man sat with his still hand on the rail and let them.

They landed by the reeds at a quarter past two.

Nobody had bled. Not a drop, not a trace on a wrist, in three hours at the foot of the Band. Tobe stood on the deck and stretched. Cade had the pole on his shoulder like a fisherman coming home. Ned came down the plank on both legs at the same pace, without seeming to know he was doing it.

Ferra was at the foot of the plank with the book and the lamp. She looked at their faces as they came down, each one, as at a column she meant to add twice, and said nothing, and wrote.

When Emeric reached the bank she was standing a little apart with her own book open on her arm, under the lamp. Under A. ALOFT, the date and the hours. Under THE HOD, the same, the hour up and the hour down; and then a new line, ruled across the whole page, and under the line, in her small square capitals, a new heading.

HOURS AT THE MARK.

And under it: 3. And beside it, in her ordinary hand: Nobody.

She saw him read it, and did not close the book this time.

"Nobody," she said.

"Nobody."

"Then I'll want to know what it costs," said Ferra, "per hour, per man, against the clock." She shut the book. "Tomorrow. Not now. Now you look like you've been reaching through a wall all night."

He had. His head had begun, at last, to decide.



He walked back along the shore with Ward, past the K Discovery's shed.

Its doors were shut. Behind them the ship lay on her cradle in the dark, the long oiled hull and the copper dome and the brass irises, her sealed inner shell inside the outer planking with the matrix ring between, and her saloon where an old man had sat with his hands on the rail and told stories about a girl who hummed. Wood and leather and felt. The Band came into her as it had come into the oak house: everywhere, with a clock to meet it.

He stopped at the shed doors. Ward stopped beside him.

A bigger job. Her weight sat on her lift stones to the ounce, and a skin of iron behind her inner planking was a ton he would have to find somewhere. And she should not carry a box of stones in a drawer. She should carry something that could catch the Band out of her air and keep it, liquid, against the night her stones went dark. He knew what it would be. He did not have it. And after her, hull two, the same, and every hull he would ever build.

Ward waited.

"Not yet," said Emeric, to the shut doors.

"No," said Ward, as if he had been asked, and they walked on.



In the morning there was fog on the lake again.

Ned came down the reed bank at ten to look at his boat, as he did every morning, and took the bank one step at a time with his left knee stiff under him. He stopped at the bottom and looked at it.

Imani was there, on her way up to the town from a birth on the shore farms with her bag on her shoulder. She stopped too, and looked at his knee, and at his face.

"It came back," said Ned.

"I said it would."

"You did." Ned bent the knee, carefully, and winced, and straightened it. "On the reeds. In the wet. Like you said." He looked out at the Hod lying among the reeds in the fog with her caulked house on her stern, quite still. "It was gone for an hour and more. Up there. I'd forgotten what it was like, a knee that didn't talk."

"It'll be gone again on Saturday."

"Saturday," said Ned, and nodded, and went down to his boat on one leg and a half, and Imani went up the hill to the town; and neither of them looked back, and both of them, Emeric thought from the top of the bank, were smiling.
