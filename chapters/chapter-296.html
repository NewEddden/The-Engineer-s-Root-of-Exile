Chapter 296 - No Word for Interest

The capital's pulpits found the usurer's house on the first Sunday of the year, all of them at once, which was how Saban knew somebody had written the sermon.

His man's slip came south on the Monday night express folded inside a seed merchant's price list, and Saban brought it up to the study at eleven with his hat still on, which he never did, and laid it on the table between the lamp and Ferra's book and stood back from it as though it might want to bite.

"Seven churches," he said. "That I know of. The cathedral at the morning office, from the steps, before the bishop's sermon; then the six big parishes before noon. The same text in every one. Out of the Book of the Builders, the one about the nobles who lent to their brothers and took their fields." He touched the slip. "And the same three lines after it, near enough, which is how you know. A house of usury sits in the duke's town, in the old tithe house, where the Light's own tenth was gathered. It lends the Light's people their bread at a price and takes the price back out of their harvest. And every parish in the realm that took its seed from that house in the locust year owes its next harvest to a banker in Kaldo."

"That's article thirteen," said Ferra, without looking up. "Read out in a church."

Emeric read the slip twice. It was in Saban's man's careful upright pencil, and it had the text and the three lines and, under them, a fourth that the man had heard in one church only and written down anyway: that a house which would not say what it took was a house which knew what it took.

"That one's good," he said.

"That one's the cardinal's office," said Saban. "Nobody in a parish wrote that."



He went to the tithe house in the morning anyway and asked Vantic, at her rail, what she needed.

"I told you in December." She turned a page. "Ferra's press for a week from the eleventh, which she's given me. Her best compositor, which she's given me, though she says he sulks. The cheap grey, which Bram's mill is making by the cartload because I paid for it on the Thursday." She wrote a figure. "And for the duke not to answer for the House. Which he's about to offer."

"I was going to offer the Almanac's back page."

"I know you were." She put the pen down and folded her gloved hands on the book, and looked at him properly for the first time, the cool even look that priced a man and told him nothing about the price. "The Almanac is the duke's. Everything with the press's mark on it is the duke's, and the close reads every line of it, and they would read the House's answer on the duke's back page and say, there. The banker hides behind the prince. The prince prints the banker's sermon." She shook her head, once. "The press will print what I bring it, at the board price, as it prints anybody's handbill. With the House's name at the foot and nothing of yours anywhere on it. I am paying Ferra for the paper at the same price she charges the four from the close for theirs. She showed me the receipt book so I could see what it was."

"They'll say it's the same thing. The duke's press."

"They'll say it. They'll have to say it in a church with my contracts in the hands of half the people in it." The corner of her mouth moved, very slightly. "Your Grace has a habit of fixing things that are not broken in a way he can see. This is not broken. It is being preached about. Those are different."

"If it goes wrong," said Emeric.

"Then it will go wrong in my book, under my name." She picked up her pen. "Good morning, Your Grace. Tell Ferra I'll be at the works at six on the eleventh, and that I'd like the compositor sober."



There were three kinds of contract when it was done, and Emeric saw them on the fifteenth, when Ferra brought the first pulls up in her arms still smelling of the ink and laid them on the study table in three piles.

The first pile was the shares. Every loan the House had made to a working thing in Kaldo was written that way and had been since the bank's first morning: the House put in so much and took, in return, a share of what came out. A share of the bricks. A share of the brewery's barrels. A share of the fish off the two new boats at the north quay for five seasons, and nothing in a season the boats lay up; a share of the cheese from a dairy at Milford; a share of the rent from the first block of flats in the garden suburb. And under each, because she had put it there, what the share had come to, year by year, in figures, and in two places what it had come to when it came to nothing.

The second pile was the rent charges. The House bought from a man, for a sum, the right to a fixed rent off a piece of land for a term of years, and the land stayed his, and so did the work; and if he paid the House back the sum he had been given, the charge ended, on any quarter day he liked. There were forty-one of them. Most were the new houses in the garden suburb, bought by men off the works on Friday wages, each with its sum and its rent and its term printed out, and the number of men who had paid off their charge early and owned their house outright: nineteen, by the first of January. Ferra had checked them against the land register herself and written the register's page against each in the margin of her own copy.

The third pile was the bills: a merchant paid the House in Kaldo coin for a paper its coast desk would pay in coast coin a month later, at a different price, and the difference was the House's living. Two hundred and some of them, every price on both sides.

On the front of every sheet, at the top, the same heading in the plainest capitals the works owned: THE CONTRACTS OF THE HOUSE OF DUCATIS IN KALDO, PRINTED ENTIRE. And at the foot of every sheet, smaller, in the same type: The House invites any reader, of any order, to find in these pages the word he has been told is in them. V.

"It's what she said," he said. "There's no interest in it anywhere."

"There's no word interest in it anywhere." Ferra was squaring the bills. "Everybody who's ever owed anybody anything knows what it is. A share that comes to more than you put in. A rent you can stop by paying back. A price at one desk and another at the other." She tapped the six lines. "But it's all written down. What she took and what she lost. The priests have been saying for a week she won't say what she takes. She's said. To the copper. For six years." She looked at him. "That's the bit they didn't think of. They thought she'd hide it. Bankers hide it. That's the whole story about bankers."

"And the seed?"

"The seed's the last sheet." She found it and turned it round.

It was the three hundred parishes, every one, as the close had listed them; but Vantic's list had two more columns than the close's. The first was what each parish had been lent in seed corn at the autumn's price. The second was the House's terms, which were the same for all three hundred: repaid out of the next harvest, in grain, at the next autumn's price, with a share for the House of one part in twenty; and if the harvest failed, nothing that year, and the same terms the year after. And the last column, which was the one Emeric looked at, was headed Repaid at the harvest of year twelve, and it had a figure in it against two hundred and six of the three hundred; and against the other ninety-four, in every one, the same two words. Harvest failed. Waiting.

Brackenfold had two l's.



The House's paper went north on the sixteenth by the morning express in eleven sacks, which Ferra said was a coincidence and nobody believed; and it went the way Kaldo's paper always went, which was everywhere at once and for nothing, or as near nothing as the House could price it. A copper the bundle at the Wayford halt and the Grayrock landing. Free at every parish door that had taken the House's seed, carried there by the House's own clerks in their grey coats on the same trains that had carried the seed in June, and pinned up beside the church board, under the bounty leaflet, where the parish clerk could read it out on the Sunday to anybody who had not yet learned to read it for himself.

Saban's man's slips came back all fortnight. The pulpits had gone quiet on usury. "In the north they read the last sheet," Saban said at the table. "The three hundred. Every parish clerk who took the seed has the sheet on his board with his own parish's line on it and his own figures, what they had and what they paid back. And ninety-four of them have it written there in print that the harvest failed and the House is waiting." He turned his hat once. "A parish priest at Torrow, which is one of the ninety-four, read the usury sermon in December as he was told. He's read the sheet now. Last Sunday he preached on the parable of the forgiving master, who forgave the debt; and his people knew perfectly well whose debt he meant." Saban looked at the table. "I'd not want to be the man in the cardinal's office who wrote the first sermon. He's made seven churches full of people look at a banker's books. And they liked them."

That was all anybody at the table said about it. Vantic herself said nothing about it at all, to anybody, that Emeric ever heard; when he passed the tithe house at the end of the month the queue was longer than it had been, and there were three men in it in black whom he did not know, with the House's paper in their hands, waiting their turn at the counter with the dock quarter's wives to ask, very politely, how a man might buy a share in a fishing boat.



The K Discovery came off the slip from her refit on the eighteenth, and they took her to sea on the night of the nineteenth, because Fletcher said so, and because the moon was down.

The refit had taken the whole of the autumn and the first fortnight of the year, and it did not show unless you knew where to look. That had been the point. The can lay along her propeller arm between the stone's housing and the hub, a steel tube as long as two men and as wide as a barrel, painted the bronze-brown of her fittings so that from the yard's fence it looked like part of the arm; inside it the potter's tiles, a finger apart on their hooks, six rings of nine. A gate in the housing that shut or opened the stone's blast down it. The fire ring in its iron pockets a third of the way along. Bram's iris at the mouth. Behind the arm, on the stern, where the wooden hull came closest, copper sheet on firebrick laid over the oak in a broad shield shaped like a spade, with a finger of air between brick and wood; and in Emeric's own cabin aft, behind the felt, a wall that would be hot.

They went out from the lake at ten in silent running, as she always went, dark, the gate shut and the stone's blast turning the propeller through the hub vanes, a long breath of wind going away over the reed beds. Fletcher at the bow helm. Ward in the top cockpit. Emeric at the stone-keeper's station with every stone she had in his head at once. And Ferra in the saloon behind the bow cockpit, at the chart shelf, with her book and her watch and the wool plugs Imani had sent, in a twist of paper, with the same note as in September in a different hand, because Imani was at the pass-box and had dictated it to Bettany.

She had not been asked. She had said at supper that she was coming, because she paid for the stones and would like to see them spent; and Ward had said nothing very loudly.

They flew south and east for an hour, high, until there was nothing under them but the sea and nothing on it anywhere but the stars lying on the water; past the bar, past the last of the Pull's bad water that Kaldo's fishermen would not go near, into the dark beyond it where the nearest man was a long way off and asleep. Then Fletcher brought her down. Down to fifty feet, then to twenty, the trim keys clicking under his hand, and held her there, hovering, still, her lift stones quiet, with the swell going by under her keel black and slow and smelling of salt.

"Twenty feet," said Fletcher's voice, small and brassy, from the speaking tube. "Holding. Wind's nothing."

"Ward," said Emeric.

"Ready."

"Ferra."

"Ready," said Ferra, from the saloon, with her watch open in her hand. He could feel her through two bulkheads, a small bright note, very steady, with her thumb on the watch's crown.

"Fletcher. From the bow. When you like."

"Plugs," said Fletcher. "All the way in. She'll know."



He lifted the guard. Emeric heard it through the tube: the pin out, the click of the brass on its hinge. Then the lever.

In his sense it went all at once, as it had on the stand: the gate swung in the housing and the stone's blast, that had been turning the propeller, went straight down the can instead; the propeller's blades turned edge-on and idled; the fire stones dropped onto their seats in their ring and woke, eight of them, a ring of heat coming up in the can's wall; and the iris opened.

The sound was not the stand's. On the stand it had been a howl across a river. In the ship it came up through the frames into his feet and his teeth and the bones of his face, a scream that started where the roar had been and went up past it into something that was not a sound at all any more but a pressure, a hand flat on the whole of the ship, and the felt in the walls did nothing to it whatever.

And she went.

He watched the airspeed gauge, because somebody had to. The needle went round past a hundred, where it lived when she was hurrying; past a hundred and fifty, where it lived in the Band; past two hundred, which was the end of her life until tonight, which she had made twice, at Band height, with a following wind, and never low; and went on, slower, and slower; and stood trembling at two hundred and forty-five, in the thick air a thousand feet over the sea, and would not go further.

Far back and below them, at the end of a long faint shimmer that bent the stars behind it like water bends a stick, the can's lip had gone cherry red, as the plan said it would after the first minute; and the sea under it, a mile back, was giving it back. A red eye lying on the black water, going away, and smaller, and smaller, as though something on the sea had opened one eye to watch them go and was too far off now to follow.

A minute and ten seconds, by his own count, from the hover to the place where nobody on that patch of water could have reached them with anything they had.

The lever went home. The fire stones lifted off their seats. The iris closed, its leaves sliding in. The gate swung and the stone's blast went back into the hub, and the propeller's blades turned back to bite the air; and the scream came down off its height in one long dying fall, through the roar, into the ordinary breath of a ship in silent running, and stopped being a sound inside his skull.



Ferra was in the saloon, where he had left her, standing by the chart shelf with one hand on it and the watch open in the other; and when he came through the bulkhead door she looked up at him and her mouth moved. He could not hear a word of it.

He pointed at his ear.

She looked at him with enormous patience, took the wool out of her own ears, put the plugs back in Imani's twist of paper and the paper in her pocket, and said it again, slowly, so that he could read it off her face.

"What does it cost? A mile."

"Don't ask," he said.

She heard it, or read it.

"I'm asking," said Ferra.

He had had the ship's true figure since the fortieth second of the burn, off the ring's drop. He held up both hands, all ten fingers, and then shut them and held up two more. Twelve. Then he pointed down, toward the propeller housing, and drew a slow line in the air with one finger, flat, a ship cruising; and held up one.

Twelve to one. For every mile she flew burning, low, at two hundred and forty-five, the ring spent what the main stone spent to fly her twelve miles at her ordinary pace. A burn would take her ten miles from a hover in a little over two minutes, and put her in a place nobody could follow; and it would cost what it cost to fly her to Wayford and halfway back.

Ferra watched his hands. She took her book off the shelf and her pencil from behind her ear and wrote it, there, standing, as the ship went level and quiet round them. Twelve. A short line. Then she looked at it for a while, and did not draw a box round it; and he understood that she did not need to, that the box from September was round all of it already, the four and the eight and now the twelve, and that this was simply a figure in the box.

Then she closed the book and went aft.

He went after her, because she had gone to his cabin, and it was his cabin; and she was standing in it in the low light of the one shaded bulb by the bed, by the after wall, the one behind the felt that Fletcher had faced with a sheet of thin copper over a lining of firebrick. Her palm was flat on it.

He put his beside hers. It was hot. Not too hot to touch; hot as a stove's side an hour after the fire is out, the heat coming off it into his hand steadily and without any hurry, from the can on the arm outside, thirty feet aft, cooling.

"It's warm," said Ferra. He heard her, a little. His hearing was coming back from the bottom up, the low notes first.

She did not take her hand away. He did not either. They stood there side by side in his cabin with their palms flat on the hot wall, and the ship went on through the dark at her quiet pace with Fletcher at the bow and Ward in the top cockpit and nobody coming aft; and after a while Ferra moved her hand a little, sideways, along the copper, until the side of it was against the side of his, and left it there.

"That's what twelve feels like," she said.

"I know where it is. I paid for it." "I wanted to know what it felt like from in here. The thing I'm paying for. You'd be in here, with this wall at your back. If it came to it. With somebody behind you on the water you couldn't see." She was quiet. "I've written down every stone that went into this ship. I'd like to have touched one."

The wall cooled under their hands. It did it very slowly, as the can did, back to front, he could feel it in his sense; and some time in the second quarter of an hour Ferra leaned her shoulder against his arm, and some time after that her forehead, and they stood there with their hands on the copper until it was only the ordinary temperature of a wall in a cabin on a winter night, and a little after.

"It's cold," she said at last.

"It's a wall."

"It was better hot." She took her hand away and looked at the palm, which was pink, and then at him, and he saw that she was trying not to laugh at herself and very nearly managing it. "Don't tell Imani I said that. She'll want to know what I meant and I'll have to say I meant the wall."

"You did mean the wall."

"Mostly," said Ferra, and kissed him once, briefly, as she did when they were not alone in a building, though they were very nearly alone in this one; and went forward to the saloon to rule a line in her book under twelve and write the date.



Escort one was on the water twenty miles south, dark, and on the way home they hovered a mile off her beam and watched her fire her first big gun off the sea: one white flash that lit her end to end, a blow like a church door slammed, a column standing up five miles off by the target raft's lamp. Ward did not watch the column. He watched the hull heel away from the gun, and hang, and come back, and sit where it had sat, rocking; and said only, "She stayed," and then, "I should like to know when the stone comes."

The figures went into Fletcher's yard book the next morning in Ferra's hand, under the date: the K Discovery's burn, two hundred and forty-five, one minute and ten seconds to clear, the can held, the shield held; and below that, because the can had held and nobody could now say it might not, a second line. Escorts three and four to be laid on the north slips in February, the can built in from the keel, steel to steel, no shield. Fletcher signed it.

They flew home in silent running, high, dark, and came in over the reed beds at four with the lake grey under them; and on the slip, in the shed's lamp, with his hat back on, Fletcher looked up at the can along her arm, cool now and bronze-brown and looking like nothing, and said what Emeric had been going to say. So Emeric said it anyway, aloud, to the four of them on the slip, so that it was said.

"Silent running is how she flies. That's her. That lever's for getting out of something. It's never for getting home early."

"Nobody's lifting that guard to get home early," said Fletcher. "Not on my ship. Not if the soup's on." He put the pin back through the hasp of the bow lever's guard himself, though he had put it back already, out over the sea; and then he took it out again and put it in a third time and made sure. "I don't like what it does to the stern. I don't like what it does to my teeth." He looked at the can. "She liked it. I could feel her like it." He went off up the slip toward the shed, and said, over his shoulder, not to anybody: "Daft thing."



Ilsa's Review came up from the records hall on the twenty-second in four bound volumes the thickness of a man's wrist, with a fifth, thinner, of the counts, on Ottoline's cards transcribed; and Ferra put all five on the study table and said that the Stewards' Council would sit on them on the first of February and she would like the duke to have read at least the first one by then, if the duke could manage it between ships.

It had taken Ilsa and forty clerks since September. That had been the council's ask at its summer sitting: Bryda's, in so many words, that if Kaldo was closing the gap it had promised to close, she would like to see it closing on paper that had not been written by Kaldo; and Ferra had said that every paper in the four baronies was written by Kaldo, one way or another, but she would find somebody to write it who could not be made to write it wrong. So it was Ilsa, with her own clerks and Ferra's printed form, house by house, in Grayrock first, then Milford and Wayford and last of all Kaldo: water, light, heat, school, wages, health, and at the bottom of every form, in Ilsa's own words, which Ferra had not changed, a line that said What would you change, if it were yours to change, and a space under it a hand deep.

Emeric read it at night, at the pass-box, on the crate by the collar where Bram had sat, with the first volume on his knees and the lamp on the shelf and Imani on her stool at the inner door with the sheet; between her hours, and through some of them. Inside the glass the old man lay under the tree with the lines coming and going on his arms in their slow silver. The Review was very long, and very plain, and Ilsa wrote the way she had read Grayrock's figures at Ferra's side table, in a clear dry voice that did not lean on anything; and he had not meant to read more than the method and the summary, and found at two in the morning that he was in the Kaldo pages and had not noticed getting there.

The ninth in Kaldo was Nessa.

Water, first. You'll want to know about water, everybody does. I carried it. From the north well, every morning of my life from when I was eight, two buckets on the yoke, and in the plague years three times a day because Imani said boil it and you can't boil what you haven't carried. I've a tap in the kitchen now. Hot. It comes out hot. I stood at it the first morning with my hand under it till Rylan asked was I ill. I still put my hand under it some mornings. I don't know why. I'm not going to stop.

And our Wren. You'll have heard. Her belly opened up on a table in the ward and a thing cut out of her and sewn up again, and she was home in the week eating everything in the house. When I was a girl you'd have sat by the bed. That's all there was. You sat by the bed and you said your prayers and the priest came. My mother's sister went like that, at twelve. The same side. I knew the minute I saw Wren holding it, I knew what it was, I'd seen it. And she's sixteen now and she's at the Motion Shop and she's at the school still, at sixteen, at a desk, and she'll be at it at seventeen, and nobody's sent her out to a mill at twelve to bring in a wage. I went out at eleven. To the salt runs. She doesn't know what a salt run is. I've not told her. I'm glad she doesn't know.

A copper takes me to Grayrock now on the people's train to my cousin's, and I'm home for my supper. I never once in my life went to Grayrock before. It's not far. It was always too far.

And Rylan came back. That's the one you want, isn't it. From the rite. You'll not put that in about the water and leave that out. He went at twenty, before we were wed, before any of this, and I'd been told, everybody's told, one in ten. One in ten come back. I knew it. I'd heard it in the lane since I was small. And I waited on the step for him the whole of that summer, and the next, and the letter came, and it was his own name in a clerk's hand, and he was one. He was one of the one. I've never told him how long I sat on that step. He was nine years the duke's father's man after, and he came home off the road with the prince and I didn't know him for a minute in the lane, he'd gone that grey round the face. And then the fire on the road, and he wouldn't say my name in the keep, he thought I didn't know. I knew. And now he stands up in front of the whole school with steam coming off him and swears in front of the children and tells them how the others died, so they'll know, so they'll not eat past the plate, and the children listen to him. They listen. They write it down. He can write now. His own name and everybody's. He came home and Doran taught him and the duke paid for the lamp. He writes the children's names in the yard book at the knights' base, every one, in his own hand, and he showed me, he showed me the page.

Who'd have thought. That's what I keep saying, Rylan's sick of it. Who would have thought, one day, we could. That's all. Put that. Who would have thought, one day, we could. You can finish it yourself, miss, I can't, there's too much. Every way I finish it there's something left over.

Marked by the speaker, with a mark like a small bird; and under the mark, in a different hand, a child's careful upright letters that had been to Sela's school: Nessa. Read to her by me, Wren.

Inside the glass Imani was on her hour, bent over the old man's right arm with the cuff turned to the lamp. She came out at a quarter past two and wrote came and went, and looked at him on the crate, and at the book, and did not ask; and went back to her stool and put her mask round her neck and her shoulder against the iron, and said, with her eyes shut, "You've a face on again. Not the sorry one. A new one."

"It's Nessa."

"In the Review?"

"She says the tram bell wakes her."

"It wakes everybody in that lane. I've had three of them in my surgery about it." Imani did not open her eyes. "Is that the face?"

"No."

"I didn't think it was," said Imani, and after a while reached out without looking and found his ankle by the crate and held it, the way she had held Ferra's under the study table in December, and went on holding it until the watch came round to the hour.



The neighbours complained. Ilsa had printed every word of that too, and he read it the next night, because he had made himself; and it was, he found, harder reading than Nessa, and better for him.

The rent in the new blocks was too high, said a lighterman in ward 4, and it was all very well being given a tap if you couldn't afford the room it was in. The trams were noisy, said eleven people out of the twenty in Kaldo, which Ilsa noted was more than said anything else. Strangers on every lane, said a carter's wife in the dock quarter: you used to know everybody, you knew every face and whose it was, and now there's men from up the line and their wives and their children and men off the Emberfell boats and you can't walk to the well without saying good morning to somebody you've never seen, and they don't say it back, half of them, they've not been brought up to it. The old street was gone, said a cooper in the lower ward, his father's street and his grandfather's, gone for the canal ring's wall and a row of printed houses that were very good houses and he'd not live in one if you paid him, and they had offered to pay him, and he'd said so.

And an old woman in Kaldo, in the upper ward, by the clerk's note very old, a widow, living with a daughter: asked what she would change, she had said that she liked the dark better.

The dark, the old woman had said. At night. Before the lamps. You'd go out the door and there was the dark, and the stars in it, all of them, right down to the roofs. My husband used to stand in the door with his pipe. I can't see the stars from my door now. There's a lamp on the corner and it's very good and I can see my step, and I'd rather have the stars. I'm too old to want to see my step. I know where my step is.

He read that twice. Then he got up off the crate and went outside the pass-box's outer door into the inner ward and stood in the frost and looked up. The great dome's ribs went up black over him with the town's glow on the cloud above; the lamps on the wall walk; the lamps down every lane below the inner ward, a little rope of them going away down toward the lower ward and the river, and the arc lamps at the port, and the long string of them on the viaduct round the wall where the new trams would run in the spring. The cloud was low. There were no stars at all.



Grayrock's pages were plain and fair, and he could not have argued with a line: the rows rebuilt, a tap in every kitchen, the pithead baths, the powerhouse; and two teachers for four rooms against Kaldo's eleven for fourteen, the two figures printed side by side the same size with nothing between them. Bryda had signed the foot of it in her heavy hand without a word in the margin. Ferra said at breakfast that this was the word, and that Bryda would never say so.

The last volume, the thin one, was the counts.

Ottoline's cards had done it, the health cards Imani had begun in year ten with one number for every person, the same as the census, and every birth and every illness and every death written against the number as it came; and Ottoline's tabulator had run them in the long room through a week of nights in December and printed what Ilsa had asked it for: how many children born in each barony in year ten were alive this January; and for Kaldo alone, joined to Doran's book and Imani's old lists, how many born in the plague's last year had lived to five. It was the first time, Ilsa's note said, that it could be counted at all. Before the cards there had been Doran's book for the burials and nobody's book for the births, and nobody could have said how many children in any barony of the realm had lived past five, because nobody had ever written down how many had been born.

He took the volume and went down the keep stair and across the inner ward to the hospital, at nine in the morning, and found Imani in her surgery writing up the night with Bettany, and put the book on her desk open at the page without saying anything, because he did not trust himself to say it well; and she read it, and looked at the figure for the plague's last year, and at the figure for Kaldo's children born in year ten, which was the year she had started the cards; and put her hand flat on the page.

"Bettany," she said. "Go and get my mother's book. The blue one. Off the shelf over the chair."

Bettany went.

Imani did not look up. Her hand stayed flat on the page. "Six in ten," she said. "Before. Six in ten lived to five, and that was a good year, that was a year without the plague in it, that was a year my mother would've called good." She was quiet. "Nine in ten and some. For Kaldo, the year ten babies. Nineteen in twenty, near enough."

"Grayrock's eight in ten."

"I can read, Emeric." She took her hand off the page at last and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, quickly, as if there were something in them. "Grayrock's eight in ten because Grayrock had the smallpox and the rows were last. Next year it'll be nine." She looked up at him. "I want that page. Ilsa can have it back when I've copied it. I want it on the wall."



The Dust House on South Sentinel's works shore was a long shed of poured stone roofed with turf, so that from the bay it looked like a dune with a door in it, walls thick enough, Bram said, to stop a ship. The K Discovery took them out on the twenty-sixth in the afternoon, an hour and a half over the grey water, Linus at the helm. Emeric, Ferra with her ledger and a canvas bag of tins, and Bram, who had wanted to see his walls with something in them.

Tess had been on the island since the twelfth. She had gone out on the K Endeavor with two of the Seers' Table, Pell, who was twenty-four now and taught the microscope class, and Corin from the dock quarter, to do the thing Tess had written at the back of her black book in November under Why strong?: to find out what made a body of dust do anything at all.

"Parts of dust," said Tess. "In a hundred parts of cement and dust, by bulk. Fire dust." She was in her chair at the bench's head with her black book open on her knee and charcoal on her fingers, and she looked, Emeric thought, like a woman who had not slept properly for a fortnight and did not mind. "Sorted. Ferra's sorted. I'll come to that." She waved the charcoal at the bars. "The stone warms its own end. That's the stone. Anybody can see that. You put your hand on it, it's warm as a cat." She put her hand on the nearest bar's stone end, in the row marked 5, and then slid her hand along to its far end, and held it there. "Cold. Thermometer says what the room says. Five parts, nothing. Ten parts, nothing. Fifteen, twenty, twenty-five. Nothing. It's cement with grit in it and a warm stone at one end, and the warm stays where the stone is, the way it would in a brick." She moved her hand along the rows as she spoke, and stopped at the row marked 30. "Thirty. Nothing. I'd near given up. I'd written in the book that the old ones had something we hadn't, and I didn't know what. Pell said do the thirty-three before you cry about it." She put her hand on a bar in the row marked 33, at its far end, the cold end, the end away from the stone.

She held it there. Then she took his hand, without asking, and put it where hers had been.

It was warm.

"Thirty-three," said Tess. "Thirty-five. Forty. Fifty. All the same. All the way along." Her voice had gone a little high. "And thirty, nothing. Three parts in a hundred. I went back and cast the thirty and the thirty-three again, three times, the twelfth and the sixteenth and the twentieth, so you'd not tell me I'd mixed one wrong. Same. Nothing, nothing, nothing, and then all of it." She looked at him. "It doesn't come up slow. It's not a bit at twenty and a bit more at twenty-five. It's off. And then it's on."

"Because the grains have to touch," said Emeric. He had had the page for it for twenty years, a materials text on filled rubbers with a graph like one step of a stair; he had not expected to feel it under his palm.

"Below a third, every grain's sitting in the cement on its own. Islands. The stone talks to the grains next to it and they've nobody to pass it to. Put in enough, and somewhere along the bar the islands start touching, one to the next, all the way through, in one long chain. And the minute there's one chain from end to end, the whole bar's one thing." He took his hand off. "It's called percolation. Like water through coffee. Nothing comes through the grounds until there's a road all the way down, and then it all comes."

"Percolation," said Pell, from the bench's far end, trying it, and wrote it down.



Ferra's tins were the other half of it. Tess's first bars, before Christmas, had been cast with dust from the charging room's bucket, every figure from five to fifty, and not one had done anything at all. It had been Ferra who saw why, and she had seen it in her book. She had the charging room's ledger in her canvas bag, and she took it out and opened it on the bench beside the bars at the page she had marked with a strip of the cheap grey, and put her finger on it.

"Every stone that goes onto the racks goes in the book," she said. "What it is, what it's for, when it's charged, when it's spent. Fire. Water. Air. Lightning. Light, for the lamps, the old ones, before the bulbs. Gravity, for the lifts. When one's drained to nothing and goes to dust, the rack man writes it struck off and sweeps the dust into the bucket." She turned a page, and another. "Every kind into the same bucket. For nine years. Because dust was dust and nobody wanted it." She looked at Emeric. "Tess said a fire stone's dust ought to be fire, if the stone was. And I said, then the bucket's every kind at once, by the book, about a fifth fire and the rest everything else. And if each kind's grains only talk to their own kind, then the bucket's not a third of anything. It's a fifth of fire and a fifth of water and a fifth of air, all in the way of each other."

"So she had the rack men sort it," said Tess. "From the first week of January. Every stone that goes to dust, its own tin, by kind, labelled. The way she labels everything." She held up one of the tins from Ferra's bag, a small square tin with a paper label pasted on it in the counting room's hand: FIRE. 4 Jan, yr 13. Racks 2-6. And under it, smaller, a number. "Twenty days of fire dust. That's what's in the thirty-three."

"And the bucket?"

"Muddled," said Ferra. "Every kind in it pulling a different way. It's in casks, written up. From now on nothing goes in the bucket. I've written to every rack."

Bram, who had been standing at the back with his arms folded looking at his walls, said, "Hm," which from Bram was a speech; and then, after a while, to the bars: "So it's like ore. You don't throw your copper ore in with your tin ore and wonder why the bell's soft."

"It's exactly like ore," said Tess.



The pockets were on the dockhand.

The banded layers went round the shoulder in their fine grey rings, as they had in the Motion Shop's back bay; and at the joint, where the arm had once met the body, set into the bands and running down through them, there were three small hollows in the cast, each the size of a thumb's end, each lined with a darker grey glaze, smooth, and empty. They had been cast into the body on purpose. The layers went round them, not through them.

"My bars have their stone on the end," said Tess. "These are in the middle of the joint. In the strongest bands. Three of them. And there's three more at the other shoulder, and two at each hip, and there were nine in the cave golem's knee that we thought were just holes where the water had got in. I went back through Wren's drawings." She looked at the empty hollows. "They had stones in them. Small ones. That's where the stone goes. Not the end. The middle. Where the work is."

"I don't know." Tess put the sheet back. "I don't know what they said, the stones. I don't know if they all said the same thing or each said something different. I don't know why that glaze, or why that size." She put her hand flat on the sheet over the hollows, and was quiet. "He'd know. Armand. He'd look at a hole in a cast and tell you what went in it and what it was saying and who taught the man who wrote it to say it badly." She took her hand away. "I'm not cutting any more of it till he's looked. I've written it at the top of the page. Pockets. Wait."



He went to the dome when they landed, before he went to bed, because he went every night.

Armand lay as he had lain for a month, on his back on Bram's straw with the blanket to his chest and his cuffed hands on it, and Ward's key still standing in the lock of the right cuff with Ward's knot on its cord. His white hair had grown out past his collar on the straw. His face was turned a little toward the trunk, and the corner of his mouth was the same. On the inside of his left wrist, above the iron, a silver line came up as fine as a hair, branching, and dulled, and went. Over his face and the backs of his hands lay the faint cloud that had come two nights ago, like breath on a cold pane; Imani had written that it looked like dying and was not.

"Tess has written wait at the top of a page," he told the old man, quietly, under the tree's hum. "So you'll have to."

Emeric knelt there a while longer with his sense drawn in as close as he could hold it, to the old man's door, still standing open, and the stone spread through him like a map being read; and then he got up, and went back along the path, and out through the box, and sat down on the paving by Imani's stool with his back to the warm iron; and she put her hand on his head without opening her eyes, and they waited for the hour.
