Chapter 329 - He Comes Anyway

The plan for the hill house above the falls was that nobody went up to it.

Ward said it at five in the morning on the fifteenth of December, at the K Amara's chart table, with his strapped ribs creaking and a mug of Maren's tea going cold by his elbow; and then he said what they would do instead, which took longer. Saban stood at the end of the table with his hat on the board in front of him, upside down, like a man who meant to collect something in it. Osric sat on the chart chest. Emeric stood with his right hand inside his coat and his left flat on the tracing of the hill, which he had been looking at since four.

"Four chimneys," said Ward. "A wall and a gate. Two canons, the new cardinal, and him. One road up and one down." He put his finger on the road. "We've done one stair this week with a man in the wall I didn't know was there. I'm not doing a house with two of them in it, in his rooms, where he knows every board."

Nobody argued. Osric looked at his boots.

"So he comes to us." Ward moved the finger down the falls road, from the house in its trees above the river to the place where the road came in under the second wall by the little west gate and ran along the hill's foot between the houses; and stopped where Tess's boy had inked an open space with a circle in the middle of it. "Here."

The fountain square lay at the foot of the castle's gardens, under the lowest of their walls. Emeric had known it as a boy. The gardens' water came down off the terraces through a culvert in the hill and out of a bronze pipe into a great round basin of grey stone, and over the basin's lip, and away down the square's slope to a gutter. It was a sloping square, setts on sand, higher on the north under the garden wall than on the south; three storeys of houses round it with shops under, a cookshop, a chandler's, a saddler with a painted horse over the door; an iron gate in the garden wall at the top, shut since the palace emptied; the falls road coming in at the north-west corner, which Ward called the mouth; and at the south-west the lane down into the lower town, two carts wide.

"It's the first open ground he'd cross coming down," said Ward. "And we've stood a shore team in it every day since the ninth, so it's ours, and he knows it. Today it's yours." He looked at Emeric. "You stand in it. In your suit. From ten. Low, where every window on the hill can see you and he can feel you from his bed. Nobody else in the square. Osric's people and forty off the aprons on the roofs round it, under the eaves. The Hod three in the saddler's doorway by the mouth. When he's in, they step out behind him. That's the plan." He drank some of the cold tea. "He won't come."

"He'll come," said Saban.

Ward looked at him.

"He'll come. He's been told to." Saban turned his hat a quarter turn by its brim, as if it had been facing the wrong way. "His cathedral's a hole and his faithful walked out of it on a priest's letter. A man in that chair doesn't sit behind a shut gate for a fortnight because he likes the view. He's been waiting for an order, and he's had it by now, or he'd have gone home." He put the hat on. "He'll come because somebody he's more frightened of than he is of you has told him to. And because you'll be there, and he wants you there. Both."

"The tower?" said Osric. He meant the one on the crown wall, under the empty palace, on the hill's east shoulder; one of the last two.

"Its chest's ring stops on the gardens' third terrace," said Ward. "Its bombards don't stop anywhere. So no ship comes into the square. The K Discovery stands off over the river with the king." He looked at Emeric again, longer. "And the tins stay in Fennick's store."

He said it as a fact, not a question, and Emeric heard what that cost him. Eight dead-zone rounds were left. One from a roof into that square, and the man in white and gold would be an old man in a cape with nothing in his hand.

"Yes," said Emeric.

Saban opened his mouth.

"He's said," said Ward, and Saban shut it again.

There was one more thing, and Emeric said it, because it had to be said at the table or it would not be said at all. "Nobody shoots him. Not the self-loaders. Not anybody's. Not while his right hand's shut."

"Because?" said Saban.

"Because I don't know what's in it," said Emeric, which was nearly true; and Ward chalked it on the board under the rules, NOT AT HIM, and drew a line under it.



Imani came across from the K Endeavor at six on the mail boat, as she had every morning since the twelfth, and looked at the hand on a stool by the after apron's hatch with the wind coming in round the door.

She took off the bandage and the strap and the splint and felt each knuckle with her thumb, watching his face. The swelling had gone down to a hard pad across the back of the hand, yellow at its edges, and under it the two knuckles were there and nearly where they belonged, a weld not yet cold.

"Better," she said. "Not good. Better." She reached for the clean splint.

"Leave it off."

She stopped with the splint in her fingers.

"The gauntlet won't close over it. I tried last night. The fingers stand out straight and the plate over the back won't seat. I'd be flying in a mitten."

"Then don't fly."

He did not answer that, and she had not expected him to. She sat with the splint in her hand and looked at the knuckles as if they belonged to somebody else.

"If you hit anything with it," she said, "a wall, a man, the ground, it goes again. Both. And the third time it doesn't knit straight whatever I do, and you'll write the rest of your life like a man holding a spoon." She put the splint back in her bag. "Strap it, at least. The soft strap. It'll seat."

She strapped it, a long figure of eight over the back of the hand and round the wrist and back, and he flexed it, and it closed most of the way with a small grating under the pad that he did not mention.

At ten to nine on the after apron, with the suit on and the knights going off the rail in pairs below, he found that the gauntlet would not seat over the strap either; the plate rode a finger's breadth proud and pinched at the wrist. So he unwound the strap one-handed and folded it small, and did not throw it over the rail. He put it in the pouch at his belt. Then the gauntlet.

When he looked up Imani was in the hatchway with her bag, on her way back to the boat. She had watched the strap go into the pouch.

"You kept it," she said.

"It's a good strap."

"It is," said Imani, and looked at the pouch, and then at him, and went down the ladder without saying the rest; which he could hear anyway.



He stood in the fountain square from ten o'clock, and nothing happened for two hours and a quarter.

It was grey and cold, the cloud low on the hill. Osric's people had carried the market's last trestles out at first light and swept the square, which nobody had told them to do; so the setts lay clean and pale and dry on the high side under the garden wall, and dark on the low side, where the basin's overflow went. The water came out of the bronze pipe in a fat grey rope, smacked into the basin, went over the lip on the south side in a sheet the width of a door, and spread down the slope toward the gutter in a moving skin a finger deep and twenty yards wide, shining like wet slate. Nobody in the capital had ever thought to cut the basin a deeper drain. Ward had stood on the lip at nine and looked at it a long time and said, "Stay on the dry side," and gone up to his roof.

The square was empty. The windows round it were not.

In two hours of a man in black standing in a square doing nothing, word had gone round the way it does in a city, and the windows had filled. Two and three to a sill. People on the steps of the lanes that came in under the houses, arms folded against the cold, the way people stand to watch a fire in a street not theirs. A boy on the chandler's sign. Osric's people had told them twice to go in. They had gone in, and come back.

Emeric let them. He could not stop a city looking, and he had come down to be looked at.

He stood low on his held stones, a man's height off the setts on the dry side by the basin, turning now and then to keep the falls road's corner in front of him, his sense out over the hill and the river and the roofs in the great soft roar of a hundred and twenty thousand notes. Osric on the saddler's roof with six of his people along the leads. Ward on the chandler's, on the south side, his note hard and tired and very still, and the set's two short spikes standing up off his helm over the parapet. The forty off the aprons flat on every roof round. And in the saddler's doorway by the mouth, three notes like nobody else's, packed and heavy and certain: Arthur, Mira and Bren in their MK3s with the big violet visors down, standing in the dark of the door like three suits on a rack. Mira had been let out of the hold that morning to stand and not to fight; Imani had said it to her face, and Mira had taken it as the order it was. She held her spear in her left hand.

And up the falls road, at last, he let his sense go where he had not let it go since the second.

The house lay in its trees like a cold stone in a field. Two canons, thin ordinary notes, in a kitchen. A second cold, younger and smaller than the one he wanted, standing at an upper window. And a long way below the gate already, coming down toward the river and the city at a walk, the one.

Great and cold, the way the bottom of the sea is cold. And inside it, held the way a coin is held in a closed fist, the small one: fine, clear, warm.

A pace behind it came a thinner note, a man's, careful, with a thread going out of it ahead along the road like a blind man's stick.

"He's coming," said Emeric, into the set.

Nobody on the line said anything. Then Saban's voice, from four miles up, very dry: "Told you."



He came down the falls road on foot.

Kember had a team in a loft inside the west gate with a set and a glass, and said what he saw, low: a tall man in white and gold, walking, and a narrow man in black a pace behind him leading a bay horse with nobody on it.

"No guard. Nobody. He's walking like a man going to church." A pause. "The Lieutenant's lot have opened the gate for him. They've not come with him. They're stood there with their hats off."

He came in under the second wall at a walk and along the hill's foot, a quarter of a mile of shuttered shops with a well at a crossing. Emeric felt every step. The cold went along the lane like weather going up a valley, and the notes in the houses went still as it passed, the way birds go still under a hawk; and at the well it stopped.

"There's a woman at the well," said Kember. "With a bucket. He's talking to her." A long pause. "He's blessed her. With his left. She's on her knees on the wet." Another. "He's helped her up. He's carrying on."

Emeric felt the woman's note behind on the lane, small and shaken all through, like a cup that has been knocked and is still ringing. Nothing had gone into it. A blessing given in the street to a woman with a bucket, and every window between the well and the square had seen it.

He had been told to come, and he had come as the Church.

Then the corner, and Augustine walked into the square.

He looked as he had always looked. Tall and spare, a man of fifty from across the square with grey not finished coming in under the red cap, and from closer a man of no age at all, very pale, as if his skin had been kept from the light since he was born. The cardinal's white and gold to the ankle. The Gilded Eye on its chain. His right shoulder sat a little low, as it had sat all Emeric's life, and his right hand hung closed at his side; his left was open and empty. He stopped on the dry setts under the garden wall and looked round at the windows, all of them, slowly, as a priest looks round a church before he begins; then down the slope at the basin, and at Emeric.

"Now," said Ward, on the set.

The Hod three came out of the saddler's doorway into the falls road's mouth behind him, three black suits with their visors violet in the grey, and stood across it. Arthur in the middle. Bren on the left with his blade out. Mira on the right with her spear in her left hand, standing, as she had been told.

Augustine did not turn round.

The narrow man did. He looked at the three black suits across the road he had come down, and at the bay, and dropped the reins and ran: not back, not at them, but along the square's west side under the house fronts toward the cookshop's lane, fast, coat flapping, like a man who had decided years ago exactly which way he would run if it came to running.

Bren caught him at the cookshop's steps. He did not use the blade. He went across ten yards of setts and hit the narrow man the way he hit everything, low, with his shoulder, and the narrow man went up the cookshop's three steps on his face and lay on the top one with his arms out; and Arthur came after him at a walk and sat down on him.

The thread the narrow man had been laying out in front of them went out like a dropped line. A hand at the end of a rod, with no float.

Augustine had not looked round for that either.

"You've hurt your hand," he said.

He did not raise his voice, and it came down the square anyway: the voice Emeric had heard over his father's table for twenty-two years, saying grace, warm and level and patient, a voice for telling children things for their own good. Every window heard it.

"Twice," said Emeric.

"The same knuckles." It was not a question. Augustine looked at the gauntlet as a physician looks at a splint. "You'll want to keep it out of things today."



The first thing came out of his left hand on the end of the sentence.

A fire glyph, red, the ring and the core and the four long strokes of a bolt, sunwise, closing, the size of a cartwheel; and Emeric was already inside it. Not his body. His sense, down into the slow place the delve made, where a breath stretched to the length of a sentence and a glyph could be read stroke by stroke like a page. Element, amount, direction. The turn of it, not the mirror. He cast it back the other way round, and the two met over the basin and were nothing, a little warm air going away down the slope and a smell like a blown-out lamp.

"Good," said Augustine.

The second came on the end of the word. Then the third. Then two at once, fire and an earth glyph under it, the setts at Emeric's feet beginning to lift; and he killed the fire and took the earth on a plate, a flat shield of his own mana in front of his knees the size of a door, which the setts struck and rang off like hail off a cart roof. A plate was cheap for a conjured thing. It was not cheap for stones, and those were stones. He felt every one behind the eyes.

He went sideways on the held stones the length of the basin and came round its far side and threw a bolt up the slope with a glyph on it for every window to see.

Augustine reversed it.

A small turned ring opened in front of his left hand as the bolt arrived, and the two went out together. And Emeric felt in his stomach what he had known at the chart table at four in the morning: the man was doing what he did. The slow place. Every glyph read and turned. Armand said Emeric was the best in the kingdom at it because nobody else had the delve's slow time to read in; and here was another man who had it, and had had it for longer than Emeric had been alive.

So for a quarter of an hour by the saddler's clock they did that, and there was nothing to watch. A glyph out of the white figure's hand at the top of the square, a glyph out of the black figure's at the bottom, the two meeting in the air and going out with a puff of warm air or a flicker or a smell of wet stone; again; again; until the windows, which had come to see a fight, began to talk among themselves. And all of it cost. A reversal cost the same as the spell it killed, to the grain; and Augustine was throwing small and quick, the way a man taps a cask with a mallet all over to hear where it rings hollow. Emeric's arms ached to the shoulder inside five minutes.

And behind every glyph there was the other thing.

Through the thread any two notes make when they lean on each other across a square, he could feel the man mean things a moment before he did them; that was the foresight, which he had found in himself the spring before last and used on Ward in the yard ever since. And through the same thread, inside the first minute, he began to feel Augustine mean things he did not do.

A fire meant to the left, quite clear, arriving down the thread like a word heard before it is spoken; and the glyph came up on the right. An earth meant at his feet, and nothing. Twice he started a reversal for a spell that never came and paid for the start of it. Then a real one on the heels of a false one, so that he was half a breath late, and the fire got past his plate's edge and went across the setts beside him into the overflow in a long hiss of steam.

Armand had done this to him in February, in the yard at the knights' base in the slush, with the Hod three and Declan watching from the gate: spells hung in the air and let go by no rule, and a decision he had not made laid down the thread like false tracks in snow. Armand had beaten his foresight to a standstill with it, and they had both stopped, because there was no safe way for either of them to win.

So he knew it. He did what he had learned to do after that morning: stopped trusting what the thread said the man meant, and watched the mana for what it did. Slower. Truer. It cost him the moment's warning and gave him back the half breath.

Augustine felt him change. Emeric felt him feel it: a small pleased attention down the thread, a teacher's, at a pupil who has found the answer at the bottom of the page without being told.

The glyphs stopped.

"Do you know what the Light is?" said Augustine.



He said it into the quiet with his left hand down at his side, and every window heard that too. Emeric hung over the wet by the basin with his arms aching and said nothing.

"It's the reason a farmer doesn't own a sword. It's the reason the man with the most mana doesn't take the next village. Somebody has to decide who can, and who can't, or everybody tries."

He let that go round the square.

In Emeric's ear, quietly, Arthur's voice said, "We've got him, sir. On the step. He's not fighting." A pause. "He's asking for water."

"Give him water," said Ward.

"There's only the trough."

"Then give him the trough."

"You've read the rite," said Augustine. "Armand's sheet. Somebody brought me one at the house, wet." He looked at the windows. "Nine in ten die on that floor, yes. And the one who stands up is a knight who knows exactly who let him live."

"Sir," said Arthur, apologetic. "He says the water's from the gutter."

"It is from the gutter," said Ward.

"He says he can taste the tannery. He says his name's Matthias Brey, with a y, and nobody ever spells it right, and he wants it written down." A pause. "He says it's cold."

"Arthur."

"Sir."

"Not on this line."

The set went quiet.

Augustine had not heard a word of it, and waited for it to finish all the same. He looked down the slope at the black figure over the basin, kindly.

"You're tired already," he said. "You're doing it the hard way. You know you are."

Emeric went up the slope at him.



Low and fast on the held stones, a foot off the setts, every node pushing at once; the way the knights had gone at the Church's mages in the cloud, because a glyph takes time to close and a man coming at you takes less. Twenty yards in a count of one. His short blade was out in his left hand, the charged edge humming in his palm through the gauntlet.

Augustine's glyph was half closed when he arrived. Emeric did not reverse it. He went through the side of it with the blade, the charged edge into the ring's outer stroke, and the ring came apart round him like a dropped plate in two bursting halves; and he was inside.

He had never been so close to the man. A yard. The long pale face with its bones standing out. The eyes, a grey so pale it was nearly white, looking at him as they had looked across a hall at a boy of seven: as a physician considers a case already decided.

Augustine stepped back and the blade went past his chest by a hand's width.

He moved like a man of fifty, spare and quick on the dry stones; but he was past ninety, whatever his face said, and Emeric could feel down the thread that he had not expected anybody this close this soon.

The second cut went in.

Not far. Augustine had a skin up, a shield laid over himself close as a coat, and the charged edge went into it the way a saw goes into green wood, chewing, slowing, and came through at the end of its swing and opened the white and gold across the left shoulder, and the shoulder under it, in a long shallow line. The blood came up very red on all that white.

From every window round the square there came a sound: short, indrawn, from a great many people at once, the sound a crowd makes when a thing it has been told all its life cannot happen has happened in front of it. Then nothing.

Augustine looked down at his shoulder.

Then he looked up, and there was no glyph.

Fire came out of his open left hand at a yard with nothing in front of it, no ring, no core, no strokes, no light in the air before it; only a hand, and then the fire, as if the hand were a door into a furnace and somebody had opened it.

Emeric was not there. He had felt the mana gather, by feel, the only way there was, and was already going back down the slope on his stones with the heat going past his visor like a chimney catching. He came round at the basin with his plate up and the steam rolling off the overflow where the fire had gone into it, and stood off.

The windows were absolutely quiet.

A man on the steps of the chandler's lane said, to nobody, in a city voice, quite loud in the quiet, "There wasn't a ring."

Nobody answered him. Every face at those windows had been told all its life by the Church that a glyph came first, every time; and the Church's own cardinal had just thrown fire out of his bare hand like a man throwing water out of a bucket.

Augustine had heard the man on the steps. Something went over his face that was very nearly regret, and went away.

"Well," he said. "They'll have to know sooner or later."



It was faster after that, and worse, and Emeric understood inside a minute why the Church kept its glyphs.

There was nothing to read. A glyph was a page. This was a man's hand moving and the mana in it going somewhere, and the only warning was the gathering, felt in the slow place a breath before. He could reverse by feel. He and Armand were perhaps the only two in the kingdom who could, and they had practised it since the end of October on the yard with buckets of water standing by; but feeling was not reading, and it was not quick enough at the speed a glyphless spell came. The first he killed clean. The second he killed most of, and the rest burst between them over the basin like a lamp thrown on a hearth, a flat hot blow that put him back a yard in the air and took the red cap off Augustine's head and sent it bowling down the slope into the water.

Augustine left it there.

The third Emeric did not try to reverse. He took it on a skin close over the suit and paid for it behind the eyes, a long ache like a tooth; a skin was dearer than a plate and cheaper than being wrong.

And the man was cheaper too now, every cast, and faster. Emeric could feel the old note easing as it went, like a man who has carried a heavy coat on a warm day and has at last been allowed to take it off.

He could have done the same. The rings that went up out of his own hands were for the windows, as on the dyer's roof, so that the hill would see a mage; and every one was a page Augustine could read before it closed. He went on drawing them, honest and slow, because a great deal on that hill was not yet anybody's business, and a cardinal at twenty yards was the last man alive he wanted to make it his.

So he fought the way a man fights who cannot hide from his opponent: on the ground, with what was there.

What was there was the basin. He put the blade back on his hip and went down to the lip. Water was not his; it came at three times the cost of fire, slow, the way his left hand wrote; but a mage beside water, Armand said, does not need to be clever. He put his hand in the overflow and stood it up, a sheet of grey water the height of a house, and threw it up the slope.

It was not a spell by the time it arrived. It was a ton of water going up a hill. Augustine read the lift and the throw and reversed them both, and the water did not care. It was only water, and it hit his skin and went over him in a long grey slap that took him off his feet and rolled him three yards along the foot of the garden wall in his white and gold like a dropped coat.

The windows made their sound again.

Augustine got up slowly, on his left hand, with his right held closed against his chest, and stood with the water running off him and his grey hair plastered to his skull and the cut on his shoulder bleeding pink through the wet. He looked down at the basin, and at the way the water lay on the setts now over the whole bottom half of the square in one shining sheet where the throw had come back down.

"Yes," he said, thoughtfully. "That's better."



Then a lull, because both of them needed one.

Augustine stood under the garden wall with his breath going long and slow through his nose, the way a singer breathes; and Emeric hung over the basin's dry side with his arms gone to wet sand and the thin cold hollow opening in the middle of him that he had felt on the dyer's roof. He had not touched the other half of himself. He had emptied his arms of it before the man came round the corner, out of his legs and his back and his hands, as he had on the riverbank in July, until there was nothing inward in him that a reader at twenty yards could find; and the half he had left to fight with was going.

The man knew it. Emeric could feel him knowing it, patiently.

"Your mother," said Augustine.

Emeric went still on his stones.

"You'll want to know. Everybody wants to know why, as if why were a medicine." Augustine spoke to the windows as much as to him, as he had spoken from the chancel steps on feast days with his left hand raised. "A delver at a king's side is a second Church. Nobody lets a second Church live. It isn't hatred. It's arithmetic."

He let it lie there on the wet square in front of the whole hill.

Emeric had nothing to say to it that he wanted the whole hill to hear. Far down in the slow place he could feel the root bead in his band against his wrist, his mother's, that had been warm there for years; and it was warmer now than he had ever felt it. It knew something across that square. He did not let himself ask it what.

"And you," said Augustine, gently, as if it followed. "You're raising every commoner into a noble. You've put lightning in commoners' children's hands." His eyes went past Emeric to the falls road's mouth, where Mira stood with her spear; and to the cookshop's steps, where Arthur sat on the narrow man with Bren over them both. "Do you know what you do to a world built on who can and who can't? You don't free it. You start the war that ends it."

On the set, very low, as if he had taken a long time deciding whether it was allowed, Arthur said, "Sir. The man. He says to tell you." A pause. "He says His Eminence goes for whatever's holding you up. He says that's what he does. He says he's watched him do it nine years." Another pause. "He says I'm heavy."

Ward did not tell him to get off the line.

Emeric put it away where he could find it, and in the slow place he went over the suit the way a man pats his pockets. Two hearts, the breast and the back, nine cells each. The forty nodes under their violet glass, and the rods from every node to the hearts. The held stones in the shoulders and at the ribs. And over the breast heart, in its round window on his chest, the small steady, spinning in its gimbals on its battery, telling every stone in the suit where still was.

Augustine smiled, very slightly, for the first time that day, as if he had watched him think it.



The sett came from the left.

It was the oldest trick a mage had, a plain stone out of a plain street, and Emeric had been waiting for something cleverer. Augustine cast it nowhere near himself: at the foot of the cookshop's wall, behind and to Emeric's left, glyphless and quiet, while his left hand moved in the air in front of him and the mana gathered there for a fire that the thread said, loudly, was coming. Emeric turned to the fire. He had his reversal half made.

The fire never came. The sett did: a grey granite cobble the size of a loaf, torn out of its sand by the cookshop's step and thrown flat and hard across the square at a man's chest height, round the edge of his plate, where a plate does not go.

It hit the round window on his breast.

He heard the glass go. He felt the rotor behind it stop, not as a top stops but all at once, a small sick jar through the breastplate as the gimbal's ring jammed on its pin. In the helm the lit ball in front of his left eye rolled over and over and lay down at the bottom of its glass.

And every held stone in the suit, that had been pushing him wherever he told it, forgot which way anything was, and fell back to the one direction a stone knows without being told.

He went straight up off the square like a cork let go under water. Not fast; a lift is never fast; but he was a lift now and nothing else, and the push he had been leaning toward the slope did not know what toward meant any more. Past the first-floor sills, past the second, the faces going by and a woman putting her hand over her mouth; and the eaves coming, where the rule was, never above them; and above the eaves on the crown wall the tower with its two bombards upright on the leads and its wind crossbows and every man on it looking down.

He cut the suit through the band, every stone at once, the way he had cut it in the cloud under the third ring, and dropped.

Thirty feet, with no steady to choose where and no time. He came down onto the wet half of the square below the basin like a sack off a cart, feet first and then not, the suit's whole weight driving him into the setts and over; and he put his hand out to stop himself, because a body has never once in its history agreed not to; and it was the right hand.



Harrold had been at the K Discovery's rail since ten, as he had been at it for the cathedral, with his collar up and his hands on the cold brass.

She hung over the river two miles west of the hill, where Ward had put her, outside the crown wall's reach on Fletcher's figures. Fletcher had Ivo's long glass on its stand and said what he saw in it, a few carpenter's words at a time. The king did not use the glass.

He had found his son's note at ten and had not let go of it since. From two miles he could not have said what the boy was doing; only that he was doing a great deal, and paying for it. He could feel the other thing too, at the top of the square, the cold one, and he had not let himself look at it straight all morning, the way a man does not look at the sun; because he had stood beside it at his own table all his reign, and been blessed by its left hand on every feast day, and had not known.

"He's gone up," said Fletcher at the glass. "Straight up. Like he's on a string." Then, sharp: "He's cut it. He's coming down."

And in the same breath something happened in his son's note that Harrold had no word for.

It was very short. Less than a second. The boy was casting as he fell, a shield going up round him out of his hands, a mage's mana going out into the air as any mage's does, as Harrold's own did on the rare days he let it; and in the same instant, not after it, not with the small stumble every man Harrold had ever known made between the one thing and the other, the mana went in. Into the body. Down the arm into the hand, hard and packed, the way a knight's goes when he braces for a blow.

Out and in at once. One note doing both, with no join in it anywhere.

Then the in was gone as if it had never been, and there was only the casting, a mage's, tired.

Harrold took his hands off the rail.

He had been made a knight on no floor. He had come out of his mother that way, his tutors said, the knight's hardness in him and the mage's sense on top of it, and he had spent his life doing the one and then the other, with a gap between that the tower's old men had told him every man like him had and always would. He had felt knights and mages past counting. He had never felt that, from anybody. Only, now it was gone, he thought he might have felt something a little like it once or twice, long ago, from inside his own skin, on a morning when he had not been thinking at all.

"Sir?" said Fletcher.

"Nothing," said the king. "Is he up?"

Fletcher looked.

"He's down. He's moving. He's on his knees." A pause. "He's got his hand in his lap."



The bubble held.

It had gone up round him as he fell, out of his hands without his deciding, a sphere of his own mana with its own air in it, the dearest shield there was; and when he came down in it onto the wet setts it came down with him, its floor the street. Two fires went into its skin in the half minute he spent on his hands and knees and ran off it in long smears of light like rain off a glass. He felt both behind his eyes. He did not lift his head for either.

He got the gauntlet off with his teeth and his left hand, slowly, the plate grating, and knew before he saw it. The two knuckles had gone down into the back of the hand like two stones into mud, and the skin over them had split on the gauntlet's inner edge, and blood was coming out and down his wrist in a way that did not mean to stop.

And for the length of a breath, coming down, his arm had been full.

He had not decided it. He had put his hand out and the knight in him had sent the mana down the arm into the fist, hard and all at once, the way it had every time he fell over for thirteen years; the way he had spent the whole morning not letting it. It had not saved the knuckles. It had been too late and too much and the wrong shape. And he had caught it almost as it came, and emptied the arm, and shoved it all back down behind the door.

He did not know whether the man at the top of the square had felt it. He did not know who else had.

He knelt in the bubble with the basin's water running round his knees, and got the strap out of the pouch at his belt; and with one end in his teeth and the roll in his left hand he wound it the way Imani had wound it at six that morning, the long figure of eight over the back and round the wrist and back. Badly. He was not her. The blood came through the second turn and the third and darkened the strap from inside, slowly, like ink coming up through a blotter. He tied it off with his teeth.

Imani would have a great deal to say about it. He found that he was looking forward to it.

Augustine had stopped casting. He stood under the garden wall in his wet white and gold, his right hand closed against his chest and his left hanging open, and waited while Emeric did it, the way a schoolmaster waits at a slate for a boy to finish his sum.

Behind him the iron gate in the garden wall stood open. Forty of the Lieutenant's men had come out of it while Emeric was in the air, crossbows and copied fire-tubes, and stood in a long untidy line along the top of the square; and among them a Vein-silvered knight of the Lieutenant's own household in plain mail with silver to the jaw, a big fair man with a face like a shut door.

"Leave them," said Ward on the set, before anybody asked. "Nobody moves. The roofs have them."

Augustine lifted his left hand an inch without looking round, and the line behind him, which had begun to shuffle forward, stopped.

"You're bleeding," he said. "Keep that hand up."

Emeric got up.

It took longer than he wanted. The hearts were whole and the held stones awake, and they could take him straight up into the tower's sight and nowhere else. So he stood on his own feet in the water below the basin, inside a bubble that cost him something every second it stood, and held his bound hand against his chest in his left, as the man at the top of the square held his.

It came to him that they made a pair.

"You built that yourself," said Augustine, looking at the suit. "Every stone in it written in your own hand. I could read it from the top of the square, the whole of it, as if you'd left it open on a desk. It's very good work." He tilted his head. "You put the little wheel where you'd put your heart. People always do."

Emeric said nothing.

"You think I'm the worst thing you'll meet," said Augustine, quietly, so that only the nearest windows heard it, and Emeric. "I'm only the one who knocked first."



The water was the thing.

He had known it since Ward stood on the basin's lip at nine. He had known it a great deal longer than that: on another world, in a yard full of cable drums on a cold morning, with a man in a hard hat standing over him, the first thing anybody had ever told him about power was never to stand in water near it. He had been nineteen and thought it was too obvious to say. It was the first thing they told everybody because it was the thing that killed everybody.

He was standing in it now, ankle deep, at the bottom of a square that sloped toward him, in a suit of steel with forty nodes in it and a rod from every node to two hearts, and eight of those nodes in his boots, flat against wet granite. The bubble came down round him to the street and stopped at the street, as a bubble does. Its floor was the setts. Its floor was the water.

At the top of the square Augustine took his left hand off his chest, stooped, and laid it gently flat on the wet stones where the sheet of water began under the garden wall, as a man lays his hand on the flank of a horse.

Emeric felt the gather. Not in the hand: in the water, the whole shining sheet of it at once, running down the slope toward him faster than he had ever felt mana move, quicker than thought and a great deal quicker than a reversal by feel. He had one breath, and in it he did the only two things there were. He reached into it and turned what he could. And he threw what was left of himself down through the soles of his boots into the street, the sand under the setts and the clay under that, the slow heavy thing that lightning grounds out in if it is given anywhere to go: earth, at three times the cost, with nothing left to pay it with.

The lightning came anyway.

It came up out of the water into the boots. There was no sound he remembered afterward, only a flat white light under his feet that lit the bubble from inside like a lantern, and every joint struck at once as if with a hammer, his teeth, his knees, the backs of his eyes. Most of it he had turned. Most of what he had not turned went down into the clay where he had told it. And what was left went where current goes when a man in a suit of steel gives it a road round him, which was the only reason he was standing afterward: up the rods. Up the eight from the boots, the shins, the knees, along the whole inside of the suit like water up a wick, to the breast and the back, into the two hearts.

He felt them go. The breast first, against his ribs, a small hard crack like a walnut in a door. Then the back, between his shoulder blades. Nine cells in each, cracked across, the charge going out of them into nothing, quietly, the way blood goes out of a cut.

The held stones went still. The nodes under their violet glass went dark from the boots up, one ring at a time, like a street putting its lamps out. In the helm the set gave one crackle like fat in a pan and died in the middle of somebody's word, he never knew whose; and he was alone in it with his own breath.

The bubble went last, because it was his and not the suit's, and he let it go, because he had nothing left to hold it with.

He stood. He was surprised to be standing. The suit stood round him black and heavy and dead, plate that did nothing now but weigh, with a smell coming off it of hot silver and scorched leather; and the water ran on past his boots toward the gutter as if nothing had happened to it at all.

At the top of the square Augustine took his hand off the stones and stood up, slowly, and looked at him.

"Ah," he said. "There."



It was very quiet.

On the roofs round the square nearly fifty of Kaldo's knights lay flat under the eaves with their self-loaders, and Ward on the chandler's with his blade across his knees, and not one of them had a set that could reach him now; and he felt every one of them not move, because Ward had said nobody moved and Ward had said it once. At the cookshop's steps Arthur sat on the narrow man and Bren stood over them. At the falls road's mouth Mira stood with her spear where she had been told to stand, and he could feel her packed heavy note leaning toward him down the square like a dog on a short rope, and not coming.

Twenty yards up the slope, on the dry setts, Augustine stood with his right hand closed and held to his chest and the blood on his shoulder and his grey hair drying in tufts, and waited.

Emeric did his sum.

The suit was dead. The set was dead. The blade on his hip was plain steel unless he lit it, and he had nothing to light it with, and one hand to hold it in, and that not his sword hand. The steady's window on his breast was a hole of broken glass. His gear, all of it, everything that had flown him and spoken for him and been, for years, the reason anybody gave when something he did was too fast or too strong for a man: gone, at once, in the middle of the fountain square, in front of the whole hill. There was nothing left on him to blame anything on.

And inside him the mage's half was a cup with a little left in the bottom, and the other half was where he had put it at ten o'clock that morning, behind the door, untouched. All of it.

He reached up with his left hand and undid the helm.

Two buckles under the jaw and a strap at the back of the neck, which he had to work at with his fingertips until it came. He lifted the helm off. The cold came in at his face, and the smell of the square, wet stone and steam and the cookshop's fat, and the sound of it, which was no sound: a whole hill holding its breath. He let the helm down onto the setts. It rang once and lay on its side in the running water with its dead visor looking at the sky.

Augustine watched him, and did not move.

The left gauntlet, with his teeth; he let it fall. The pauldrons, laced at the collar and buckled under the arm: the left he managed by feel, and the right he had to reach across himself for, with the bound hand held up against his chest out of the way, as he had been told to hold it. It came off and fell, and the lion's dead glass eyes went into the water face down.

The breastplate and the back were one cage, buckled under each arm on three straps, with the back heart's iron housing between his shoulder blades and its harness over his shoulders and the chute pack under it. He did the left side first, top to bottom, the cold brass tongues stiff in their holes, his fingers going numb from the lightning and the cold. Then he stood with his left hand on the right side's top buckle, breathing, because his arm had stopped doing what he told it.

At the top of the square Augustine waited, as a man waits for a pupil who has been sent to the board and is taking his time; as if there were nowhere in the world either of them had to be.

The right side's three buckles. The harness. The cage came apart and he shrugged out of it, breast and back together, the dead heart behind its broken window and the dead heart in its iron box and the chute nobody had opened, and let it go down behind him onto the setts with a noise like a dropped stove; and stood up out of it a great deal lighter than he had been.

The belt with the blade. The tassets. He went down on one knee in the running water for the greaves and undid them by feel, the buckles behind each calf, and the sabatons with their dead boot nodes, and stood up out of those too, one foot and then the other, in his padded undersuit and the boots he wore under the steel, with the water going over them.

It had taken a long time. The saddler's clock over the door said twenty to two, and he did not believe it.

The black plate lay round his feet on the wet stones in the shape of a man who had been taken apart, the helm and the cage and the arms and the legs of him, dull in the flat grey light, with the water from the basin running through all of it on its way down to the gutter.

He stood in the middle of it in his undersuit with his bound hand held to his chest and his left hand empty at his side.

Augustine looked at him a long time across the twenty yards, at the hand and the face and the plate on the ground; and then, very slightly, he inclined his head, as he had inclined it once over a young man at a farewell banquet thirteen years ago, giving him a blessing on his way out.

"Now," he said kindly. "Let's see what's under it."
