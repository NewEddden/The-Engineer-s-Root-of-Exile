Chapter 146 - A Mining Engineer

In the morning Fennick Crake was asleep on the guardroom bench with his coat over his face, snoring, and the guardroom smelled of burnt hair and sulphur and Maren's honey.

Ward woke him by kicking the bench. He sat up all at once, the way soldiers and sailors and men who have slept in a great many places they were not supposed to do, and took the coat off his face and looked at them, Ward and Emeric, in the door, with the grey morning behind them. Then he grinned. He had a long thin face that was mostly jaw and the grin took up most of it.

"You're the prince," he said.

"I am."

"The lantern prince." He said it with approval. "I heard about you on the road. Three hundred lanterns on a river. I thought, now there's a man who'd want to see a proper green." He sat back against the wall. "You did see the green?"

"I saw the green," said Emeric. He came in and sat down on the other bench, across the guardroom from him, with his elbows on his knees. Ward stayed in the door. "How did you make it?"

Fennick's eyes went very bright.

He told him. He told him for nearly an hour. It began with the green, which was copper, verdigris scraped off old roofs and ground fine with something he would not name, and a trick with the binding that kept the copper from going blue in the heat, which had taken him six years and the hair off his arms. It went from there to the red, which was harder, and the white, which was easy and therefore boring, and the screaming ones, which he was proud of, and how you made a flower burst inside another flower, which was a matter of two fuses and one prayer. And from there, without stopping, it went into everything else.

He had worked for the royal fireworks makers in the capital, the crown's own, from when he was fourteen. They had thrown him out at twenty-six for blowing the roof off their powder house. Then they had taken him back, because nobody else could make the green, and thrown him out again at twenty-nine for blowing the roof off the new powder house, which had been built specially, with a stronger roof. The second time, he said cheerfully, he had been found afterward sitting on the roof beam, which was the only part of the roof left, laughing. He did not know why he had been laughing. It had seemed funny at the time. It still seemed funny. Since then he had made squibs and bangers and crackers for fairs, and sold them out of a cart, and lost two fingers to a fuse that was shorter than it said it was.

He held up his left hand to show them, and wiggled what was left.

And he talked about what he wanted to make. A powder that would split a mountain along a line you drew on it in chalk. A charge that would knock a wall down flat in one direction, outward, and leave the house on the other side of it with its windows on. A powder you could knead like bread dough and drop off a roof and stand on, that nobody could set off at all except the one way you meant. He knew these things were possible. He did not know how. He had been trying to find out for fifteen years, in powder houses and cart beds and the back rooms of inns, and every time he tried he either blew something up that he had not meant to or failed to blow up the thing he had, and either way, he said, the Watch came.

"They say I'm dangerous," said Fennick. "The Watch. In every town I've been." He grinned. "They're right. I'm very dangerous. I just don't want to hurt anybody." He shrugged. "It's never been a lot of comfort to them."

Emeric looked at him across the guardroom.

He thought of a man in a coat full of burn holes in the back room of an inn somewhere in the north, with a northern lord's money in front of him, being asked to make a charge that knocked a wall down flat in one direction. He did not think Fennick would have asked whose wall. He thought Fennick would have been too happy to be asked.

"Do you know what you are?" said Emeric.

Fennick considered it.

"Trouble," he said. "Mostly."

"You're a mining engineer," said Emeric, "who hasn't met his mine."

Fennick looked at him. For the first time since he had woken up, he did not grin.

"What's a mining engineer?"

Emeric told him. Rock. Grayrock's iron face, and the coal, and a shaft on the east hill full of bad air and something very valuable that had to be reached through forty yards of hard stone. A railway that would one day want to go through a hill instead of round it. The old tithe houses on the plague rows, that would one day have to come down so that something else could go up. A quarry, for the cement kiln. All of it rock. All of it in the way. All of it wanting to be split, along a line you chose, so that nobody got hurt and the thing on the other side was left standing.

Fennick listened to all of it with his mouth slightly open and his maimed hand forgotten on his knee.

"And the lanterns," said Emeric. "Every year. On the sixth of March."

"Fireworks," said Fennick.

"Fireworks."

Fennick sat on the bench, and looked at the floor, and then up at the window of the guardroom, where the light was coming in grey through the bars. Then he started to laugh. It began before anything was funny and went on well after, and Emeric, who had heard it the night before over the water as the hayrick went up, found that he did not mind it at all.



There were rules. Ward made them, and Fennick heard them standing in the guardroom with his hat in his good hand, and agreed to every one of them, and plainly resented every one, and plainly meant to keep them all.

He would work outside the walls. Always. The old charcoal burner's yard past the quarry road would be his, with a powder house that Ward would choose the place for and Bram would build, earth-banked and stone-roofed, a long way from anything. Ward would keep the powder house's key. Ward would keep the count. Every ounce of powder that went into it would be weighed in on Harl's beam and written in Ward's book, and every ounce that came out would be signed for in Fennick's own hand, by weight, with the job it was for and the time. If the count was wrong by an ounce, the key went back on Ward's belt for a month.

"An ounce," said Fennick.

"An ounce."

"An ounce isn't anything. An ounce is a squib." He looked at Ward's face. "Right," he said. "An ounce."

He signed the first page of Ward's book that afternoon, for nothing, because there was nothing in the powder house yet and the powder house was a chalk line in a field. He signed it with great care, in a large, looping, surprisingly elegant hand, and drew a small firework in the margin next to his name, bursting, and Ward looked at the firework, and at him, and did not say anything, and turned the page.

The cart of fireworks he had not set off went into the old guardroom cellar under lock until the powder house was built. Ward counted it in, every tube. Fennick watched him do it, hovering, wincing every time a box was set down too hard, and told Ward the name of every one as it went past, and what it did, and how long it had taken him, and Ward wrote the names down in his book, which Fennick had not expected.



Saban came to the table on the twelfth with his hat on his knee and the face he wore when a thing had cost him.

"There's a man in the north been asking after our powder man," he said. "Since January. A buyer, for a lord he won't name, in the hill country past Aldane's." He turned the hat. "Asking in the capital, round the fireworks makers. Where's the one who made the green? Where's the one who blew the roof off twice? Nobody knew. Crake moves about." He was quiet. "Then the man found him. Found out exactly where he'd be, and when. The sixth of March, at Kaldo, on the river, for the lanterns."

"How?" said Ferra.

"Through me," said Saban.

He let that sit on the table.

"One of mine," he said. "My man at one of the posting inns on the north road, or my man at the capital end, or one of the carters between. I don't know which yet. I'd heard about Crake in February. My man in the capital told me there was a fireworks maker coming south for the lanterns, and I thought, well, there's a man Kaldo might want, and I told him to keep an eye out. And somebody between him and me sold the same news north." He put the hat back on his knee. "Same date. Both ways. Somebody was paid twice for it. If Ward hadn't had Crake in the guardroom by midnight, the northern man had two riders and a wagon waiting on the Aldane road, and Crake would have been in the hill country by morning making whatever a lord in the hill country wants made out of powder."

Nobody said anything.

"That's the cost," said Saban. "Of a network. It's people. People talk both ways. I always knew it." He picked up his hat. "I'll find which one. It'll take me a while. And when I find him I'll have to decide whether to tell him I know, or keep him and feed him what I want the north to hear." He looked down the table at Emeric. "I'll need your leave for the second. It's not a nice thing to do to a man."

"You have it," said Emeric.

"I thought I might," said Saban, and put his hat on.



The hayrick did what the war's dock fire had not.

The dock fire in the war had come close enough to the dock quarter's roofs that Kaldo had talked about thatch for a month afterward, and then gone on roofing with it, because thatch was what you roofed a house with, and slate came from the hill country at a price and tile came from nowhere. The hayrick burned nothing but hay. But the whole of Kaldo had watched it go up. They had stood on the river wall with their faces turned up to a sky full of paper and fire, and then they had seen a single falling spark turn a rick into a torch in the time it took to say so. Every person on that wall with a thatched roof had walked home under it that night and looked up at it.

So when the table said, the next week, that there would be no new thatch inside the walls, from the first of April, and that every thatched roof inside the walls now would be off and tiled by the first of April next year, the town did not riot. It grumbled. It grumbled loudly, in the market, for a fortnight. It did not riot.

It would have, if the table had not also said how it would be paid for. Ferra and Vantic had worked that out between them in the counting room over three evenings, with the door shut, and Emeric had not been asked and had not offered.

The kiln would make the tile. It was making brick already; tile was a smaller mould and a hotter fire, and the cement kiln's fire mage could hold the first firing while the coal kiln beside it learned to. The sawmill would cut the new rafters, because a tiled roof was a great deal heavier than a thatched one and most of Kaldo's old rafters would not hold it. That was the barony's side. The household's side was the cost of it, and that was where the House came in.

The House of Ducatis would offer fire insurance.

Vantic laid it out at the table in her even voice, with a sheet of figures in grey ink, and it was very simple, and Emeric, who had known the idea from the other life and had never once thought to raise it, sat and watched her lay it out and felt the particular shame of a man who has had a thing in his head for years and watched somebody else take it out and use it. A household paid the House a small sum every quarter. If its house burned, the House paid to rebuild it. The sum depended on the roof. A thatched roof paid a great deal. A tiled roof paid a fifth as much. So every household that tiled its roof paid less to the House forever after, and the difference, over a year, came close to the price of the tile.

And the tile itself could be had now, and paid for after, out of wages, a little every week for a year, through the counting room, with the House carrying it. Ferra had the figures for that. A mason's wage, a dyer's, a works hand's. What a roof of tile came to over fifty weeks, for each. It was not nothing. It was something a family could carry.

"And if they don't want it?" said Saban.

"Then they pay the House for thatch," said Vantic. "Which the House will be very happy to insure. At the price."

"And if they don't want insurance at all?"

"Then they're a fool," said Vantic, "and they tile their roof by next April anyway, because the table said so, and they pay for it all at once, and they've nothing coming back if it burns." She folded her hands on the sheet. "I don't expect a great many fools. I expect a few. The House will write them down in a column."

Ferra did not look up from her figures. Her mouth moved, very slightly, at one corner.



Tess had found two things wrong with the chair by the twelfth, and she had been right about both.

The first was the cooling joint. Not the one that had let go into his lap in the keep yard in February; that one Bram had re-brazed, and every other joint on the loop with it, and Emeric had passed them, every one. It was the one under the seat, where the water tube came off the stone's housing and turned up toward the plate behind the backrest. She had found it on the third evening, with her hand under the seat, because the cushion over it was a shade warmer than it should have been and she could feel that with her palm when she could not feel it with anything else. A hairline. Not a leak. A weep, that would have been a leak in a month.

She showed him. She did not say anything about it. She did not have to. He looked at the hairline under the lamp and did not say anything either.

The second was the twist. At the middle of the stick it was fine. At the edge, where she pushed it hardest, the turn came on in a jump instead of a slope, because the ink strip on the mana rheostat ran to its end too steeply; it was the part of the strip he had laid last, at three in the morning, and his hand had hurried. She rewound the stick's spring herself, to her own hand, a little stiffer, so the edge took more pushing and she could feel it coming. And she moved the ripcord. An inch and a half back from where he had measured it, to where her hand actually rested on the arm. She did it with her own tools at the works bench while he stood and watched, and did not ask him to help, and when she was finished she wrote all three changes in the operator's manual in her own hand, in the margin, beside his.

Then she wrote them in her data book too, with the date, and the reason, and what she had measured.

He read both later, when she was not there. In the manual, in the margin, beside his drawing of the ripcord and the small mark he put in the corner of everything he drew, a ring with one stroke through it that stopped short of the far side, she had written: Moved. He measured where it ought to be. This is where it is.



The arc was her find too.

It happened on the twenty-sixth, at the powerhouse, at the test lamp on the bench by the door, where every new lamp housing went to be run before it went up on the wall. Tess was in the chair at the bench with the housing open and the carbons in it lit, white, behind the smoked glass. A boy from the line gang was handing her tools. He handed her a spanner the wrong way round, and it slipped out of both their hands, and fell into the open housing, straight down, across both carbons at once.

There was a crack and a flash and a sound like fat in a pan, very loud, and the boy fell over backward off his stool. When they could see again the arc had gone out. The lamp was dark. The spanner was lying across the bottom of the housing where it had fallen.

It was stuck there.

Not lying. Stuck. Where its end had touched the housing's iron bottom, it had become part of it. There was a small bright lump of metal, rough, bubbled, where the spanner's steel and the housing's iron had both gone liquid together for the length of a breath and then gone solid again as one.

Tess did not swear. Emeric was across the powerhouse, at the dynamo, and he heard the crack and turned in time to see her not swear. She sat in the chair and looked down into the housing at the spanner, and her face did the thing it did when the line had a fault in it somewhere that she could not see yet. Then she reached in, carefully, with a cloth round her hand, and took hold of the spanner, and pulled.

It did not come. She pulled harder. The housing came up off the bench with it.

She put it down. She looked across the powerhouse at Emeric.

"What else would that join?" she said.

He knew. He had known since the arc lamps went up. Two pieces of iron touched in an arc, and the arc melted them where they met, and they flowed together, and cooled into one piece, stronger at the joint than a rivet and tighter than any brazing. On Earth they had called it welding, arc welding, and it had built ships and bridges and engines and the frames of half the buildings of a century. He had meant to bring it out for the boats one day. He had not had the time, or he had not made it.

He looked at her across the powerhouse, sitting in her black chair with the spanner welded into the lamp housing in front of her, and the question on her face.

"I don't know," he said. "Try it."

She tried it. All that week, after the shift, at the test bench, with a pair of the arc lamp's carbons in a holder Bram made her and a lead off the dynamo. She joined iron to iron. Steel to steel. Iron to steel, which was harder. Cast iron, which cracked the first four times and did not crack the fifth, when she warmed the piece first. She burned her wrist. She burned the bench. On the third night she burned her eyes, not badly, from looking into the arc too long, and Imani came up the hill at midnight with a basin and a cloth and a face like thunder, and the next morning Ivo had the darkest glass he had ever made sent up to the powerhouse, in a frame with a strap, and Imani's rule went up on the powerhouse door in Imani's own hand: Nobody looks at the arc without the glass. Nobody. I mean you.

By the end of the week Tess had a page in her data book headed Joining, and a table under it, and Bram at her shoulder reading it, saying nothing, with a look on his face that Emeric had seen on him only once before, over three plates and a pot of red lead.



He saw the grey seal on the first of April, in the gate passage, and he did not ask.

It was in the post bag. The bag was on the bench in the passage, open, waiting for the rider to the coast, with Hale at the other end of the bench doing the tally; and on top of the bag, where the last letter put in had been put, there was a letter folded small, in the House's heavy paper, sealed with a disc of grey wax the size of a thumbnail, and addressed in a small upright hand to the chief of the House of Ducatis, at the House's seat, by his own name.

Emeric had heard the rumour. Saban had brought it up from the capital a week before, with his hat on his knee, as a thing he had heard and could not yet price: that the House had written to its factor in Kaldo, from its chief, proposing a thing. A match. The House's daughter and the prince who had just paid it back early. A great many people at court would think it a very natural thing, Saban had said. The House had been lending to princes for longer than anybody remembered; it had never yet married one.

Emeric looked at the letter on top of the post bag.

He did not touch it. He did not ask Hale who had put it there, or when. He looked at the grey seal for as long as it took to see what it was, and then he went on through the passage to the yard, where Ward was waiting for him with the horses.

At the top of the east tower, the window was open. As he crossed the yard he felt somebody at it, high up, still; the faint quiet note he had felt in that tower since the first day she came, that he had never once mentioned to her because of what it would have meant to say he could feel it. He did not look up. He did not need to. She had been at the window when he came into the passage, and she had watched him come out of it, and she had seen exactly how long he had stood at the bench, and that he had not asked.

He mounted. When he rode out of the gate he felt the note in the tower go back from the window, slowly, the way a person goes back from a window when they have seen what they came to see.



Saban had the rest of it by the middle of April.

He had it from the same man in the capital who had sold the lantern date north, as it happened, whom he had found, and kept, and was now feeding; and the man had it from a clerk in the House's capital office, who had copied the factor's answer into the House's letter book and had found it worth a drink to tell somebody about.

Saban told it at the table. He told it plainly, as news, with his hat on his knee, in front of all six, and Ferra at her place with her book open in front of her.

The factor had refused. In writing. In a letter of one page to the chief of the House, her father. She had said that she meant one day to take the largest seat in the House, and everybody in the House knew it, and half of them meant to stop her. And that the day she married a prince, every one of them who had ever doubted her would have their explanation for her, ready-made, for the rest of her life. That the woman who had made the House's best book in fifty years out of the books the House had given up on had done it on her back. She would not hand them that. Not for a prince. Not for any man. The House could find another way to be near the crown, and she would help it find one, from the tower, where she was.

Saban put his hat back on his knee.

"That's all," he said. "Clerk says the chief read it twice and didn't answer."

Nobody said anything. Armand, at the end of the table, made a small sound that might have been approval.

Ferra did not look up. She was writing, in her book, at her usual pace, and her pen did not stop. Emeric watched her not stop. He saw her finish the line she was on, and blot it, and go on to the next, and he understood that she had understood it all, all of it, faster than anybody else at the table; that a letter of one page in a grey hand had taken a thing off the board that might have been very hard for her, and had been written so that it could never be called a kindness to her by anybody, least of all by Ferra.

She never spoke of it to Vantic. Vantic never spoke of it to her. In the morning Ferra went up the east tower stair with the quarter's commission figures for Grayrock, as she did every quarter, and Vantic checked them, as she did every quarter, and found nothing wrong with them, and said so, and Ferra went back down. That was all anybody ever saw.
