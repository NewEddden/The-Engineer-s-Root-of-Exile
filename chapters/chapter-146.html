Chapter 146 - Witnesses

By the middle of the morning there were about two thousand of them.

Ferra had the figure by the fourth bell, from the knights at the corners of the square counting heads in blocks the way Ward had taught them to count a column, and she wrote it in her book and showed it to him without saying it, and he looked at it and looked out of Pennock's window at the square and thought that it was wrong, that there were far more than that, and knew that it was right. The square was full. The streets that came into it were full back to the first corner, and the church steps were solid with people, and there were boys on the roofs of the houses on the north side and on the Staple's own leaded walk behind the parapet, where somebody had let them up and nobody had the heart to fetch them down. The inn had put its benches out. People had brought bread. There was very little noise. That was the thing he noticed most, looking down: two thousand people in a space that held a market on a Tuesday with twice the din, and they were talking in low voices, and a great many of them were not talking at all.

In the middle of it, on the flags by the pump, the gallows stood finished. The column's carpenters had worked on it by torchlight until the second bell of the night and started again at first light, and it was two posts and a beam, plain, of new wood that still had the pale of the saw on it, with a platform under it at the height of a man's chest and five ropes over the beam, coiled.

He had paid the town's hangman that morning. He was a Wayford man, a cooper by trade, who had hanged thieves for the barony in Sethe's time at a Silver a head and had not been asked to since the judgment, and he had come to the counting house door at dawn with his hat in his hands and said that he supposed it was him again. Emeric had put five Silver on the table in front of him, which was the town's rate, and the man had looked at it for a long time before he picked it up.

They brought the five out at the noon bell.

They came out of the Staple under the arches with a knight on each side of each of them and Osric in front and Declan behind, the way Ward had set it: Placit first, because he was the warden, and then Hollin, and Vennor, and Strade, and last Dray, on a litter carried by four knights, with his leg bound straight and his face grey. They crossed the square to the counting house steps through a lane the knights had held open in the crowd since dawn, and the crowd did not make a sound as they went through it. It drew back from them a little as they passed, the way a crowd draws back from a cart with something on it that smells.

Emeric stood on the counting house steps and watched them come.

The steps were the barony's. That was why he had chosen them, and not the Staple, and not a platform in the middle of the square. The counting house was the barony's house and its steps were where the barony's business had been done in front of the town for as long as there had been a Wayford, and the five were brought to the foot of them and stood there, or in Dray's case were set down there, facing up at him with the square at their backs.

To his left on the steps, on two benches brought out from the inn, Imani sat with the ones who had chosen.

There were eight of them. They sat in a row on the benches in clean shirts that the column's carts had given up, and blankets over their knees though the day was warm, and Imani sat at the end of the row on a stool with her register on her lap and her hand, most of the time, on the arm of the one beside her. Two of them could not have stood if they had wanted to. One of them was Wat, who had not asked to sit and was standing, behind the bench, with his hand on the back of it, very straight.

He did not point at them. He did not need to. Two thousand people had been looking at them since they came out of the counting house doors and were looking at them now, and at the five at the foot of the steps, and back.

The five looked well. That was the whole of it. They were well-dressed, still, in yesterday's clothes brushed down; they were well-fed; they were a warden in his fifties and a lord of sixty and a lord in his forties and a widow of sixty-odd and a young man, and in this world, where the years sat lightly on anybody who had eaten all his life, they looked what the world would call well. Placit's hair was dark. Hollin's hands were steady. Strade stood straight as a rule. They stood at the foot of the barony's steps in the noon sun looking like five people who had come to a hearing.

And on the benches above them, eight people who had been in their chairs sat in a row in their blankets, and not one of them looked less than twice Placit's age, and the eldest of them, by the book, was thirty-four.

Emeric did not say it. Nobody said it. The square looked, and saw it, and then there was a sound that went through the whole of it at once, low, from the front to the back and up the streets, that was not a word.

Ferra read the charge.

She read it from the notice, the one line she had left of his paragraph, in her ordinary voice, which did not carry, and Ward repeated it after her from the top of the steps in his, which did. Then she opened the brown calf book on a lectern Pennock had brought out, and read the column heads, and the first line, and the note in the margin beside the first line, the family to be told he has gone to work for the Staple, and Ward repeated that too, word for word, and she turned to the back of the book, to the losses, and read the running figure at the foot of the last page.

"Eighteen," she said.

"Nineteen," said Strade.

It was the first thing any of the five had said. She said it from the foot of the steps in her small dry voice that went down a table like a rule laid on a page, and it did not carry, and nobody but the people nearest heard it, and Ward did not repeat it. Ferra looked down at her.

"There's a loose sheet in the back, under the board," Strade said. "The last one isn't entered. It was a fortnight ago and I hadn't brought the book up. Nineteen."

Ferra turned to the back board. There was a loose sheet under it. She read it, and looked at it for a while, and then took her pen out and made the correction in the book, in her own hand, beside Strade's, with the date.

Strade watched her do it. When it was done she folded her hands in front of her again and looked at nothing, and her face did not change, and Emeric understood, looking down at her, that she had not said it to save anybody anything or to make anything worse, and not out of shame either. She had said it because the figure was wrong. She had kept those books for six years in a small upright hand without a day's illness or a clerk she had not trained herself, and she knew exactly what was in every column, and why she was standing at the foot of these steps, and she was not going to stand there while somebody got it wrong.

Then the eight.

It was slow. He had known it would be slow and it was slower than that. He had thought, in the night, that he knew what it would be like, and he had not.

Hew went first, because Imani asked him and he said he would. He was from a holding of Placit's up the mill road, and he had been taken, the book said, four years ago in the autumn. He stood up from the bench with Imani's hand under his elbow and faced the square and said his name, and his holding, and whose tenant he had been, and then stopped.

"How long," Imani said, quietly, beside him, because that was what they had agreed she would ask.

He did not know. He tried. He said it was a winter, and then that it was two, and then he stopped again and stood there in front of two thousand people with his mouth working, and said that he did not know, that there were no windows, that the lamp was always the same, that they came down with the food and he didn't know if it was once a day or twice. He said that sometimes he slept in the chair. He said that in the chair a man felt it going out of him, not like blood, like getting cold, and that after a while you didn't feel it any more, and that was worse. He said that he had had a wife.

He sat down. He had not said what had been done to him in any order anyone could have followed, and every word of it was true, and the square had heard every word.

Colley went next. He was younger by the book and looked older, and he stood by himself and faced the square and began steadily, in a flat careful voice, like a man saying a lesson.

"I went to work for the Staple," he said. "In the spring. Two years back. The rent was behind and Lord Hollin's steward said there was work at the Staple and I'd be paid and it'd clear the debt, and I went," and he stopped.

He stood there. The square waited.

"No," he said. "No. I didn't. I didn't go." He looked at Imani, and then at the square, as though he had just noticed it. "They told me. They told me so many times. They told me that's what I'd done, down there, every time they came with the food. You came to work for the Staple, Colley. You're working off your rent. That's what they said. I've been saying it." He put his hand on the back of the bench. "I didn't go. They came to the house at night with a cart. Two of them. My mother was there. They told her I'd gone to work for the Staple, and they told me, and I've been saying it."

He sat down very suddenly, as though his legs had gone.

Jem was angry. He stood up before Imani had asked him and turned to the square, not to the five, and he was angry the whole time he spoke, a thin high anger with nothing behind it to hold it up, and it was not at the five. It was at his father. His father had owed the rent. His father had owed it for three years, and let it run, and drunk, and when they came for somebody it was Jem they took, because his father was old and Jem was nineteen and strong, and his father had stood in the door and let them. He said that over and over. He had stood in the door. Jem pointed out into the crowd as though his father were there, and perhaps he was, and the people where he pointed looked at each other and at the ground.

Imani put her hand on his arm and he sat down, still talking, and went on talking under his breath for some time after, and Imani let him.

Perrin said his piece plainly, because it was the newest and he had had the least time to lose it. The Tollet holding. A Thursday after dark. The cart with the cover, down the south road and round by the lanes, and in at the stable gate, because they did not want to bring him across the square. The chair. He did not say how long he had been in the chair. He did not need to. He had been nineteen at midsummer and he stood at the top of the barony's steps with his hands on the bench-back, shaking, with thin white hair.

Maud came out of the crowd.

Nobody had asked her. She was not on Imani's benches and was not in any book Ferra had brought. She was a woman of perhaps fifty, which in this world meant nothing, in a brown dress and a working apron, from the Tollet road by the look of her boots, and she came up the lane between the knights from the back of the square, pushing, and the knights moved to stop her and Ward said something short and they let her through. She came to the foot of the steps a little way from the five. She had a letter in her hand.

She held it up. It was folded small and soft with handling, a half sheet of the old grey Wayford paper they had made at the Ambry house before Wendel sold it, with a seal on it that had been broken a long time ago.

"This," she said. "This came. Six years back. This spring six years."

Ferra came down the steps and took it from her, gently, and unfolded it, and read it. Then she turned to the lectern and the brown book and turned back through it, a long way back, to the front, to the spring six years ago, and ran her finger down the intake column until she found the line, and laid the letter on the lectern beside the page, open, the one beside the other, where Emeric could see them both from where he stood.

The letter said, in a clerk's hand he did not know, that the Staple regretted to inform the widow that her son Lorn had taken a place with it for the season to work off what was owed on the holding, and would write when he could.

The line in the book said Lorn, and the holding, and Hollin, and the sum, which was one Gold four Silver. In the margin beside it, in the small upright hand: family told, Staple.

And near the back of the book, in the losses, the same name. The spring after.

Ferra did not read any of it aloud. She did not need to. Maud was looking at the two sheets side by side on the lectern from the foot of the steps, and could not read, and did not need to, and then she was not looking at them. She was looking along the row of five at the foot of the steps, and her eyes stopped on Hollin.

"I know that man," she said.

Her voice was not loud. It went up, and cracked, and went on.

"Kill them," she said. "I know that man. I paid him. I paid him the rent every quarter day for twenty years on the Tollet road. My son." She was pointing at Hollin, and the whole square was looking where she pointed, and Hollin was looking at the flags. "They told me he was working for the Staple. My poor boy. They told me he was working for the Staple."

She did not say anything else. She stood at the foot of the steps with her hand still out, and then it came down, and a woman came out of the crowd behind her and put an arm round her and took her back into it, and she went without looking round.

Wat stood.

Imani had asked each of the eight at the start whether they would speak, and seven had, and she had said that to the square, that there were eight here and seven would speak and the eighth had chosen to be here and not to speak and would not be asked, and Ward had repeated it. Wat had been standing behind the bench the whole time with his hand on its back. Now, after Maud, he let go of the bench and came round the end of it and down one step, so that he stood in front of the others, alone, facing the square.

He did not say anything. He stood there, very straight, a thin old man in a borrowed shirt with his hands at his sides, and looked out at two thousand people, and they looked at him.

Imani stood up off her stool.

"This is Wat," she said. She did not have Ward's voice and she did not try to use it. She said it the way she said a name in the ward, clearly, so that the people nearest heard it and passed it back, and it went back through the square from the front to the rear, one mouth to the next, until it was all through it. "He was a tenant of Lord Hollin's on the Milford side. He was taken four years ago. He's thirty-one."

Wat stood there for a while longer. Then he went back up the step, and round the end of the bench, and put his hand on the back of it again.

The three hired men were brought up from the counting house with their hands tied in front of them and stood on the steps below the benches, and Emeric asked them, one at a time, in front of everybody, what they had done in the Staple, and they told him.

He had told them in the night what would happen if they did, and what would happen if they did not, and had told them it once and plainly and not argued, and Garth and Madoc had been talking since the back yard and had not stopped. Crisp had said nothing at all until the morning. In the morning he had asked whether the prince's word was good, and been told that it was, and had said, well, then.

They said it all. They said it badly and in the wrong order and each of them said the part that made him look least bad first, and the square heard that too. They had fed them. They had kept the lamp. They had brought them in at the stable gate in the covered cart and taken the stones out at the stable gate in the straw crates, and put the crates in the book-man's cart when he came, and where the crates went after that they had never asked and never been told. They had put the ones who died under the boards. Garth said that he had not wanted to, the first time, and that Crisp had told him it was that or go in a chair himself. Crisp did not deny it. Madoc said that the lords came down on the usual nights and sat with the stones in their hands, an hour, two hours, and that they did not look at the ones in the chairs while they did it, and that Lady Strade brought the book down and sat at the desk and wrote while the others sat, and that Lord Dray was the only one who ever talked, and that he talked about horses.

That was more than Madoc had meant to say. Emeric watched him hear himself say it, and go white.

Dray, on his litter at the foot of the steps, began to laugh, and then to shout.

"Ask him about the demons!" He had his hands on the litter's poles and was trying to sit up, and the knights beside him did not stop him. "Ask him! They fly! They came in off the roof and they fly, I saw it, one of them took me up off the ground in his hands and carried me over the wall, they're not men, you're all standing there looking at a boy on some steps and he's got demons that fly, ask him!"

The square looked at him. The nearest of them looked at him with something that was not quite pity and not quite disgust, the way people look at a drunk at a burial. A man at the front said something to the man beside him, and the man beside him shook his head, and they both looked back at the benches, at the eight in their blankets.

Nobody believed him. Nobody so much as looked up.

He gave the sentence himself, from the top of the steps, in his own voice, and Ward did not repeat it, because it did not need repeating; he had found, standing there, that his own voice carried further than he had ever known it to.

He said that Aubrey Placit, Garrat Hollin, Mattias Vennor, Oriel Strade and Edric Dray, holders of the Staple at Wayford under the Crown's charter, had held the people of this barony in secret, unlawfully, against rent they had made up themselves, and had used them in a way he would not describe because they had heard it described by the people it was done to, and had caused the deaths of nineteen of them, and put them under a floor, and told their mothers they had gone to work. He said that the barony of Wayford held high justice over every person in it, lord or tenant, and that he was the lord of Wayford, and that he did not need the Crown's leave for this and had not asked for it. He said that they would hang, today, here, in front of the people they had taken, and that it was his order and nobody else's.

Then he said the other thing, because it was his to say and he was not going to leave it for somebody to find out afterward.

"The three men who worked the cellar will not hang."

The square did not like it. He had known it would not. He felt it go through them, the whole mass of them, not a sound yet, a stiffening, the way a horse stiffens before it shies.

"They were in it," he said. "They fed the chairs and they kept the lamp and they put the dead under the boards. I know that. You heard them say it. I told them in the night that if they stood here and said all of it in front of you, they would live. They've said it. I gave my word, and my word is the barony's, and if the barony's word is good only when it's convenient then it's good for nothing, and every tenant in this square will know that by harvest." He looked at the three, and at the square. "They'll be held, and they'll work off what they did for as long as I say, in irons if need be. But they won't hang. I said so."

Somebody at the back shouted something. He did not hear what. It was taken up by a few voices and then let go.

"And one more," he said, "will not hang. You don't know his name. There's a man in the counting house behind me who carried the Staple's books between these five houses for three years, and took the crates away at the stable gate, and was the one they sent for when they wanted something done that they didn't want to do with their own hands." He let that go out over the square. "He will not hang either. Not because I forgive him. Because he knows who stood above him. These five were not the top of this. They bought the rite from somebody. They sold the stones to somebody. He knows who. If I hang him today, it dies with him. So he lives, and I'll find out."

The square did not like that either.

He could see it did not. He could see their faces. Two thousand people who had stood in the sun since the morning and listened to Hew and Colley and Jem and Perrin and seen Wat stand, and seen Maud, and had come here to see the ones who did it hang, and he was standing on the barony's steps telling them that four of the ones who did it would not. There was a muttering in it now, low and spread through it, and a woman near the front turned her back on him deliberately and stood that way.

He said it anyway. He had said it. He stood there and let them not like it, and did not say anything to make it easier, because there was nothing true that would.

He looked for Lisle once, before they went to the gallows.

He did not expect to find him and he did not. Lisle's town house on the lane behind the stream ground had its shutters up, Saban said, and had had them up since the day of the summons, and there was nobody in brown anywhere in the square. It was noticed. Emeric heard it noticed, in the crowd near the steps, a man asking another where Lisle's people were, and the other saying he didn't know, and both of them looking round, and then not saying anything more about it.

Near the church steps, at the edge of the crowd, a little apart, there was a man in a good plain coat whom Emeric knew by sight. He was the Ducatis clerk who kept Vantic's room two doors from the Ambry ink house, who had nodded to him in the street all summer. He was not writing anything. He was only standing there, watching, with his hands behind his back, the way a man watches the weather.

They hanged them one at a time, because the town's hangman said that was how it was done and Emeric did not know enough to say otherwise.

Placit went first. He walked up the steps to the platform by himself with a knight on each side and turned at the top and said something to the square about the Crown's charter and the Crown's justice and murder, loudly, and nobody listened, and the hangman put the rope on him and the hood over his head while he was still talking, and the platform's trap went, and he dropped, and it was quick.

Hollin was quick. He did not say anything at all. He looked, at the top of the steps, for Maud in the crowd, Emeric thought, or for somebody, and did not find them.

Vennor's was bad.

It was not the hangman's fault. Emeric understood that afterward and made himself understand it. Vennor was a light man, and the drop was the drop, and the rope did not do what a rope is meant to do when a man goes through a trap, and he did not die when he dropped. He went on living on the end of it, for a long time, a very long time, and there was a sound from the square that Emeric did not ever want to hear again and knew that he would. The hangman went down under the platform in the end and did what hangmen do, which was to take hold of the legs and pull, and after that it was finished.

Emeric watched it. All of it. He stood on the counting house steps with Ward at his shoulder and did not look away from it once, not at the beginning and not when it went wrong and not at the end, because he had given the order and a man who gives an order like that and looks away while it is done has no business giving it.

Strade went up the steps by herself and did not say anything and did not look at anybody. At the top she stood still while the hangman did what he did, and just before the hood went over her head she looked across the square, once, at the counting house steps, at Emeric, steadily, the way she had looked at him across her own table and across the strongroom floor, as though she had been waiting all summer to see how long he would keep it up, and had seen.

It was quick.

Dray was carried up on the litter and stood up at the top by two knights because he could not stand by himself, and he was talking about demons when they put the hood on him and still talking when the trap went.

Then it was five.

The square went home slowly, over the rest of the afternoon, the way a tide goes out over flat sand, a little at a time, leaving things behind it. Nobody cheered. Nobody had cheered at any point in the day. The benches went back into the inn. The eight went back into the Staple with Imani, slowly, on the knights' arms. The hangman cut the five down at dusk, which was the town's rule, and they were put in the counting house cellar under a sheet to wait for whatever their families wanted, which Ferra said would be decided tomorrow, and would not be decided by him.

Ferra sat at Pennock's table in the inner room in the last of the light with the book open at the day. He stood in the doorway. Ward was in the outer room with Hobb and the relay keeper, and the door between was open.

She wrote it in the same hand as everything else in the book, the one she kept for the barony, small and even and exactly as legible as it had to be and no more. He watched her write it. She did not look up while she wrote it, and when it was written she did not look at it again, and she did not show it to him, and he did not need her to.

Wayford. Five, by his order, not by writ. Entered because it happened.
