Chapter 134 - The Ditch

Imani came back from Wayford on the twenty-third of December, on the coast carrier's cart, in the dark, in the rain, and did not go to the ward. She came up the keep stair with her bag still on her shoulder and the rain running off her coat, and knocked on Emeric's door, and when he opened it she was standing in the passage with a sheet of paper in her hand held flat against her chest under her coat to keep it dry, and it was not dry.

"I haven't told the table," she said. "I came here first."

He let her in.



Wayford's lower town had been burying children since October.

Aster had written about it in his first weekly letter to Ferra's office in September, a line at the bottom under the barns and the boats, in his neat narrow hand: a flux in the lower town by the ferry, among the children, three dead. By the end of October it was nine. By the end of November it was twenty-two, and Aster's line had become a page, and the page had gone from Ferra's desk to Imani's in an hour, and Imani had read it standing at the ward desk and packed her bag.

She had been in Wayford three weeks. She had taken the girl from the north well with her, her first apprentice, and the kit, and her brass hinge with the bead in it, and a box of the hospital's plan paper with nineteen drawings on the back of it in two hands. She had written to Ferra twice, short, and to nobody else.

She put the wet sheet down on his table and smoothed it flat with both hands.

It was a map. Wayford's lower town, drawn in ink that had run in the rain: the ferry slope, the square stone house at the head of it, the lanes going down to the river on either side. Every house. And on the houses, small, in red, crosses. One for each child. Twenty-two. And three more, newer, in a different red, that had come after Aster's last page.

They were not scattered. He saw it the moment she took her hands away. They lay on the map in a long narrow fan, spreading downhill, from one point at the top of the lower town to the river at the bottom, and every cross was inside the fan, and there was not one outside it.

At the top of the fan, where it narrowed to a point, there was a well. And running past the well, a hand's width above it on the map, from the backs of the houses on the slope above, a line in brown.

"A ditch," said Imani. "An open one. It takes the slops from every house on the upper slope and every privy, and runs them down past the well to the river." She put her finger on the place where the brown line went past the well. "The well's lined with stone. The stone's cracked, here, on the uphill side, where the ditch is. In the wet months the ditch is full, and it's a foot from the crack." She took her finger away. "Every child with the flux drank from that well, or from a house that drew from it. Every one. I asked every mother. The ones who drew from the well at the bottom of the square, by the inn, not one."

He looked at the fan of crosses.

It was the first summer in Kaldo again. The rows. The wells below them. Imani's own map, the first one, that she had brought him in the first week and that he had stood up in front of the town and credited to her by name, and seen something in her jaw change and stay changed.

"You found it on the ground," he said.

"I found the mothers on the ground. I walked every lane. I asked every door." She was quiet. "I didn't find the fan on the ground. Not like that. Not all at once."

He looked up.

"I went in," said Imani.

She said it to the map, not to him.

"On the fifteenth. At night. In the room at the inn Aster found me. With the grey stone you gave me at the reading." She did not look up. "I'd been walking the lanes for two weeks and I had the crosses in my book, all of them, every house, and I couldn't see it. I had it in a list. A list of doors. And I kept thinking of your room above Kaldo, with Ferra putting the census in, house by house, and the whole town coming up out of the glass in front of us all at once, and you could see the wet ground under it like a shadow." She put her hand flat on the map. "So I went in. Alone. It took me most of the night to get through the door. And then I built Wayford's lower town in there, out of my two weeks of walking, the way we built Kaldo. Every lane I'd walked. Every door I'd knocked on. And I put the crosses in." She was quiet. "And it was there. The fan. Lying on the glass. Pointing at the well like a finger."

He did not say anything.

"I came out at dawn," said Imani. "I was sick in the basin. I slept until noon." She took her hand off the map. "Then I went and looked at the well, and found the crack, and found the ditch, and had Aster's men put a board over the well before dark, and a man on it, and told the lower town to draw from the well by the inn. All of them. And carry it." She looked at the three newer crosses. "These three were already sick. Two will live. The girl from the north well is sitting up with the third."

He looked at her. Standing at his table, in his room, at nine at night, in her wet coat, with the rain still in her hair, which had come down out of its cloth on one side and was hanging in a long dark wet curl against her neck, and she had not noticed, or had and did not care. She looked thinner. Three weeks. She looked as if she had not slept in a bed for most of them.

"You came here first," he said.

"Yes."

"Before the table."

"The table will want to know what it costs." Imani looked at the map. "Ferra will want to know what a covered ditch costs and what a capped well costs and what standpipes cost from Kaldo's foundry to Wayford by cart. And she'll be right to. And I'll tell her tomorrow, at the table, and she'll write it down, and Wayford will get it." She looked up at him. "I wanted to tell somebody first who'd know what it was. Before what it cost."

"What it was."

"That I went in alone." She held his eyes. "That I built a town I'd never lived in, in a room I'd only been in with you, and it was true enough to show me where the children were dying." Her voice did not change, but something under it did. "And that when I came out I was sick in a basin in an inn at Wayford, by myself, at dawn, with nobody to hold my wrist."

He did not say anything. Then he came round the table.

He did not touch her. He stood in front of her, close, the way she had stood in front of him in the gate passage in July with her hand on his chest. And she looked at him, and he saw her see what he was going to do, and not stop him.

He took her wrist. The left. In both his hands, the way she had taken his at the desk in December, with his fingers round it and his thumbs along the inside, over the pulse.

He did not count it. He only held it.

It was fast. And then, while he held it, standing in his room with her wet coat dripping on his floor and the map between them on the table, it was not as fast. And then it was slow.

Imani looked down at her wrist in his hands. She did not take it away.

"You're not counting," she said.

"No."

"I always count."

"I know."

She stood and let him hold it. She stood for a long breath, and another, with her eyes on her own wrist in his hands, and he felt the tiredness come up in her, all of it, the three weeks and the lanes and the doors and the twenty-five crosses and the basin at dawn, felt it come up through her wrist into his hands like heat coming out of a stone. And she let it.

Then she took her wrist back. Not quickly. She turned it out of his hands and held it against her coat, the way she had held the brass hinge in April.

"Ferra will want to know what it costs," she said.

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Imani agreed. She picked the map up off the table, carefully, by its dry corners, and held it against her chest under her coat again. "Goodnight, Emeric."

She went to the door. She stopped at it, with her hand on the latch, and did not turn round.

"Thank you for not counting," she said, to the door. And went out.



Wayford got Kaldo's answer at the table the next morning, in front of all six, and Ferra wrote it down. The ditch covered, in brick, with the new cement in its joints, from the upper slope to the river, and the houses on the slope run into it through pipes, not slopped over the back step. The cracked well capped with a stone, and a second well sunk on the far side of the square, uphill of everything. Standpipes, from Kaldo's foundry, on order, six of them, to go down the lower town's lanes when the ground thawed and Aster could lay a main to them off the new well. Ferra priced it while Imani talked, and when Imani had finished Ferra read the figure out, and nobody at the table said anything about it at all. Ward said: yes. Bram said: yes. Saban said that it was cheap at twice the price and that Wayford would remember who had paid it, which was what he was for. Armand, from his chair, said yes, and then, looking at Imani down the table, said that he had been the second of the six to go into the room alone and he hoped she had been less stupid about it than he had, and Imani said that she had not been, and the table laughed, and she did not.

Kaldo's own lower ward joined its house drains to the trunk under the main lane the same month. Imani walked every joint.



He asked Saban for a surveyor on the twenty-seventh.

He asked for one to plot the telegraph to the three new baronies: a line of poles and wire from Kaldo to Grayrock, and to Milford, and to Wayford, so that the four could speak to each other in an afternoon instead of a week by road. That was what he said, at the table, and Ferra wrote it down, and nobody questioned it, because Tess's telegraph had run from the line room to the South Gate for three years and everybody in Kaldo understood what it was.

And then he asked Saban, afterward, on the stair, for something more. The surveyor should give him the gradients. The slope of the ground every quarter mile along each route, up and down, as exactly as he could measure it. And the radius of every bend the route had to take round a hill or a river, the tightest a road could turn there without climbing.

Saban looked at him.

"A wire doesn't care about slopes," said Saban. "A wire goes over a hill on a pole the same as it goes along the flat."

"I know."

"And it doesn't care how tight it bends."

"I know."

Saban looked at him a while longer. Then he put his hat on.

"I'll find you a man who measures slopes," he said, "and doesn't ask what for."

He found one in a fortnight, in a hamlet in the hills west of Milford, and sent him down the river road with a letter. Linus Vance was sixty, thin and brown and weathered, with a theodolite in a leather case that he had made himself and would not let anybody else carry, and forty years of the weather in the south written down in twelve books, one line a day, that he brought with him on a mule because he said he could not leave them behind and could not explain why. He took his latitude from the stars. He told Emeric so in the first five minutes, at the gate, and then showed him, that night, from the wall walk, with a brass quadrant and a lantern and a book of tables he had worked out himself, and got it right to within a mile, by Emeric's reckoning, which was a reckoning Emeric could not explain.

He listened to what Emeric wanted. The telegraph to the three baronies. The routes. The gradients, every quarter mile. The radius of every bend.

He did not ask what for.

But Emeric saw him hear it. He saw Linus Vance listen to the word gradient, and then to the word radius, and look down at his own theodolite in its case, and then out over the wall at the dark country to the north where the routes would go, the hills and the rivers and the long flat valleys between. And then at Emeric. A long look. The look of a man who has been surveying roads for forty years and knows exactly what a road wants that a wire does not.

"Every quarter mile," said Linus Vance.

"Every quarter mile."

"And the bends."

"Every bend."

Linus Vance nodded slowly. He did not say anything else. He wrote it in his book, in the margin of the day's weather, and Emeric saw him write it, and saw him draw a small mark beside it that was not a word, and close the book.



The paper mill began that week on the works' wheel, in the old lower floor beside the sash saw, because the wheel was there and the river was high and it did not need much: a trough, and a stamper. Four heavy wooden hammers on a cam shaft off the wheel, iron-shod, rising and falling one after another into a stone trough of rags and water, all day, pounding the rags to a grey pulp like porridge. And Tess's moulds.

She made them on her draw plate. Wire, drawn fine, finer than she had ever drawn it for the telegraph, as fine as a hair, and woven by her own hands into a mesh on a frame the size of a page. A man dipped the frame into a vat of the pulp and lifted it out level and shook it, once, side to side, so that the fibres lay down across each other, and the water ran out through the mesh, and what was left on the wire was a sheet of wet grey felt that would be paper when it had been pressed and dried.

The first sheets were thick and grey and rough as sacking. Ferra looked at them and said they would do for wrapping fish.

Tess said they would get better. She made a finer mould that night.



Rylan asked Ward for a week's leave on the twenty-ninth.

Emeric learned that he had asked. That was all he learned. Ward told him on the stair, in passing, the way he told him the rota: that Rylan had asked for a week, in January, to go north, and that Ward had given it. And that Rylan had come to the guardroom with two letters in his hand, folded, in his own downhill hand, and had shown Ward the outsides of them, the names and the places, two villages in the north Emeric had never heard of, and had not shown him the insides.

"He wrote at the top of each one," Ward said. "I saw it through the paper. As near as I have it." He did not say anything else. He did not need to. Emeric had sat by the yard fire in the April of the third year and heard Rylan tell about the night before the rite, and the four men on the floor trading messages, each telling the man beside him what to say to whom if he died. Three had died. Rylan had carried three messages out. He had delivered one. He had not trusted his memory of the other two, and had not been able to write them down, and had carried them for twelve years.

He could write them down now.

Emeric did not ask anything else. He went on up the stair.



The chair failed for the first time on the thirtieth, in the yard behind the keep, with nobody in it but him.

He had built it in eight days. Not the chair Tess would have: that was months off, and it would be Tess's chair, and she would help draw it. This was the frame, the test piece, a plain oak seat on a square iron cradle, with the fist-sized gravity stone from the bottom of the cursed gallery set under the seat in an iron cage, and four small air stones from the chest at the four corners in a light iron lattice, and a stick in the middle under the right hand. He had cut the stones in the room. Gravity to take the weight off the ground, so that the chair floated a hand's width above the cobbles. Air at the corners, to push it: forward, back, left, right. And to turn it, because a chair that could not turn on the spot was no use in a passage, the stick twisted, and the lattice fired opposite corners against each other, front left and back right, so that the chair spun where it stood.

No governor. He had torn that one up.

Ward was in the yard, because Ward was always in the yard. Tess was at the kitchen door, in her wheeled chair, with her slate, because she had asked to be there and he had not been able to think of a reason why she should not.

He sat in it and took the stick and lifted it a hand's width off the cobbles, and it held there, steady, floating, the way a boat floats on still water, and Tess made a small sound at the kitchen door. He pushed the stick forward and the chair went forward, smoothly, at a walk, across the yard. Back. Left. Right. Smooth. Steady. Under his hand like a good horse.

Then he twisted the stick, to turn.

The chair turned. It spun on the spot, smoothly, exactly as it should, front left and back right firing against each other, and the yard went round him: the stable, the keep wall, the kitchen door with Tess in it, the stable again. Good. He let go of the stick to stop it.

It did not stop.

The corners stopped firing, as they should. But the chair went on turning. Nothing was pushing it now; but nothing was stopping it either. It floated a hand's width above the cobbles on the gravity stone, with nothing touching the ground, nothing to rub against, nothing to drag, and it went on spinning, exactly as fast as it had been spinning when he let go, round, and round, and round, the yard going past him in a smear, the stable, the wall, the door, Tess's face, the stable, the wall.

He grabbed for the stick. He missed it. It was going round with him, under his hand, and his hand was going round, and his head, and he could not find it. He could not find anything. The yard was a grey wheel and his stomach was somewhere behind him, going round a little later than the rest of him.

"Seven," said Ward's voice, from somewhere. Calm. Counting.

He found the stick on the ninth turn. He found it and could not think which way to twist it.

"Ten," said Ward.

He twisted it the wrong way, and the chair went faster.

"Twelve. Thirteen."

He twisted it the right way, hard, and the opposite corners fired, back left and front right, against the spin, and the chair slowed, and slowed, jerked, and stopped, a hand's width above the cobbles, facing the stable.

"Fourteen," said Ward.

Emeric let the gravity go. The chair settled onto the cobbles with a thump. He got out of it, and walked, in what he believed was a straight line, to the slag bin by the stable door, and was sick in it.

When he straightened up, Tess was laughing.

She was sitting in her wheeled chair at the kitchen door with her slate in her lap and both hands over her mouth, laughing so hard that her shoulders were shaking and there were tears on her face, and trying to stop, and not stopping.

"I'm sorry," she said, through her hands. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. You went round so many times."

"Fourteen," said Ward.

"Fourteen," said Tess, and laughed harder.

Emeric leaned on the slag bin and wiped his mouth and looked at the chair, sitting on the cobbles in the middle of the yard, innocently, a plain oak seat on an iron cradle, facing the stable.

It had done exactly what he had told it. It had turned when he said turn and stopped turning when he said stop. And it had gone on turning, because nothing in a chair that floats has any reason to stop. A boat has the water to drag on it. A cart has the road. A floating chair has nothing. Once it was turning, it would turn until something turned it back.

"When you let go," said Tess. She had stopped laughing, mostly. She had the slate on her knee and was writing on it. "When you let go of the stick. The other corners should fire. Just for a moment. Just enough to stop it." She did not look up. "Every time you let go of a turn, it should push back against itself until it's still. By itself. So you don't have to find the stick when you're going round."

He looked at her.

"A counter-burst," he said.

"I don't know what it's called." Tess turned the slate round so he could see it. She had drawn the lattice, four corners, and arrows, and under the arrows, in her neat hand: let go, fire back. "I know you need one." She looked at the chair. "And a cushion. If I'm going to be in that, I'm not being sick in the slag bin in front of Ward."

Ward, by the stable wall, did not smile. But he wrote fourteen on the stable wall with a piece of chalk, at the height of a man's eyes, and drew a line under it, and Emeric understood that it was going to stay there for a very long time.



At the dawn drill on the last morning of the year, in the cold, in the yard, Ward put him on the ground twice.

Not four times. Not three. Twice. And the second time he did not stay down; he rolled, and came up, and had his blade up before Ward's had come round again, and held Ward's next cut off, and the one after it, and stood there in the frost with his breath going up white and his arms aching from the cellar, and Ward stepped back, and lowered his blade, and looked at him.

"Better," said Ward.

It was the first time in four years he had said it.
