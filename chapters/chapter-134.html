Chapter 134 - The Junction

They went openly, by road, with a cart.

Emeric had thought about going the other way and had decided against it on the first evening, and Saban had agreed with him before he finished saying why. A prince who came down out of the dark over Wayford was a prince who had something to hide. A prince who came up the only road in the south at a walking pace behind a cart of paper was a man with something to sell, and everybody in the south understood that, and nobody in the south was frightened of it.

So it was a cart. It was the barony's good cart with the new springs, and a canvas over it lashed at the corners, and under the canvas, packed in straw in crates Bram's carpenter had made to the size, were forty reams of the standard sheet, rag and straw both, and two casks of the plain ink, and a case of Kaldo black in stoppered bottles, and a flat box of pens. The pens had come off Jorrin's dies on the lathe ladder in the winter, a hundred of them, every nib struck off the master Emeric had made the summer he gave Ferra hers, and slit afterwards by a girl in the finishing room with a steady hand and a lens. They were not as good as Ferra's. They were better than any pen in the south.

Ward rode beside the cart. Saban rode ahead of it, most of the time, because Saban liked to be the first into anywhere. Hobb drove. Hobb was the one of Ferra's three clerks who had come down from the Grayrock tally house, where he had kept the only honest book in it, a narrow quiet young man who wrote a better copy of Ferra's hand than anybody but Ferra, and he had been sent because a thing that was going to become a contract wanted a clerk at the signing, and because, Ferra had said, he was the one of the three who could sleep on a cart.

The road was the road from Rag and Gall, and it was not.

The relay posts were finished. Two summers ago they had been footings and holes; now they stood one every hard ride on the high ground, squat and grey, each with its mast and its lamp-box and a keeper's cottage beside it with smoke coming out of the chimney. At every one of them the keeper came out to the door to see who was on the road, and saw the barony's cart, and went back in, and Emeric knew without asking that the marks were going north ahead of him along the ridge faster than any horse. By the third day the people in the villages were already standing at their doors when the cart came through. By the fourth, a boy on a pony met them at a ford and rode alongside for a mile without saying anything, looking at the canvas, and then turned and galloped off north.

"They know," Saban said.

"They'd have known anyway."

"Faster than this?" Saban looked up at the next post on its ridge. "You built them a road for news, Highness. You're on it."

On the fourth afternoon the road came down off the high ground into woods, old woods, oak and beech, with the leaves just coming and the ground under them brown and bare. It ran through them for most of a day. There were no farms in them and no villages, only once a charcoal burner's clearing, and once a keeper's lodge with a gate across a side track and a man leaning on the gate who watched them go by and did not touch his cap.

"Whose?" Emeric said.

"Lisle's," said Ward. "All of it. From the stream behind us to the edge of the town. He's got the woods and most of the ground along the water."

"I don't know the name."

"You wouldn't," Ward said. "He's never been to court. He's never been to anything." He looked at the man on the gate as they passed. "Old house. Keeps to itself. Pays what it owes."

Emeric wrote the name down that evening, with the rest of the day, and did not think any more about it.

Wayford had not changed, and it had.

It sat where it always sat, on the rise above the place where the three roads met, the Kaldo road and the Milford road and the road that went on north toward the capital, with the stream running under the town in its stone channel and the square at the top of the town where all three roads came together. The junction. Every cart in the south that went anywhere went through it, and the town lived on the carts the way a mill lives on a river.

The counting house stood on the east side of the square, where it had always stood, with Pennock's lamp in its window. The inn stood on the north side, above the junction itself, where the cold room was. And across the square from the counting house, on the west side, taller than either of them, was the Staple.

He had walked past it four times. He had walked past it twice at the judgment and twice in Rag and Gall, and looked at it every time, and seen what everybody saw: a great old stone hall, older than anything else on the square, with a weighing floor open to the street on the ground storey under a row of arches, and a crane on a beam over the top arch for lifting bales, and above the arches two storeys of shuttered windows and a slate roof with a weathervane on it in the shape of a ram. It was where the factors weighed. Wool and hides and rags and galls, whatever came through the junction in bulk, went across its floor under the arches and over its great beam, and was weighed, and was chalked, and went on. He had taken it for a guild hall. It had the look of one. There was a lamp in its upper window at night, and he had seen that too, and supposed that guilds kept clerks late.

He looked at it now across the square from the cart, as he always had, and saw what he had always seen, and went into the counting house.

Pennock had the papers out before Emeric had his gloves off.

He was exactly as he had been. Thin, neat, colourless, with a face like a closed ledger, and on the table in front of him, laid out in the order Emeric would want them in, were the terms from two autumns before, and the Heskeths' letter from Milford, and the Ambrys' declarations and the Keelings' and the Pells', and a fair copy of every one of them for Hobb.

"Master Ambry is waiting at the ink house," Pennock said. "Master Keeling is waiting at the inn, because he did not want to be seen waiting here. Master Pell is at his vat." He did not say what Pell was doing at his vat. He did not need to.

Old Wendel Ambry sold.

He did it in his own shop, among his own coppers, with the smell of galls and iron and his grandfather's ink in the walls, and he did it the way a tired man sets down a load he has been carrying further than he meant to. He was a big old man with a white beard and stained hands and a voice that had shouted at Pennock for a quarter of an hour two years before and had not much shout left in it. His son was not in the room. His son was a clerk in the post now, at the Milford office, one of the men Ferra's lists had found, and his daughter had married into the post too, at the Wayford gate. There was nobody to leave the ink house to.

"You'll want the name off the door," he said.

"I want it on the floor," said Emeric. "Master of the ink house, for a wage, for as long as you want to stand in it. The name on the floor."

Ambry looked at him for a long time from under his white brows.

"That's what you said last time," he said.

"I meant it last time."

"You did," the old man said, as though that surprised him more than anything else, and signed. The ink house went to the barony for fourteen Gold Rand and four Silver, the coppers and the stock and the lease of the building, and old Wendel Ambry at two Silver a week as master, and Hobb wrote both figures in Ferra's hand, which he had been practising.

Keeling would not sign the thing he had signed before.

He said so at the inn, in a corner of the tap room, with his cap in his hands. He was a small anxious man with a vatman's arms and four children, and two autumns ago he had taken the wage in the time it took to read it to him. Now he had a letter in his coat from his eldest girl, in Kaldo, who was teaching Kaldo's vat floor to dip, and who had written to him about paper made of straw.

"She says it's rough," he said. "She says it's cheap. She says you've four vats down there and thirty hands and not enough sheets." He turned his cap round. "I've a vat standing cold in my own shop, Highness. Standing cold two years, on your wage. I'd sooner it wasn't."

Emeric had come ready for the terms he had brought. He looked at the little man with his cap and understood that he had come ready for the wrong ones.

"Say what you want," he said.

Keeling wanted his vat hot. He wanted to make the good paper in his own shop, with his own name on it, to Kaldo's process, and sell it through Kaldo. It was the third of the four offers Emeric had made two autumns before, the one nobody had taken, and Keeling had had two years at a cold vat to think about it.

So they wrote a partnership. Saban drafted it at the inn table, with Hobb writing. The Keelings would make to Kaldo's method and Kaldo's standard sheet, and Kaldo would take all they made. Ferra had set a margin for a vat that size, which Saban had in his book, and the Keelings would keep what they made up to that margin, and Kaldo would take its cut above it. And the Keelings would not sell, teach or tell the method to anybody, for as long as the partnership ran, or for ten years after it ended, whichever came later, on pain of the whole of it. The wage ended the day the vat was hot.

"She'll not come back," Keeling said, when he had signed. "Anse. Will she."

"I don't know," said Emeric, which was true.

"No." Keeling put his cap on. "She's the best of us. She always was."

The Heskeths did not come. Old Hesketh had sent a letter from Milford by the carrier, in his grandson's hand, which Pennock had on the table: he would supply the works with hide glue off the Milford tanneries and coarse wrapping stock for the bales, at a price he named, by the cart, from the spring; and would His Highness please remember that the grandson was still waiting to be sent for. Emeric read it twice and signed the price and wrote under it, send the boy, and Hobb copied that too.

Pell was at his vat.

He was exactly as he had been, lean and bent, his arms wet to the elbow, dipping and lifting and shaking, once side to side and once front to back, gently. He let Emeric say the whole of it again. It was a better offer than the first, because Emeric had two years more of numbers and the works was built and there was a partnership on the inn table with ink still wet on it that Keeling had asked for himself. Pell listened to all of it with his back half turned, working.

"No," he said.

He did not say why. He had not said why the first time.

Emeric stood in the shop for a moment. He had been going to leave. And then, instead of leaving, he asked a thing he had not asked two years ago, because two years ago it had not occurred to him to ask it.

"Whose land is this on?"

Pell stopped. The mould stayed in his hands, dripping into the vat.

"The shop?"

"The shop. The vat. The channel your grandfather cut." Emeric looked round at the walls. "Who do you pay the rent to?"

"Lord Lisle," said Pell. "Always have." He looked at Emeric as though it were a strange question for a baron to ask. "All the vats along the stream are Lisle's ground. Keeling's is. Ambry's building is. Half the town is. We pay Lisle's man at the autumn quarter and we always have."

"Thank you," Emeric said.

Pell looked at him a moment longer. Then he dipped the mould and lifted it and shook it, and turned the sheet out onto the felt, and did not look round again.

Saban was gone two days.

He did not go to find the vats. Everybody in Wayford knew where the vats were. He said, when Emeric told him about Lisle at supper, that a man who had bought four vats and never asked who owned the ground under them had better find out who owned everything else, and went out after supper and did not come back until the second night.

He came back to the inn table in the cold room at the top of the stairs with his book, and his face had the particular stillness it had worn at the long-room table over Wayford's vats, and he sat down and opened the book and turned it round.

"The Staple," he said.

Emeric looked at the page.

"It's not a guild," Saban said. "It's a charter. A private one, from the Crown, three kings back, to five houses who had land here and money, to hold the weighing at the junction. Every bale that goes across that floor pays a duty to the charter. Rags. Galls. Wool. Hides. Paper, by the ream. Everything bulk that comes through the junction." He put his finger on a column. "The five houses. Placit. Hollin. Dray. Vennor. Strade. They've got townhouses on the square and the upper roads, and the land under half the barony that isn't Lisle's, and a room at the top of that hall with a lamp in it." He moved his finger down. "The duty's not the barony's. It never was. When the Crown gave you Wayford, it gave you what Sethe held. It didn't give you what they hold. A charter's a private thing."

"Pennock never said."

"Pennock audits the barony's tolls," said Saban. "The Staple isn't the barony's. Did you ask him about it?"

"No."

"He'd have told you. He'd have had the charter on the table before your gloves were off." Saban turned a page. "Every rag your works buys that comes up the Milford road or down from the north pays at that floor. Every ream of Pell's paper that goes out pays. Every gall off the coast road." He looked up. "Your sulphur doesn't. It comes by sea to the lower water and never sees the junction. Your straw doesn't. It comes out of your own fields. Your ink doesn't. The iron salt's off your own spoil and the galls come in at the coast." He closed the book. "You've built a paper works that goes round the Staple on every side but one. I don't suppose you meant to."

"No."

"They'll have noticed," Saban said.

He sat back. He was not pleased with himself, which Emeric had expected he would be. He looked tired, and a little grim, and he looked at Emeric across the table the way he had looked at him over the wine the night he first said Wayford.

"You bought the vats," Saban said. "You never asked who owns the ground under them."

Emeric sat on after Saban had gone to bed.

It was the cold room again, the same room at the top of the inn, with the window over the junction and the square beyond it. He sat at the window with Saban's page copied into the notebook and did not look at the page.

He was tired of being told things.

Not the telling. He was glad to be told. He was tired of being told things he could have found out by asking one question, at the right time, of a man who would have answered it. Pennock would have answered it. Pell would have answered it two years ago, with his arms wet, if Emeric had stood in his shop one moment longer and asked it. He had not asked. He had come to Wayford with a trial sheet in his hand and a plan in his head and looked at the vats, because the vats were what the plan needed, and he had walked past a building with a lamp in its upper window four times.

It had happened at Emberfell. He had flown five hundred miles to a woman who held a foreign duchy and not once asked how she came to hold it, and he had known, dimly, for eight years, that there had been a brother. He had not asked. He had gone there with grain.

He sat with that for some time. He could feel that there was a shape to it, a thing he did, and that the shape had a name, and he could not, tonight, make himself say what it was. He looked out at the square instead.

The card came the next morning, before breakfast.

It came by a manservant in a dark blue coat with a silver button at each cuff, who waited in the tap room with his hat under his arm until Emeric came down, and then bowed very correctly and gave it into his hand. It was a heavy card, cut square, with a house mark embossed at the top, a sheaf of wheat in a ring, and under it a few lines in a clerk's hand.

Lord Placit presents his compliments to His Highness Prince Emeric, and begs to say that he and the lords of the Staple would be honoured if His Highness would dine with them, at His Highness's convenience, during his stay in Wayford.

The card was written on Pell's paper. Emeric saw it when he held it to the tap room window. The fibres were laid close and flat, with a thinness at the edges that Neta, after a summer, could not yet get anywhere near.

"Tell Lord Placit," he said, "that I'd be delighted."

The manservant bowed again and went.

Saban, at the breakfast table, did not say anything. Ward, at the door, did not say anything either.

He went across the square that afternoon to see Pennock about the fair copies, and on the way back he passed the room the Ducatis House kept at the junction, a narrow shopfront on the south side between a saddler and a chandler, with a brass plate on the door and nothing in the window. The door stood open. Vantic's clerk was at the desk just inside, a grey young man Emeric had seen perhaps three times in two years, writing, and he looked up as Emeric passed, and nodded, once, correctly, and went back to his writing.

Emeric nodded back. He did not stop. Everything that happened at the junction went through that room to Vantic, and through Vantic to the House, and he had built it that way on purpose, and he was not going to start minding it now.

He sat at the window of the cold room that night, with the lamp out.

The square was dark and empty. The counting house was dark, because Pennock went home at the bell. The inn below him was quiet. Across the square, on the west side, the great old hall stood black against a sky that was a shade less black, with its arches shut and its crane drawn in and its ram turning slowly on the roof in a small wind.

There was a lamp in its upper window.

There had always been a lamp in its upper window. He had seen it every time he had sat at this window, two years before, and at the judgment before that, and had taken it for a clerk. He looked at it now. It was a single lamp, steady, behind a shutter left open a hand's width, in a room at the top of a building he had walked past four times and never once asked about, and somebody was in that room tonight, sitting up late, and he did not know who.

He sat and looked at it until it went out.
