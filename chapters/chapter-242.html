Chapter 242 - Seed Coat

The second chest came up from the bonded shed on the morning after the feast, at ten, on Osric's back, with the snow still lying in the inner ward and half the keep still in bed.

Zale had not opened it at the table. A man did not open a second present beside a goose, he said; it made the first one look mean. So it had sat in Saban's shed on the quay for a week under Saban's seal, with a tarred cloth over it and a boy paid a copper a day to tell anybody who asked that it was salt fish. The other thing had sat there beside it, under a sailcloth, and the boy had been paid a second copper not to lean on it.

The other thing came up the keep stair after the chest, on a hand barrow, between Declan and two of the garrison, and Zale walked up the stair beside it with his hand flat on the sailcloth the whole way, the way a man walks beside a bier.

"Level," he said, on the turn. "Level. If you tip it, I'll have to go back for another, and it's four months there and four back, and I'll take one of you with me to carry it."

They set it down on the long room's table, where the goose had been, and stood back, and Zale took the sailcloth off.

It was a box of glass. A low wooden tray the length of a man's arm, black with tar at the seams, and over it a little house of glass panes in a frame of some hard dark wood, with a pitched roof like a cottage's, every pane bedded in putty and every joint of the frame puttied over again so that nothing could get in or out. Inside, on a bed of dark soil, stood a crowd of small plants in their own small weather. The inside of the glass was beaded all over with water, and as the room's warmth came into the panes the beads ran down in threads, and the green behind them came and went.

Emeric knew it on sight, and kept the name to himself.

A doctor in a smoky city on his other world had shut a moth's chrysalis in a jar of earth, and found a fern growing in it that he had never planted, living for years on its own water going round and round inside the glass. The doctor had built cases like this one, and men had carried tea in them across the world, and the bark that cured the fever, and the rubber trees, and the world had been different afterward. And here it was on a duke's table in a country that had never heard of the man, built by somebody on a spice coast nobody in Kaldo could find on a map, because somebody there had needed to move a plant that died when it dried, and had sat down and worked it out.

He had not made this. Nobody here would ever think he had. He found he liked that more than he could easily have said.

"The spice coast moves them this way," said Zale. "A clove seed's no good dry. Nor the other one, the nut. You plant them wet off the tree or not at all." He looked down the long room at Maren, who had come up from the kitchen in her apron with her arms folded. "I sent your cook a box of cloves two winters ago. She wrote to me three times before the spring asking for more. In a hand like a bailiff's. So I thought, I'll bring her the tree, and she can stop writing."

"I'll write if I like," said Maren.

"You'll write to the tree," said Zale.



They counted what had lived, which took longer than it should have, because everybody in the room wanted to look.

Imani had the case's end panel out by then. She had found the one pane that was held by pegs and not by putty, on the side away from the light, and had worked the pegs out with a blade from her new leather case before anybody could stop her, and was leaning in at the opening with her face among the plants, breathing the air in there, which smelled of wet earth and something sharp and sweet that nobody in the room could name.

"Two of these are dead," she said. "The small dark ones at the back. And this." She touched a seedling with leaves like a laurel's, gone brown at the edges and curled. "This one's dead and doesn't know it yet."

"Nutmeg," said Zale. "They told me I'd lose a third. I've lost a third." He leaned past her and pointed, one by one, at what was left. "Clove. Clove. The nutmeg that's still standing. That one with the shiny leaf is the black drink. The harbour clerks in the western ports live on it; they'd sell their mothers for it at four in the morning. The tall ones are the fever bark. And that sad stuff in the moss is cane, or was."

The cane cuttings lay in a trough of wet moss at the case's end like sticks of green bamboo, and about half of them had put out a shoot at a joint, and the other half had gone soft and black.

"What do you do with cane?" said Sela, who had come up with the school's broken biscuits from the night before in her handkerchief and stayed.

"Chew it," said Zale. "It's sugar, the whole stalk. The islanders give it to children instead of bread."

Sela looked at the black half of the cuttings as if they had been stolen from her school personally.

The chest held the rest. Zale took it out piece by piece and laid it down the table, and named each thing as it came, the way he had named the spices at the gate.

A sack of ears of grain, long and fat, every kernel the size of a pea and gold as a coin, set in rows round a white core. "Island corn. They grind it. They boil it. They make a bread of it you can only eat hot."

A bag of small hard yellow beans. "Bean that won't cook soft. I boiled a pot of them for a day once and broke a tooth. The islanders do something to them I never learned. Your cook will work it out."

A sack of grey-green beans the size of a fingernail, hard as pebbles, with a groove down one face. "The black drink, raw. You roast them till they're brown, and grind them, and pour water through." He held the sack out to Maren. "I'll warn you now. The first cup is like licking a pot. The second is the best thing you ever had."

A bundle of curled bark tied with fibre, brown outside and red-brown within, the length of a forearm. "Fever bark. The islanders chew it for the shaking fever. There's a fever out there that comes every third day like a man collecting rent, and the ones who chew this are the ones who get up again." He put the bundle on the table, and Imani took it before it was properly down.

A stoneware jar the size of a man's head, stoppered and waxed, which he unstoppered so the room could smell it: something white and set hard with the cold, like lard, that smelled of nothing Kaldo had a word for, sweet and clean. "Oil from a nut. A big brown hairy nut the size of your head that grows on a tree on the beach. It's water when it's hot and that when it's cold, and they cook in it and rub it in their hair."

Abloh, who had made Kaldo's soap since the second year and did not believe in anything he had not boiled himself, came round the table and put his nose to the jar and kept it there.

And last a knife, a seaman's knife in a sheath, with a dull brown handle; and beside it a dull brown lump the size of a fist, like old toffee.

"Tree gum," said Zale. "It's like the stuff in your boots, the white sap, but not. Put it in hot water and it goes soft as dough. Take it out and it goes hard as horn, whatever shape you've left it in." He picked the knife up and showed them the handle. "They make handles of it. Nothing rots it. Nothing eats it. The sea doesn't touch it."

Tess put her hand out from her chair by the window without a word, and Osric, who was nearest, put the lump in it, and she weighed it in her palm and smelled it and then asked Maren, very politely, for a bowl of hot water.



By noon the long room had become a market. By two it had become a quarrel.

Maren held the green beans and would not give them up. Abloh held the oil and would not give it up. Saban, who had sat on the end of the table with his legs crossed through all of it, had worked out what a soap would fetch in the capital that lathered in hard water and did not stink of tallow, and what a pound of fever bark would fetch in any port where a fever came every third day, and was looking at the case of seedlings with an expression of great sadness, because none of it could be sold yet and some of it might never be.

Tess had the gum soft in Maren's bowl and was pulling it into a rope, and folding the rope, and pulling it again, while the water steamed up round her face.

Ferra took the room back at three, by opening her book on the table and drawing two lines down a clean page, so that it had three columns, and writing a heading at the top of each in her square capitals.

MILFORD NOW. NURSERY. DRY STORE.

The room looked at the page. The room stopped quarrelling.

"Corn and beans," said Ferra. "They're seed. They want ground. They go to Milford in the spring, to Hollis, and Hollis does what he always does, which is plant a strip and look at it before he believes it." She wrote them in the first column. "Not all of it. Half of each, held back in the dry store, in the reserve granary, in case the strip fails. Hollis will say the same."

"He'll say less than half," said Emeric.

"He'll say a third, and I'll say half, and it'll be half." She wrote it in the third column. "The seedlings and the cuttings can't go to Milford. There's nowhere in Milford warm enough in March for a thing off a spice coast. They go in the nursery."

"Which nursery?" said Saban.

Ferra did not look up.

"The glasshouse at the ice works," she said, and wrote it.

Imani, at the case with the bark across her knees, looked up at Ferra; and Ferra, writing, did not look up at anybody; and Emeric looked at the green behind the wet glass, and thought of the long green room on the warm side of the works with the snow coming down past its panes, and the bench at its end, and the lamp guttering, three winters ago this month.

"It's warm," said Ferra. "It's lit. It's never been cold a night since it was built, because of the wall." She ruled a line under the column. "And nobody will steal a twig out of it, because nobody will know what it is."

"The beans," said Maren.

"Are yours," said Ferra. "Write me what you do with them."

"The oil," said Abloh.

"Is yours, for one test batch, and not one ounce in the capital until there's a supply." She looked at Saban. "Which there isn't."

"Which there isn't," Saban agreed, sadly.

"The bark," said Ferra, and looked at Imani.

Imani said nothing. She had both hands on the bundle in her lap.

Ferra wrote IMANI in the margin beside FEVER BARK, and went on.



He found Imani in the dispensary at eight, with the bundle untied on her bench and three curls of bark laid out in a row under her lamp, and the rest put away in a jar with a lid.

She had broken one of the curls across and was looking at the break. It was red-brown and grainy, and the dust off it on her fingers was the colour of brick.

"Taste it," she said, without turning round.

He touched his tongue to the end of the broken curl. It was bitter: not the bitter of strong tea or green walnuts but a clean deep bitterness that spread out across the whole of his mouth and stayed there, and went on getting worse after it ought to have stopped.

"Zale's coast has a fever that comes every third day," said Imani. "Shaking, then burning, then sweat, then nothing, then shaking again. I've never seen it. Nobody in Kaldo has ever seen it. It doesn't come this far north." She turned the break toward the lamp. "But Zale's men will bring it up the river one day, in their blood, because Zale's men go everywhere. And this is what the islanders chew." She looked at him. "You know what it is."

The door was shut. The shutter was shut. He looked at it anyway.

"Cinchona," he said. "That's the tree. Where I was they called the fever bark that, after a countess who was supposed to have been cured by it, though she probably wasn't. The thing in the bark that does the work is quinine. It's what makes it bitter." He touched the brick-coloured dust on her bench. "It doesn't cure the fever where it's caught. The fever's a small thing in the blood, carried by a gnat that bites at night. The bitter kills it in the blood."

"How much?"

"By weight. Dry the bark, grind it fine, and weigh it on Ottoline's balance, not by the spoon, because no two spoons hold the same. A dose of the powder a few times a day while the fever's on, in water or wine, because it's foul." He thought. "And the body tells you when it's too much. The ears ring. A hum, then a whistle. Then the patient can't hear you properly, and gets a headache, and feels sick. If the ears ring, you're at the top. Stop, or go down."

Imani took a card from the stack on her shelf, and a pencil, and wrote. He watched her write it: FEVER BARK, and under it the tree's other name, spelled as he had said it, and under that the line about the ears.

"There's no bark here to dose with," she said. "A forearm's worth. That's one bad fever, if that."

"There are three trees in the case."

"Three seedlings. How long?"

"Years."

"Years," said Imani. She put the card in her box under F, between the foxglove and the feverfew, and closed the lid on it. "Then I'll keep the bark for the man Zale brings home shaking. And I'll keep the card for the one after that."

She turned round on her stool and looked at him properly for the first time since he had come in.

"You liked that," she said. "The glass box. You liked that you didn't make it."

"Yes."

"Good," said Imani, and turned back to her lamp.



The alphabet cost them a week.

They had decided it on the twenty-third, and he had been sure of it; and because he had been sure, and had said so, and had been right about the counting in the summer, nobody at the table argued very hard.

The carving had thirty-four signs. The brown hand in the margins was letters, plainly, a sloped quick script of twenty-odd shapes that Zale could make out a few words of and no more. And the brown hand did not only sit in the margins. All through the book, between the rows of carving, the little sloped notes ran along under the signs, one under another, like the glosses he had seen in old manuscripts on his other world, where a monk had written the meaning of a hard word over it in small letters. So Vey had read it, Emeric said. Or begun to. He had sat at the wall and written under each carved sign the sound he thought it made, in his own letters. Find which brown letter sat under which carved sign, often enough, and they would have the carving's sounds; and with the sounds, and Zale's ear, a word would come up out of the stone sooner or later, and then another.

It was how the stone with three scripts had been read. It was a good idea. He set the table to it on the morning of the twenty-seventh, an hour after breakfast, and it ate the week.

Ferra made the key. A sheet ruled into thirty-four rows, one for each carved sign, and against each sign every brown letter that had ever been found written under it, with a tally. Zale read the brown letters out to her, page by page, as best he could; Emeric called the carved sign above each one; Ferra tallied. It was slow work, and dull, and they did it for two hours every morning and some of the evenings, while the snow went off the ward and came back and went off again.

Maren brought the first pot of the black drink up on the twenty-eighth.

She had roasted the green beans the evening before in her wide iron pan over the gas, stirring them with a wooden spoon and swearing, until they went from green to yellow to brown and began to crack like twigs in a fire and give off a smoke that went up the back stair and through the whole keep and brought the watch in off the wall, because it smelled, the sergeant said, like a church burning down. She had ground them in Bram's altered pepper mill. She had poured hot water through the grounds in a cone of the works' heavy paper, as Emeric's sheet told her to, not boiling, and had then, because she did not trust the sheet, boiled it anyway for a minute to be sure.

It came up black and thick and furiously bitter, and Zale drank his cup down in one go and held it out for another.

"That's it," he said. "That's the harbour. You've boiled it, mind. They'd hang you for that in the west."

"Then they can come and hang me," said Maren.

Saban drank half his and put it down and looked at it for some time without speaking, and then drank the other half. Imani drank hers and said it was a stimulant, and that she would decide what for after she had seen what it did to the garrison. Ferra drank hers with milk, at the table, while she tallied, and then a second; and she was still tallying at two the next morning, alone in the counting room, wide awake and very cross about it, and told him at breakfast that if he had known it did that he ought to have said.

"I did say."

"You said it was bitter."

"I wrote it on the sheet. Under the bitter. In capitals."

"I don't read Maren's sheets," said Ferra. "Maren reads Maren's sheets."

By the thirty-first the key had a great many tallies on it and no answer at all.

Ferra said so on the morning of the thirty-first. She did not say it to the table. She put the key down in front of him, on top of the book, with her finger on the fourth row.

"This one," she said. "The one like a fish hook. It's had nine different brown letters under it. Nine. On different pages." She moved her finger down. "This one's had seven. This one's had twelve, if you count the one with the tail as different from the one without, and I do." She took her finger away. "If he was writing the sound of each sign under it, he can't spell."

"Spelling drifts," said Emeric. "In a long copy. A man gets tired, he hears it differently, he writes it differently. And the brown hand's not his first language for writing that sound, it's his letters for his own tongue, bent to fit."

"Nine ways?"

"The commonest one should come up the most. Give it a few more days and the right letter will stand out of the noise."

Ferra looked at him across the table a while. Then she picked the key up and went back to tallying, and said nothing else about it; and that was worse than anything she might have said, and he knew it, and went on anyway.

It snowed again on the first, a thin dry snow, and the long room did two hours on the key that morning anyway, because he asked. It snowed on the second. By the evening of the second the right letter had not stood out of the noise for any sign at all, and the key looked like a wheat field after a storm, and Zale, who had read brown letters aloud for six mornings to people writing down something he could not see the point of, had started to carve a pine block into a small boat at the end of the table while he read, and to read more slowly.



It came apart on the morning of the third, at a quarter past ten, on page thirty.

Zale was reading out the brown letters under the eighth row of carving. He had a habit, by then, of sounding them under his breath as he went, the way a man runs his finger along a line he cannot quite read, and he was doing it now, with the pine boat in his other hand and his knife in his teeth; and he stopped, and took the knife out of his teeth, and read the line again aloud, quite slowly, in his sea voice, as one long word.

Then he laughed.

It was not a polite laugh. It came out of him all at once and went on, and he put the boat down and put his forehead on the back of his hand on the table and laughed into the wood, while Ferra waited with her pencil up and Emeric watched him with a cold feeling arriving somewhere under the breastbone.

"My feet are wet," said Zale, when he could.

Nobody said anything.

"That's what it says. Under your fish hook and your three little ladders and whatever that one is." He wiped his eyes with his thumb. "I can hear it, if I say it fast. It's not sounds for the carving. It's him. It's his own talk. He's sat in front of his wall, in the wet, copying, and he's written in the gap, my feet are wet." He turned back a page, and found another run of brown letters under another row, and read it under his breath, and then aloud. "Bread gone. Rain since the morning." He turned back again. "Lamp oil for two days. The man with the goat came again." He looked up at them, delighted. "He's not reading your carving. He's keeping a diary in it."

Ferra put her pencil down on the key.

She did it very gently, laid flat beside the ruled sheet, parallel to its edge, the way she put a pencil down at the end of a column that had come out right. Then she folded her hands.

"A week," she said.

"A week," said Emeric.

"Seven mornings and four evenings. Thirty-one pages." She looked down at the key, all its tallies, all its nine and seven and twelve. "I've had mill clerks lose me a week. I've never had one do it so tidily."

Then she opened her book, at the page with the year's number on it that she had started on the night of the twenty-fifth, and wrote in it, and turned it round so that he could see.

3 Jan. One week, the alphabet. E.

"It's an entry," she said. "Not a punishment. Things that cost go in the book."

Zale, across the table, had his forehead back down on his hand.



Nothing about the carving changed after that. It was as shut as it had been on the day Zale put the book down in front of Armand, and it stayed shut; whatever the thirty-four signs were, sounds or syllables or something with no name in any language at the table, no brown letter under them had ever been a key.

What changed was the brown hand.

They had been treating it as a lock. It was a man talking. And there was one man at the table who had heard that man's language spoken in a ship's fo'c'sle three winters running, and the trick was not to read it but to say it, letter by letter, as best they could, until Zale's ear caught the sound of a word inside it.

So they worked the other way round. Ferra took the brown letters, all twenty-three of them, and made a new sheet; and against each one Zale gave the sound he thought it made, from the numbers and the dozen words he had written out for Emeric two winters before; and where Zale did not know, Ferra counted, and Emeric guessed from the count, and they tried the guess aloud. A line would be read out by the three of them together, badly, like children reading a primer, in a language none of them spoke, and nine times in ten it was noise. The tenth time Zale would sit up and say, that's water; or that's old, like a man who's old, not a thing; or that's a word for what you do to a fish when you take its insides out, I don't know why he's written that. And the word went on Ferra's list, and the next line had one more word in it that they could hear.

By the end of the first day they had forty words. By the end of the third, two hundred. It came faster as it came, the way a frozen pipe comes when the first of the ice has gone, and by the fifth morning there were whole lines in the margins Zale could read straight off with only a word or two to guess at, and Vey had begun to talk.

He was a cheerful man, mostly. He complained a great deal: about rain, and his feet, and a man with a goat who came every day to look at him and would not say why; about lamp oil and lice and a fever he had for a week in the spring. He loved his wall. He wrote about the carving the way a man writes about a woman he has not been introduced to: what he thought it might mean, and then that he was a fool, and then what he thought it might mean after all. He could not read a sign of it. He said so, often, and cheerfully, and went on copying anyway, because, he wrote, somebody would one day, and they would need it exact.

"I like him," said Zale, on the fifth day, closing the book at noon. "I'd have shipped him. He'd have been sick all the way and written it down."



Ferra found the numbers.

She found them on the fifth evening, alone, at her own table in the counting room near midnight, with the lamp low and the book open in front of her and the key, the ruined one, turned over to its clean back for scrap; and she did not find them in the brown hand, where Zale's numbers had been all along. She found them in the carving.

He came in a little before midnight to fetch her to bed, and found her not looking up.

The counting room at night had been hers before it was anybody else's; before the war, before the wire, before the six. He had come up to it at this hour a hundred times in the first years, with a question about a figure, and found her here with the lamp and her columns and her shawl round her shoulders, and the room smelling of ink and tallow and her; and they had sat on either side of this table and argued about money until one of them gave in. It was the same table and the same lamp. The book in front of her was a stranger's book and the columns were rows of carving, and she had the same line between her brows that she had had then, at a figure that was not right yet.

He came round behind her chair and put his hands on her shoulders.

"Bed," he said.

"No."

He bent and put his mouth to the side of her neck, under her ear, where the hair was pinned up and the skin was warm from the lamp.

"Bed."

"Work first," said Ferra, and did not move her head away; she tilted it, very slightly, to give him more of her neck, and went on looking at the book. "Then fun. You taught me that. Don't stop. But don't talk."

So he did not talk. He stood behind her with his mouth at her neck and his hands on her shoulders and his thumbs moving slowly at the top of her spine, where she held all of the day; and she leaned back into his hands a little at a time, and went on looking at the carving, and after a while she put her finger on the page.

"There," she said. "Look."

He looked. It was the page of the drawings, the first of four: a small round shape drawn very carefully with a fine pen, the size of a thumbnail, with something like a net laid over it. Beside it, along the bottom of the page, ran a short row of carved marks, separate from the long rows above. Not the thirty-four signs. Smaller marks, lines with dots beside them.

"They're not in the alphabet," said Ferra. "The thirty-four. They're never in the long rows, except at the ends, sometimes, a row's last mark. Never in the middle. And they're always next to a drawing." She turned the page. The next drawing: the round shape split, and something coming out of it, a curl of green. Another short row underneath, of the dot marks. She turned again: a tree, a grey trunk, wide-branched. A short row. She turned again: a long pod hanging from a twig, drawn open down one side to show what was in it; round shapes in a line, like peas.

And under the pod, a short row of dot marks, longer than the others.

"Count the peas," said Ferra.

He counted. There were seven.

"Now the dots," said Ferra.

The last mark in the row under the pod had a long line with a dot above it and two beside. The mark before it, a long line and one dot beside. Before that, three short lines and a dot. She had copied all of them out on the back of the ruined key and numbered them in her own figures in a column underneath, one to seven, against every place in the book where the dot marks came in order, and they went up, one at a time, as regularly as a ledger's page numbers.

"It's a list," she said. "You said so. There's always a list somewhere, and things in a list get counted." She tapped the row under the pod. "There are seven of them in there, and he's counted them for whoever comes next."

He was quiet a while, with his hands still on her shoulders.

"You found the numbers first," he said.

"I found the numbers first." She looked at the column of her figures on the back of the key, all the way down to seven, and he felt her shoulders do something under his hands that was very nearly a shrug and was, in fact, a woman as pleased with herself as she had been in a year. "I'll have the rest of them by Saturday. He goes to at least twenty. There's a twenty on page fifty-one by a drawing of a wall, and I think it's a count of rows."

"Bed," said Emeric.

Ferra closed the book, and squared it on the table, and laid her pencil beside it, parallel. Then she stood up, and turned round inside his hands, and took the front of his shirt in her fist.

"Now," she said. "Fun."



Zale read them the seed on the morning of the eighth.

He read it aloud from the margins round the four drawings, with Ferra's word list at his elbow and the black drink going cold beside him, slowly, stopping for words, with the long room's winter light on the page. Emeric sat across the table from him with his hands round his own cup.

"He says it's a seed," said Zale. "The little round thing. He says the carver has drawn it more carefully than anything else on the whole wall, every thread of the net." He bent closer. "The net is the seed's coat. Like a nut has a shell. Like..." He frowned. "Some word. Like a fruit from his country with a stone in it that has lines all over. A peach, maybe. The net's the coat."

Emeric looked at the drawing.

He had seen that net every day for nine years. It was in the band at his wrist, under the leather; it was in his mother's stone in Bram's silver star on the shelf in his room; it was on every one of the grey stones Corbin had sent down to Kaldo in a box as an insult. The court called it the carvers' work, the lattice. Old craftsmen, long dead, who had cut the grey stones so finely that nobody since had been able to work out how, and the craft was lost. That was what the Hollow Crystals were, in every book the tower owned: carvings. Pretty, useless, mysterious things. The kind a king kept locked in his treasury for his boys, because they were the last things his dead wife had left him. And Vey, who had never seen one in his life, had looked at a drawing on a wall and written beside it, in the wet, with his feet cold, that the net was a coat.

He held his cup and kept his face where it was. Zale did not know what a control stone was. Zale did not know that Kaldo had any. Zale was reading him a dead man's notes about a seed.

"Go on," he said.

"He says it sleeps." Zale's finger went along the margin. "Where the air is thin, it sleeps. Where the air is thick, it wakes." He looked up. "That's what he's written. Thin and thick, like soup. I don't know what he means by it. Thick air." He went back to the drawing. "He says look how the carver has drawn the air. Round the seed that's asleep, nothing. Round the one that's woken up, lines, lots of lines, close together, all round it." He put his finger on the second drawing, the split seed with the curl of green, and Emeric saw it: the hatching all round the sprout, close and fine, that he had taken for shading. "He says the carver's showing the air thick round it. Like a fog. And then the tree, with the lines round it too, but thinner. He thinks the carver means it needs the thick air to wake but can grow in thinner, after." He turned a page. "And then this. He doesn't understand this one."

It was a fifth drawing that Emeric had not seen before, small, low on the next page, half in the margin as if the carver had added it later: the seed again, with its net, and a hand above it holding a jar, tipped, and something pouring out of the jar onto the seed in a thick stream. The seed under the stream was drawn broken. Split, not as a seed splits to let a sprout out but across, the net torn, with short sharp lines coming off it like the lines a child draws round a thing that has hurt itself.

"He says it's a warning," said Zale. "But he can't think what about. Don't drown it, he thinks. Don't water it too much. He says he's not sure, it might be something about their gods." He shrugged. "He wrote 'I am not a gardener' beside it. With a line under it."

Emeric put his cup down.

He got up and went to the window, because it was the only thing to do with his face that would not need explaining. Below him the inner ward was grey with old snow and wet with the thaw, and Osric was crossing it with a coil of rope, and the sky over the keep's roofs was white and very high. Thick air. Not air. Not soup. The carver had not had a word for it either, or had had one and Vey could not read it, and had drawn it the only way a man draws a thing that has no shape: as more. More of it, packed close, round the seed that woke. And a hand pouring more on from a jar, all at once, and the seed broken.

Density. Below some floor it slept, the way a seed in a dry granary sleeps and will sleep for a hundred years. Somewhere about the density of the Band, where the air was thick with it, it woke. And above that, if a man came with a jar and poured, it broke.

He knew the Band. He had flown through it more times than he could count. He had liquid mana in a niche in the cellar in a bank of glass.

"You've gone a colour," said Zale, behind him.

"It's the drink," said Emeric.

Under the table, unseen, Ferra put her foot on his empty chair, and left it there until he came back and sat down on the edge of it, and then put it on his.



He told her that night, in the counting room, with the door shut and the bolt across and Zale asleep two stairs away. He told her standing up, because he could not sit.

"They're seeds."

Ferra did not say anything. She waited for the rest, as she waited for the rest of every column.

"Every one of them," he said. "Every grey stone in the world. Every Hollow Crystal in every treasury and every lord's cabinet and every curiosity shop where they sell for less than they cost to steal. Corbin's nine. My mother's. Every one we've cut for a band or the Forge or the gate. Never stones. The seeds of a tree. The lattice is the coat. Nobody carved it. The tree grew it." He walked to the window and back. "They don't sprout because there's nowhere in the world thick enough to wake them. Not since whenever it was. The ground's gone thin. So they sit in drawers and people call them lost craft and break them testing them and cut them into beads, the way I did, and a cut seed's a dead seed."

"Then the ones we've cut are dead," said Ferra.

"As seeds. They work. They'll work till I die." He sat down at last on the corner of the table. "As seeds they're dead. Reshaped. I cut them myself."

She looked at him across the lamp. He saw her take it in, all of it, the way she took in a set of accounts from a new holding: everything at once, and then the places where it would not balance.

"Then it's not a stone," she said slowly. "It's stock. You don't have ten stones in your band and a spare in a brooch. You have a crop. Or you would have, if there were a field."

"If there were a field."

"Thick air," said Ferra. "Like the Band."

"Like the Band. About the Band. Not more." He looked at his hands. "That's the drawing he didn't understand. The jar. You don't pour it on. You soak it. Too much, too fast, and it cracks, the way a bean cracks if you boil it before you've soaked it." He breathed out. "And I'd have poured. If I'd found this out on my own, with a seed in my hand and a niche full of liquid in the cellar. I'd have poured, to see it go faster."

"Yes," said Ferra. "You would."

She said it without the least softness, and he loved her for it.

"Which one?" she said. "Not the bands. Not the Forge's. Those are all cut. Which one isn't?"

He had known she would get there before him. He was there already, and had been since the window.

"The one in the brooch," he said.

His father had sent two of his mother's stones south with Ward, the winter Ward asked him for them at court, in a bag in Ward's coat. One had gone into the first bead of his band, cut and shaped. The other had a chip out of its edge, an old break nobody remembered the cause of, and a chipped stone would not take the shaping well, and so it had sat in a drawer for two years, the spare that nobody wanted, until Bram had set it in a silver star for a queen's dinner with the chip turned to the back against the coat so it would not show. Never cut. Never shaped. And chipped.

A farmer on his other world, sowing sweet peas or the hard grey seeds of some flowering vine, took a file to every seed first and scraped through the coat on one side, or nicked it with a knife, because a seed with a coat that hard would lie in the wet ground for a year without waking. The nick let the water in. Scarification, the gardening books called it. A wound on purpose, so the thing would grow.

"It's chipped," he said. "Its coat's broken. If any seed in the world is ready to wake, it's the one nobody wanted."

Ferra looked at him for a long breath across the lamp, with no expression at all.

Then she got up and came round the table and put her arms round him where he sat on the table's corner, and held his head against her, against the front of her dress, with her chin on the top of his head; and he sat there in the lamp with his face against her and did not say anything, and she did not say anything, because there was nothing either of them could have said about his mother that was any better than that.

After a while she took her arms back and sat down again in her chair, and picked up her pencil.

"There are more of hers," she said. "In the capital."

"In the treasury. My father kept them. I never knew how many." He looked at the lamp. "They were hers, so they're the crown's, so they're his."

"Seeds," said Ferra, and wrote it down, and drew a box round it, which was what she did with things that could not be done anything about yet.



Armand was awake. Armand was nearly always awake now, at any hour; he said that a man who had slept for eighty-five years had done enough of it, and Imani said it was the heart.

He was in his chair by his own fire with a rug over his knees and the blue book on the rug, locked, with his hand flat on it, as he sat most nights; and he listened to all of it without interrupting, which was not like him, and when Emeric had finished he sat for some time looking at the fire.

"Sixty years," he said at last. "I've handled them for sixty years. I've had them under a lens. I wrote three pages on the lattice's symmetry when I was twenty-five that my master said were the best thing I'd ever written, and they were." He turned his head. "I never once thought it might be a coat."

"Nobody did."

"A gardener would have." Armand looked back at the fire. "Any farm boy. I was one. I stood in my father's barn every autumn and watched him pick over the seed for spring, and I went to the tower as a boy and forgot every word of it, because what does a mage want with a barn?" He made a dry sound. "Your Vey wrote 'I am not a gardener.' Neither was the tower. That was the trouble with it."

The fire settled. Emeric sat on the stool across from him with his elbows on his knees.

"Did anybody ever try to make a stone that watched?" he said. "And did something about it."

Armand's eyebrows came up.

"Sentry magic," said Emeric. "A thing at a door. Not a watch stone that tells you somebody's there. A stone that sees them and acts."

"That's a jump."

"It's the same question. If the grey stones are seeds, and they decide, then the deciding grew. It wasn't carved into them by a craftsman. Something grew a thing that chooses." He looked at the fire. "I want to know how far choosing goes."

Armand thought about it, which with Armand took the form of a lecture beginning somewhere a long way back.

"Watch stones report," he said. "That's their whole nature. A watch stone feels a body come into its reach and tells another stone, and the other stone rings or lights or warms in a guard's pocket, and the guard gets up and does the acting. The stone never chooses. It can't. It reports the same thing every time, to anybody, friend or thief or cat." He held up one long finger. "You can hang something worse than a bell on the end of a report, of course. Mages have been doing it since there were mages. But whatever you hang there happens to whoever walks in. Nothing a mage can write on the outside of a stone tells one man from another, and the outside is all the tower ever had."

"Somebody must have tried it anyway."

"Somebody did." Armand's mouth went thin at one corner. "It's in the tower's book. A High mage, four masters before mine, very rich, very old, and very frightened of his own apprentices, which is a common condition in High mages and with reason. He stood an old suit of plate in his hall, off a dead knight. He put a gravity stone in each gauntlet, which is what being very rich was for, and a watch stone behind the breastplate, and he told the watch stone that when a body came into its reach it was to tell the gravity stones, and he told the gravity stones that when they were told, they were to push down. Hard. He set it with his own hand every night on his way up to bed. Stop any man who comes in at night, he said. It was the nearest he could get."

"What happened?"

"His apprentice came home late on the first night from a tavern," said Armand. "The arms came down. The boy got his own arm up over his head, which is the only reason he had a head in the morning, and it broke in two places. And then they stayed down on him, on the flags, pushing, because there was still a body in the watch stone's reach and nobody had told it what to do about one it had already stopped. The High mage came down the stair in his nightshirt and put the watch stone out with his bare hand through the gap under the breastplate, and it took him three tries, his hands were shaking so. Then he had to lift the arms off the boy himself. At two in the morning, with the boy screaming." He turned the blue book a quarter turn on the rug. "It couldn't tell an apprentice coming home from a thief coming in. They're both a man at the door at night. The tower has called it a vanity ever since. When an apprentice in my day built something too clever by half, the masters would say, mind it doesn't break your arm. Every one of us knew what it meant."

Emeric sat with his elbows on his knees, looking at the floor between his feet.

It had not been the stones' fault. He knew that, sitting there, as clearly as he had known anything in a year. It had been the instruction. A body in reach, bring the arms down. The stones had done what they were told, exactly, and nothing else, because nothing the High mage could put on them had any way to tell one man from another. A mage could only tell a stone what to do from the outside: one thing, the same every time, to anybody.

A delver could write inside a seed.

He did not say so. Armand watched him not say so, with his old eyes half shut.

"You've the look of a man about to break somebody's arm," said Armand.

"Not yet."

"That's what the High mage said." Armand shifted under the rug. "There's another one. You'll not like it. Every child in the tower's told it, the first winter, by the older ones, in the dark, to frighten them." He looked at the fire. "Of things under the ground that walked about without breathing. Before men. That served somebody, and went on serving after the somebody was gone, and had to be put down. I always took it for a sermon. Don't make what you can't stop." He was quiet. "I still do. Mostly."

"Mostly."

"I'm eighty-five," said Armand, "and I've just been told by a dead man's diary that I spent sixty years cataloguing a bag of seeds." He looked down at Emeric's hand on his knee. "What have you done to your thumb?"

Emeric looked. The heel of his thumb was still pink from the feast, a burn the size of a fingernail where the cart-wheel pan had got him.

"A pan," he said.

"A pan." Armand looked at him over his spectacles. "And the patch on your palm in the summer, after Lantern Night? Ward told me. And the one on your wrist in October that Imani put grease on and said nothing about to you, but said a great deal about to me." He waited. "You've been pulling cold."

"I've been trying to."

"And putting the heat in your own hand."

"It has to go somewhere."

"It does," said Armand. "That's the whole of the rule, and you've had half of it since you were a child, because it's the first thing they tell the fire students and you were in the room." He leaned forward a little, which cost him; Emeric saw it cost him. "There's a name for the other half. It's old; I had it from my first master, who had it from his. The heat must have somewhere to go that isn't you." He sat back. "A cold stone doesn't hold what it takes. It throws it out the back, into its fin. You copied that with your fingers last summer, Ward says, at the ice-cream barrow. Throwing it past your fingertips. Good. But your fingertips are you. You've thrown it as far as your own nails and called it gone."

"How far, then?"

"Into something that can take it. Something big and cold and not yours." Armand looked at the shuttered window, toward the dark where the lake was. "Tomorrow, after the noon bell. Bring a chair for me and a coat for yourself. And tell that knight of yours he can come and stand behind me and disapprove. He'll want to."



The Mere had frozen at the edges in the cold after the feast and gone soft in the thaw, and by the ninth it lay grey and open in the middle and rimmed with a thin skin of ice along the shallows, a few yards wide, that would not have held a duck.

Armand sat in a carrying chair on the shingle at the water's edge with two rugs and a hot brick under his feet and Ward behind him, as he had said, disapproving. It had taken four of the garrison to get him down. Imani had given him an hour and said she would be counting.

"Squat," said Armand. "Hand on the ice. Not in the water. On the ice."

Emeric squatted on the shingle and put his palm flat on the shore ice. It was so thin that he could see the stones of the bottom through it, brown and green, and the water moving under it.

"Now pull," said Armand. "The way you did at the barrow. But don't throw it to your fingers. Throw it out there." He lifted a hand out of the rugs and pointed past Emeric, past the ice's edge, at the open grey water of the middle of the lake. "Out there is a hundred thousand barrels of water that doesn't care what you put in it. Give it your heat. Not your hand."

It was harder than it sounded. Everything was.

The pull came easily. He had that much from the summer: the heat coming up out of the ice into his palm, the ice going hard and white under his hand. But it came into him; and when he tried to send it on, out past the edge, he could not find the edge, because there was nothing there to send it to that he could feel, only grey water a long way off, and the heat stayed in his palm and grew, and he took his hand off the ice with a hiss, and shook it, and the patch on his palm was pink again.

"Again," said Armand.

He did it again. And again. On the fifth try he found that it helped to put his other hand in the water itself, past the ice's edge, flat, a foot out, as if he were holding hands with the lake: so that the cold came up into one hand and the heat went out of the other, through him, as a thing goes through a pipe, and he was only the pipe. It still went through him. It was still warm going through his chest, and it ached in both forearms like a long day at a pump. But it went out. He felt it go: into the water at his left hand, and away, into all that grey.

The ice under his right hand went from a skin to a crust. From a crust to a plate. He took his hand off it at the end of the hour, when Imani's count was up and Ward had said so twice, and there was a slab of white ice on the shore of the Mere the width of his hand and an arm's length long and as thick as a ledger, standing up out of the grey skin round it like a step.

A hand's breadth. An afternoon.

His forearms ached to the elbow, and they would ache till the morning, and he was hungry as if he had not eaten in a day.

Armand leaned forward in the chair and looked at the slab of ice for some time.

"Badly," he said at last. "Good."

"Badly good?"

"Badly, because a first-year ice student would have done that in a quarter of an hour and gone for his dinner. Good, because you didn't burn yourself." He sat back in the rugs. "Next time, no hand in the water. You'll find the water without it, once you know where it is. Your sense knows where everything is. You simply don't ask it."

Ward, behind the chair, said nothing at all, and that was approval.



The last pages of the book were the shortest, and Zale read them on the morning of the thirteenth, the day the river ice went out.

They had read it through by then. Everything between the first leaf and the last: Vey's wall, his weather, his goat, his fever, his long cheerful arguments with himself about the carving, the four drawings and the jar. The dot marks went to twenty-eight; Ferra had them all by the Saturday, as she had said she would, and found three more lists in the carving by counting, and could say how many rows were on Vey's wall and how many pods on the tree in the third drawing, and nothing else, because nothing else in the carving would open.

The last pages were not about the wall at all.

"He's found it," said Zale. He had the book on his knees by the long room's fire with Ferra's list beside him. "Where the carving came from. He's been to every port he can think of, asking if anybody's seen marks like these, and he's been laughed at in most of them, and then somebody in a port on a hot coast, a man who sells dates, says yes, there's an island." He read on under his breath. "A desert in the sea between two crowns. Black water in the middle of it that doesn't move. And stones coming up out of the sand all over it like teeth, and glittering, and soldiers at both ends of it who've been killing each other for it for as long as the date man's grandfather could remember." He stopped. "That's the Isle of Shards."

Ferra's pencil, on the far side of the table, stopped moving.

It stopped for no longer than it took her to reach the end of a figure, and started again; and Emeric, by the fire with his hands round his cup, watched the flames and did not move at all.

"Everybody knows it," said Zale, to the fire, not looking at either of them. "Every sailor on the western sea gives it a wide berth. Your father's one of the two crowns." He went back to the page. "Vey went. He says he found where it came from, at last, under the cliffs on the west side. A way down in the rock at low water. And the same carving down there, going on, and on, a whole hall of it, and things he can't describe yet." He turned the leaf. "And he's sending this home first. The fair copy. From a port on the coast across from the island, by a ship going north, so that if he doesn't come back up out of the rock, somebody will have it." He looked up. "So it wasn't sent before he went to look. His family had it wrong. He'd already found it."

"He went down," said Ferra.

"He went down." Zale turned the last leaf, which was blank except for a few lines in the brown hand, larger than the rest, as if written standing up. He read them twice under his breath before he read them out. "The rest is under the island. I will copy it there, and bring it up, and make another fair book of it, and send that home too. Don't wait for me with the soup." He closed the book. "That's the end."

The fire settled in the grate. Out in the ward somebody was shouting to somebody about the river.

"Thirty years his family kept that," said Zale, with his hand flat on the cover. "Waiting with the soup."

Nobody said anything to that.

"There's one more thing," said Zale, after a while. "Back near the start of the drawings, in the margin, there's a line I couldn't get on the eighth. I've got it now." He opened the book again at the page with the tree, the third drawing, the grey trunk and the wide branches, and found the little sloped line at its foot. "He says the carver's written something by the tree that he can't read, but he thinks, from where it sits, it must say where the tree grows. And he says, if I were to guess, I would guess it grows wild where the air is thick." He shut the book. "Which is the thick air again. He's mad about thick air, your Vey."

Emeric put his cup down on the hearth.

He got up and went to the long room's west window, which looked out over the roofs of the old town to the river and the hills beyond it, and up. The sky over the hills was white and empty and very high.

Where the air is thick. He had been up there. He knew what lay up there, four miles over his head: a layer of the world so dense that a ship's stones filled again as she spent them, and men bled at the nose if they stayed too long. If the tree grew wild anywhere, it grew where the world was thick with mana, and the only place in the world he knew of that was thick with mana was up. So it had grown up, once. On high ground, maybe, high mountains, when the ground was thicker than it was now and the Band hung lower; or else the dragons in the children's stories had carried its seeds up into the high places and dropped them there, as birds plant hawthorns in the hedges.

He did not have a mountain three miles high. He did not have a dragon.

He had glass. Ivo's glass, which held mana and let the light through. He had a vial rig and a niche full of liquid in the cellar, and the Band within reach by ship, and Bram, who could build anything that held pressure if he was told what the pressure was. A room. A glass room, closed, filled not to the pouring point but to the soaking point, and held there, at the Band's number, the way Imani held a fever at a dose; with a chipped grey seed in a pot of earth in the middle of it.

Behind him, at the table, he heard Ferra close her book, and stand, and come round the end of the table, and stop at his shoulder; and say nothing at all for some time, with him, watching the white sky.

"How big?" she said at last.

"Big enough for a tree."

"It'll be glass," said Ferra. "All of it. Ivo will want paying."

"Ivo always wants paying."

"And Imani will want her beds in it."

"Not the seedlings," said Emeric. "Nothing that anybody's going to eat. Anything that grows in that air is mana food." He looked at the hills. "The cloves stay in the glasshouse."

"The cloves stay in the glasshouse," said Ferra, and went back to the table, and opened her book to a clean page, and wrote at the top of it, in her square capitals, a heading he could read from the window without turning his head.

THE GLASS ROOM.

Under it she drew a box. Not round a thing that could not be done. Round a space, empty, the size of a page, for whatever was going to go in it.
