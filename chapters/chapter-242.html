Chapter 242 - Honest Paper

Ward brought it in at the end of a short grey afternoon, with sleet on the windows of the table room and the lamps already lit, and laid it on the table in front of Emeric without a word.

It was a single sheet folded twice and sealed with plain black wax, and the direction on the outside was in a small dry hand that leaned backward, like a man leaning away from what he was writing. Emeric knew the hand. He had had a good many letters in it over the years, all short. He had never had one by this road.

"It came in the bag," Ward said. "With nothing else."

That was what made Emeric look up. The bag was Ward's. It had been Ward's since before Emeric was born: one carrier, one bag, a cipher that two men knew, going round the whole barony like a river round a stone. In all the years Emeric had known of it, it had carried the king's letters to Ward and Ward's to the king and nothing else, and once, two winters ago, a letter Emeric had asked Ward to write, which had come back with no answer at all. It had never carried a letter from a third hand. The only man at the far end of it who could have put one in was the man who read the king his cipher.

He broke the seal.

He read it twice, which was Keller's habit and had become his own, and then he laid it flat on the table and put his hand on it, the way he had put his hand on Keller's last letter in the autumn, and did not say anything.

Ferra waited. Ferra always waited. Saban, at the end of the table with the quarter's wool accounts, put his pen down. Bram, who had come up from the works to argue about injectors and had been arguing about them for an hour, stopped. Imani was by the window with her back to the sleet. Nobody asked.

"Read it out," Emeric said to Ferra, and pushed it down the table to her, because he found he did not want to hear it in his own voice.

She read it out. She read it the way she read everything, as a figure, without putting anything into it, and it took less than a minute. The burials, the rolls, the tower's register, the Circle's postings, Master Armand's letter, the soldiers' prayer, the chapel licence, the school list. Heresy. His grace took the whole of it on the Crown. The oath. Two parts in three. The first sitting after the new year. Until then his grace has placed himself in the keeping of the lords spiritual, in the palace, of his own motion, so that nobody should be sent for. Nobody has been.

She put it down.

Bram said, "The king's in custody."

"Of his own motion," said Ferra.

"For us," said Imani, from the window. It was not a question.

Nobody answered her. The sleet ran down the glass.

Emeric sat with it.

He did not need Keller to tell him how it had been said. He could hear it. The loud voice, the voice for the door, dry and flat and carrying to the back of the hall, everything done at Kaldo was done under my leave, and every word of it going out under every door in the building to be in the kitchens by supper. That was what the loud voice was for. It was the voice his father said things in so that the house would hear them and carry them away and never think to look under them.

And it was true.

That was the thing he sat with. In all his life he had never once heard his father say a true thing in the loud voice. The loud voice was the mask; the other thing, under the breath, was the man. He had built half his life on the difference. And here was the loud voice, in the one room in the kingdom where it mattered most, saying a thing that was true all the way down.

Everything done at Kaldo under his leave. Emeric had had Ward write to him once, by that road, with a foreign power digging on the Crown's own claim across the water, and asked. There had been no answer. He had read the no answer as leave, because he had needed to, and gone and done it: the island, the well, the ship on the flats at night. He had never known whether he had read it right.

He knew now. His father had read the letter, or had it read to him, and said nothing, and meant by nothing exactly what Emeric had taken it to mean. And now he had stood up in the council and said it aloud, in the voice that everybody heard and nobody believed, so that it would be on the record in his own words that it had been his.

What the Crown has looked at, and what it has not troubled itself to look at. Keller had not written that line. Keller could not have known to. But Emeric could hear it anyway, as if he had been in the hall, because it was the only way his father could have said it.

He stood up.

"I'm going north," he said.

Ward was by the door, where he always was. He did not move from it. He did not need to. He had been by the door of every room Emeric had tried to leave in a hurry for twenty years, and he had never once put a hand on the latch to stop him, and he did not now.

"You're not sent for," Ward said.

"He's in the Church's keeping."

"In his own house. With his own household. At his own word." Ward's voice was the voice he used for reporting a road, flat, in order. "You're not sent for. He said so himself, in front of the whole council. Nobody will be sent for. That's the whole of what he did." He looked at Emeric across the room. "And he said it to you before that, in the day room, loud. You will not come up this road again unless I send for you. He hasn't sent."

"He can't send. That's the point."

"Then you're not sent for."

It was Ward's answer to everything that mattered, and Emeric had never once found a way round it, and he stood in the middle of the table room with the letter on the table and his hands at his sides and did not find one now.

"Sit down," Ferra said.

He did not sit down. She did not look up from the letter. She had turned it round on the table so that it faced her square, and she had her pencil out, not writing, only holding it, the way she held it when she was adding in her head.

"He's put himself in a room," she said, "so that you don't have to be put in one. That only works while you're here. The day you're seen north of Wayford without a writ, the room's for nothing. You've come up the road your father forbade you in front of the whole house, uninvited, the week after the lords spiritual asked for you and he said no. That's not a son coming to help his father." She laid the pencil down beside the letter, exactly parallel to it. "That's the lord of Kaldo answering the summons the king refused. And it's the brother at the gate, gently, without the brother having had to ride a mile. It's Corbin's proof, and you'd be carrying it to him yourself."

"And the king," Saban said, "would be a man who took the Church's custody to shield a son who then came anyway. Which is a different thing from a king who took it on himself." He said it quietly, entering it. "The first is a fool. The second's a king. At the moment he's the second."

Emeric looked at the two of them, and then at Ward by the door, and then at Bram, who had not said anything and was not going to, and at Imani at the window, who was looking at him with the look she gave a patient who was about to do something stupid and would have to be let.

He sat down.

"Zale," he said, after a while, because it was the first thing that came into his head that was not the road north, and because it had to be said by someone. "Somebody will have to write to Zale."

Zale was at sea. He had gone back to it three weeks after the cave, on the first southerly, with Ferra's buying list folded inside his coat next to the scholar's name, saying he would be home by the spring and that he had never in his life been home by the spring. There was no road to where he was. There never had been.

"I'll write," Ferra said. "It'll wait for him at Emberfell." She made a note. It was the first thing she had written since the letter came.

"Get them out," Emeric said. "All of it. Everything he lists. Ours."

Ferra did not ask what he meant. She got up and went through to the counting room, and came back with her keys, and went out again, and came back with her arms full; and then Saban went with her, and then Bram, and in a quarter of an hour the table was covered.

Kaldo kept copies of everything. That had been Ferra's rule from the first winter, when there had been nothing to copy but the debts, and it had been Emeric's before it was hers, from another life, where a thing that was not written down had not happened. Every letter out had its copy in the letter book. Every return to the chancery had its fair copy on Ferra's shelf, in the order it went. The school printed three hundred of everything and kept thirty. Nothing had ever been thrown away.

So they laid it out, in the order Keller had listed it, on the table under the lamps, and it was the case against the king.

The rolls. Every quarter's return of the company since the settlement, in Ferra's clerks' fair copies: each man by name, each by his parish, each by his trade, as the settlement required. They had never once been late. Ferra had seen to that because the settlement said so, and because a late return was a thing a clerk in the capital would remember, and a return on time was a thing nobody remembered at all.

The copy of the Crown's line after Wayford, the one line in the king's own hand under Emeric's record: The barony's justice was within its rights. Emeric had written that record over four nights, carefully, leaving nothing out that could be put in. It lay on the table now as the Crown's warrant for everything that followed it, which was what it had been meant to be.

The licence form. Ferra's copy of it, in Emeric's own hand, from the bench in the chancery's outer room. Under the substance to be made he had written powder. Under the quantity he had written the figure Bram gave him for a year of the beds, not a pound under. Under the place of making he had written the works, at Kaldo, because that was where it was. He remembered writing it. He remembered thinking, with the form on his knee and the Circle's master complaining in the doorway, that a lie on a chancery form was a thing that could be found, and that if it were found it would be the only thing anybody read.

He had been right about that. He had not thought about what they would read instead.

The tin case, out of Ferra's box, where it had lain since the week Tessaly came home with it: Rafe's licence from the tower, with the tower's mark on it and the stone written in, the tenth. Tessaly had carried the boy up the road to be registered because a boy who could lift a load of earth like that belonged on the tower's books, and she was a tower woman to the bone. She had written the letter to the tower herself, honestly, as Armand's former pupil and mistress of the wall at Kaldo. It had not occurred to her, or to anyone, that the tower kept a column for the place where a mage was raised.

The chapel licence, the close's own grant, four lines under the cathedral's seal. Doran had not told anyone he had asked for it until it came.

The school list. One of the thirty the school kept, printed on Kaldo paper in Nevin's type. Aubrey, second son of the lord of Esterby, north riding. Eighth.

And the soldiers' prayer, a spare sheet from the finishing-room's file, with the small letter in it that a frightened boy had taken from the wrong box, and that had gone out to two hundred men, and come back to Kaldo, years later, in a cardinal's hand.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Emeric walked the length of the table and back, slowly, reading it, the way he walked a line he had drawn to make sure it ended somewhere. He knew every sheet. He had caused most of them to be written. He had taught the barony to write them: that a thing proved itself on paper or it was not proved; that a book kept honestly was a wall no enemy could climb; that the way to be safe from a court was to have nothing in your books that you would not read aloud in one. He had built Kaldo on it. He had beaten Wexler with it, and Sethe, and the five, and the duke, every one of them with honest paper laid on a table in the right order.

Every page on this table was true.

And every page was a line in a sum, and the sum came out the same way whichever end you began to add it from. A parish where the old did not die. A boy with a knight's stone and clean arms. Mages walking south. A master of the Circle asking the Church to change the rite. A barony printing its own prayers. A licence for powder, honestly filled, in the lord's own hand. A chapel for a city that had not needed one five years ago. A lord's son sent south to drink.

Keller had said to him once, in the room with the window at the wrong height, that he had never convicted a man on a pattern, because a pattern was a thing a clever man made out of the world by choosing what to count. Emeric had admired that. He had gone away and learned to bring Keller evidence instead of patterns, and had won by it.

The cardinal had not needed to choose what to count. Kaldo had counted it for him, all of it, every quarter, in fair copy, on time.

"It's all ours," Bram said. He said it slowly, the way he said a thing he had checked. "Every sheet."

"Yes."

"There's nothing on here that isn't so."

"No."

Bram looked at it a while longer. "Then what did we do wrong?"

Nobody answered him either.

Imani had come away from the window. She stood at the foot of the table looking down at the school list, at the eighth line, and Emeric knew what she was thinking because she had said it at this table months ago, before the list was printed, quite quietly, while Orrin looked from face to face and did not understand. He'll drink the water. She did not say it now. She did not need to. She looked at the line for a while, and then at him, and then she went back to the window, and that was worse than if she had said it.

Doran came up from the chapel at dusk, because Emeric sent for him, on his stick, with the parish book under his arm in its calf binding, as he would have brought it to any visitor who asked, and the cold on his coat.

He saw the table as he came in. He stopped in the door and looked at it for some time, the whole length of it, under the lamps. Then he came and sat down in the chair Ward set for him, and set the book on the table among the others, square to the edge, and put his hand flat on it.

Emeric told him. He told him the way Keller had told it, short, in order. He did not leave anything out. Doran listened with his hand on the book and his eyes on the lamp, and when Emeric came to the burials he nodded slightly, as a man nods at a thing he has been expecting to hear for a long time without knowing it, and when Emeric came to the chapel licence he closed his eyes.

When it was finished nobody spoke.

"Father Benedek," Doran said at last. "He read the burials. The tenth night. I gave him the book myself." He opened his eyes. "He sat where I'm sitting. I was proud of it. I've kept it since the year you came, the same way, every soul, the name and the date and the age, and a word for the cause if it was known. It's the best-kept book on the circuit. I'd have told you so myself." He looked down at the calf under his hand. "He never told me he'd added it up."

"No."

"I never added it up." Doran said it with a kind of wonder. "Forty-seven, the last year he saw. In a parish of four and a half thousand. I wrote every one of them and I never once added them up. I thought God was being kind to Kaldo, if I thought anything. I thought it was the water, the first time, the wells, the midden. I wrote that in the margin. The second time I didn't write anything, because I didn't know what to write." He was quiet a moment. "And the chapel. I asked for the chapel because the people had gone to the new city and it was four miles and I'm old. I cited the hill chapel I served at twenty-four. I wrote it out in three drafts so as not to explain anything, because a man who explains to a chancery has admitted there's something to explain."

He took his hand off the book.

"My lord," he said. "What did I do wrong? By keeping it well?"

Emeric looked at him across the table.

He had an answer. He had several. That Doran had done nothing wrong. That the book was true and the truth was not Doran's fault. That the fault, if there was one, was in what the book described and not in the describing, and that what it described was a thing Emeric had done, on purpose, knowing, to every soul in the parish, without asking any of them, because he had thought it was good and had been afraid of what would happen if anyone knew. That Doran had kept a true record of a thing he had not been told, and been used for it. All of that was true. None of it was a thing he could say to a priest, at this table, tonight, with the parish book lying among the others and the priest's hand just lifted off it.

"Nothing, Father," he said. It was the only true word he could give him, and it answered nothing, and they both knew it.

Doran looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded, slowly, as a man accepts a thing he has been given because it is all there is, and sat back in his chair, and left the book where it lay.

Armand came last, across the bridge from the keep in the dark, with a lamp and a boy to carry it and his stick in the other hand. He did not need to be told what was on the table. He had been told by the boy, who had been told by the gate, which was how the keep learned everything. He came in and stood at the end of the table in his coat with the melted sleet on his shoulders and looked at it, and his eyes went straight to the place in the row where his own letter should have been, and was not, because Kaldo had no copy of it.

He had written it alone, years ago, and sent it alone, and told the table afterwards. The only copy in the world was the one on the cardinal's desk.

"It was mine," he said.

"Sit down, master."

"The letter. It was mine. I wrote it. I asked him to change the rite." His hands were shaking, which they did in the evenings now, and he put them on the back of the chair Ward had brought him to still them, and did not sit. "Three nights instead of one. The same total. Because I had seen what the rite did to a boy, and I had seen what the barony's water did to a child, and I could not say the second, so I asked for the first. I thought it was the most careful letter I had ever written. There was nothing in it that I could not have defended before the whole Circle." He looked at the empty place in the row. "He has read it, Keller says, many times."

"It's one sheet among nine."

"It's the only one written by a master of the Circle to the Church about the rite." Armand's voice did not rise. It never did. "The rest is burials and rolls and a boy's error. Mine is the one that says someone in Kaldo knew there was a better way and asked the Church for it. It's the one that makes the rest a practice and not an accident." He took a breath. "I will go north."

"No."

"I am a master of the Circle. I am sworn to the tower and I stood in the chamber for forty years. If they want a man to answer for what was done at Kaldo, let them have a man who can answer in their own language, and not the king. They can try me. It was my letter. It should be my trial." The shaking had gone up into his arms. "I am old, Emeric. It will not cost them much, and it will cost Kaldo less."

"No," Emeric said.

He said it quietly, and he did not give a reason, because every reason he had was a reason that Armand could have argued with and would have: that Armand's going would prove the letter mattered, that a master of the Circle walking into the close to confess would be the tenth sheet on the table and the heaviest, that the king had gone into a room so that nobody from Kaldo would have to, and that Armand was somebody from Kaldo. That he could not spare him. That he would not.

He waited for the argument.

It did not come.

Armand looked at him for a long moment down the length of the table, over the rolls and the licence and the list. Then he took his hands off the back of the chair and sat down in it, slowly, like a man who has been told a thing by a doctor and has decided not to ask the question after it. He folded his hands on the table in front of him, where they shook. He did not say anything else at all.

Emeric had been refused by Armand a hundred times. He had been lectured by him, corrected by him, told he was a fool in the old tongue and the new, and once, memorably, struck across the knuckles with a pointer. He had never once been obeyed by him without an argument. It was a great deal worse than any of the others.

They went at last, one by one, because there was nothing to be done that night and every one of them knew it. Doran first, on Ward's arm to the gate, with the parish book under his coat. Bram, back to the works, where he would not sleep. Saban, to the counting room next door, where he would not sleep either. Imani, without a word. Armand, with the boy and the lamp, toward the bridge.

Ferra stayed until the last, putting the copies back into their order to go back on their shelves. At the door she stopped with her arms full.

"Everything we can't say, and everything that's true," she said. "I told you they were the same box." She looked at the table, emptied now but for Keller's letter and the lamp. "I didn't say whose box."

She went out.

Emeric sat on alone in the table room with the letter.

It was very late. The sleet had stopped and the sky had cleared behind it and gone hard and black, and when he got up and went to the window he could see across the court to the drum of the palace and the glass on top of it, with a little starlight in it, and under the glass, too small to see from here, a tree a forearm high.

He had come south with a notebook and a box of things he had made, and a rule from another life: write it down. Anything you cannot write down, you do not understand. Anything you do not write down, you will lose. He had lived by it every day since. He had made a barony live by it. It had never once failed him. It had beaten every enemy he had, because every enemy he had kept his books badly, or burned them, or lied in them, and Kaldo did none of those things.

And tonight, for the first time, it had turned in his hand. Not because anyone had lied. Because nobody had.

He stood at the window until the lamp behind him began to gutter. Then he went back to the table and trimmed it, and sat down, and took a clean sheet from the pile, and squared it, and picked up a pen.

He did not write anything. He sat with the pen in his hand for a long time and looked at the sheet, and found that he did not know, for the first time since he had come south, what it would be safe to put on it.
