Chapter 322 - The Convoy

On the last night home, Imani brought a book to bed.

It was not one of Ferra's. This was a ward book: a long narrow thing in brown card, split down the back from a month in an apron pocket, with a ward's smell of carbolic. Imani sat cross-legged in the middle of the bed in her nightdress with it open on her knees, a pencil behind her ear and two of Ottoline's cards on the blanket beside her, and did not say anything for so long that Emeric, at the washstand, stopped with his toothbrush in his mouth to look at her.

His kit was by the door, and Imani's. Ferra's was not, because Ferra was staying; she sat at her desk under the little ledger lamp with the war chest's last column open in front of her and her shoes off, adding.

"You've been at that since supper," said Ferra, without looking up.

"Since the scan," said Imani.

Ferra's pen stopped.

"Every morning," said Imani. "Before the ward. You know I have. My hand on your breastbone and then on mine, a slow count of fifty each. It's worse than the plates and it's quicker, and it's every day, and the plates aren't." She turned a page. "And I've had your book."

"You've had my..."

"His days. Where he was. Where he slept. You keep it for the war. I copied it." Imani held the ward book up, a page of small figures in her capitals, ruled into columns. "There's the number, every day. Yours and mine. It goes up. Not much. A hair a week, and some weeks not at all. And beside it, every day, what he was doing."

Emeric took the toothbrush out of his mouth.

"The days he was in the next room don't do anything," said Imani. "Or nearly nothing. A day at the long table, a day on the hill with the rods, a day at the yard with both of us in and out of it: nothing I can read by hand. The capital." She put her finger on a line. "Twenty-first and twenty-second. I was with him two days and a night, in a cart and a cathedral and a hold. Mine went up. Yours didn't move. You were here." The finger went down. "The nineteenth, when the Well went up and he was on the K Discovery all night. Flat, both. The nights he was in this bed and we all went to sleep like decent people: up. A little. Every time." She stopped. "And then there's this column."

"What's that column?" said Ferra.

Imani looked at her.

Ferra looked at the column, at the little marks going down it beside some of the dates and not others; and the colour came up out of her collar from the throat, slowly, all the way to her hair.

"You've been keeping a column," said Ferra.

"I'm a doctor."

"You have been keeping a column of..."

"Dates," said Imani. "Only dates. I'm not an animal." She tapped it. "And those are the big ones. Every one. Both of us. The next morning, every time, more than any other day in the book, by twice and more. It's the nights, Ferra. Not the days. Not the sleeping, even, much. The nights."

The ledger lamp hissed very faintly under its glass. Emeric stood at the washstand with water on his chin and said nothing. He had understood about three lines before the column, and had been holding very still since.

Ferra put her pen down on the blotter, exactly square to the edge of the book.

"So I'm sleeping with a battery," she said.

Imani laughed. It came out of her all at once, the way it did when she had been holding it, and she put the ward book face down on her knees and laughed into her hands, and Ferra kept her face perfectly straight for as long as she could, which was not long.

"I'm not a battery," said Emeric.

"You charge a stone by holding it," said Ferra. "You fill a dead one in your hand while you're talking about something else." She turned her chair round to face him. "And when you lie down, you're holding us."

"That's not..." He stopped, because it was.

"It's the best kind of battery," said Imani, from behind her hands. "It charges itself. It doesn't need the Band. Nobody has to fly it to four miles twice a week." She took her hands away. Her eyes were wet. "I'm going to write it up. I'm going to prescribe it. Bed rest, with him. Twice daily."

"That's not a prescription," said Ferra. "That's a schedule."

"Everything's a schedule to you."

"Everything is a schedule." Ferra had turned back to the desk and pulled her own book toward her, and was writing in it with her lips pressed together hard; and Emeric came and looked over her shoulder. 29 Nov. I. says it is the nights. Has kept a column. Prescribes bed rest, with him. F. She underlined column.

"That's going in the book," he said.

"Everything goes in the book."



He was delighted. That was the trouble. He sat down on the end of the bed with the toothbrush still in his hand and the ward book across his knees and went down the columns with his finger, and could feel his face doing it, and did not try very hard to stop.

It was so simple. A body that held a great deal more than it needed was not a closed jar. It was a stove in a room. It did not push anything out; it was simply fuller than everything round it, and what was in it went slowly into whatever was close and empty, the way a stove's heat went into the hearth stones and the cat asleep on the fender. On another world he had known it in a different shape: a coil beside a coil, and the current in one woken in the other with nothing touching. Slow induction. Only the nearness; and the nearer and the longer, the more.

And a commoner's body, put in the warm long enough, did what bodies did with anything they were given steadily over years. It grew to hold it.

"It's not a cast," he said. "Nobody's doing it. I'm not doing it. It just goes. The way it goes into a stone, only slower, because you're not a stone, you're people, and people take what they can use and stop." He looked up. "That's why it's small. That's why there's no harm in it. Your bodies are making room for it as it comes. You can't take more than you've grown." He went down the column again. "And the nights are the longest you're ever that close to me. All of me. Hours." He heard himself, and did not stop. "And I'm not... I'm not careful, then. I'm not holding anything in."

"We'd noticed," said Ferra, to her book.

"A mage's wife stays young," said Ferra, after a moment, to the desk. "Maren said it yesterday, over the bread. She said it was money, and vanity." She wrote something small. "I said nothing."

"It's not money," said Emeric. "It's this. Every mage who ever married a farmer's daughter." He laughed. "Four hundred years of people saying it in kitchens, and nobody in a tower ever wrote it down, because a tower doesn't read wives."

He was still smiling when it caught up with him.

He felt it come. It came quietly, from somewhere behind the delight, the way a cold comes in under a door; and he sat on the end of the bed with the ward book on his knees and looked at the dates in the first column, and counted back. The pod stone was in the spring of the year eleven. Before that, faintly, for longer: since the year he had first come back from four miles with the Band in him and lain down beside them full of it. Years. Every night of them. In their sleep.

And not once had he asked.

Six days ago Imani had written ROOM on a board in front of ninety students, and told them that before you did anything to a body you told it what you were going to do and what it would cost, and that it could say no. He had been proud of it all week.

He shut the ward book.

"I never asked you," he said.

Nobody said anything for a breath. The lamp hissed.

"No," said Imani. She had stopped laughing. She looked at him with her head on one side, the way she looked at a patient who had just told her something that was not the thing that was wrong with him. "You didn't know."

"I should have known. I know what I do to a stone if I sit with it. I sat with you." He put the book down. "Years. I changed you both in your sleep, and you had to find it with a column."

"You didn't do anything," said Imani. "That's the difference, and I teach it, so listen. I tell my class you say what you're going to do to a body. You weren't going to do anything. You were asleep." She considered. "Mostly."

"Imani."

"And it's not harm. I've read us every day for a month. Nothing's wrong. Nothing's strained. Everything I can read's better than it was." She held up one finger. "I don't get colds. She doesn't get colds. We wake up like girls and you wake up like a man who's carried the Forge's anvil up the keep stair, and I thought that was the other thing, and it's half this." She held up a second finger. "And I can feel heat on a leg from a yard off, and I mended a dock man's hand last week, and I couldn't have done either of them a year ago. You didn't take anything off me. You put it in."

"Without asking."

"If you'd asked," said Ferra, from the desk, "I'd have said yes." She turned round in her chair. "And then I'd have wanted it in writing, with a clause for if it went wrong, and you'd have been unbearable about it for a month, and I'd have been unbearable about the clause." She looked at him steadily. "It isn't a debt. Stop looking at it like one. I can see you putting it in a column."

He opened his mouth.

"And it's prescribed now," said Imani, "so you can't argue with it anyway." She reached over and took the toothbrush out of his hand, which he had forgotten was there, and put it on the bedside table, and took hold of his shirt. "Doctor's orders."

"Work first," said Ferra.

"Your work's done. You've been adding the same column for an hour. I watched you."

"It came out different twice."

"Then it'll come out different in the morning." Imani pulled, and he came, because he generally did; and she put him on his back on the bolster with her hand flat on his chest in the place she always put it, and looked down at him from very close, with her hair coming down, and said, "There. Stop it. You look like a man who's broken something in a shop," and kissed him, slowly, until he stopped it.



It was later, and the lamp was down, that she said the other thing.

She said it into the dark, quietly, on his shoulder, in a different voice from the one that had prescribed him; and Ferra, who had been nearly asleep against his other side, opened her eyes.

"The king," said Imani.

He waited.

"At the long table. The night Armand read us." She did not move her head. "Your father looked at me. Across the room, from his bench. Like he'd seen it somewhere before and couldn't think where. Then he looked at the window." Her hand moved a little on his chest, and was still. "And in July. At Ferra's table, after the road. He said a sadness came over him near Augustine. On the stair, when the man had his arm. Like a draught from a door. And he thought he'd caught it off the man who said her office." She was quiet. "I had my hand on his shoulder when he said it. I've thought about it since."

"What have you thought?" said Ferra.

"That if a few years in this bed leaves him in us," said Imani, "then twenty-odd years beside her would leave her in the king. She was the old king's fosterling. They grew up in the same house. In and out of every room he was in, all his life." She breathed out. "And she's in that stone. In that man's right hand. Every day the man stood at his elbow." She stopped. "I think he felt her. That's all. I think that's what it was. Her, in the stone, and the bit of her that was in him knowing it, and him not."

Nobody said anything.

"It's a guess," said Imani. "I've no column for it."

After a while Ferra reached across him in the dark to the desk and found her book by feel, and the pencil in it; and she wrote, without a light, under what she had written before, smaller than she wrote anything, in a line that would run uphill in the morning. He heard the pencil go, and stop, and the book close.

Then she put it back and lay down again and took his hand and held it, loosely, and did not say what she had written; and Imani's breath went long against his shoulder and slowed, and stayed slow; and he lay between the two of them with his own warmth going out of him into them, steadily, as it had every night for years, and as he could feel it doing now that he knew to feel for it.

He could have pulled it in. He knew how. He did not. They had said no to that too, in their way. He lay and listened to them sleep, and thought about his father on a stair with a hand on his arm, and did not sleep for a long time; and in the morning they were both up before him and bright as birds, and Imani looked at him across the breakfast tray and said, "You look terrible," with enormous satisfaction, and Ferra wrote it down.



The fleet went aboard on the thirtieth, at the yard, in the last of the afternoon, and Ferra came down to the quay to see it go and did not cry, and did not expect anybody else to.

The yard had been full since noon: crews going up gangways with their bags, the last crates of Fennick's shells going down into the launches on the crane in slings of four, Bram on the slip in a temper because something on hull four's stern had not been signed for. Escorts three and four lay at the quay with their gangways up, where the Isle detachment had slept aboard. The K Amara lay on the lake beyond the yard's cut, black and enormous, with the knights going out to her in the launches; and along the quay's edge the people who were staying stood in a long uneven line and watched the people who were not.

Ferra stood at the end of it by the crane, in her grey coat, with her book under her arm. She had been up since four with the manifests, and she had a line in her book for every soul who had gone up a gangway since noon, and she had not missed one.

He came to her last, because he had left it last, and she had known he would.

"The K Discovery's lading," she said, and gave him the sheet. "Sign it."

He signed it on her book, on the crane's foot. She took it back and looked at the signature and put it in the book, and then she looked up at him; and in front of the whole of the quay she put her hand flat on his chest and stood up on her toes and kissed him, once, briefly, exactly, the way she would have handed a receipt across a counter to a man she trusted to keep it.

"I'll count you back in," she said.

He stood there. He had his hand half out, because he had thought she would give him something: a slip, a sheet, a number, as she had on Hollow Night, as she had every time he had gone anywhere for three years. She had always given him something to carry.

She looked at his hand.

"You've got the leaflet," said Ferra. "In your coat. That's enough paper." She tucked the book back under her arm. "Go on. You're the last name on the sheet and I'd like to close it."

He went. At the foot of the K Discovery's gangway he looked back, and she was writing.



The lift hull went up first, at dusk, from her mooring on the castle side, where the whole town could see her.

She was the court's ship, the widow's pleasure hull Saban had bought in the year nine, refitted behind iron so that she looked, to anybody under her, like a hull that flew because an old mage sat in a chair and poured. She went up slowly, with her lamps lit. And from her stern, on the staff over her little gilded house, the king's standard went out on the evening wind, for every watcher on every road out of Kaldo to see and count and write down and send north: the king was home, his court in the air over his son's town, where it had hung most evenings since the summer. Anybody who wanted the king of Randillia could look up.

Old Edrin was at her casting seat in the glass, where he had sat for four years in plain view of every mooring, with his hands on the rail and his eyes shut, pouring nothing at all into stones that did not need it. He was seventy-five. He had told Ward in October that this would be his last flight, and Ward had believed him. He had crewed his first hull in the capital at twenty, and he would fly his last one over Kaldo with the king's standard on it, so that the king could be somewhere else.

The king came down the yard steps a little after that, in the dark, in a plain brown coat.

It was the coat he had worn to Commencement. He had no star on it and no chain, and nobody walking with him but Hale with his bag; and the people on the quay who knew him stood up straight and did not know what else to do. Waylon was at the K Discovery's gangway. He had come down from the South Gate to see his father off, and he stood at the foot of the gangway in his own coat with his hands behind his back, as he stood on the wall.

Harrold stopped in front of him.

"The gate," he said.

"I know which lever," said Waylon.

The king looked at him a moment longer, and put his hand on the side of his son's neck, briefly, the way a man touches a horse he is fond of and does not want to make a fuss of; and went up the gangway. Emeric, at the head of it, stood aside for him. His father went past him onto the K Discovery's deck and stood by the rail and looked up at his own standard going away over the town on the lift hull's stern, lit, small, flying for nobody; and said nothing at all.



They went at nine, in the dark, without lights, ten hulls from the yard and the secret dock, and climbed.

Emeric felt the stones wake hull by hull below him: the escorts first, quick and light, then hull two and the Hod, then the K Endeavor and hull four, heavy and careful with their racks; and last of all the K Amara, off the black water of the secret dock beyond the cut, coming up out of the dark like a hill getting up, with the lake falling off her keel in sheets.

They found their places over the sea. The K Discovery at the point, small and unarmed, leading; escorts one and two a length behind her on either hand; the K Amara on the centreline behind them, commanding, with Ward on her tower; the K Endeavor and hull four flanking her, Imani's hospital in the one and the shore teams and the leaflets in the other; and escorts three and four closing the tail, with Rylan's detachment in their holds. An arrowhead, eight ships, every pair a pair. The Hod and hull two ran loose outside it, as they would all the way: chargers, between the Band and the line.

At four miles, over the Mere, the Well was waiting for them.

He saw her lamp first, a steady red point in the starlight with nothing under it; and then the rest of her, as they came closer: the grey platform, the house on it with its one floor of small lit windows, the spire going down under it into the dark, the rail lights round the edge. She had been up eleven days. Orla Garran's voice came over the set, rough and ordinary, giving the K Amara her approach as if the K Amara were a coal barge coming alongside a quay; and the K Amara came in, slowly, the whole black length of her, until she lay against the platform's side under the dock arm with her stern toward the spire, and the arm reached out to her, and the fill boom swung over with its hose.

They filled her tanks from the Well's. That was what the Well had been built for, before it was anything else. The plant had been running ten days, the silvered tank in the spire was most of the way full, and the hose went taut and the K Amara's gauges began to rise in their columns on her tower. Orla's dock hands worked the transfer in their suits on the open walkway, orange and white in the lamps, clipped to the tether, with Orla counting it on the radio. When the mana was in they changed hoses and sent the Well's water across too, the clean water the plant froze out of the Band air; so that the K Amara's seeder went to the capital with its tank full of the sky.

The K Endeavor came in under the platform's lee and put a line on the rail. Her people crossed to the Well's airlock one at a time, in masks and furs, eleven yards of open walkway on the tether; because Imani had set up her table inside.

Emeric went across to the Well as he went across anything now, off the K Discovery's rail, without a suit, and walked in at the airlock with the frost on his coat, and Orla Garran looked at him and said, "Clip on next time, Your Grace, or I'll put you off," and he said he would. He went aft to the board with her, to look at the plant and the pin and Tess's stone in its cradle in the keel. He did not go near the table.

That was on purpose. It was not his.



Imani had the table set up in the Well's mess, under the one good lamp, which meant under the air vent, which meant that everybody who came to it came with their collar up. She was not cold. Cade had never once in four years seen Imani cold. She sat with a tin of Ottoline's cards at her right hand and a rack of glass ampoules at her left, each one the length of Cade's thumb, each one full of something faintly blue; and she looked at him over the lamp the way she had looked at him in the autumn of the year nine, in the Hod's house, when he had asked her a perfectly reasonable question.

"Collar," she said.

"It's cold."

"Collar down, Cade. Breastbone."

He undid two buttons. She put the flat of her hand on the bone, under the shirt, and kept her eyes on the rack and not on him, like a woman listening at her neighbour's wall; and he stood there with her hand on him and the vent blowing down his neck and tried to think about nothing.

He was a bit fond of her. He had been since the fog. He had told her so, once, in the second winter, and she had said that was the cold talking. It had been, a bit.

The duke was somewhere aft, with Orla Garran and the board, and had not come near the table once. Cade could not decide whether that was tact or cowardice. With the duke it was generally both.

"I've got three," he said, to fill the quiet. "On the card. Master Armand put it on in the winter. Three, he said, and a bit over. So I thought, if there's a bit going..."

"You had three on Tuesday."

"And today I've got three."

"Today you've got two." She took her hand away and wrote on his card with a pencil stub, in her capitals. "You slept badly. You've a cold three days off and you won't know it until Thursday. And your back is doing the thing it does. The thing you've told nobody about. Which I've known about since the autumn of the year nine, so don't make the face."

"I'm not making a face."

"You are making a face. Two."

"What happened to going by the number?" He was a talker by nature, and when he was worried he talked more. "I'm only saying. The card says three. You can read a card, I've seen you read a card..."

"The card says what you can hold. I'm telling you what you can hold tonight." She lifted one of the ampoules to the lamp, and the blue in it moved against the glass. "Two smalls is what's empty. Three's how big the room is, and tonight there's furniture in it. Give you three, and you'll not be having a nice time. You'll be on my ward with a basin by the bed, like I told you you would, four autumns ago." She was not smiling, but it was close. "You asked me once if a bit was good, wouldn't more be better. In the Hod's house. And I said no."

"You did."

"I said no because I couldn't tell you what was in you. Only what the mark was." She slid the ampoule across the table to him. "Now I can tell you what's in you. That's the whole of what's different. Twist the neck on the ring. Drink it in one go. It won't wait."

There was a stir behind him at the airlock end, the inner door's dogs going over and a gust of cold, and a short laugh from the back of the line that was Osric's.

Master Armand had come in from outside.

He had come across from the lift hull, which was lying off the platform's far side with her lamps dark and her standard furled, in a Band suit with the helmet under his arm, and he had not clipped on. There was frost on the plain glass of his spectacles. He took them off and wiped them on his cuff, and looked about the mess with great interest: a man of perhaps thirty-two, on his own two legs, with no stick and nobody's arm, who four winters ago had come down the lake path to the plant on Ward's arm and sat on a crate to get his breath. Orla Garran came out of the passage behind him like a squall. Cade heard her say "clip" twice and "four miles" once. Armand said he was very sorry, and did not look it.

He did not come to the table. He sat down on a crate by the bulkhead, where the stores were, as if he had sat on it for years, and looked at the rack of ampoules for a good while without saying anything at all.

Then he said, "Sergeant."

Osric, third in the line, did not turn round at once. "Sir."

"You asked me a thing once. In the plant. A spoon."

"I did."

"I told you once, and you told the yard." Armand put the helmet down beside him. "I said nobody drinks it. I said it plain, and I'd say it again of any man in that plant on that night. Because nobody alive could tell a man what was in him. I couldn't. I read every soul in that castle every winter for years. I could give you your number to the year, Sergeant. And I couldn't tell you what you'd got room for on a given Tuesday, after a bad night and no breakfast. Not one of you." He nodded at the table. "She can."

The mess had gone quiet. The Well's people at the far table had stopped eating. The vent blew.

"So I can ask again," said Osric.

"You may ask."

"One spoon," said Osric, perfectly straight.

"A spoon's more than your room tonight, Sergeant, because you've been up since four hauling the count's lamps aboard, and I watched you do it, and so you may have one small and not a drop over." Armand looked at Imani. "Is that right?"

"One small," said Imani, without looking up.

"There." Armand sat back against the bulkhead. "I was right about two ounces. I was wrong about the Band." He looked at his own hands. "It's not the Band that kills a man. It's not knowing."

Nobody said anything to that. Cade, who would have said something to anything, found his thumb already on the ring at the ampoule's neck, and let it go. The glass gave with a small dry click. It did not smell of anything. He drank it the way you drink a cold cup of tea in a hurry, and it was like swallowing the inside of a bell: a cold, and then a hum, low down in his ribs, and then, quietly, as if somebody in another room had put down a heavy thing they had been carrying, his back let go.

He stood there with the empty ampoule in his hand.

He stood up straighter than he had in two years.

"Don't say anything," said Imani.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to sing."

"I was going to say thank you," said Cade, "and then I was going to sing."

"Sing on the walkway. Next."

He did not go out. His ship would be another quarter hour at the rail, and he wanted, for once in his life, to watch something without being asked to carry it. He stood by the stores with the empty ampoule in his fist and watched the line go on, the way a line goes on. The K Amara's people were coming through now from the dock arm in their watches, warm, in their shirts, because the arm's way was covered. Ruan came through, the gun captain, between two breech checks, with a rag in one hand; he thumbed an ampoule open with the other hand as he passed the table, drank it, put the empty back in the rack, and went out again without once breaking off his sentence to his mate about a cap on the after turret that wanted a quarter turn. Nobody looked up. Bettany came through, the K Amara's surgeon, Imani's own nurse once, with her sleeves rolled, and said "Don't" to Imani's face before Imani had said anything, and got two. Tolliver came through, nineteen and new to the table and very serious, and was offered his, and held up a hand.

"I'm all right, thanks. Topped off an hour ago."

"Show me," said Imani; and put her hand on his breastbone, and was still, and nodded, and wrote it down. "Fair. Next."

A Well hand at the end of the line, one of Orla's fill crew with the suit's ring still red on her neck, asked the thing that everybody had wanted to ask since Armand came in out of the cold.

"Is it true, sir? About the sky?" She had a Wayford voice. "That it makes you live longer. My gran said a mage's wife stays young, and I've been up here ten days, and I've never slept so well in my..."

"Your gran," said Armand, "was a woman of sense, and she didn't know the half of it." He settled his shoulders against the bulkhead. "The tower keeps a register. Four hundred years of it. Every mage read for his number the day he takes the oath, and every ten years after till he dies; and the age he died at, written in beside it. The Church keeps it as a staffing book, to know how many masters it'll have in twenty years. It never once asked what the book said." He looked at the ceiling. "I read it as a young man, the way you'd read a list of race times, to see where I'd come. I'll give it you in Mistress Imani's measure; I've been turning it into smalls in my head all the way up, for amusement. Under twenty smalls at the oath, and a mage lived as long as a ploughman and looked it. Forty, and he lived ten years longer, and looked younger than his brother at sixty. The top of the book, at a hundred and more, lived half again what a ploughman lives. A hundred and five. A hundred and ten. And three of them, in four hundred years, past that." He shrugged. "The more a body holds, the slower it goes. Four hundred years running, as plain a line as I've ever seen in ink, and nobody in the tower ever drew it."

Imani had stopped writing on the card in front of her. She had pulled a second sheet across, a plain one, and was writing on that, fast, in her capitals.

"Forty," she said. "Ten years. Say the others."

He said them again, slowly. She wrote them.

"Bettany," said Imani, without looking round.

Bettany was halfway to the arm's door. She stopped.

"Your death book," said Imani. "The rats. Say it for him. He's said his."

Bettany came back. She stood by the table with her arms folded and said it to Armand, flatly, as if it were a ward round, and only her colour gave her away. The plain shelf died between two years and three. All of them, every litter, as rats did; she had it in the book, every one, the day and the cage. The dense shelf, the ones that had worked the peg out of the feed door and then the wire: the oldest of those were past three years and a half, and still breeding, and she had stopped looking at them in the mornings in case.

"And it's not the feed," said Imani. "I thought it was the feed. So for the last year I had the plain shelf fed the same and handled the same, every day, by Bettany's own hands. It made no difference. They died at two and at three." She put her pencil down. "Half again. The same as his book."

Armand looked at Bettany for a long moment.

"Half again," he said. "In rats." And then, more quietly, "Well. That's two books."

Imani wrote it at the bottom of her sheet, and folded the sheet, and put it in her apron pocket, and picked up the next card.

"Next," she said.

Osric came back with his empty ampoule held in his hand like a pipe he had forgotten to light, and stood beside Armand's crate, and looked down at him.

"Two ounces," said Osric.

"Don't," said Armand.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

Osric put the ampoule back in the rack, carefully, and went back down the arm to the K Amara; and Cade put his own back beside it, and went out through the airlock to his ship, and sang on the walkway the whole eleven yards, a little flat, in his mask, so that nobody could hear him but himself.



Imani sent for Emeric at the end of it.

He came from the board, and found her alone at the table under the lamp with the cards stacked in the tin and the rack two-thirds empty, and Cade in front of her again, on a stool this time, with his coat off and his shirt pulled up at the back and his face doing its best to look like a man who had been sent for and not a man who had been caught.

"His back," said Imani. "The dose quiets it. It doesn't mend it. In three hours it'll be as it was, and it'll be as it was in the capital when the K Endeavor's hold is full of wounded and he's the one at her helm." She looked at Cade. "I'm going to put my hand on it, and send some of what's in me into it, slowly, and it'll be warm. You can say no."

"Will I still be able to sing?"

"Sadly."

"Go on, then," said Cade.

Emeric stood behind her with his hand flat between her shoulder blades, as he had on the ward, and his sense in her beside hers; and felt her go in.

She was better already than she had been on the dock man's hand. Her reading went in by the spine slowly and found the thing at once, low on the left: an old tear in the long muscle beside the bone, healed badly into a knot, and round the knot the muscle pulled tight and hot and tired, holding a back up crooked. And she began to send, as he had shown her, a thread; and the thread went into the first flesh it came to, the good flesh, the muscle over the knot that had nothing wrong with it at all, and began to spend itself there.

"Not there," he said, low.

She stopped.

"That's well. It doesn't need it. It'll take it, because a body takes what it's given, and you'll have spent half your thread on the part that was fine." He kept his hand where it was. "Go past it. Go through it as if it's a door you've already opened. Don't feed anything you go through. Feed the place you're going to."

"How?"

"Want the knot," he said. "Not the back. The knot."

She was quiet. He felt her try, and fail, and spend a little in the good muscle again, and pull back with a hiss through her teeth; and then try once more, slower, with her eyes shut; and the thread went down through the good flesh without touching it, like a needle through cloth, and came to the knot, and the tired hot flesh round it, and there, only there, began to spend itself, a little at a time.

Cade said a word Emeric had not heard him use in front of her before, and then said he was sorry, and then said, wonderingly, that it was warm.

She held it for a count of sixty. Then she took her hand away and sat back in her chair, and put both hands flat on the table and looked at them, and was grey under the brown.

"That's twice," she said. "Two places. The good and the bad." She breathed. "I can tell them apart from inside. I didn't know I could until I'd put it in the wrong one."

"Do it again tomorrow," said Emeric, "on the K Endeavor. Every day you've got him."

"Every day I've got him," said Imani, and looked at Cade, who had pulled his shirt down and was sitting very still on his stool with his hand on his back where hers had been, and did not seem to have anything to say at all, for once.

Armand had gone by then. Emeric felt him go, unhurried, still not clipped, with Orla Garran's small furious note at the airlock window watching it; and then the lift hull going down, slowly, through four miles of dark toward the town, to be over it by daylight with the king's standard flying, and the king not on her.



The fleet went on at one in the morning, north, in the Band, with the Well's lamp going away astern until it was a red spark and then nothing.

Linus had picked the night, a week ago, at the long table, off forty years of his own wind tables and Sabina's weather letters and three days of the Well's readings from its roof; slowly, aloud, crossing out. Not the twenty-ninth. Not the thirtieth. The first of December: the wind backing south-west and falling light by dusk, and the wet air lying in the river valley under it as it lay there every winter, the low cloud the Church's hulls had always come up out of. Wet air for the seeder. Light wind for the wind watch. A young moon, down early. He had written it on one line and pushed it across to Ward, and Ward had said, "That one."

So they went north slowly, keeping off the land, and did not hurry.

The coast came up under them a little before four. Emeric felt it before anybody saw it: the sea's long cold quiet under his sense going ragged at its edge, and then the land coming in, the first farms, the first notes, scattered, asleep. And at the coast, where the fleet's course turned inland, escorts three and four left the arrowhead's tail and went on north.

They did it without lights, as everything was done that night. He felt them go, the two of them together, a pair keeping its pairing, curving away out of the fleet's line to the right and opening the distance a mile, then two, then ten; and he knew who was in their holds without reaching for them, and reached anyway. Rylan's note, which he had known since the castle yard, steady and plain as a good wall. Declan's beside it. Forty knights and five hundred regulars, packed into two escorts' holds with their rifles and their chutes. Sergeant Fenn, who in September could not float. Robin Sennet, the youngest diver. A boy called Wilf Abery who had leaned into Merrin's sound camera on the gangway two days ago and told his mam he had his scarf.

The set by the K Discovery's helm clicked twice, which was escort three's call sign in Ferra's cipher, and then Rylan's voice, small and flat through the speaker.

"Three and four. Turning. Course north. Three's back to you once we're off."

"Go on," said Emeric.

The set went quiet. He stood by it with his hand on its case for a while after, and then went up into the cupola to take the watch, because he would not sleep now and Linus might as well.



The K Discovery's long glass stood on its brass stand in the cupola, where Linus kept it for the coasts and the stars.

His father was asleep below on the saloon bench in the brown coat, on his back, with his hands folded on his chest and his boots still on, as Emeric had seen soldiers sleep and never once a king. He had slept like that since midnight. Fletcher, at the wheel, had looked down the companion way at him twice and said nothing either time.

The sky was clear above them, as it always was at that height, black and so thick with stars that it was hard to find the ones you knew. The main moon was down. The far moon was a small pale disc in the west; and low in the south-east, sliding along among the stars so slowly that you had to watch a quarter of an hour to be sure, was one of the two small moons. A bright spark. The Almanac called them the moons nobody plants by. Gunners used them for bearings, and children made wishes on them, and the Church said nothing at all about them.

He swung the glass onto it, for something to do with his hands.

It took him a while to find it and longer to hold it; and then he had it. Not a spark. A small world, dark grey, the colour of old lead, lit on one side by a sun that had set for everybody under him hours ago. Its face was scarred all over, pocked with round marks and crossed with long shallow grooves. And across the lit half of it, from its edge to the line where the dark began, as clear in the glass as a rule laid across a page, there was a line.

It was too straight.

He looked at it for a long time. He moved the glass off it and found it again, to be sure it was not a hair on the lens; and it was still there, exactly where it had been, a thin straight dark line across a dead grey moon, with an end to it at each end, square.

Then he found the other one, higher, smaller, fainter, and looked at that too. It was grey as well, and scarred. He could not make out a line on it. He was not sure he could make out anything on it at all.

Emeric put the glass's cap back on, and did not wake anybody, and did not write it down; and in the morning, when Linus came up for the watch and asked whether there had been anything, he said only that it had been a clear night, which was true.



They lay all the next day above the cloud, south of the capital, in the Band, out of the sight of anybody on the ground, and waited.

It was the longest day of the war, and nothing happened in it. The cloud came up under them in the morning as Linus had said it would, a long grey floor from one edge of the world to the other, and they lay over it on their held stones with their propellers feathered, while the sun went round in a sky so blue it was nearly black. The Hod and hull two went up and down with racks. The cooks fed people. Ruan's voice came now and then over the set about a cap, and Ward's once, about Ruan. Imani, on the K Endeavor, sent across a line by radio at noon in her capitals, read out by her clerk: CADE IS SINGING. TWICE TODAY. I BLAME YOU. And Emeric read it twice, and laughed for the first time since the coast, and Fletcher asked what was funny and was not told.

His father shaved in a basin in the galley, and stood at the rail most of the day in the brown coat looking north at the cloud, where under it, two miles down and fifteen miles off, the capital was. Once in the afternoon he put his hand into his coat's inside pocket, and took it out again with nothing in it, and Emeric thought of a sadness that came in like a draught from a door, and of a small line in Ferra's book that ran uphill, and said nothing to him about either.

A little after noon escort three came back out of the north, alone, and took her place at the arrowhead's tail where the pair had been; and her captain said on the set that the detachment was on four, and four was on the strait, waiting for dark. Then the set was quiet again.

At three the wind backed to the south-west and fell light, exactly as Linus had written, and Linus looked at his watch and at the vane on the cupola's roof and said, mildly, to nobody, that he would take that.

The fleet went north the last fifteen miles at Band height, slowly, over the cloud. Under it the low country went by unseen, and then the river, and then, at the edge of his sense, faint and enormous, the roar of a hundred and twenty thousand notes, and round it in its ring the cold still points of the chests.

Emeric went across to the K Amara at three, off the K Discovery's rail, the way he had gone to the Well. Ward wanted him on her tower for the bank, and the K Discovery would hold the point without him: Fletcher at her wheel, Linus at the glass, his father at the rail. His father did not turn round when he went. He lifted one hand off the rail, and put it back.

At a quarter to four, with the sun low over the cloud and the city under them, Ward said it beside him on the tower, once.

"Seeder."

And off the K Amara's stern a long soft white came out into the evening and lay on the air, and thickened, and began to spread down along the Band's foot under their keels. The wind watch held it, three of Armand's students at the stern rail with their hands out. Emeric put his sense into it, and it came back blurred, a great soft smear with nothing in it he could put a shape to, like breath on a pane.

"Good," said Ward; and nothing else.
