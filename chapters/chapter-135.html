Chapter 135 - Sized

The first sheets of Kaldo paper that a pen would not ruin came off the drying lines in the loft above the mill on the fourth of January, and Ferra took the top one down herself, and laid it on the counting room table, and dipped her pen, and wrote her name on it.

The ink sat.

It sat on the paper the way ink sits on good paper from the capital: a clean black line, sharp at the edges, that dried where it lay. It did not spread. It did not run along the fibres in little grey hairs, the way it had run on every sheet that came off Tess's moulds in December, and the way it had run, Emeric remembered, on the barony's old paper in the first year, the cheap rag paper from the coast that the tithe men had used, so that every census entry had come out looking like a spider caught in a puddle.

The secret was glue. The tannery's waste: the scraps and parings of hide that the tanner had thrown in the river for forty years, boiled down in Maren's biggest copper until they gave up a thin clear jelly, and every sheet dipped in the jelly warm and hung to dry again. Sized. It filled the gaps between the fibres so the ink had nowhere to go but where the pen put it. On his other world paper makers had done it for six hundred years, and on this one, apparently, nobody south of the capital had bothered, because nobody south of the capital made paper.

Ferra looked at her name for some time.

"It'll do," she said.

Tess, who had made every mould and been in the loft every night for a fortnight turning the sheets, went pink to the ears and wheeled herself out of the counting room before anybody could see.



The ink was the other thing.

Two inks, because everything in Kaldo was two now. The first was plain: lamp black from the soot of the keep's lamps, ground fine with gum and water, the way ink had been made since there was ink. Anybody could make it. Anybody did. It was what the school would use and the clerks and the market, and if every pot of it in Kaldo were stolen in the night the barony could make more by dinner.

The second was Kaldo ink.

The same lamp black and gum. But ground with it, a pinch to the pot, the dust of drained stones: the grey grit that a mana stone turned to when every last thing in it had been drawn out, that the charging room swept into a bucket every week and that nobody had ever found a use for. It looked like nothing. It made the ink a shade greyer. And when Emeric put his hand flat over a line of it written on the new paper and pushed, the way he had pushed ink across a page with his own mana in the first winter in Kaldo because he had nothing else to do it with, the line moved.

Not much. A hair. But it moved, the whole line together, and it moved easily, much more easily than plain ink, as if the dust in it were listening.

It held a little mana. The dust did. Not enough to charge anything. Enough to answer a push.

He did not tell anybody what it was for yet. He did not entirely know. He knew it was the first piece of something.



The crown's letter came on the ninth.

It came in the crown's bag, by the north road, in nine days from the capital. Not the chancery's black wafer. The red seal of the crown's treasury, which Kaldo had never had a letter from in its history, on a sheet of paper so heavy and so white that Ferra held it up to the window to look at the grain before she read a word of it.

The crown, it said, had considered the barony's Return of Stone Found. The crown observed that the quantity estimated in the barony's survey was without precedent in the records of the realm. The crown observed further that the law of a fifth had been written for finds of stone in the usual way, by the pound, and had never contemplated a body. The crown therefore proposed, in the interest of the realm, that its share of the Grayrock working be not a fifth of the stone raised but a third, and that the crown's assessor, arriving in the spring, should be given a seat at the barony's table for the purpose of overseeing the working's management.

Ferra read it twice. Then she read it out, at the table, on the Monday, to all six.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"A third," said Bram at last.

"And a seat at the table," said Ward.

"And a seat at the table," said Ferra. She put the letter down. "The law says a fifth. The law has always said a fifth. They want a third because it's a lot, and a seat because they want to see what we do with it." She looked down the table at Emeric. "And they asked. They didn't take. That means somebody in the treasury read Keller's matter and the nine sacks and doesn't want a second one."

"We give them a fifth," said Emeric. "And the law."

"And no seat," said Saban.

"And no seat. The assessor can see the working. He can see every pound that comes out of it, weighed, with his own man at the scale, the way Bryda had our man at hers." Emeric looked at the letter. "He can't sit at this table."

Ferra wrote the answer that afternoon on the new paper, in Kaldo's plain ink, not the other. It was four lines long, and polite, and cited the law by its number, and offered the assessor every courtesy of the working and a room at the inn at Grayrock and a seat at Bryda's table for his dinner. It did not mention the third at all.

It went north in the crown's bag on the twelfth. Everybody at the table knew it would not be the last letter.



The other letters began the week after.

They came from houses that had never written to Kaldo in four years. Not by the crown's bag. By their own riders, in their own colours, with their own seals: a baron in the north-west whose name Emeric knew from a banquet; an earl's second son on behalf of his father; two houses in the capital, old ones, with arms he had seen carved over doors on the Street of Gates. Six in a fortnight. And every one of them, under the compliments and the congratulations on the barony's recent good fortune, under the hopes for the holder's health and the regards to the holder's royal father, said the same thing.

A daughter. A sister. A niece.

Ferra put them in a pile on her trestle, unanswered, and read them all twice, and said nothing about them.

Saban priced them.

He did it on the Monday, at the table, when Emeric asked him to, the way he priced a cargo or a crop: dry, flat, commercial, a line each, with his hat on his knee. This house was old and poor and wanted Grayrock's stone. This one was rich and new and wanted the old name a prince would give its grandchildren. This one owed money to a house that owed money to the House of Ducatis, and was probably being pushed. This one had a daughter who had been offered to three other men in two years, which was a thing a buyer should know about any goods. He did not say any of it unkindly. He did not say it kindly either.

"And this one," said Saban, and put his finger on the last letter in the pile, which had come on the sixteenth, by a rider in green and silver, under a seal Emeric had turned over twice in his fingers before he broke it. "This one is the one that matters."

"Whose is it?"

"Sallow," said Saban. "The first queen's brother. Your brother's uncle." He took his finger off it. "It's his daughter. Isolde. Nineteen. Well taught, they say, and sharp, and pretty." He picked up his hat. "And she'll have her aunt's instructions sewn into her travelling cloak, and the first thing she'll do in this keep is count the doors."

"She's coming here?"

"She's already on the road," said Saban. "My man in the capital saw her party leave on the ninth. With her aunt's own steward riding beside her. They'll be here inside the month." He stood up. "You can't refuse a letter that's already in the gate yard. Not from the queen's house. You'll have to give her dinner."



Ferra made a column.

He found it on the twenty-second, late, in her room in the keep, on the trestle under the lamp, when he came down from the line room and saw her light under the door and her name over it in Hale's black paint and knocked. She said come in. She did not look up. She was writing.

It was the alliances.

All of them. Every house that had written, down the left side of a ruled sheet of the new paper, in her small upright hand, with the daughter's name or the sister's or the niece's beside each. And across the top, columns. What the house had. What it wanted. What it would bring to the four baronies in a year, in five, in twenty. What it would cost: in Kaldo's freedom, in its secrets, in the eyes it would put in the keep. Who it was tied to at court. Who it was tied to through them. And at the far right, a last column, headed simply: worth.

She had filled it in. Every line. With the honesty she gave every column, no line left blank, no figure softened. The Sallow line was the highest. She had written why, in four words, and the four words were true.

He stood at her shoulder and read it. He read it all the way down.

"You asked for it," Ferra said, without looking up. "At the table. You said you'd want to know what every one of them was worth before you answered any of them." She dipped her pen. "So you'll know."

He read it again.

He read every line of it twice, from the old poor house that wanted stone to the Sallow girl at the bottom with her aunt's instructions sewn into her cloak. He read the columns across. He read what each of them would bring and what each would cost and what each was worth.

And he saw what was not there.

There was no line for her.

Of course there was not. It was a column of alliances. Houses. Daughters of names. The worth of each to the four baronies, in land and stone and friends at court and children with old names. And Ferra was the daughter of the smith of Kaldo, whose mother had gone out on the first cart of the plague, and who had no house and no land and no name in any book but the one Keller had put her in by her office, and who would bring the four baronies nothing in any column she knew how to rule except the four baronies themselves, which she already held, every book of them, in her hand.

She had not put herself in it. It had not occurred to her that there was a line to put.

Or it had occurred to her, and she had looked at the columns, and worked out what she was worth in them, honestly, the way she worked out everything, and come to the answer that was there, and left the line out, because she did not write down figures she could not stand behind.

He did not know which. He stood at her shoulder in the lamplight and looked at the sheet and could not tell which, and found that it mattered more than anything in the column.

"It's good," he said. His voice was not quite right. "It's very good. It's exactly what I asked for."

"I know," said Ferra. She blotted the last line. "It's done."

She did not look up at him. She sat with the pen in her hand and the column in front of her, finished, and waited for him to go, and he did not go, and after a while she looked up.

He was looking at the column. At the bottom of it. At the place below the Sallow line where the ruling ran on, empty, to the foot of the sheet.

Ferra looked where he was looking.

She did not say anything. Neither did he. They looked at the empty place at the bottom of the column together, in the lamplight, for a long breath. And then she put the pen down, carefully, beside the sheet, and folded her hands on the trestle, and did not look at him, and did not look at the column, and looked at the lamp.

"Goodnight," she said.

He went. He went up the stair to his own room and sat at his table under the window in the dark and did not light anything.



The chair failed for the second time on the twenty-sixth, indoors, in the cart shed behind the keep, because Ward had said it was not going in the yard again where the kitchen could see it.

He had fixed the spin. The counter-burst worked: when he let go of a turn, the opposite corners fired, once, short and hard, and the chair stopped where it was, facing wherever it faced, and nobody was sick in the slag bin. Tess had made him do it forty times in a row, round and stop, round and stop, while she counted, before she would let him go on to the next thing.

The next thing was weight.

The gravity stone under the seat took the weight off the chair and whoever was in it. That was all it was meant to do: cancel it, so the chair floated a hand's width above the ground, as light as a leaf on still water. He had cut it to read the weight on the seat and take off exactly that much and no more. Lighten. Not lift.

He had cut it to read his weight.

Bram was in the cart shed with him, because Ward was at the gate, and Bram had taken the shed's door off its hinges in the morning and was rehanging it, and was halfway through when Emeric got into the chair and lifted it off the shed floor, and floated, a hand's width up, steady, and then got out of it, to look at something on the lattice, with the stone still running.

He had his foot on the floor. He had his hand on the seat. And the stone was still taking off the whole of his weight, as he had cut it to, and he was no longer in the chair.

It took the weight off the chair instead. All of his weight's worth. Off a chair that weighed a third of what he did.

The chair went up.

It went up out of his hand like a cork out of a bottle, straight up, past his face, and he grabbed it, because it was the only thing in the shed that mattered, and it took him with it.

He went up the inside of the cart shed holding on to the seat of the chair with both hands, with his legs hanging, past Bram on the ladder with a hinge in his hand and his mouth open, and the chair hit the roof beam above the hay loft with a crack that knocked the dust out of every timber in the shed, and stopped there, pressed up against it, and he hung from the seat underneath it, twelve feet up, with his feet kicking at nothing.

He got a knee onto the beam. Then the other. He got himself up onto the beam and sat on it, astride, in the dust, with his back against a rafter, and the chair pinned against the roof beside his head, humming faintly, pressing up into the timber with the whole of his weight, and nothing he could do would make the stone let go of it, because he could not reach the lattice from where he sat.

He looked down. Bram was at the bottom of the ladder, looking up.

Neither of them said anything.

"Lighten," said Emeric at last, from the beam. "Past the weight. It's lift."

"I can see it's lift," said Bram.

He did not go and get a rope. He went back up his ladder, and went on hanging the door. Emeric sat on the beam in the dust with the chair humming against the roof beside him and watched him do it. He hung the door. He oiled the hinges. He tried the door, twice. He came down the ladder and wiped his hands. Then, eventually, he went and got a rope, and threw it up over the beam, and Emeric came down it, hand over hand, while Bram held the end and did not laugh, very carefully, with his whole face.

The chair stayed against the roof until the stone ran down, at dusk, and came down by itself, slowly, and settled on the shed floor, and Tess, who had been told about it at the noon bowl by Bram and had not stopped laughing since, wheeled herself into the shed and looked at it and then up at the mark it had left on the roof beam.

"It wants a notch," she said. "On the stick. A stop. So it can't take off more weight than it's carrying." She looked at the beam. "And it should read the weight all the time. Not once at the start. All the time. So if somebody gets out, it knows."

"Yes."

"You'd have thought of that," said Tess, "if you hadn't been in such a hurry to get to the parachute." She wrote on her slate. "Notch."
