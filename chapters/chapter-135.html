Chapter 135 - Sethe's Boxes

The keys were still tied to the first hasp, in the envelope, where she had tied them two years before.

Ferra untied them at the strongroom table with the door shut and the lamp lit, though it was the middle of the morning, because the strongroom had no window. She had brought two of her three clerks down with her. Hobb was in Wayford at a cart's tail. Wenna and Dace stood at the end of the table in their sleeves, Wenna with the pens and Dace with the blank book, and waited, and did not ask why they had been brought down to a room with no window to open a dead man's letters on a fine spring morning. Wenna had come to her from the top of Orrin's school, and Dace from the Milford road, where he had kept Paxton's factor's accounts. They had worked for Ferra for two years. They had learned not to ask why until they had been told what.

The relay had brought three lines from Wayford the evening before, in the barony's private marks, which only Ferra and the keeper at the keep post could read, and the keeper could not read them all. The Staple is a charter. Five houses. Sethe will have dealt with them. Read his letters.

She had read the three lines, and sat for some time, and then written in her own book, before she did anything else:

Fault. Read the barony's books. Did not read the baron's letters. Two years.

She had not written anything under it about how she felt. It was not a thing that went in the book. What went in the book was that the fault was hers, and when, and how long, and she had written that.

It was the same fault as the Staple. She could see that as clearly as Emeric would, and sooner. She had read every line of every seized book in both baronies, because books told you what a house did, and she had shelved four boxes of letters on the bottom shelf under the books because letters told you what a man wanted and she had not had the time. She had been right about the books. The books had given them Pennock, and the post, and every honest clerk in two baronies. And she had put the boxes under them, and tied the keys to the hasp, and gone on to the next thing.

She fitted the first key.

It took them nine days.

Four boxes, iron-bound, each the size of a small chest, each full to the lid. Sethe had kept everything. Every letter he had received for eleven years, in bundles tied with tape, in order of the day they came, and a copy of every letter he had sent, in his secretary's hand, in books. He had been a careful man in the one way that mattered to a clerk. He had never thrown anything away.

Ferra did it the way she did everything, in order. Wenna opened each bundle and read out the sender and the date. Dace wrote them in the book, one line each, with the box and the bundle. Ferra read the letter. If it was nothing, and most of them were nothing, a factor's bill, a cousin's wedding, a complaint about a road, she said nothing and Wenna tied the bundle up again. If it was not nothing she laid it to her left.

The pile to her left was small for five days and then it was not.

The first thing was the Staple, and it was in the first box, and it was money.

The charter had been granted three kings back to five houses for the weighing at the junction, and in the charter, which Sethe had kept a copy of, there was a clause that nobody had read aloud in a hundred years: a tenth of every duty taken at the beam was reserved to the lord of Wayford, for keeping the road. A tenth. It had been paid. Ferra found the payments, quarter by quarter, eleven years of them, in the letters from the Staple's clerk that came with each purse.

She did not find them in the barony's books. She had read the barony's books line by line, and there was no tenth in them.

Sethe had taken it to himself. Every quarter, the Staple's clerk had sent the tenth to Sethe's private hand and not to his counting house, and Sethe had written back each time to acknowledge it, and in the fourth year he had written, in his secretary's copy, that he and the lords of the Staple understood one another, and that he saw no reason why the arrangement should trouble anybody's clerks. After that the Staple's letters stopped calling it the tenth. They called it the usual.

Ferra did the sum for eleven years in the margin of Dace's book and looked at it.

"That's the barony's," Wenna said, reading it upside down. Wenna had come top in every sum at the school for three years, and she did sums upside down now as well.

"It was," said Ferra. "It still is." She laid the bundle to her left. "Nobody's paid it since the judgment. There's been no baron in Wayford to pay it to, and the Staple's clerk hasn't written to ask where it should go."

The second thing was the post.

She had known about the post. Everybody had known about the post; it had been the whole of the judgment. What she had not known was what it cost. Sethe had paid his riders by the month, out of his own purse, a Silver and a half a man, and he had kept the list of what each one was paid to lose. It was in his own hand, not his secretary's. It had the Kaldo letters in it by date. It had Jory, the courier who had died on the road, and against his name, in Sethe's hand, a line she read twice and then did not read again.

She laid it to her left.

The third thing was in the fourth box, near the bottom, and it came after the war.

After the war, after Kaldo had held and the coalition had broken on the wall and gone home, the letters from the five houses changed. They stopped being about duty and weighing and the usual. They were shorter. They came more often. They came in pairs, from Placit and from Hollin on the same day, or from Dray and from Vennor, and they spoke about the second matter.

They did not say what the second matter was. Nobody who wrote to Sethe in those months said what anything was. They said the second matter wanted money, and the money was sent, and the money was named: so much from Placit, so much from Hollin, so much from each of the five, into Sethe's hand, for the second matter, over one summer. It was a great deal of money. It was more than Kaldo's whole treasury had held in the first year.

And against the last of the money, in Sethe's own hand, in a small account book that had been tucked inside the last bundle as though he had meant to take it out again and had not had time, there was a line:

To M., for the second matter, in full. Forty Gold.

It had a date.

Ferra looked at the date for a long time. Then she took her own book out of her case, the barony's book, the one in which everything that had ever happened to Kaldo had been entered in her hand, and turned back through it, a long way back, to the page for that summer, and found the date that sat three days after it.

She did not need to read the entry. She knew what it said. She had written it the morning they got him back, with her hands not steady for the only time anybody had ever seen them not steady, and she had written it anyway, because it had happened.

The Deep Channel.

Six names, in the end.

Five of them were the five houses of the Staple, over and over, in the tenth and the usual and the second matter. Placit, who wrote most and longest. Hollin, who wrote about money and nothing else. Dray, who wrote too eagerly and was answered least. Vennor, who wrote seldom and said less. Strade, whose letters were in a small upright hand and were the shortest of all, and never once used a word she did not need.

The sixth was Lisle.

Lisle was in the boxes three times in eleven years. Once to decline an invitation from Sethe to dine. Once to acknowledge a boundary, in the woods, along the stream. And once, in the second summer after the war, in the middle of the second matter, a single line, unsigned but in the same hand as the other two: I have no part in this and will take none. Sethe had kept it with the others. He had not answered it, so far as the copies showed.

That was all. Three letters. It was thin. It was so thin that Ferra, reading it, could not tell whether it was a man keeping out of a thing or a man making sure there was a letter saying he had.

She laid it to her left, on its own.

Emeric came home by night on the ninth day.

He had sent the marks from Wayford that afternoon, two lines in the barony's private hand, which had come down the ridge post to post to the keep before the evening bell, and at the keep post the keeper had written them out and sent them to Ferra and to Osric and to nobody else.

At Wayford, His Highness was keeping to his rooms. He had a chill. Saban told the inn so at supper, and ordered a posset sent up, and Hobb sat outside the door of the cold room at the top of the stairs with a book on his knee, and anybody who came up the stairs found a clerk reading by a candle and a door that stayed shut.

Emeric was not behind the door.

He was on a hill a mile south of the town, with Ward, in the dark. It was a bare round hill with a single thorn on it that Ward had chosen two days before on a ride, because it could not be seen from any road and nobody in Wayford had a reason to climb it at night. They had gone out of the inn by the yard, in plain coats, while the tap room was loud, and walked. At the top of the hill Ward set a lamp on the ground behind the thorn and lit it and put a hood over it, a tin hood with a slot cut in the top, so that the light went straight up and nowhere else, and they stood beside it and waited.

Emeric felt her before he heard her.

The sense was out over the hill, idly, the way he let it out when he was waiting, and he felt her come into it from above: the long dense weight of her stones, far up, coming down slowly, a notch at a time, and the thin cold air sliding off her hull. Then he heard her, the faint rush of the air stones, and then he saw her, a long darker shape against the dark, with no light on her at all, settling toward the hill.

She came down over the thorn at the height of a man and hung there, and a rope came over her side.

Osric was at the levers. Ward had taught him in the dead weeks after the voyage, every clear night on the lake, until Osric could bring her down onto a lamp and hold her level in a wind, and Ward had said so to Emeric in one sentence, which from Ward was a warrant. Osric did not say anything when they came over the rail. He waited until they were in, and then he took her up, a notch at a time, into the dark.

Kaldo was an hour.

They sat in the long room, all seven of them, with the lamps lit and the door shut and the fire built up, at the second hour after midnight.

Ferra read them the boxes first. She did it in order, from her pile, the tenth and the usual and the post and the second matter and the forty Gold to M., and the date, and the date three days after it, and the six names, and the thin one on its own. She did not look up while she read. When she had finished she laid her book open on the table at the page where she had written the fault, and turned it round so they could see it, and said nothing about it, and nobody else did either.

Emeric looked at the page a long moment. Then he told them about the ink.

He did it plainly, as he had told Armand. The ink boiled beside stone. The voice it gave off drying. Armand's bench, and Armand's word under the figures. And then, because it had to be said now if it was ever going to be, what a voice in an ink could be made to do: that a stone which could hear it could be put near where a man wrote, and a printer could print what it heard, in another room, a mile off, and the man would never know he had written it twice.

It was very quiet in the long room.

Imani spoke first.

"It's another file," she said. "On people. Like the census." She said it without any heat at all, looking at him across the table, the way she had said the census was a surveillance file on the day he asked Ferra for it. "You'll read what they write to their wives, and their physicians, and their creditors, and their children. You'll read all of it, to find the one line you want. That's what it is."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"I want that said. That's all. I want it said at this table that it's that."

"It's that."

She nodded, once, and sat back.

"The bench confirms it," said Armand. "I said so. I will say one thing more." He folded his hands on his stick. "Nobody else will find it. I have been in the medium forty years and I would not have found it. If you do this, nobody will ever know it was done. Not the men who wrote. Not the Crown. Not the Church. Not the lords at court." He looked at Emeric. "That is not an argument for it. I am telling you what it weighs."

"It only works if they write in it," said Saban. "They won't buy it. They'll buy Pell's paper and whatever the Ambrys were selling before you bought the Ambrys, and they'll go on buying it out of spite." He turned his cup. "You'd have to give it to them. As a gift. Between gentlemen."

"I'm dining with them."

"I know you are," said Saban.

"The listeners," Ward said. "Stones near where they write. Can they be put there without being seen?"

"By air," said Emeric. "Yes."

Ward looked at him for a moment, and then at the table, and did not say anything else.

Bram said nothing at all.

The table noticed it. Bram had said something at every table since the first one, even if it was only straw, and he sat now with his big hands flat on the boards and looked at them, and did not say anything, and everybody at the table saw him not say it, and nobody asked him to.

It was Armand who asked the question that settled it.

"When you are not in the room," he said, "what decides what the printers print?"

It was the right question. It was the question under all the others: whether this was a thing that needed Emeric in it every hour, like the printer, or a thing that could stand on its own. Emeric had known somebody would ask it, and had known it would be Armand.

"A control stone," he said. "Bound, and set to decide. The way the printer's page is bound into its deciding stone, but larger. It hears the listeners and tells the printer what they heard."

"You have no spare," Armand said. "You told us so in the autumn. You built a whole winter round it."

"I have one now."

They looked at him.

"My father sent me my mother's stones," Emeric said. "The ones she was buying for me when she died. They came by Ward's hand."

He did not say how. He did not look at Ward. The road was Ward's to tell and Ward did not tell it. Ward sat at his corner of the table with his hands on the boards, as he had sat on the night of the box, and said nothing, and after a moment Armand, who had given a box into Ward's hand at the castle gate six weeks before and had not asked what was in it, looked at Ward, and then looked away, and did not ask now.

Ferra, who had the stones in her strongbox, did not say anything either.

The table decided on the ink.

It did it the way the table decided everything, by going round. Saban for, because it was the only way to see inside the Staple without a man inside it. Armand for, with what he had said about the weight. Ward for, in one word. Bram, asked, looked up from his hands at last and said, "Build it, then," which was not a yes and was not a no and was taken as the one it was nearer. Imani for, and she said why: because the other thing on the table was the forty Gold to M., and the date, and she had been in the room when they carried him up out of the channel.

Then it came to Ferra.

Ferra had the ninth clause. She had had it since the night of the Standing Order: the Chancellor may stay any act of the holder by a stay in writing, and the holder may not overrule it. She had never used it. She had a stay form in the back of her book, drawn up and ruled, that had been there since the week after the seating, and had never been filled in.

She did not take it out.

She took out a clean sheet instead, and uncapped her pen, and wrote, and read it aloud as she wrote it, in the voice she used for terms.

Conditions on the ink.

One. Who reads. Two of my clerks, named by me, and myself. Nobody else reads a page until one of us has read it. What goes up to the table goes up from me.

Two. What it costs. A ceiling, set by me, on a line in the barony's book. It does not go over. If it must go over, it comes back to this table first.

Three. What is kept. A page that bears on no crime is destroyed at a set interval, which I will set, and a line entered that it was destroyed, with the date. Not read again. Not kept against a day it might be useful.

Four. The key. Mine. The itemised account of what this costs and what it finds is kept in a box keyed to me alone. The holder does not hold a key.

Five. When it ends. This net is built for the Staple and the second matter. When that matter is closed, the net comes down. All of it. It is not moved to another town. It is not kept against another matter. It comes down.

She put the pen down, and turned the sheet round, and pushed it across the table to Emeric.

He read it. He read the fifth condition twice.

"Yes," he said.

"Sign it."

He signed it. She witnessed it, and then she passed it round the table, and each of them witnessed it, one after another, Armand slowly, Bram with his thumb holding the corner down, and it came back to her with seven names on it.

"You didn't stay it," Emeric said.

"No." She was folding the sheet. "A stay says no. I haven't got a no. I've got a how." She put the sheet into the back of her book, where the stay form was, beside it. "A stay that isn't in a form is a mood. So is a yes."

He left again before dawn, with Ward, for the ship and the hill and the inn and the chill.

Ferra stayed at the long-room table after the others had gone up. The fire was down. She opened the barony's book to the current page, and dipped her pen, and wrote, in the column for lines the barony would pay out of that year, a line that was general and true.

Discretionary. Held under the Chancellor's conditions of this date. Ceiling as set.

Then she took a second sheet, a small one, and wrote on it what the line was for, and what it would buy, and folded it once and put it into the box she had had Bram make in the winter for exactly this, the one with a single lock, and turned the key, and put the key on a cord round her neck, under her collar.
