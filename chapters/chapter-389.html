Chapter 389 - The Primer

The first class was a hundred and twenty knights in the school's great hall on the Monday morning, and Ilenne had been sick before it in the yard behind the kitchens.

Nobody was meant to know that. Imani knew, because Imani had been in the yard, by arrangement, with a cup of water and a cloth, and she'd told Emeric only because he'd come round the corner at the wrong moment and found the three of them there: Imani with the cup, Ilenne bent over the drain with her hood down and her colour gone the flat dull grey he'd only seen on her when she was dry, and Zale with one hand on her back, saying nothing in two languages.

"It is nothing," said Ilenne, straightening up. "It is fish."

"It isn't fish," said Imani.

"It is a hundred twenty knights." Ilenne took the cup and rinsed her mouth and spat, neatly, into the drain. "Is the same."

She'd taught before. She'd taught the six on the slab in July and the divers in the shallows in February and Ferra every night of the winter at the bay house kitchen table. She'd never taught a room. Her people's teachers, she'd told Ferra, taught in the water, in a ring, everybody level, everybody able to see everybody's colour; there was no front. There was a front to the school's great hall. It had a platform at it a foot high, and a lectern, and a slate on an easel, and behind the slate a long window onto the playground where Sela's youngest were out at their morning break, shrieking. The benches went back from the platform in rows, and every row had knights on it, Ward's and the Crestless and the flight knights off the K Amara and the escorts, in their working blues with their caps on their knees, and they'd all stopped talking when she came in.

That had been the worst of it, Zale said afterwards. Not the size of it. The quiet.

She went up the step onto the platform with her hood up and stood behind the lectern with her hands on it and looked at them all, a hundred and twenty faces in rows, all of them below her and looking up, and her colour did something at the throat that Emeric, standing at the back by the door with Ferra, couldn't read and Imani, beside him, could.

"She doesn't like being above them," said Imani quietly.

"It's a platform."

"I know it's a platform. In her water it's how you stand to someone you mean to eat."

Ilenne looked at the hall a moment longer. Then she took her hands off the lectern and came down off the platform, the one step, onto the floor, and stood in front of the first bench on the boards, level with the front row's knees; and after a moment, without being asked, Arthur, who was on the front bench at the end, got up and moved his bench back two yards so there was room, and the man next to him helped, and then the whole front row was doing it, and then the second, with a great scraping of wood, until there was a sort of half circle in front of her, rough and loud and full of knights saying mind your feet.

Ilenne watched them do it. Her colour came up warm.

"Good," she said, in Kaldo, to all of them. "Now I see you."

She put her hood down.



The primer had been on Ferra's sheet since July, when Saban had first said what it would be: the crew taught the courtesies before they sailed, by her, in her language and theirs, with the colour signs, so that eight hundred and thirty people from the land didn't arrive at a queen's door in a thousand years and say hungry. It ran in nine classes over six days, a hull or a watch at a time. The knights came first, because Ward had said that if anybody was going to start a war by accident at Aumere it would be a knight trying to be polite.

Zale was her assistant.

That had been Ilenne's idea, and she'd announced it at the long table the week before in a way that had made Zale go slowly red from the collar up. On the slab at South Sentinel last June, the evening she'd taken his job off him with a hand on his face, she'd told him how it would be: Ferra teach. Zale, hit. When bad. Ferra would teach her Kaldo, and Zale's work was to stop her when she got it wrong. Now it was her language, she said, and she would teach, and Zale would stand at the end of the room, and when she said, he would do the signs, wrong, so that the knights could see what wrong looked like. And she would stop him.

"Wrong on purpose," Zale had said.

"You don't need on purpose," Ilenne had said, and Ferra had had to put her pen down.

So he stood at the end of the half circle in his good sea coat, with his hands clasped behind his back like a junior officer, and Ilenne taught.

She taught them the colour first, because everything else hung off it. She stood in front of them with her hood down and her throat bare and showed them, one after another, the colours that were on the page already in Ferra's book and on Imani's shade card, and for each one the sign that Kaldo could make instead, since Kaldo had no colour to speak with. The pale, the deep bow, for respect: the flat hand lowered, slowly, palm down, like a bow made with the hand alone. The warm one, for gladness, for liking, for yes, you: two fingers drawn up the inside of the other wrist. The dark at the collarbone, for no, for stop, for a door shut: the hand snapped shut in front of the chest. The slate, for fear, for danger, for something is wrong here: the hand laid across the eyes.

And then the new one, from the wall water. The bow, and then the lowered hand turned over, palm up, and held out.

"This is my mother's," she said. "This is: come in. My house. My word." She did it again, slowly, so they could see. "When my mother does this, you are safe. In all the water. Nobody touches you. If somebody touches you, he touches her." She looked round the half circle. "You do not do this one. Never. It is not yours to do. Only, when she does it, you do the bow back. Like this." The flat hand lowered. "And you do not look up."

"Why not look up?" said somebody at the back.

Ilenne thought about it.

"In the water," she said, "the one who comes from under you is hunting. Always. Fish, seal, shark, my people. You come up from under, you are the teeth." She held one hand flat, palm down, and brought the other up under it from below, fast, so the fingers knocked. "So you never come from under. Never swim under somebody. Never look up at somebody when they speak, like from under. You come level." She held both hands flat, side by side. "Level is friend. Over is old man, telling. Under is teeth."

A hundred and twenty knights looked at the platform she'd come down off.

"Yes," said Ilenne. "That."

Somebody laughed, and stopped, and then a great many of them laughed, and Ilenne's colour went warm to the jaw and she didn't try to hide it. Arthur, on the front bench, was writing it down.



Zale got it wrong in the third hour.

He didn't mean to. It was the part of the class where she had them stand and do the signs back to her, a bench at a time, while she went down the line looking; and to show them how it went, she turned to Zale at the end of the half circle and said, in her own tongue and then in Kaldo, so that everybody had it: "Zale. Greet the captain. You come to his ship. He is above you. Greet him."

Zale stepped forward. He'd done this. He'd done it for real, in the wash at South Sentinel the night the guards came, up to his waist in the sea with twelve of them standing in a ring with their points down, and he'd been the only man in Kaldo the captain had understood. He squared his shoulders and lifted his hand, and said the words, the captain's greeting, very properly, in his rough sailor's version of her tongue that the guards had understood and she said made him sound like a fisherman's uncle; and as he said them he brought his hand up, flat, and laid it across his eyes.

The slate. I am afraid of you. Something is wrong here.

There was a short silence.

Ilenne looked at him.

"No," she said.

Zale took his hand down off his eyes.

"That was..." He looked at his hand. "That's the bow. Isn't it? That's..."

"That is slate." Ilenne walked down the half circle to him, not fast, and took his wrist in her hand, and moved it, the way she'd moved their arms and ankles in the shallows in February. She took it down off his face and turned it palm down and lowered it, slowly, in front of his chest. "Bow. Here. Low." Then she lifted it again and put it across his eyes, the way he'd had it, and held it there a moment so everybody could see, Zale standing with his own hand over his face and his wife-to-be holding it there by the wrist in front of a hundred and twenty knights. "Slate. Here. You say to the captain: I am afraid. Danger. Something bad in your ship." She took his hand down. "In August, you say this to the captain, he puts his point in you."

"I didn't say it in August."

"No. In August you say hungry." She let go of his wrist. "Hungry, he only laughs."

The hall came apart.

It went from the front bench to the back like a wave going up a beach: Arthur first, who put his pencil down and his face in his hands; then Mira, beside him, out loud; then Bren, who had to be held up by the man on his other side; and then the whole of the K Amara's knights, a hundred and twenty of them in rows, and the Crestless, and Ward at the side wall with his arms folded and his face like a church door, who didn't laugh at all and was the only person in the hall Emeric had ever known shake from not laughing. Zale stood in the middle of it with his hands at his sides and his ears gone scarlet.

Ilenne waited until it had gone down enough to be heard over. She didn't smile. Her colour was doing something so bright at the throat that the knights on the front bench could probably have read it by.

"So," she said, to all of them. "This is wrong. Now you see. Zale is very good. He is wrong so you see." She turned to him and, very formally, in front of everybody, did the warm one at him: two fingers drawn slowly up the inside of her own wrist. "Thank you, Zale."

Zale looked at her.

Then he did the bow, properly, flat hand lowered, palm down, at the level of his chest, and didn't look up; and the hall, which had been about to start again, didn't, quite.

"Better," said Ilenne. "Again. All of you. Up."



The courtesies took the rest of the morning. Ferra had them in the book in a list, and had cut the list twice, because the first list had had forty things on it and Ilenne had looked at it and said that if she told a hundred and twenty knights forty things they would remember the four stupidest.

So there were seven. Ilenne gave them one at a time and made them say each back.

The colour first, and the signs: never speak to her people without one, because a sentence without colour was what a thing in the deep said before it ate. Never touch the throat, anyone's, for any reason, not to help, not to wipe the sea off: the throat was where they spoke from, and a hand on it was a hand over a mouth. Level, never under. Don't follow anyone into a house who hasn't done the turned-up hand, however friendly; they'd be standing in somebody's house with no word, and in her mother's city that was the same as standing in the street at night with your purse out.

And then the two that Ferra had underlined.

"No gift," said Ilenne, "before the greeting. Before the colour, the bow, the hand. Never." She held up one finger. "You come, you give a gift, first? Before anything? It is a price. You are buying. My people say: what does he want to buy? What does it cost, after?" She held up a second finger beside it. "You come, you greet, the bow, the hand, you speak, you sit. Then the gift. It is a friend's. It is: I am glad. Same gift. Same fish. Different."

"What if they give us a gift first?" said Mira.

"Then they want to buy you," said Ilenne. "Then you are careful."

Ferra, at the back, beside Emeric, made a small mark in the margin of her book, and he leaned over and read it: gift chests: hold till after. Tell the Board. Tell Vantic.

"And no promise," said Ilenne, "in the water."

The hall had gone quiet again, the good kind of quiet.

"In my water," she said, "a thing you say, with people to see, in the water, is said. Forever. It is like..." She looked for it, and found it, and pointed at the back of the hall, at Ferra. "It is like her book. It is written. You say in the water, I will bring you a boat, you bring a boat. You say, I will come back, you come back. If you don't, everyone knows you are..." She said a word in her own tongue, and made a sign Emeric hadn't seen before, a hand drawn sideways across the body, low, like wiping something off a table. "A nothing. A man whose word is water." She looked round at them. "So. In the water: you are polite. You greet. You do the bow. You say thank you. You don't say I will. Never I will. Not to anybody. You want to say I will, you come out on the dry, on the boat, and you say it to the king, and the king says it." She nodded at the back of the hall, at Emeric. "He says it in the water. Only him. Because his word is the country's. And if he says it wrong, the country pays." She considered him. "So he is careful also."

A hundred and twenty knights turned round on their benches and looked at the king of Randillia, standing by the door with his arms folded.

"I'll be careful," said Emeric.

"You say that on the dry," said Ilenne. "So it is not counted." And for the first time that morning she smiled, properly, with her whole face.



It was after that, in the last quarter of an hour, that a flight knight off escort three asked her where her people got their iron.

He was young, a boy of perhaps twenty from the Wayford intake, with a farmer's hands and a serious face, and he asked it because she'd said, in passing, explaining the gift chests, that her people would want iron more than gold. And he'd thought about it, the way the Wayford intake thought about things, and put his hand up.

"If you've no fire under the sea," he said, "where does the iron come from? Do you trade for it? With who?"

Ilenne looked at him.

Emeric, at the back, felt Ferra go still beside him. He felt Ward, at the side wall, unfold his arms.

It had been on the sheet since July. Customs before gifts. The others live: the crew before they sail, at the primer; not the city, not the school, not from her. Saban's rule, from the Hollow Night after the beaching, when Saban had sat at the long table and said that the day the town found out there were other people in the world, the Church would find out the same day, and he'd like that day to be one he'd chosen. Ferra had pencilled Primer beside it in July and underlined it. And they'd all of them, every one of the six, spent eight months not saying it in front of anybody.

Ilenne looked at the back of the hall, at Emeric. She asked him with her eyes.

He nodded.

"The smiths," said Ilenne, to the boy from Wayford. "Under the mountains. Far. They make iron, and they bring it to our dry city, on the top, and they trade it for fish and salt and pearl. And they always cheat." The colour went faintly sour at her throat, the way it always did when she talked about the smiths' prices. "Two of them live in my city. They complain about the wet."

The boy from Wayford looked at her.

"Smiths," he said. "You mean men. Smiths."

"No," said Ilenne. "Not men. Smiths." She said their word for them, the one that was in Ferra's book with no Kaldo after it but smiths, under mountains, cheat. "Short. Very strong. Beards. A different people. Like my people are a different people." She tilted her head at him. "You think there is only you? And us?"

Nobody in the hall said anything.

"There are the tall ones," said Ilenne. "They send us wood. They don't come. They don't let us come. Cold people. There are the winged ones on the cliffs, who tell my people when a storm comes, for the fishing. There are..." She moved her hand, west, north, a wide vague sweep. "Many. Far. I don't know all. Nobody knows all." She looked at the boy from Wayford, whose mouth had come a little way open, and then at the hall. "You didn't know."

And she did the sign that was in Ferra's book with a gloss beside it in Ferra's hand, the one from July on the slab, the two fingers laid across the palm of the other hand and held there, like something small that had fallen out of a nest.

You knew nothing. Not unkindly. The way you'd say it to a child.

A hundred and twenty knights looked at her hand.

At the back of the hall, Armand, who had come in quietly at some point in the third hour and was standing by the other door with his cane, said, in his tower voice, not loudly, so that it went to every bench: "It's true. I was taught there weren't any. I taught it myself, for forty years, to novices. I was wrong. She has two of them in her town, complaining about the damp."

And Ward came off the side wall.

He walked out into the middle of the half circle, beside Ilenne, and stood there in his blues with his hands behind his back, and looked round at all of them, a hundred and twenty of his own knights, the way he looked at a yard before a drill.

"That's on your card from today," he said. "Every one of you. It's on the card with the bleeding and the colours and the clip on your chute. You know it, and you'll know it at the door, and you'll not be surprised by it, and you'll not say it." He let that sit. "Not in the town. Not in a tavern. Not to your mother. Not to your sweetheart. Not in a letter home in a held message on the net when you're a thousand miles out and lonely. When the town learns there are other people in the world, it'll learn it from the king, on a day he picks. Until then you've a secret, and you'll keep it, because you're knights, and because if the Church learns it from a drunk boy in a harbour it'll learn it before we're home." He looked at the boy from Wayford. "Good question. Sit down."

The boy from Wayford sat down.

"Again," said Ilenne. "The bow. All of you. Level."



He went down to the Forge on the Tuesday night, a little after eleven, with two sheets of drawing folded in his coat that he hadn't shown anybody.

He didn't use it for this. He told himself so on the old stair, with the warm air coming up at him and the hum in it, as if somebody had asked. Every rifle Kaldo owned had come off Bram's bench, from Bram's gauges, made by men who could make the next one without him. Ferra had said not from the Forge across a table years ago with her hand flat on her book, and he'd said not one barrel, and he'd kept it through every war since and a great many nights when it would have been quicker not to. The Forge made the machines that made things. It didn't make the things.

But in four weeks they'd be on three hulls. Ferra on the K Amara's bridge deck at the chart table, Imani on the K Endeavor's hospital deck with Bettany, and him a mile ahead of both on the K Discovery with the relays in the hold; and at Aumere, for however long the queen kept him, he'd be Below, under the sea, where no radio went. He couldn't be on three ships. He couldn't be on any of them from the bottom of the sea. And everything the two of them could do now, which was a great deal more than a year ago, went out in a dead zone the way his did, like a lamp in a draught. Their bodies wouldn't. A dead zone took what you cast; it didn't take what you were. They'd be two very strong women standing in a dead zone with nothing in their hands.

So he'd drawn them something to put in their hands.

A revolver. It was the only name he had for it, and it was the right one, and it was small. A steel frame that would ride in the pocket of a sea coat or under an arm without anybody seeing the shape of it, and a short barrel, two and a half inches, under a ribbed steel shroud. Behind the barrel a cylinder with six chambers bored through it, which swung out sideways on its crane when you pushed the latch, so you could load it and empty it, and swung back and locked. Pull the trigger and the cylinder turned a sixth, the hammer came back and fell, and the next chamber was in line for the next pull. Six pulls, six shots, and then it was empty, and you knew, because you'd counted.

He'd drawn an automatic first, a bigger thing with fifteen rounds up the grip, and torn the sheet in half on the stair. An automatic fed itself off a spring and wanted keeping clean, and a bad round could choke it at the worst moment, and then you needed both hands and a drill to clear it. A revolver that met a bad round needed one more pull of the trigger. And an automatic that size wouldn't go under a coat on a hot deck without everybody knowing it was there. This would. Nobody would know it was in her hand until it was. Six was enough for the worst day. He hoped six was too many. Nothing in it was magic. There wasn't a stone in it anywhere, not a chip, not a grain of dust in the steel.

That had been the easy decision. Everything else Kaldo built had a stone over a mundane floor, and he'd have done the same here if the stone had bought anything. It bought nothing. A charged stone in a pistol was something any mage could feel at a few paces, under a coat, in a crowd, which was the opposite of what a pistol was for; and in the one place this pistol was meant for, the stone would be dead weight. He wanted them carrying something nobody could feel and nothing could switch off.

The round had taken longer. On the other side of everything, the men behind gun counters had a way of handing a woman the smallest thing in the case, a little gun in a little round, because she had little hands. He'd thought about that, and about Ferra in the autumn absently taking the handle off the records hall door while she was reading something, and thrown it out. Their hands would hold a heavy round the way most people's held a light one. So it was a magnum, as the other side had called it: a rimmed brass case an inch and a quarter long, with a flat-nosed bullet of hard lead a little over a third of an ounce, nine millimetres across, going out of that short barrel at about thirteen hundred feet a second. On the other side a gun that small firing that round was a handful for a big man, and a thing he practised with and didn't enjoy. It would go through a man and through the door behind him, and that was its fault, and he'd tell them so.

The Forge made what it was told out of what it was given, and nothing else. He laid it out on the slab under the lamp, declared and weighed, the way he laid out everything: two short bars of the mill's best tool steel; a coil of spring wire; brass strip; a little block of hard steel for the dies; and an offcut of walnut from the old pews, the last of the board his own rifle's stock had come out of, which he'd kept in a drawer for years because he couldn't bring himself to burn it. Then he put his hand on the index and went down into it and gave it the drawing, all of it at once, every part and every number, out of the place where he kept such things whole.

The slab hummed for two hours and ten minutes. Somewhere above him in the dark, the plain shed's vent would be breathing out its little grey cloud of dust over the roofs, and anybody awake to smell it would think the Works was burning rubbish late.

He didn't go up while it ran. He never did. He sat on the crate by the brass cabinet with his coat over his knees and watched the head go round under the top collar, and the light of the work come and go in the glass, and after a while he found he was looking at the ring on the floor instead.

Nine feet across. Anything longer than eight feet that had to be true came out of here in sections and was shrunk together outside, the way the big crankshafts were, and for years that had been enough. It wasn't going to be enough for half of what was on Tess's list. And it was the only one. One room at the bottom of one stair: a cheap stone burst on the landing and every machine that made machines in the kingdom stopped until the air came back. In four weeks he'd be a thousand miles from it, with Corbin holding the city over it.

The Isle's shard was coming in now, a quota by weight off the live seam every month, more stasis stone in the Forge's stores than the kingdom had seen in a lifetime. He was turning it over without having decided to, the way he turned most things over at this hour.

The lattice pushed off the rings. That was the whole trick of the tube: the frame was the stiffest thing in the room, bolted to the rock, and every stone in its cradle took its down from the frame. At nine feet, gunmetal and iron could be that stiff. At forty they couldn't. The frame would breathe under the load, and the work would follow it, and a crankshaft that followed its frame was scrap. But the pin didn't push off iron. The pin took hold of the world's mana, which hung where it hung and belonged to nobody, and a ship on a pin couldn't be slid off it by a gale. Pin the lattice to the world instead of the frame, and the frame would only have to hold the glass up.

And a big piece under the head rang, like a bell, which was half of why the long runs went slow. A stasis facet set to slow and not to stop would lay a hand on it. And if a run let go, the faster a piece flew, the harder a stasis field took it, which was more than the sand could say. Not in a dead zone. In a dead zone the stasis went out with everything else and the sand caught what fell, and that was what the sand was for.

He took the Forge book onto his knee and turned to the back, where nobody wrote, and headed a clean page THE BIG ROOM; and looked at it, and thought of Ferra's page with nearly the same heading years ago, and left it. Under it he wrote South Sentinel. Under the bay hill, in the rock. He drew a ring and wrote 40 across it and 60? up the side of it, and under that, three lines: Pin the lattice. Slow the ring. Catch the burst. And at the bottom, because the index would have to come from somewhere and he knew exactly where Ferra would start: Its own stone. Off the roll. Ask the Hearing. Don't take it.

Then one more line, for Tess, who'd have the key while he was gone: Cut the hall if Fletcher has hands to spare. Nothing goes in it till I'm home. E.

The hum stopped at twenty past one. He put the book back on its shelf and opened the door.

The fitting he did by hand, at the bench, with a file and a stone and a loupe, until a little before three. The Forge made parts. A pistol that ran was parts that had been made to agree with each other, and that was a hand's work, and he wanted it to have been his. He timed each cylinder to its frame, chamber by chamber, until it turned and locked up on every one of the six without a shake, and the crane swung out and back like a door on a good hinge. He set the triggers heavier than he'd have set anybody else's, a long smooth pull of about ten pounds with no stacking at the end of it, because the six were still stronger than their hands had learned and he'd rather nothing went off because Ferra was thinking about the accounts; and if there was time, the hammer could be thumbed back first for a short light pull, and if there wasn't, it didn't need to be. The walnut he shaped last. He didn't need a cast of their hands. He'd held both of them more than he'd held anything in his life, and he knew without looking where Ferra's thumb lay and where her long ink-stained fingers wanted to wrap, and that Imani's palm was a little wider and her grip came in lower and harder, the way she held a retractor. He shaped each grip to the hand he knew and checked it against nothing, and cut a fine chequer into it for a wet hand, and rubbed it dark with oil and lamp soot until the pew's yellow was gone and it didn't show in the dark.

On the left of each frame, small, under the cylinder, he stamped REVOLVER 9 MM. KALDO. and under it the number. He stood with the punch over the second one for some time before he struck it. Then he stamped it 1, the same as the first.

Before he went up he did the dies: a case die so that the long shop's press could draw a rimmed nine-millimetre case out of brass strip in the same four strokes it drew the rifle's, and a primer cup a size smaller than the rifle's for Tess's mixture. The powder was the rifle powder. The drawings went into the Forge's flat drawer in a sealed sleeve, with a line on the outside in his own hand: Revolver, 9 mm. For Bram's floor, from gauges, when we're home. Not before. Ward writes who carries one. E. Kaldo had pistols already. The team medics carried one, a light thing in a small round, signed out on Ward's card and handed back at the end of a tour. This was more than that, and it hid under a coat as easily, and he wasn't going to set a line running for it on Bram's floor four weeks before he sailed and leave it turning a thousand miles behind him without its rules.

He signed the run in the Forge book. 17 Mar. Two pieces, personal. Dies, two. Flask, a third. E. He knew who read the Forge book.



Ferra had read it by nine the next morning. He knew because there was a slip on his desk in her hand when he got up from the Works, with a figure on it for a third of a flask of liquid mana at the Board's own rate, and under it, Personal. Payable by E. What are they? And under that, smaller: Back page. Forty what?

He wrote under it, The butts behind the cart shed, half past five. Both of you. Boots., and sent it back. He didn't answer the second line. She'd know he hadn't, and she'd know it meant later.

They came at twenty to six, together, in the long yellow light, Ferra straight from the Polytechnic with the book under her arm and two pens through her bun, and Imani from the hospital with her sleeves still pinned up and her hair tied up for work. The butts were empty. He'd sent the range sergeant home at five with half a day he hadn't asked for, and locked the cart shed, and there was nobody on the long strip of trodden ground between the trestle and the earth bank but him and two straw men on posts and a great many crows. He had his sense open behind him, as he always did, out past the cart shed and the yard: nobody close.

On the trestle there were two flat cases in grey canvas that Abloh had sewn that morning, with a belt holster in each and a rig for under the arm, because Abloh hadn't known what they'd wear on a deck and hadn't wanted to be asked twice. He hadn't asked anything else. Beside the cases, two felt-lined trays. In each tray, the pistol. Beside the pistols, a row of short brass rounds in a wooden block, sixty to a block, that Fennick had loaded on the long shop's press between seven and noon and brought across in a covered basket like eggs. And four little steel rings with a knob on the back, two to a tray, each of which held six rounds by their rims in a circle so that a whole cylinder could be filled at once. He called them speedloaders, because that was what they were.

Ferra looked at the trestle for a long moment.

"That's from the Forge," she said.

"Yes."

"You said not one barrel."

"For the rifles."

"That's two barrels."

"It's two barrels," he agreed.

She opened the book on the end of the trestle and wrote something, and he didn't try to read it. Imani had come round the other side and was looking at the nearer one with her hands behind her back, the way she looked at an instrument tray she hadn't laid herself.

"Don't touch it yet," he said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

"I was," said Imani, "going to think about it."

He told them the rules first. There were four of them, from the other side, and he said so, which he could only ever say to these two, and only here, with the cart shed locked and the crows. Men had written each of them down after somebody died for want of it.

"It's always loaded. Even when you've just watched me empty it. Especially then." He held up a finger. "It never points at anything you aren't willing to put a hole in."

"That's nearly everything," said Imani.

"Then it points at the ground. Your finger stays off the trigger, along the side here, flat, until the sights are on what you mean to shoot. And you know what you're shooting at, and what's behind it." He nodded at the bank. "This goes through a man. Through him and through the wall behind him and into whoever's on the other side of the wall."

Imani said, after a moment, "Those are like ours."

"Which?"

"The ones you gave me for the wards. Wash your hands. Count the swabs in and count them out. Every one of those was somebody first."

"Every one," he said.

Then he told them why, which he'd meant to do more neatly than he did. The three hulls. Below. The radio that didn't go through water. What a dead zone did to the two of them, which they knew, and what it didn't, which they knew too but hadn't put next to the first thing. "I can't be on three ships," he said. "And I can't be on any of them from the bottom of the sea. I'd like you to have something in your hands."

Ferra was looking at him over the book. Imani was looking at the pistol.

"I spend my life closing holes in people," said Imani.

"I know."

"And you've made me something that opens them."

"For one person," he said, "on the worst day you'll have out there. So that on the next day you're standing up and closing everybody else's."

Imani thought about that for longer than he liked.

"That's a good answer," she said. "I hate it." And she held out her hand, palm up, the way she held it out for an instrument, and waited for him to put the pistol in it the right way round.

He showed them everything empty first, on the trestle, slowly, twice. How the cylinder swung out when you pushed the latch forward with your thumb, and how the rod under the barrel, slapped, threw all six cases out at once. How you looked into each of the six and counted them, and put your little finger in to be sure, because looking wasn't being sure. What the hammer looked like cocked and what it looked like down, and that it was never, ever carried cocked. Ferra picked hers up and weighed it on her flat palm without being asked, the way she weighed coin.

"One pound eleven," she said. "Empty."

He looked at his sheet. It said one pound eleven.

Then they loaded. He showed them how the rounds went into the chambers one at a time, and then with the speedloader, all six at once and a twist of the knob to let them go. Ferra did six without looking, by feel, counting under her breath, and stopped at six and shut the cylinder with the heel of her hand, and Imani did hers looking, slowly, the way she'd have counted swabs. He held one round up between them so they could see the base of it.

"That little cup in the middle. The pin hits it, and it fires the powder."

"What's it called?"

"The primer."

Imani looked at Ferra. Ferra didn't look up from the cylinder.

"That's two this week," said Ferra.

"Ilenne's was louder," said Imani.

"You haven't heard this one yet," he said, and gave them the ear cups off the nail.



Ferra went first, because she'd stepped up to the line first, and nobody argued about it.

He stood behind her. That was where you stood to teach it; there wasn't anywhere better. She came up to his chin and he had to bend his head, and the pens in her bun went past his chin and one of them caught him under the jaw, and she said "Sorry," without meaning it. He put his arms round her and his hands over hers on the grip and moved them where they ought to be: her strong hand high on the back of the grip, up into the web of her thumb until the steel sat against it; her other hand wrapped round the front to fill the gap her fingers left; both thumbs forward along the left side, laid there, pointing at the target, as though they were the ones that would do it. He moved her feet with his. He pressed on the small of her back with his body until she leaned in, her nose over her toes, her shoulders forward of her hips, so that whatever the pistol did it would do into her and not past her.

"Front sight," he said by her ear. "The blade at the end. That's the only thing that's sharp. The man's a blur. The notch at the back's a blur. The blade's sharp."

"I'm trying."

"Try harder."

"There's a distraction."

He looked down. There was nothing between his front and her back but two layers of cloth and no room at all, and he'd put it there himself, on purpose, for teaching.

"I can feel all of you," said Ferra, to the front sight.

"That's my belt."

"It isn't his belt," said Imani from the trestle, where she was sitting on the end of it in her ear cups with her chin on her fist, watching with enormous attention.

"There's big Emeric," said Ferra, and patted his forearm where it lay along hers. "And there's little Emeric."

"I would like it noted," he said, "that he isn't little."

"Noted."

"Not in the book."

"Front sight," said Ferra severely, to herself, and fired.

It was loud even in the cups, a hard flat crack that went up off the bank and put every crow in the field into the air at once, and a little spit of flame came out sideways at the front of the cylinder where he'd told her it would and she hadn't believed him. The muzzle came up an inch and settled. That was all. She'd been braced for a horse to kick her and a cat had pushed her.

Ferra lowered it a little and looked at the straw man. There was a hole in its chest, left of where she'd meant, a hand's width.

"That's all it does?"

"For you."

"What does it do for other people?"

"Ward would feel it," he said. "Most of the knights would. Anybody who hasn't eaten a stone would want both hands and a minute to think about it." He let go of her hands and stepped back half a pace, and left the other half. "Again. Take the trigger straight back, slowly. Don't decide when it goes. Let it surprise you."

She fired the rest of the cylinder at seven yards, and two more after it, slower than she wanted to, with him behind her moving her elbows a little after each shot and saying nothing else, and when the hammer fell on the last empty chamber she stood and looked at the straw man a while and then said, "Eighteen. One's off the left shoulder. The other seventeen are in a circle three and a quarter inches across, give or take an eighth, and it's going left."

"You're pushing it with your thumb."

"I'm not."

"You are. Leave it lying there. It doesn't have a job."

On the next cylinder he loaded for her, with his back turned, and put a dummy in one of the six chambers, a case with nothing in it and no primer, which he'd made for the purpose and didn't mention, and spun it shut so neither of them knew where it sat. On the fourth pull the hammer fell on nothing with a small dry click, and the muzzle of Ferra's pistol dipped two inches toward the ground all by itself, hard, the way a horse's head goes down when it expects the whip.

She stood very still.

"What was that."

"A flinch."

"I didn't do that."

"Your hand did."

"My hand," said Ferra, "did that without asking me." She was outraged. She was more outraged than he'd seen her in a month, more than at the Church. "Load it again."

He loaded it again. She flinched again, a little less. He loaded it a third time, and a fourth, and on the fifth cylinder the dummy came round and the hammer clicked and the front sight stayed exactly where it had been, on the straw man's chest, without moving a hair; and Ferra said, "Good," to herself, very quietly, the way Amara said it to a relay.



Imani came up to the line, and he stood behind her.

He gave her the same as he'd given Ferra. He'd counted. He didn't mean to count, but he'd stood behind Ferra for twenty minutes by the bell from the Polytechnic, and he stood behind Imani for twenty, and he'd known he would before he started. Imani knew it too. She leaned back into him exactly once, at the beginning, before he'd fixed her stance, with her whole weight, and turned her head a fraction.

"Hello, little Emeric," she said.

"Can we not name him at the range."

"You named the pistol."

"I named it what it is."

"So did she," said Imani, and he heard Ferra's pen going at the trestle.

Imani didn't flinch at all. He put a dummy in two of her cylinders, wherever his hand fell, and both times the hammer fell on nothing and her revolver didn't move, not by the width of the front sight, and she let the trigger out and pulled again on the next chamber, as though a round that didn't go off was a thing that happened on a table and you dealt with it. She shot like she cut. She was slower than Ferra and she didn't care. By her third cylinder her group at seven yards was so tight in the straw man's chest that it was one ragged hole you could have put an egg into, and Ferra came down off the trestle with the tape and measured it twice and wrote it down and didn't say what it was.

"What is it?" said Imani.

"Smaller than mine."

"How much smaller?"

"Smaller," said Ferra, and shut the book.

After that he made them work. Reloading: the latch, the cylinder out, the rod slapped and the empties left where they fell, the speedloader off the belt and into the chambers and twisted and the cylinder shut, until they did it without looking down. What to do when it didn't fire: nothing clever. Pull again; the next chamber was a new round. That was the whole of it, and he made them do it until it was in their hands and not in their heads. Three yards, from the holster and from a coat pocket, both hands. Fifteen yards, where the straw man got small and the front sight didn't. Ferra fired a hundred and twelve rounds and Imani a hundred and eight, and Ferra knew both numbers without having been told the second. Their hands were black at the fingertips and the light had gone orange and long across the field, and the crows had given up and gone to roost in the elms behind the bank, complaining.

He felt Ward before he saw him. A knight's density, coming through the yard from the gate at a walk, not hurrying and not hiding: Ward, who'd signed the powder out of his own store that morning without asking what a magnum was, and who'd been listening, Emeric suspected, to two hundred and twenty shots at the cart shed from his office window, counting them.

He came and stood at the end of the trestle with his hands behind his back and looked at the straw men, and then at the two pistols on the felt, and then at the stamp on the nearer frame.

"Who signed for these," he said.

"Nobody yet."

Ward nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he'd come for.

"Saturday," he said. "Eight o'clock. Here. I'll test you both, the way I test the medics, and if you pass I'll write your cards, and if you don't, you'll come back Monday." He looked at Ferra. "And you'll carry it on the deck. Every day. Not in your cabin in a drawer under the ledgers."

"I wasn't going to put it under the ledgers."

"You were," said Ward, and Imani laughed out loud, and Ferra looked at her.

"And him?" said Ferra. "Who tests him?"

"I've seen him shoot," said Ward, and went back across the yard the way he'd come.

They cleaned them at the trestle in the last of the light, the three of them, with the rods and the oil and the rags, because he wouldn't let them leave them dirty and they wouldn't let him do it for them. Ferra had the cylinder and crane off hers in the time it took him to show her once, and back on in less. Imani laid hers out on the felt in a row, cylinder, crane, rod, grips, frame, the way she laid out a tray, and put it back together in the same order backwards, and looked at it.

"There are two number ones," she said.

"Yes."

"There can't be two number ones," said Ferra, who kept the register.

"There are," he said, and went on oiling.

Ferra looked at him for a while. Then she opened the book on the trestle between the two pistols and wrote, and this time she turned it round when she'd finished, so they could both read it.

18 Mar. The butts. Two revolvers, 9 mm, the Forge (two barrels; E.: "two"). Both numbered 1. Entered 1 (F.) and 1 (I.). A third of a flask, charged to E., paid. Little E. present.

Imani took the pen out of her hand, and leaned over, and under it, in her own small doctor's hand, wrote:

Not little. I.

"That's the record now," said Ferra, and blotted it.



The word book went out on the Thursday, quietly, the way Ferra did anything she didn't want talked about.

There were fourteen copies, not counting her own. She'd had them made at the Polytechnic by the sworn copyists who did the school's books, in the copy room on the second floor that had a lock on the inside of the door: every page of her word book, the five columns, Kaldo and the word and her marks for the stops and the lifts and the clicks and the source and the colour, written out fair in a dozen hands, with the blank lines left blank. Not printed. The printer left a record in its formes and its waste, and the word book wasn't on any rung of the ladder that let a printer near it. Each copy had a number inside the cover in her own hand, and a line in her register beside it, the way the dose cards had.

Number one went in the K Amara's strong box for the queen, with Abloh's dress.

Number two she gave to Sela herself, at the school door at a quarter to eight, and Sela turned it over in her hands and opened it in the middle and looked at a column of words she couldn't read and marks she didn't understand, and said, "I'll not be able to teach this," and Ferra said, "Not yet," and Sela looked at her a moment and put it under her arm with the register and went in.

Number three went to Emberfell on the Thursday packet in a parcel with a letter, for Rowena. Ferra didn't say what was in the letter.

Numbers four and five went to the Motion Shop. Tess took hers on her stick at the bench and read the first page standing up, and turned to the counting, and then went very quiet over the night count's page with Ilenne's marks all the way to the turn; and Wren, at the end of the bench, opened hers to the back and found the blank lines and said, "What goes there?" and Ferra said, "Nothing, yet," and Wren said, "I'll leave them, then," and did.

Number six went to the dome, where Maeve had come up out of her stone on the Monday after a week under and was sitting on the gravel in a blanket eating a bowl of Maren's broth with the face of a woman who had woken in a stronger body than the one she'd lain down in and was trying very hard not to test it on the furniture. She put the broth down to take the book. She held it in both hands.

"I'll learn it on the deck," she said. "On the way. Mira says it's a month."

"Mira doesn't know anything about it," said Ferra.

"Mira knows everything about everything," said Maeve, and then heard herself, and went pink, and opened the book very quickly to the first page.

Seven to thirteen went to the knights: one in each hull's mess, in a locked box on the mess table's end, with the key on the mess sergeant's chain and a line in the register for each one. Ward signed for them. He signed for each separately, seven times, in his own hand, and Ferra watched him do it and didn't say anything, which was how she thanked Ward.

That left one.



Emeric took it to the box room himself, on the Friday, at six, when the papers were done.

He hadn't told Ferra he would. He'd asked her, on the Wednesday, whether the last one could be his to give, and she'd looked at him over the register for a while and then written his initial against the number in the margin, E. to give, and nothing else.

The box room door was open. Corbin was at the desk, with the lamp on and the box shut and a sheet of Cade's in front of him that he'd read and wasn't reading. He'd been up off his stone nine days. It showed, a little, the way it had shown on Zale and on Waylon: he sat straighter, and his colour was better, and he'd stopped holding the edge of the desk when he stood up. Under the desk, his left boot. No band. Emeric had stopped looking there, he found, about the Wednesday.

"Majesty."

Emeric put the book on the desk.

It was a plain thing, like the others: a copyist's ledger in a green board cover, a hand's width thick, with nothing on the outside at all. Corbin looked at it. He didn't pick it up.

"Ferra's," Emeric said. "Her word book. Ilenne's language, all of it Kaldo has. The counting. The colours, in signs. The courtesies. What we know of her mother's house, which is a door and a name we haven't got." He paused. "There are fourteen. One's for the queen. Sela has one, and Rowena, and Tess and Wren, and Maeve, and the knights. This is the last."

Corbin still didn't pick it up.

"Why," he said.

It wasn't quite a question. It was what he'd said at the Forge door in December, about Tess: Why would I ask her why? The same flatness. The same waiting to see where the trick was.

Emeric thought about how to answer. He'd had an answer ready; he'd had it ready since the Wednesday. It was a good one. It had the chair in it, and the box, and the year, and the held messages that would come up the net from a thousand miles out with words in them that only the book could read, and the fact that if anything went wrong at the door, the man in the chair in Kaldo would be the man who had to understand what had gone wrong. All of that was true.

"Because you're in it," he said instead. "Kaldo is. The whole of it. And you're holding it. If somebody comes up the net from the bottom of the sea with a word in it that matters, I'd like the man in the chair to be able to read it." He stopped. "And because a fortnight ago you sat here and an old man in grey offered you the world, and you said no, and you didn't know the door couldn't open. And the world's bigger than he told you. It's a great deal bigger. I'd like you to have the bit of it we've got."

Corbin looked at him for a long time across the desk.

Then he opened the cover.

Inside, in Ferra's hand, in the top corner: 14. C. By E. And under it, in a different ink, smaller, which Emeric hadn't known was there and hadn't asked for, the way she wrote things she wasn't ready to have read:

Your world is getting bigger. Mind the blank lines; they're hers. F.

Corbin read it twice. He didn't say anything. He turned the page, and the next, slowly, past Ferra's preface and her key to the marks and into the first column, and his finger went down it, a line at a time, the way a man reads a deed: Kaldo, word, marks, source, colour.

"This is a great deal of fish," he said at last.

"Some of it's fish."

"The rest?"

"Manners," said Emeric. "Mostly manners. And numbers."

Corbin nodded slowly. He turned another page. It was the counting, the reckoning shell's marks in Ferra's careful copy, and on the facing page the night count, the first seven in Ilenne's hand from August and then the line, and then the rest in her hand from the wall water, all the way to the turn.

He looked at it for a while.

"Is that hers?" he said. "The writing?"

"The copyist did it from hers."

"What is it?"

Emeric told him. The counting her mother counted her to sleep with. That she'd sent the first seven to the wall in August so her mother would know her, and had stopped in the middle, like sleep. That at the wall water a fortnight back her mother had counted the rest back, all the way to the end, so she'd know she'd been heard.

Corbin listened to all of it without looking up from the page.

"She stopped in the middle," he said.

"Yes."

"On purpose. So only her mother could finish it."

"Yes."

Corbin was quiet for a while. Outside, in the inner ward, a tram went by with its bell, and somewhere down the corridor a clerk laughed at something and shut a door.

"I'll learn it at night," said Corbin. He closed the book, and put his hand flat on the green cover, and left it there. "Thank you."

He didn't say Majesty. Emeric noticed that on the stair afterwards, going down, and didn't know what to do with it, and kept it.

He went past the box room again at eleven on his way up from the Works. The door was open. He hadn't expected it to be. The lamp was on, turned low, and his brother was at the desk with his coat off and his sleeves rolled up and the green book open in front of him, and his lips were moving, very slightly, over the counting, the way a boy's do over a lesson he means to have by morning.

Emeric didn't go in. He stood in the dark of the corridor a moment and looked at the open door, and the light coming out of it across the boards; and then he went on up, quietly, and left it open.
