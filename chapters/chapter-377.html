Chapter 377 - Frames

It was the cable that gave him the idea, in the end, and the cable was Tess's fault.

She had said it in October on the Works floor with the first good thread of Orrin's glass lit at one end and a bell ringing at the other, a mile away down the length of the shop: that the light went in and bounced along inside the glass and came out the other end as if the glass weren't there. And that night on the Well's deck, with BR-01 three miles off in the dark and its stones singing faintly to him across the empty air, he'd thought: the Band holds mana the way the glass holds light. And put it down, carefully, with the other things that might be hot.

He picked it up again on the twenty-seventh of December, a Saturday, two days after the Long Table, at the Well, at a little after nine at night.

"You're going to do something," said Tess.

She was sitting on an upturned crate in the Well's lab with her stick across her knees and her collar turned up, because the lab was warm by the Well's standards and cold by anybody else's. Orla Garran was at the drum rail outside the lab's glass with a lamp. Ambrose was on the line from the line room five miles below, his voice coming small out of the brass speaker on the bench, reading BR-01's last beat: pin holding, stones full, nothing on the boom.

"I'm going to try something."

"That's worse." She looked at him. "What?"

He told her. It didn't take long. A golem could be ridden. He'd known that since the cave; he'd done it in the dome with the sentries. You went into its stone the way you'd go into a person, and the stone took you, and the body did what you meant. A relay's decider was a control stone. The Well had one; Tess had put it in the Well's own drum on the deck in September, so the Well could hear the chain. BR-01 had one, three miles off in the Band, in the same thin air, with nothing between them but the Band.

"You want to ride a dustbin," said Tess.

"I want to see if I can go from that stone to that one. Through the air. Not down the radio." He nodded at the drum on the deck outside the glass. "And come back."

"And if you can't come back?"

"Then I'll be in a drum five miles over the bay, and you'll have to talk to me in Morse."

"That's not funny."

"No," he agreed.

She looked at him a while longer. Then she reached over and turned the speaker's knob up, and said, "Ambrose. Write down the time," and Ambrose said, from five miles down, "Nine fourteen. What am I writing it down for?" and Tess said, "Him," and Ambrose said, "Ah," in the voice of a man reaching for a second pencil.



He sat down on the deck with his back against the Well's drum and put his palm flat on the drum's grey plate, over the place where its decider sat, and shut his eyes.

The Well's stone was easy. He'd written half of it. He went into it the way you go into your own house in the dark: the hall, the stair, the rooms where everything was where he'd left it, the little sentences Tess had written in it about listening for the second neighbour and checking yourself before you accused anybody. He stood in it for a moment. Then he did the thing he'd been thinking about since October.

He reached out of it.

Not down the wire, not into the radio's voice. Out, into the air, the way you'd put your hand out of a window. And the Band was there.

He had flown in it a hundred times. He had felt it every time: the density, the weight of it, the way mana lay up here thicker than in any dome, as if the air itself were trying to be water. He'd never put his hand into it from inside a stone. From inside a stone it wasn't weight. It was a road.

It went out in front of him in every direction, and it carried. That was the only word. He put a little of himself out into it, the smallest piece, the way you'd let a thread go into a current to see which way the water ran, and the thread went, at once, and didn't thin, and didn't fade, and three miles off, out over the black bay, he felt it touch something.

BR-01's decider.

He knew it the way you know your own handwriting on an envelope. Tess's keywork. His own lines under hers. And he went.

There was no between. That was the first thing he noticed, and the thing he wrote down first afterwards, on the back of Ambrose's log sheet in the lab, with his hand not quite steady. He'd expected a crossing. A dark, or a pull, or the slow time of a delve stretched out over three miles of empty air. There wasn't one. He was in the Well's stone, and then he was in BR-01's, as if he'd stepped through a door from one room into the next, and the door had been three miles wide.

And BR-01 had an eye.

Ivo's mirror, looking down through its own thick window beside the pin. He'd built the drum with it and never looked through it himself; there'd been no way to. There was now. He found the place in the decider where the mirror's sense stone fed in, and opened it, and looked.

Kaldo was under him.

Five miles down and three across, at night, in winter, the whole of it: the long curve of the bay, black, with the lamp on the end of the sea wall a single point; the two towers' caps, one on the wall root and one on the point, glowing low and pale; the Harbour Board's tower with its windows lit; the coal quay with the dark hulls along it and a lantern moving on escort three's deck. The upper ward's lamps in their lines up the hill, and Gate Avenue, and the castle, and the dome beside it with the tree's green light coming up faintly through the glass. The east quay, where Leask's was a black roofless shape with a single watchman's brazier in its yard. The shoal on the north side of the narrows, a pale curve of sand under the water, quite clean.

It was very sharp. Sharper than he'd thought. He could see the watchman at the brazier, a dark shape with its hands out to the fire. He could see, if he looked, that the watchman had a dog.

He didn't look at anything else. He made himself not. He looked at the bay, and the towers, and the sea wall, and then he came home.

Coming home was the same. No between. He was in BR-01, and he was in the Well, and he was on the deck with his back against the drum and his palm on the cold plate and his eyes open, and Tess was standing over him with her stick in one hand and his wrist in the other, counting.

"Eleven seconds," she said. "You were gone eleven seconds."

"It felt like a minute."

"It was eleven seconds. Ambrose has it." She let go of his wrist. "Your pulse didn't change. Your breathing didn't change. You sat there like a man asleep." She looked down at him. "Where did you go?"

"BR-01."

"You went into it."

"I went into it. I looked through Ivo's mirror." He got up, slowly, with his hand on the drum, because his legs had forgotten for a moment that they were his. "I could see the watchman at Leask's. I could see he had a dog."

Tess didn't say anything for a while. Orla Garran had come in from the rail with her lamp and was standing in the lab's doorway, looking at him the way she looked at a cloud bank she didn't like the colour of.

"How long would it take," said Tess at last, "to get from here to the next one? If there were a next one. A hundred and fifty miles out."

"I don't know. The same, I think." He went into the lab and sat down at the bench and pulled Ambrose's log sheet toward him and turned it over and found a pencil. "There wasn't a between. It wasn't like a delve. It was like the glass. Your glass. The light goes in at one end and it's at the other end and the glass isn't there." He wrote it down while it was fresh. 27 Dec. Well to BR-01 and back, 11 s. The Band acts like the fibre. Near enough no delay. He looked at it. "The Band's a cable. For this. For a mind. We've been flying through it for six years and it's been a cable the whole time."

"You should have thought of it the first time you moved a sentry," said Tess.

"I know."

"I'm just saying."

"I know you're just saying. I'm saying it too." He put the pencil down. And then, because he'd promised himself on the way up the ladder that if it worked he'd say this part out loud straight away, the way he'd said the heartbeat's faults out loud in September before anybody could build them in: "It can go wrong. Two ways I can see, and there'll be more."

Tess sat back down on her crate and waited.

"If the relay dies while I'm in it," he said. "If something hits it, or a dead zone opens under it, or the pin goes and it falls. I don't know where I end up. Maybe home. Maybe nowhere." He turned the pencil over. "And while I'm gone, I'm not here. That body's sitting on the deck against the drum, and anybody who wanted to could walk up to it and put a knife in it, and I'd not know till I tried to come back and there was nothing to come back to."

"So you'd never do it anywhere but here," said Orla, from the door.

"Here, or somewhere just as high, with somebody I trust sitting by me." He looked at Tess. "Both ends have to be up here. In the Band. It doesn't go through thin air, I don't think. It needs the road."

"So you can't do it from the ground."

"No."

"And you can't do it from the bottom of the sea."

"Certainly not from the bottom of the sea."

Tess was quiet. She was looking at the drum outside the glass, at the Well's own decider in it, with the expression she wore over a drawing when she'd found where it was going to break.

"Anybody," she said. "Anybody who can delve."

"What?"

"You went into the Well's stone and out into the air and into BR-01's stone, and BR-01's stone let you." She tapped her stick on the deck. "Why did it let you?"

"Because it's my keywork. It knew me."

"It knew a delver. Did it check it was you?" She didn't wait. "It didn't. I didn't build it to. I built it to listen for a beat and a second neighbour, not for who was knocking. Nobody knocks on a relay." She looked up at him. "Anybody who can delve, and gets to the Band, and gets their hand on any stone with a road to one of ours. They could do what you've just done. They could go in. They could look through Ivo's mirror at the dog." Her voice went flat. "And the Church has delvers. And stones. And hulls that go up there."

He'd been going to say it. She'd got there first, which was usual.

"Then we lock it," he said.



The lock took her nine days and three of Vesk's, and it was the note plate that made it possible at all.

In October, on the Works floor, Paskin had pressed Emeric's palm and Tess's onto a foot-square glass plate of water-stone dust and read the figures that came up on it, and the figures had been different, and they'd been the same every time. Emeric's dense and branching, running off past the rim. Tess's fine and regular, like a ruled snowflake. Everybody had a note; Armand had said so for sixty years and the six had felt it for ten. Nobody had ever drawn one before. And a thing you could draw, Tess said, you could cut.

So she cut two.

She took Emeric's figure off Paskin's sheet and his mother's, which Paskin took on the second of January in the dome with Amara's made palm on the plate's rim and her own sleeping body's hand laid beside it by Imani, to be sure the note was hers and not the made body's; and the two figures came up the same, which settled a question nobody had quite liked to ask. Tess cut both notes into the keywork of every decider: the Well's, BR-01's, the eleven cut and waiting on her shelf in the Motion Shop for the drums that would carry them, the four ground relays'. Two notes, and only two. Anybody could send a message down the chain; the radio didn't care who you were, and the radio was the relays' whole job. But only those two notes could go into a decider. Only those two could travel.

And anything else that tried to go in, she gave the decider something to do about it.

"It shuts," she said, on the ninth day, in the Motion Shop, with one of the eleven deciders on the bench in front of her on a cloth and a lens in her eye. "Any note that isn't one of the two, the moment it touches the stone. The decider shuts its door. The radio goes quiet. The pin holds wherever it is. And it sends one word up the chain before it goes, the way it would if something hit it, so the neighbours shout." She took the lens out. "From outside, it looks like a dead one. Exactly like a dead one. Somebody knocks on it and it dies on them."

"And if it's me knocking, and something's wrong with my note?"

"Then it dies on you too, and we send a hull." She put the lens down. "I'm not building a lock with a back door, Emeric. Not for you. Especially not for you." She looked at the decider on its cloth. "Armand says they'll still be able to feel where it is. The stones sing. A lock doesn't stop that."

"I know."

"It stops them getting in. It doesn't stop them finding it."

"I know," he said again. "That's enough for now."

"It'll have to be," said Tess, and put the decider back in its crate.



The frames had gone to work on the Monday after the Long Table, on the twenty-ninth, and by the time the lock was finished nobody in the lower ward looked up when one went past.

There were six of them, the first six off Tess's floor and Merrin's and Dov's, and four kinds, because Ward had said in the summer that a frame that could only fight was a frame that would sit in a shed eleven months of the year and rust.

The labour frame went to the east quay first, to Leask's. It was the tallest of the four kinds, a crane on two legs, eight feet at the shoulder and broad as a cart, with a seat in its chest behind a glass and a harness of levers for a man who couldn't delve and a stone in its back for a man who could. It walked down Quay Street at seven on the Monday morning with Cade's foreman in the seat, very slowly, with its long arms folded, and half the lower ward walked down Quay Street behind it. At Leask's it unfolded its arms and put them into the black shell of the warehouse and lifted out a roof beam twenty feet long that had taken four men to put up and would have taken eight to bring down, and laid it on the quay, and went back for another. The foreman worked it on the levers all day, badly, and by the afternoon not so badly, and by the Wednesday the warehouse was a clean brick box with no roof and the beams were in a stack on the quay and the crowd had gone home.

The rescue frame went to Osric. It was shorter and wider, with a pair of hydraulic arms that could lift a fallen beam off a man without crushing what was under it, and a tank on its back and a monitor on its shoulder, and Osric took it round every station in the town in turn over the first week, and showed every crew how to sit in it, and how to get out of it in a hurry, and made Tolliver clip the seat harness on four times in front of the lower ward's crew before he'd let him drive it.

The mine frame went to Grayrock. It was the strangest, low and narrow and long, built to go on its knees down a gallery four feet high, with lamps on its shoulders and a prop-setter in its arms; and it went up the line on the second of January on a flatcar under a tarpaulin with Hask riding beside it, who had been the old Grayrock captain and ran the mine's safety now, and who had come down to Kaldo to fetch it himself and sat on the flatcar the whole way with one hand on the tarpaulin as if it might get off.

And the guard frames, three of them, went to the walls. Those were the ones people had expected. Taller than a man by half again, plated in Bram's dark steel with Kaldo's mark on the breast, with the long arms and the short legs of a thing built to stand on a wall-walk and not fall off, and a knight in each. The Crestless had them. Ward had said that in the summer too: the frame was a knight's, not a soldier's, and the knights who'd come up without a crest were the ones who'd been taught from the first to think of armour as a thing you were inside of, not a thing you wore. Three young knights of the line that Arthur and Mira and Bren had started, the ones who'd stay home when Arthur and Mira and Bren went. They rode them. They went into the frames' stones the way the six had taught them, and the frames moved the way they moved, and on the first morning one of them walked his frame along the wall-walk over the South Gate to the end and back with a tray of tea in its hand for the gate watch, and didn't spill any.

The children stared for about a week. Then they stopped. By the middle of January a guard frame could stand on the wall over the market with the pigeons on its shoulders and the market didn't look up.



Ward asked it at the long table on the second of January, the Friday, with the year's first sheets in front of everybody and Corbin at the foot of the table for the first time, in the chair Ferra had put there for him, with his watcher outside the door.

"Why four?" said Ward.

Everybody knew what he meant. Everybody had been asked it. Bram had been asked it by a carter on Quay Street on the Monday, watching the labour frame lift Leask's beams out: if Kaldo could build a thing like that, why were there only four of the big ones, the sentries, the ones that walked on their own? The town had seen the four. It had seen them at the dome's doors, very large and very patient, letting the pigeons walk on their feet; it had seen them go off on the Cradle's lighter to South Sentinel in the summer with the guard on the mermaid. And it had seen them come back last week, and go to their places, and it wanted to know where the rest were.

"Because every one of them has a stone in its head," said Ferra. She didn't look up from her sheet. "A control stone. Its own. That's what makes it walk on its own. It decides. Not much, and not well, but it decides, and to decide you need one." She turned a page. "A frame doesn't. A frame has a man in it. The man decides. The frame does what it's told by the levers or the man's delve, and the stones in it are grown stones from the press that do one thing each." She looked up at last. "So a frame costs Tess and Bram a month. A sentry costs a month and a seed."

"There are seeds," said Ward. "The tree's had two drops since the long sleep."

"There are seeds," said Ferra. "And every seed in a sentry's head is a tree not in the ground at Grayrock, and a stone not on the Board's book for a ropemaker's daughter's eyes." She put her pen down. "I can give you a fifth sentry tomorrow. Write me the name of the person who doesn't get one, and the parish that doesn't get a tree, and I'll put it in the book under yours."

Ward looked at her for a while. Then he nodded, slowly, the way he'd nodded at Imani's cards in September.

"Four, then," he said.

"Four. At the four doors that matter." Ferra ticked them off on the sheet. "The dome. The Forge's door, for Tess. The Well's hangar. The line room. Back where they were before the summer, except the Well, which gets the one that stood at the pass-box." She looked down the table at Corbin. "And when the king's gone, those four are yours to know about and nobody else's to move."

"I'll not move them," said Corbin.

"I know you won't. I'm saying it so it's in the book."

Corbin nodded and wrote something on his own sheet, in his small careful hand, and Emeric watched him write it, across the long table, and thought of a band of steel with gold leaves on it under the table's edge, and looked away.

And then, because it was the golem question and there would never be a better room for it, Emeric asked for a fifth.



He told them about the Well first. The ride, the eleven seconds, the Band like Tess's glass. The lock, which Tess had nearly finished. He told them both ends had to be in the Band, and why. And then he told them what he wanted, which was a body at the Well.

"To ride into," he said. "When I'm out there. If I'm at Band height a thousand miles off and something's wrong at home, and I can get my body up into the Band and park it somewhere safe with somebody sitting by it, I can come back along the chain to the Well. And at the Well I'll need something to come back into. A frame's no good; a frame's levers and a man's delve, and it's clumsy to ride, and its hands are spades." He looked at Tess. "A sentry rides better. But a sentry's cut for a sentry. Seven feet and violet in the slot and a voice like a coal chute. I can drive it, but I can't do anything fine in it. I can't hold a pen."

"So what do you want?" said Saban.

"A ride body cut to my own measure. My reach. My weight. My mana, as near as Tess can lay it, so the stone in its head knows where everything is the way mine does." He kept his voice even. "It rides best that way. The nearer it is to the body you've got, the less it costs to wear. I've ridden the sentries. I know."

"To your own measure," said Saban.

"Yes."

"Your height. Your shoulders. Your hands."

"Yes."

"Your face?" said Saban.

The room was very quiet.

"It'll look like me," said Emeric. "It would, cut that close. I'm not going to make it not."

Saban turned his hat round once in his hands on the table and looked at it.

"That's a decoy," he said. "Whatever you're calling it. A thing at the Well with the king's face that can walk about and talk while the king's a thousand miles off." He didn't sound disapproving. He sounded like a man who has been given a key and is working out which doors it opens. "You know what that's worth. To us. To anybody who thinks they know where you are."

"I know what it's worth."

"Good," said Saban. "So long as we both do."

It was Ferra who asked the question. He'd known it would be, and he'd known roughly what it would be, and he'd still not got an answer ready that he liked.

"Whose stone is it?" she said.

He looked at her.

"Before the wedding," said Ferra, "you came to this table and said you'd take two stones off the tree's crop for your mother's body, the way you'd take them for a sentry, and I said no. I said it was a body for a person, and a person's stone goes through the Board." She had her pen in her hand and wasn't writing. "So which is this? Is it a sentry, that walks on its own, and gets a sentry's stone off the tree, at this table? Or is it a body for a person? Because if it's a body for a person, the person is you, and you're the crown, and the Board can't give the crown a stone. I wrote that, as a line on its own, so nobody could forget it." She put the pen down. "Never the crown."

Nobody said anything. Corbin, at the foot of the table, had stopped writing.

"It's a sentry," said Emeric.

"Is it?"

"It walks on its own. When I'm not in it, it stands at the Well and does what a sentry does: watches the hangar and lets nobody in who isn't on its list." He held her eyes. "Nobody lives in it. My mother lives in hers. She sleeps in her own body and wakes in the made one and it's hers, all day, every day. Nobody's going to wake up in this one. I'll be in it for an hour, if I'm ever in it at all, and then I'll go home along the chain and it'll be a sentry again." He was quiet a moment. "It's a sentry with my face. That's all it is."

Ferra looked at him for a long time.

"Then it's sentry five," she said. "And it costs a sentry. And there's still no fifth for the walls, Ward, because this is it." She picked up her pen and wrote, and turned the book round so they could all see. 2 Jan. Sentry five, at the Well. A tree's seed, at this table, not the Board's. Wears his face. F. She looked at it. "That's what it costs. A seed and a tree. And the next time somebody at this table asks for a body with a face, they'll read that line first."

"Fair," said Emeric.

"It's not fair," said Ferra. "It's written down. That's different."



Zale swallowed his stone on the fifth, the Monday, in the dome, beside Fletcher, who swallowed his in the same hour because Imani had said she'd sooner sit up with two than with one twice.

They lay on two of the boards on the tree's north side in their shirtsleeves with the cuffs on their wrists, and the dome was filled to the brim, every breath of it heavier than air had any right to be, and Fletcher said, before he swallowed, that he'd never in his life lain down in the daytime and didn't hold with it. Zale didn't say anything. He looked up through the tree's branches at the glass and the winter sky beyond it for a long moment, with the small grey stone in his fingers, and then he looked at Ilenne, who was sitting on the gravel at the foot of his board with her knees drawn up and her hood down, because there was nobody in the dome to see.

"Go on," she said. "I wait."

He swallowed it.

Imani sat down between the two boards with her book and her watch, and Ilenne didn't move from the gravel, and the tree hummed, and Emeric left them there.



The Sand Room was the last thing, and the one he liked least.

Tess and Ambrose built it under the line room, in the cellar the line room's batteries had moved out of in the autumn, through the second week of January. It was the light sand table again: the case of stacked clear plates that the war had been planned round, that lit itself inside to draw ground and water and ships in the air. But bigger. Much bigger. A table the size of the cellar floor, with the whole of Kaldo laid out in it from the dome to the shoal, every street, every roof, the walls and the towers and the yard; and under the town, under every ward, in housings Bram had set into the walls and the lamp posts and the tram shelters all through the autumn, sense stones. Small ones. Grown ones, from the press, one job each: to feel what was near them and say so down Tess's glass to the cellar.

And in the table, what they felt came up as light.

Every card in the town was a small steady point, white, with its note on it, so that a delver standing at the table could have read who it was. Every knight on the wall was a point. The frames were brighter points with the knights in them. The towers' caps glowed where the towers stood. The hulls at the coal quay were long soft shapes full of the faint hum of cold stones. And anything that had a note the town didn't know, a visitor without a card, a sailor off a foreign ship, came up red.

"It's the exception," Emeric said, in the cellar, on the fourteenth, to the six, with the table lit in front of them and the whole of Kaldo turning slowly in it like something under water. He made himself say it. "Every machine in this town has a mundane floor. Every one. If the stones die, it still works, worse. This doesn't. Not yet. It's stones all the way down until there are cameras, and there aren't cameras, and there won't be for years." He looked at the housings Bram had drawn on the wall, each with a space beside the stone. "Bram's built every housing with room in it for a camera later. But today, if a dead zone opens over the lower ward, the lower ward goes dark on this table, and that's all."

"And there's the other thing," said Saban.

"There's the other thing."

He showed them. He had Tess bring one of Arthur's MK3 stealth plates down from the Works, the kind that hid a knight from being felt; and he had Bren, who was in the cellar because Bren was going to be on the relay team and Ward wanted him to see it, put the plate on and walk across the upper ward under one of the sense stones. And on the table, where Bren should have been a point, there was nothing. And round the nothing, the town's own faint mana, the hum of the lamp stones and the trams and the people, lay in the table's light the way it always did; except that in the middle of it, walking slowly across Gate Avenue, there was a hole. A hole the shape of a man.

"It doesn't see him," said Emeric. "It sees where he isn't." He watched the hole cross the avenue and go into the coffee house under the arch. "The stealth plate stops him being felt. It doesn't stop him being in the way. Anybody who builds one of these will be able to see our stealth suits walking about like holes in the snow." He looked at the six. "So nobody builds one of these. The method stays in this room. That's the first rule."

"And the second," said Ferra.

She'd been against it. She'd been against it since it went on his list in June, and so had Imani, and both of them had said so at the long table in plain words: that it was a room that could see every person in the town wherever they went, by their own cards, which the town had taken on the promise that the cards were for the gates and the bank. And both of them had come round, slowly, the way you came round to a thing you hated but couldn't argue with, because the fleet was going in April and the Church was rebuilding hulls nobody could see and the town would be very lightly held. They couldn't stop it. So they had written the terms.

Ferra read them now, in the cellar, off a single sheet, to the six and to Tess and Ambrose and Bren, in the voice she used for a will.

"Who watches. Two at a time, never one. The officer of the line room's watch and a second off the wall roll, drawn the way Saban draws the Board's. Never the same pair two nights running. Nobody else in the room." She turned the sheet. "When. When the line room's bell has gone, or the Board's, or a tower has answered, or the wall roll's captain has written down why. Not to see who's in the coffee house. Not to see where somebody's wife is." She turned it again. "What's logged. Everything. Every time the table's lit, who lit it, who was in the room, what they looked at and for how long. In a book that stays in this room and that the Assembly's clerk reads once a month and signs."

"And the register," said Imani.

"And the register." Ferra looked at her.

"The ward's register never goes on this table," said Imani. "Not a name, not a note, not a bed. The twins' sheet, the healers' finding on a ropemaker's daughter's eyes, the women who come to me about the things women come to me about. None of it. If a patient's in my ward, they're not on this table, they're a blank, and the blank's not marked." She looked at the slowly turning town in the plates. "I'd sooner the Church found my ward on a map than that this room did."

"Written," said Ferra, and wrote it, at the bottom of the sheet, in her own hand.

Emeric looked at the table. At the town in it, every light in it a person, going about a winter evening. A cluster of white points round a long table in a lane off the lower ward, where somebody's birthday was being kept. Three points on the wall over the South Gate, and a brighter one, where a guard frame stood. A single point on the keep's second floor, quite still, in a room where a man sat writing. In the dome, beside the tree, two still points on two boards, and a third on the gravel by one of them, red, because the table had never been told her name, that hadn't moved in nine days. And under the line room, here, in the cellar, ten points round a table, looking at the rest.

He didn't like it. He'd not liked it in June and he didn't like it now. It was a good room. It was going to keep people alive. He thought about Saban's lighthouse, and Armand's stones that sang whether they were talking or not, and a hole in the shape of a man crossing Gate Avenue.

"Put the lights out," he said.

Ambrose put them out. The town went dark in the plates, all at once, the way a town goes dark in a power cut, and for a moment, in the cellar, nobody could see anybody.



The decoy wasn't finished until the end of the month. He went up to the Well to see it on the twenty-ninth, alone, on the Hod's run, and Orla Garran let him into the hangar past sentry four, which turned its violet slot to watch him go by and let him, because he was on its list.

It was standing at the back of the hangar on a cradle, the way Amara's had stood in the Motion Shop for most of a year: unfinished, the skin not on the left arm yet, the knit showing under the open seams at the neck. Merrin had come up with it to fit the last of the drives and had gone back down that morning. Its stone was in, a pale grey seed from the tree's last drop, small, in the head. Its eyes were shut.

He stood in front of it for a long time.

It was his height. Exactly. He'd known it would be; he'd stood for the measuring himself, in his shirt in the Motion Shop, with Dov reading the tape and Ottoline writing and Tess telling them both to hurry up. It had his shoulders, and his hands, which Dov had cast from his own in Tess's mould in the same way Ottoline had cast his mother's. It had locs, Abloh's, rope-yarn dyed and waxed and bound in gold paper cuffs that Abloh had said he'd replace with real ones when he had the time, so that from the back, in the hangar's dim, it could have been him standing there in his own shadow.

From the front it was a little worse. The face was right. That was the trouble. Ottoline had had Ivo's photographs to work from, and him sitting for her for three afternoons, and the face was right in every line, and empty. A face with nobody behind it. He'd seen it in a mirror once or twice in his life, on very bad mornings, and hadn't liked it then either.

"You'll do," he said to it.

It didn't answer. It couldn't yet. He reached up and put two fingers on the forehead, over the stone, cool silicone over the duralumin, and felt it there inside, small and blank and waiting, the way a seed waits; a tree that would never be planted at Grayrock, in a head with his face on it.

Sentry five, Ferra had written. Wears his face.

He took his fingers away, and went back out past sentry four, and down.
