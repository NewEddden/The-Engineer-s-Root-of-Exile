Chapter 233 - Count of Ninety

The flasks got their number on the twenty-fourth of August, at fifty feet over the lake, under the full moon, because Ned would not do it in the dark.

"If she drops," he said, on the reed bank, "I want to see what she drops into." He looked at the lake. "Water's water. I'd sooner it was water I could see."

So they waited for the moon, and did it at midnight on the Monday, with the works asleep and the lake white and still and nobody on the far shore at all; and Ward on the bank with a coil of line and a boat; and Ferra beside him with the crew book and her own.

Ned took her up to fifty feet on the bank and held her there.

"Bank off," said Emeric.

Ned put the bank's lever back. The rail went quiet. Emeric felt it go: the long steady drink from the racks into the rail and the rail into the lift stones stopped, and the six lift stones under the deck, holding forty feet of iron and four men and a hold of charged stone off the lake, began to spend their own, which was very little; they had been made to be fed, not to carry.

"Reserve's on," said Ned. "On its own. As it should."

"Reserve off."

Ned looked at him. Then he put the reserve's lever back too, slowly, the way a man takes his hand off the rope of a bell he does not trust; and the reserve went quiet, and the lift stones were on their own, holding the Hod off the lake with nothing behind them but what they held.

She began to sink. Not fast. A hand's breadth, and another, the lake coming up toward her flat bottom a little at a time, as a boat settles when a man steps into it.

"First flask," said Emeric.

Tobe was down the ladder under the open trap with the flasks at his knees. He took hold of the first.

He opened the valve a full turn, as they had been told, and the flask let go.

Emeric felt it go into the rail: not a drink, a flood, all at once, the pressure of four miles of sky squeezed into an iron bottle rushing out of it through the pipe into the rail and down the rail into the lift stones, faster than they could take it, faster than anything could take it, so that the lift stones drank all they could hold in the first breath and the rest went past them and through the rail's sleeve and out, into the air round the hull, and was gone. He felt it go past. It was like pouring a bucket of water into a cup.

The Hod stopped sinking. She came up, a foot, two, and hung.

"Count," said Emeric.

He counted, aloud, slowly, the count Ward used for the rail in the yard. Everybody in the house counted with him under their breath, and on the bank, he could see, Ferra's lips were moving. Thirty. The lift stones holding, full from the rush, spending. Fifty. Sixty. The flask's gauge on its neck had gone from the red mark to nothing in the first breath and lay against its pin. Seventy. The lift stones beginning to empty, he could feel it, the six small bright points under the deck dimming. Eighty.

At ninety she began to sink again.

"Bank on," said Emeric, and Ned had it on before he had finished saying it, and the rail filled, and she steadied, and came back up to fifty feet, and hung there over the moonlit lake as if nothing had happened.

Nobody said anything for a breath.

"Ninety," said Ned.

"About ninety."

"That bottle's a tier of the racks. You said. A whole tier." Ned looked down through the trap at the empty flask in its felt coat. "And it bought us a count of ninety."

"Because up there it can't be let out slowly." Emeric knelt at the trap and reached down and put his hand on the flask's neck. "In the cellar, warm, I can crack a valve a hair and let it bleed. At four miles, in that cold, a valve opened a hair freezes in its seat from what goes through it; the spindle ices, and then you've a valve that'll neither open nor shut. So the drill's a full turn. And a full turn, it all comes at once, under pressure, and the stones take what they can in the first breath and the rest goes past them. You saw. You felt the floor go." He took his hand back. "Most of what's in it goes into the sky. A tier of the racks, and we keep a count of ninety out of it."

"Then make it let out slow."

"It wants a valve that lets it out a little at a time, however hard it's pushing, and doesn't freeze doing it. A valve that knows how hard it's being pushed." He thought of the forge, and the valve seats on the catch tray, true to a hair. "I can't make that yet. Not one that works at four miles in the cold. Not this year."

Ned looked at the flask a while longer.

"Then it's ninety," he said.

They did the other three, one after another, at fifty feet over the lake, bank off, reserve off, flask open, count. Eighty-seven. Ninety-two. Ninety. Every time the same flood into the rail, the same rush past it into the air, the same slow sinking at the end; and every time Ned had the bank on before Emeric finished saying it.

Then Ned brought her down onto the water by the reeds, and stood at his helm post with his hand on the lever, and turned round, and looked at Tobe and Cade in the house behind him.

"Say it," he said.

Tobe looked at Cade.

"The flasks," said Ned. "What they buy. Say it back to me."

"Ninety," said Tobe.

"Ninety what?"

"A count of ninety. Each."

"How many?"

"Four."

"Cade."

"A count of ninety each," said Cade. "Four flasks. Bank off, reserve off, flask. Then the next flask. Three hundred and sixty, near enough, all four." He thought. "Six minutes."

"Six minutes," said Ned. "From four miles." He looked at both of them. "That's not enough to get down. You know that."

"We know that."

"Then you know what the flasks are for." Ned turned back to his helm post. "They're for six minutes to think in. That's all. Six minutes to do the next thing, whatever it is." He put the lever to rest. "Every man who comes on this boat says it back to me before he steps on her. Every time. Ninety, four, six minutes. I'll not have a man on my boat who's got to work it out at four miles at the bottom of a ladder in the dark."

On the bank, Ferra had her book open. Not the crew book; her own. She was writing in the back of it, where the columns were that were nobody's business: under A. ALOFT, a new heading, small, THE HOD, and under it, four figures, 90, 87, 92, 90, and the date; and Emeric, coming up the bank past her, saw it over her shoulder before she closed it.

She had a column for every flight the Hod had made since the launch. The date and the hour up and the hour down, the height, the crew, in the small square hand, nine lines of it already; nobody had asked her for any of it. She was counting the Hod's flights the way she had once counted his lamps, in the first years, without being asked, and he had not known about the lamps until she showed him the page.

She saw him see it, and shut the book, and did not say anything, and her ears went pink.

"Ninety," she said, to the lake.



The Hod went up twice a week after that, and came down with her racks full; and the racks went to the works.

That was the thing that showed. Nobody in Kaldo outside the long table knew what the ore lighter carried or where she carried it from; but the works knew that from the last week of August its fire stones did not go cold. They came off the hearths at the end of a shift grey and dull as they always had, and went to the stone shed, and in two days they were back, warm, full, in a barrow, with a slip on the top in Ottoline's hand; and the stone shed's men, who had carried them up to Orrin's bench every morning for seven years and waited while he filled them one at a time with his hands on them and his face screwed up, carried them up to Orrin's bench on the Thursday and found it empty.

Orrin had swept it. The stone shed's foreman told Emeric so on the Saturday, standing in front of the bench with his cap in his hands, as a man reports a death.

He had come in at six as he had come in every working day since the second year, and found the barrow already full and already warm, and stood looking at it a while; and then he had taken his broom and swept the bench where he had sat for seven years, and the floor under it, and put the broom back on its hook, and taken his coat, and gone up to the school, where he taught letters in the mornings, and had taught them that morning as well, and in the afternoon too, for the first time.

Vesk did the same on the Friday. He did not sweep anything. He went to the etching floor, where he was the master, and stayed there.

Emeric went down to the stone shed on the Saturday and stood in front of Orrin's bench. It was clean. There was nothing on it at all but the ring a stone had worn in the wood in seven years, round, the size of a palm, pale, where every stone Orrin had ever filled had sat while he filled it.

He stood and looked at the ring for a while. He thought he ought to feel something about it, and found that he did, and that it was not one thing.



Arthur went up on the first of the Saturday runs after the flasks were proved, with Cade, as Ned had said.

He came down to the reeds at ten in a borrowed fur, with a chute on his back that Abloh had taken in for him on the Thursday and his mask in his hand, and stood at the foot of the plank, and Ned stood at the top of it with his arms folded and did not move out of the way.

"Say it," said Ned.

"Ninety," said Arthur. He did not have to think. He had said it to himself, Emeric suspected, a hundred times in the yard and the plant floor and his bed since Tuesday. "A count of ninety each. Four flasks. Bank off, reserve off, flask, the next flask. Six minutes."

"Six minutes to do what?"

Arthur looked up at him.

"To think," he said. "To do the next thing."

Ned looked at him a breath longer. Then he stood aside, and Arthur went up the plank and onto the deck and went to the racks beside Cade and stood there, not behind him, and Cade showed him how the straw went round the stones; and Ned looked down the plank at Emeric on the bank, and said nothing, and Emeric understood that it was the first time in Ned's life he had let a boy onto a deck he did not know the end of, and that he did not like it, and had done it anyway.



Armand went up on the Hod every time she went.

Every Wednesday and every Saturday from the twenty-second, in the chair in the corner of her house, with Wexler's coat over his knees and the heat coming up through the boards under his feet and his mask on its hook by his ear, and Imani beside him with her clock, at the Band's foot, an hour and a quarter and not a minute more; and before that the ship, every Tuesday and Saturday since the second of July. Ferra's column at the back of her book, A. ALOFT, ran to two pages. Seventeen flights. Twenty-one hours in the Band, near enough, and every one of them called by Imani's clock.

And he was well.

That was the thing. He climbed the keep stair without a stop. He walked across the inner ward to the parish house on Sundays and drank Doran's wine. He had taken over the school's top class in stone theory from his own student, who had taught it for six years and was very glad to give it back, and he taught it standing. He argued with Ottoline about the tabulator's sorting rules at the long table with his hands flat on the wood and quite still. He was better than he had been in any year Emeric had known him; he was better than he had been the day he came south.

For four days after a flight.

On the fifth, or the sixth, the right hand moved on the edge of a sheet, and he put it in his lap. On the sixth or the seventh, if the Hod did not go, he stopped on the keep stair, at the turn, with his hand inside his coat. And on the nights between, when the Hod lay in the reeds and did not go, he came down to the reed bank in the dark, in his fur coat, on his stick, and stood there.

Not for long, at first. Then for an hour. Then for two.

Ward saw him there, from the sentry walk, and said so at the long table on the twenty-sixth, in four words, which for Ward was an alarm: "Armand is at the reeds." Imani looked up from her papers. Ferra stopped writing.

And on the twenty-ninth, a Saturday, Imani was called to a birth in the dock quarter at nine at night, a long bad one, the mother's third, and could not come to the reeds; and sent word to the Hod by a runner, in her own hand, three lines, that Master Armand was not to go up without her and that she would be there next Wednesday. And Armand read the note on the reed bank by Ned's lamp, and folded it, and put it in his coat, and said to Ned that the rule in the crew book was three and one more, and that he was one more, and that the healer's note was the healer's opinion and not the crew book's; and Ned, who had no answer to that that would not have been rude to a tower master, took him up.

They came down safe. Of course they came down safe. Armand came out of the house at half past one with his hands still and his colour good and walked up the bank on his stick without help; and Imani, who had come straight from the dock quarter at one with blood on her apron to find the Hod already gone, was standing at the top of the bank in the dark, and watched him come up it, and did not say one word to him, then or the next day.

She said it to Emeric. In the dispensary, in the morning, with the door shut.

"I've had six of them in my ward," she said. She was washing her hands at the basin, and she went on washing them, over and over, long after they were clean. "Poppy eaters. In nine years. Men who had a bad tooth or a bad back or a bad year, and a surgeon in Wayford gave them the syrup, and it worked, and they were well. They were better than well." She dried her hands. "It's not the poppy they want. Not at first. It's the being well. They come in and tell me so. They don't want the syrup, they want not to need it, and they'll stop when the back's better." She hung the towel on its peg, square. "And the back gets better and they don't stop. And then they stand outside the apothecary's at night, for an hour, then two, because it's shut. And then one night the apothecary's not there, or the surgeon says no, and they find somebody who'll say yes."

"Armand's not..."

"I don't know what Armand's not." She turned round. Her face was very tired and quite steady. "I've never seen what the Band does to a body that goes up twice a week for a summer. Nobody has. I know what it looks like when a man stands by a shut door at night for two hours. And I know what it looks like when he reads a note from his healer and folds it up and goes anyway." She was quiet. "I'm not saying it's poppy. I'm saying I'm frightened that it is. And I'm saying I'll not stand on that bank on Wednesday and call the time on a thing I'm frightened of."



The three of them decided it that night, in the counting room, after the lamps.

It did not take long. Emeric had known since the dispensary what Imani would say, and Ferra had known since Ward's four words at the table, and they sat at Ferra's table with the column open between them, A. ALOFT, two pages and a half of it, and looked at it, and nobody wanted to be the one.

"A fortnight," said Imani at last. "Down. No Hod, no ship. Two weeks." She put her finger on the column. "If it's poppy, I'll see it in two weeks. I'll see it in four days. He'll sweat. He'll not sleep. He'll go to Ned. He'll go to Tobe, or Cade, or the boy, Arthur, and ask, and keep asking. He'll lie to me about where he's been." She took her finger away. "If it's not, he'll be an old man with a bad heart who misses his medicine, and cross about it, and I'll know that too."

"And if it's not?" said Emeric.

"Then he goes back up," said Imani, "and I'll stand on that bank every Wednesday and Saturday until he's a hundred."

Ferra wrote it. She wrote it on a clean sheet, not in her book, in a fair copy, the hand she used for things that were going to be read by people they were about: that the Holder, the Chancellor and the healer, having considered Master Armand's flights of the summer, asked him to remain on the ground for fourteen days from the first of September; that he might go aloft again on the fifteenth if the healer was satisfied; that this was asked of him by the three of them together and by none of them alone. She signed it F. Imani signed it under, I. Emeric signed it, all of his name.

Then she folded it, and took it up the stair herself, at ten at night, to the old man's room; and Emeric went with her, because he could not have sent her alone with it.

Armand read it at his table by the window, by his own lamp, with his spectacles on. He read it twice, and then sat with it under his hand and did not say anything. His right hand lay on the edge of the sheet, quite still; it was the day after a flight. He looked at it.

"Is this your veto?" he said to Ferra, without looking up.

"No," said Ferra. "I'm saving that."

"Then it binds him." Armand put the sheet down. "His ship. His lighter. His crews, who are sworn to him and not to me. It doesn't bind me." He took his spectacles off. "I'm a free man in this castle. I came south of my own will at seventy-eight to teach a boy, and I'll go where I like on my own legs, and if Ned Tallis will take me up I'll go up, and none of the three of you has the right to stop it, and you know that, or you'd not have written it as asking."

"We know it," said Ferra.

"Then why ask?"

"Because you'll say yes," said Ferra, quite steadily, standing in front of his table with her hands folded. "Because you're the only man in Kaldo who's ever been proved wrong at the long table in front of everybody and stood up and said so in his own voice. And because Imani's frightened, and she's never once been frightened of anything in front of me, and you know what she's frightened of as well as we do." She was quiet. "So we're asking. Two weeks. And if she's wrong, she'll say so in front of the table, and I'll write it down."

Armand looked at her for a long while. Then at Emeric, standing behind her by the door.

"Poppy," he said.

Emeric did not say anything.

"She thinks I'm a poppy eater." Armand looked at his own right hand on the sheet, moving. "Standing at the reeds. Going up without her note." He was quiet. "She may be right. I've not asked myself. I've been afraid to ask myself." He folded the sheet, slowly, with his moving hand, and put it in his coat. "Two weeks. Tell her yes. Tell her she may watch me sweat."



He did not sweat.

He was foul-tempered. He was foul-tempered for the whole of the fortnight, short with Ottoline, short with Doran, short with the top class in stone theory, who had never seen him short and spent a week frightened of him; he told Osric at the long table that his stories had the shape of a wet sack, which was true, and which Osric took to the yard and repeated to everybody as a compliment. He did not come to the reeds. He did not go to Ned. He did not ask Tobe or Cade or Arthur anything at all, and Imani asked all three of them, every day, and they said so. He slept; Imani asked the night watch, who could see his window from the sentry walk, and his lamp went out at ten every night and did not come on again. He did not lie to anybody about anything.

His hands shook. From the fifth day of the fortnight, all the time; he could not hold a cup at the long table without both hands round it, and stopped trying, and drank from it on the table with his head bent, like a horse at a trough, with great dignity, while everybody looked at something else. On the seventh day he stopped on the keep stair at the turn, with his hand inside his coat, for a count of a hundred; Imani counted it, from the landing. On the tenth he did not come down to the long table at all, and Imani went up, and came down, and said nothing to anybody, and went up again with foxglove.

On the eleventh, Emeric asked if he might look.

Armand was in the chair by his unlit hearth, the stick laid over his knees, his eyes half closed. He opened them.

"You need not ask," he said.

"I'll ask every time."

So he knelt by the chair, and put his hand flat over the old man's breastbone, and let himself in, at the old man's note, slowly; and looked.

The top of the heart was shivering again. All of it, fast and disordered, the way it had shivered on the floor of the long room in June, like the skin on a horse's flank; and the big chambers below stumbling after it, out of step, catching, going on. Not as bad as June. But going that way. And the dead place in the wall, low, on the left, the size of the end of a thumb, holding nothing, with the muscle round it dim again and stiff, working round it as round a stone.

He held there a while, with his hand on the old man's chest, and let his own mana lie in it, at the old man's pitch, and felt the shivering slow under it, and go quiet, and the beat come back into step; as it had on the floor in June. As it would, as long as he knelt there with his hand on his teacher's chest. As it would not, the moment he took it away.

He took it away, slowly, and came out.

Armand was watching his face.

"Well?" he said.

Emeric did not answer for a while. He knelt on the floor by the chair and looked at the old man's hands, on the stick across his knees, shaking.

"It's not poppy," he said.

"I know it isn't poppy." Armand's voice was very dry. "I've known that since the fifth day. I've been foul to a great many people and I've not wanted to go to the reeds once. I've wanted my hands to stop and my chest to be quiet, and to go up the stair, and those aren't the same thing." A pause. "I wanted Imani to know it. I wanted her to see it. I'll not have her frightened of me."

"She'll know it."

"Good." Armand closed his eyes. "Then you'll let me go back."

Emeric knelt there. He had known since the dispensary what the fortnight would show, if he was honest, and he had let it run anyway, because Imani was frightened and Ferra had written it and it had been right to ask; and now it had run, and he was kneeling by his teacher's chair with the old man's shaking hands in front of him and the shiver in the old man's chest still in his own palm, and there was nothing left to decide.

It was medicine. That was all it was. A man of eighty-five with a scar in his heart and a beat that shivered, who could be well for four days in seven if somebody carried him four miles up in the dark twice a week and sat him in a corner with a fur coat over his knees and stood beside him with a clock. That was what well was, for Armand, now. That was the most of it there was to be had. And Emeric could not give him more of it, not from the ground, not with his hand, not with anything he had; and he could not take what there was away, and would not, and found, kneeling there, that he hated it, quite simply, with the whole of himself, in a way he did not have a word for and did not look for one.

"Yes," he said. "Wednesday."

Armand opened his eyes.

"You look," he said, "as if somebody had died."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Armand turned his stick on his knees. "I'll tell you a thing, since you're kneeling there with a face like a wet Tuesday. Up there, the last time, on Ned's boat, in that chair of yours, with the frost on the glass, and Imani's clock, and that fool Cade singing under his breath at the winch, I felt..." He stopped. "Spry. That's the word. A word I've not used of myself in twenty years. Spry. Young. As if I were sixty." He looked at his hands. "I have been feeling very old this summer. I'd not noticed how old until I wasn't, for an hour, four miles up, in Wexler's coat." He was quiet. "I'm eighty-five, Emeric. I've been told so by my knees for some time. I had not been told so by my heart until June." His mouth moved. "I find I don't care for it."



Imani said it at the long table on the Monday, the fourteenth day, standing.

She did not have to. Nobody had asked her to; Ferra had said she would write it down if Imani was wrong, and that was all. But at the end of the day's business, when the table was rising, Imani stood up at her place with her book closed under her hand, and the table stopped rising and sat down again, because Imani did not stand up at the long table, ever, for anything.

"I was wrong," she said.

She said it to Armand, down the table, in front of everybody, in her ward voice, flat and clear.

"I said I was frightened you were a poppy eater. I've watched you for fourteen days. You've not sweated, you've not lied, you've not gone to the reeds, you've not asked anybody for anything, and you've been the worst-tempered man in this castle, which is a thing I'll not hold against you, because I'd be the same." She was quiet. "It's not poppy. It's your heart. You go up because you're well when you've been and you're ill when you haven't, and that's not wanting a thing, that's needing a medicine. I've a dozen old men in my ward on foxglove who'd climb four miles in a fur coat twice a week if it did for them what it does for you." She did not look away from him. "I was wrong. You'll go up on Wednesday and I'll call the time."

Ferra wrote it down. She wrote it in her book, in front of everybody, with the date, as she wrote every time anybody at that table was wrong, including herself; and Armand, at the end of the table, looked at Imani for a long breath, and then inclined his head to her, the exact depth a tower master gives a colleague, which nobody at that table had ever seen him give anybody.

"Thank you, Mistress Imani," he said. "For being frightened. Somebody ought to have been."



Emeric took him up himself on the Wednesday, in the ship, not the Hod, with the lamps dark and Imani in the saloon with her clock and Ward on the gangway; because it was the first time back, he said, and the ship was steadier and had Maren's kettle, and because he wanted to, which he did not say.

At the Band's foot he came down from the top cockpit and sat on the floor of the saloon by the old man's chair, with his back against the wall under the forward iris, as he had not sat by anybody's chair since he was a boy; and Armand sat in the chair with his hands on the brass rail under the iris, still, and looked out at the dark above the Band, and Imani sat across the saloon with her clock on her knee and said nothing at all.

"Tell me about the tower," said Emeric. "Before me."

Armand did not turn his head.

"You've never asked me that."

"I'm asking."

"You're asking," said Armand, "because you think if you can keep me talking I'll not notice we have to go down in an hour." He let that lie. "That's very good of you. It won't work. Ask anyway." He looked out at the stars. "What about the tower?"

"My mother."

The saloon was very quiet. Across it, Imani did not move.

Armand sat a long while with his hands on the rail.

"She came to me at fourteen," he said at last. "With a letter from the old king, your grandfather, in a hand I knew, asking the tower to take the king's fosterling for instruction as a favour to the crown. The tower did not care for favours to the crown. It took her because the old king paid for a new roof." His mouth moved. "She came up the stair with the letter and a satchel and a very plain grey dress, and the porter asked her what she wanted, and she asked him where the kitchens were. Before the library. Before the masters. The kitchens. He told her. She went and found them, and made friends with the cook, and I didn't see her for two days, and when I did she'd had the run of the tower's larder and knew the name of every boy in the bakehouse." He was quiet. "I thought she was frivolous. I was forty-six and very serious."

"Was she?"

"No." Armand's hands lay still on the rail. "She was the cleverest student I ever had. Before you. And she did everything I set her too neatly and too fast, and then, at the very end, she'd make a small mistake. Always at the end. A line drawn a hair wrong, a stone set a breath late." He looked out at the dark. "I thought it was carelessness, for four years. I told her so. I told her a mage who could do the work as well as she could and then spoiled it at the last line was worse than a mage who couldn't do it at all." He stopped. "I've thought about it since. This summer. Up here, mostly. I think now it wasn't carelessness. I think she did it on purpose. I think somebody had taught her very young not to let anybody see how good she was." He turned his head at last and looked down at Emeric, on the floor by his chair. "I've no idea who. I never asked. I wasn't the kind of teacher who asked."

Emeric did not say anything. He could not have.

"She hummed," said Armand. "While she worked. Very badly. The master on the floor below complained to the tower's chapter that the king's fosterling hummed, and the chapter sent me to speak to her, and I went up to her room and stood outside the door with my hand up to knock, and she was humming, over a stone, the same tune over and over, out of key, and I stood there with my hand up for a quarter of an hour and didn't knock." He looked back out at the stars. "I told the chapter she'd stopped. She never stopped."

"My father said she hummed," said Emeric. "Badly. He used to stand outside the door."

Armand was quiet.

"Did he," he said at last. "Well. He'd had practice." His mouth moved, at one corner. "He used to wait at the bottom of my stair. Your father. He was sixteen and a prince and nobody had ever told him to wait for anything in his life, and every week, on her free day, he'd come to the foot of the tower with two horses, and stand at the bottom of my stair, and wait for her to come down. Sometimes an hour. Sometimes three, if I'd kept her late, which I did, sometimes, on purpose, because I didn't like him." He looked at his hands on the rail. "He never once came up. He could have. He was the king's son; nobody in the tower would have stopped him. He waited at the bottom with the horses, every week, for four years, and when she came down he'd say something rude about how long she'd been, and she'd say something ruder, and they'd ride off." He was quiet. "I thought he was an arrogant boy who'd never done a day's work. I was right. I thought she was wasted on him. I was wrong about that. It took me twenty years and her death and a summer in a castle with her son to find out how wrong."

The saloon was very quiet. Imani had put her clock face down on her knee.

"She reminded me of somebody," said Armand. "All four years. The way she tilted her head over a stone when it wouldn't do what she wanted. Just so. A little to the left. I'd seen it before somewhere, when I was a younger man, and I could never think where." Then: "I've never thought where. It's the one thing about her I could never put in a column." He turned one hand over on the rail and looked at the palm, which was still. "She came to me when you were four. To my room in the tower. In a queen's dress, this time, not grey. She said, teach him. Teach him what you taught me." His voice stayed level. "She said, teach him better. And I said I would. And I didn't. I went to the castle and taught you your letters and your stones until she died, and then I didn't know how to teach a boy whose mother was dead, and I let you go." He looked out at the stars. "So I came south at seventy-eight to finish it."

Emeric sat on the floor of the saloon with his back to the wall and did not trust himself to say anything at all.

"There," said Armand, after a while, quite briskly, the teacher's voice. "That's three stories, and an hour gone, and I didn't notice it go, so you were right and I was wrong, and you'll not hear me say that twice in one summer." He looked across the saloon. "Mistress Imani. Is it time?"

Imani turned her clock over and looked at it.

"Ten minutes," she said. Her voice was not quite her ward voice. "You've ten minutes."

"Then I'll have them," said Armand, and put both hands back on the rail, and looked out at the stars over the Band, perfectly still; and nobody said anything else, and the ten minutes went, and Emeric sat on the floor by his teacher's chair with his back against the wall of a ship he had built, four miles up in the dark, and listened to an old man breathe in step.
