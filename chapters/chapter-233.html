Chapter 233 - Entrance

Orrin set the paper himself, and it took him the whole of the summer, and he was not sure until the morning it was sat that it was any good.

That was the trouble with a paper. He had never set one. Nobody in the kingdom had ever set one, as far as he knew, and he had asked. A boy went to a master and the master looked at him, and talked to him, and asked him a few things, and took him or did not, and if the boy's father was somebody the master took him whatever he answered. That was how Orrin had been taken, at nine, by the old priest in the village north of the capital, who had looked at him and asked him to say his letters off the hornbook backwards and then taken him because he could and because nobody else in the village had ever asked to try. It was how every school in Randillia took anybody. It had never once struck Orrin as strange until the prince said, at the table in the spring, that the island school would take nobody that way, not one child, and would take whoever passed, and asked him to write the thing they would pass.

"The same questions for all of them?" Orrin had said.

"The same questions. The same paper. The same hour. The same room, if we can manage it. Nobody's told the questions before, and nobody's told who wrote them, and nobody who marks them knows whose paper he's marking." The prince had put his hand flat on the table. "And then we print the list, of who passed, in the order they came, and pin it up at the cross."

"And if a lord's son fails it?"

"Then his name isn't on the list."

Orrin had gone back down the stair that night thinking that he had been in Kaldo a great many years now and it could still make his ears ring.

It was printed. That was the first thing about it, and he did not get used to it all summer.

The press was in the old tannery down by the water, which had been empty since the spring and had been scrubbed out to the stone and given to the printing, because a press was heavy and a tannery had a floor that could take it. The paper was Kaldo paper, the good white, off the mill at Wayford. And the questions went through the prince's printer in the corner of the room, the one that nobody but the prince and Bram and two of Ferra's clerks was allowed to touch, and came out of it on three hundred sheets in the time it took Orrin to walk round the room twice, every sheet the same, every letter on it the same, down to the little flaw in the tail of the g in figure in the fourth question, which was on all three hundred.

He held one up to the window. He had been holding sheets at arm's length for some years now, the way a man does whose eyes have begun to go, and he held this one at arm's length and read it, all of it, the twelve questions he had written and rewritten since midsummer, and thought that it was the first examination paper that had ever been printed in the world, and that nobody in the world knew it except the people in this room, and that the people in this room were mostly thinking about their dinner.

There were twelve questions. Four were sums, the sort a child who could reckon would do in her head and a child who could not would not do at all however long she was given. Three were reading: a passage from the primer's chronicle, and questions on it, to see not whether a child could say the words but whether she knew what they said. One was a map, a little one, of a village with a river through it, and the question was which way the river ran, and how you knew, and there was an answer and it was on the map, in the way the mill stood, and Orrin had spent a week on that one alone. One was writing: tell me about a thing you have made or mended. Two were things Orrin could not have explained to the old priest in the village north of the capital, about a lever and a weight, and a jug of water left in the sun. And the last was a question he had been told by the prince to put in and had argued about and lost, and it was only this: What would you like to learn here, that nobody has taught you yet?

"There's no right answer to that," Orrin had said.

"No."

"Then how do I mark it?"

"You don't. You read it." The prince had smiled, very slightly. "You'll want to. That's the only one on the paper you'll want to read twice."

He had been right. Orrin had read every one of them twice, at the end of the summer, in the old exchange, by the window, with a lamp, until it was too dark to see the lamp's light on the page; and some of them three times; and one of them he had copied out, and kept in his pocket, and did not show anybody. It was from a boy of eleven off the third quarter's school, whose sums had been wrong in every line and whose map had the river running up the hill. It said: Why the lamp goes blue before it goes out.

He had passed the boy on that. It was the only paper he passed on one question. He had told the prince, and the prince had said: good.

The letter from the north came at midsummer, while the paper was still in pieces on his desk.

It came up to the palace in the ordinary bag, and Ferra's clerks opened it, because it was addressed to the school and the school had no clerk of its own yet, and sent it down to Orrin, because it was about the school, and Orrin read it in the exchange at his desk under the high window and then read it again and then took it up to the palace himself, because he did not know what to do with it.

It was from a steward. The steward of a small lord in the north, a lord with a name Orrin knew only from the tax rolls: Esterby, a house with a hall and three villages and a river, a long way up the king's road. The steward wrote a good careful hand and a good careful letter, with all the titles in it and all the forms, and what it said, under the forms, was this: that his lord had heard from a cousin, who had it from a merchant, who had been in Kaldo in the spring and read a notice pinned up at the cross, that the prince of Kaldo was opening a school on his island in the autumn, and would take into it any child who passed a written paper; and that his lord's younger son was eleven, and quick, and had had a tutor since he was six and had had the whole of the tutor, and needed more than the tutor had; and whether the prince would consider taking the boy, and on what terms, and what the fee would be, and whether a letter of commendation from his lord would be of use.

Orrin read it out at the table, in the long room at the palace, standing, with the prince at the head and Ferra at his right hand and Imani across from her, and Ward by the door, and Saban looking at the ceiling, which was how Saban listened.

When he had finished, nobody said anything for a moment.

"Well," Orrin said. "I don't know how to answer it. I don't know what to say to a lord. I don't know what we charge. We've never charged anybody. Kaldo's children don't pay. I didn't know there'd be anybody else." He put the letter down. "And a letter of commendation. What do I do with a letter of commendation? Do I put it on the paper? Does he get marks for it?"

Ferra had held out her hand for the letter while he was talking, and he gave it to her, and she read it through once, fast, the way she read a bill.

"The fee," she said, "is four Silver Rands the quarter, for the teaching. Three more for his board and his bed, if he boards in the school houses, which he'll have to, since he can't go home at night to the north. Paid a quarter ahead, at the counting room, to the barony, in Rands." She did not look up. "The boy sits the paper. The same paper. On the last day of the summer, here, in the exchange, in the same hour as everybody else. If he can't be here for it, he waits a year for the next. If he passes, he stays. If he doesn't, he goes home, and the steward's out the cost of the road." She put the letter down. "And the letter of commendation is very kind, and we'll put it in the file, and it doesn't do anything."

Orrin looked at her. "That's it?"

"That's it. It's what anyone pays who isn't Kaldo's. Anybody from Grayrock or Wayford who comes will pay it. Bryda's already asked." She looked up at him, finally. "What else would it be?"

He could not think what else it would be. That was what he found, standing there. He had been ready to be frightened of the letter, and it had turned out to be a bill.

At the head of the table the prince had been listening with his chin on his hand. He had not said anything. He said now, quietly, "Write it that way. Just that. Four and three, the paper, the clerk, and thank you for the letter." And then, as Orrin picked the letter up: "It's the first one."

"The first what?"

"The first time anybody's asked." The prince did not smile. "We didn't tell anybody this was a good school. We never will. Nobody here's going to say it. We pinned a notice up at the cross, and a merchant read it, and told a man, who told a man, and now a lord in the north who's never heard of us is writing to ask whether his son's good enough to come." He looked at the letter in Orrin's hand. "That's how you find out. Somebody asks."

It was Imani who asked the other thing, and she asked it at that same table, in front of him, and Orrin did not understand it, then or for a long time.

He had sat down by then, at the foot of the table by Ward, with the letter, because the prince had asked him to stay and go through the numbers with Ferra: how many benches, how many houses, how many beds for the ones who would board. And Imani had been looking at the letter lying on the table in front of him for some time, and not at him, and when there was a pause in the numbers she said:

"He'll drink the water."

Everybody at the table looked at her. Orrin looked at her too, because everybody else had.

"The boy," Imani said. "The lord's son. If he comes. He'll board on the island, and he'll drink the island's water, from the towers. So will anybody Bryda sends. So will anybody from Wayford." She was still looking at the letter. "Kaldo's children have drunk it all their lives. Their mothers and fathers have. Everybody who lives here. It's their water." She looked up, then, down the table, at the prince. "These children's mothers and fathers don't live here. They've never drunk it. They're sending their children to drink it. And nobody's told them anything about it at all." She said it quite quietly. "Is that the barony's business? Or the parents'?"

Orrin did not know what she meant. He thought, at first, that she meant the wells. That the island water was the island's own, off its own wells, and not piped from the hills like New Kaldo's, and some mothers were particular about water; his own had been. He thought perhaps she meant the monthly jug, the one she read off every tap on the first of the month, which the whole island knew about and thought was one of her fancies. He opened his mouth to say that the island's water was the best water in the barony and everybody knew it.

He shut it again, because nobody else at the table had moved, and he had been at a great many tables in his life and knew what it looked like when a whole table did not move.

The prince did not answer. He looked at Imani for a long moment down the length of the table, and she looked back at him, and something went between them that Orrin could not read, and did not try to. Then the prince turned back to Ferra, and asked her how many beds, and she told him, and the numbers went on, and nobody said anything else about water, and after a while Orrin was not sure he had heard it.

He had. He found that out the next day.

The terms came down to the exchange the next morning, from the palace, printed, a stack of them, still smelling of the ink: the school's terms, for anyone who was not Kaldo's, to go north with the answer to the steward, and to Grayrock, and to Wayford, and to anybody else who asked. Orrin had not known they were being written. He had thought the answer to the steward would be a letter, in his own hand, saying four and three.

It was a sheet. Two columns, in the prince's plain way, the way the barony's notices were printed at the cross. The fee, four and three, in Rands, a quarter ahead. The paper, and the clerk, and the glass. The board: a bed in the school houses, three meals, a woman in each house to see to the children, and the ward for anyone who was ill. The rules: the same rules as for Kaldo's children, no more and no fewer. And at the bottom, in a line of its own, a line that Orrin read three times and could not see the point of:

The school's water is the barony's own, drawn from its towers and treated as all the barony's water is.

And under that, two names, printed, with a space above each for the hand: the prince's, as lord of Kaldo, and Imani's, as Secretary of Health.

He took the stack across to the ward himself, at noon, because the sheet wanted her hand on it before it could go north, and Imani was in the teaching room with six of her students round the skeleton, telling them which bones in a hand were which. She came out to him in the passage and took the top sheet from him and read it, standing, there under the painted word over the door.

He watched her read it. He did not know why he watched her; he did not usually watch people read. She read it to the bottom and then she read the bottom line again, and her face did not change at all, and he had the strangest feeling, standing in the passage with the stack in his arms, that she was reading a great deal more than was on the page. That she was reading, in that one line, every word that was not in it.

"Is it wrong?" he said. "It seems a strange thing to put. Everybody knows the water's good."

"It's not wrong," Imani said. "Every word of it is true." She went on looking at it. "It's the barony's water, and it's drawn from the towers, and it's treated as all the barony's water is. Exactly as all of it is. Not one drop differently." She was quiet for a moment. "It's true."

Then she held out her hand for his pen, and he gave it to her, and she put the sheet against the passage wall and signed it, above her printed name, in her plain upright hand. Imani. Secretary of Health. And then took the next sheet off the stack, and signed that, and the next, all of them, one after another, standing in the passage, forty sheets, without saying anything else. When she had finished she gave him back his pen and the stack, and went back into the teaching room to the skeleton, and he heard her voice start up again, level, naming the bones.

He carried the stack back to the exchange and gave the top sheet to Ferra's clerk to go north with the answer, and the rest to be pinned at the cross and sent to Grayrock and Wayford and anyone else who asked, and forgot about it, mostly, for a long time.

They sat it on the last day of the summer, in the old exchange and in the two long houses next to it, which had been emptied in the spring and had their walls knocked through, the three buildings together, with the doors open between them and a clerk of Ferra's at each door with a sand-glass.

Every quarter of New Kaldo had its school now. The first quarter's had been open three years, in a long room at the end of its first street, with a woman in it who had been a governess in a merchant's house in Wayford and was paid by the barony. The second's had opened last year and the third's in the spring. They took every child in the quarter who could walk to them, and taught them their letters and their sums and the primer, and Orrin had written the primer, and the three women and two men who taught in them had all at one time or another sat in the old exchange with him, in the evenings, for a winter, learning to teach it. And out of those schools, and out of the island's own, which was his, the children who wanted to came to the old exchange on the last day of the summer and sat his paper.

Two hundred and sixty-one of them sat it. He had thought there would be a hundred.

They were Kaldo children, nearly all. That was right; it was Kaldo's school. They came in their good clothes and their bad boots and sat on the benches with the printed sheets face down in front of them and did not turn them over until the clerk at the door turned her sand-glass and said now, and then two hundred and sixty-one sheets turned over at once, with a sound Orrin had never heard before and would hear, he thought, for the rest of his life: a great soft sigh of paper, like a flock of birds going up off a field.

And there were four who were not the island's or New Kaldo's.

There was a girl from Grayrock, twelve, very straight on her bench, with her hair scraped back and her mouth shut in a line, who had come down on the train the day before in the charge of a guard who had orders from Bryda Renner herself to put her down at the cross in Kaldo and not before. She had a letter. She gave it to Orrin at the door of the exchange with both hands, the way you hand a thing to a priest, and he opened it and read it. It said, in a hand like a stonemason's: This is Nell Ashby. These are her sums. And under that, a page of sums, long division, in a child's hand, every one of them right. There was nothing else. No my lord, no by your leave, no I commend to you. Orrin had read it twice, standing at the door, and then folded it and put it in his pocket with the paper about the blue lamp, and said to the girl, "Sit wherever you like," and she had looked at him as if he were simple and sat at the front.

There was a boy from Wayford, a stationer's son, sent by his father with a purse and a list of questions about the fee that the father had not waited to have answered.

And there was a boy from Milford, fourteen, big, with a farm boy's hands, who sat at the end of a bench at the back and would not take his cap off until the clerk at the door told him to, and when Orrin went down the benches before the glass was turned, as he went down every row, to see that every child had a pen and ink and a blotting sheet, the boy looked up at him and said, before Orrin had said anything at all:

"My father was in the war. The first one. With Milford's levy, in the coalition. Against you." He said it loud, so that the benches round him heard it. "I'm telling you so you know."

The benches round him had gone very quiet. A Kaldo girl two places down had turned all the way round to look at him.

Orrin looked down at him. He was a big frightened boy with his chin up, and he had been getting ready to say that on the train since Milford. Orrin could see it on him. He had been getting ready for a year, perhaps.

"Is he well?" Orrin said.

The boy blinked. "What?"

"Your father. Is he well?"

"He's well. He's got the bottom field on the second bench. Wheat." The boy's chin came down a little. "It's watered now."

"Good," said Orrin. "Have you got a pen?"

The boy had a pen. Orrin went on down the row. He heard the benches behind him start to breathe again, and the Kaldo girl turn round the right way, and after a while he heard the boy take his cap off, and put it on the bench beside him, and leave it there.

His name was Hal Dunmore. He passed, fifty-third on the list. Orrin would have taken him at a hundred and fiftieth, and did not have to, and was gladder of that than of anything else on the list.

And there was the fourth, who had come the furthest and said the least: a thin boy of eleven in a good grey coat, with a tutor's neat hand and ink already on his fingers, who had come down the king's road from the north with the steward of Esterby himself, a week on the road, and been left at the exchange door by him with a bow to Orrin that was two inches too deep. He sat where the clerk put him, in the middle of the middle bench, between a cooper's daughter from the second quarter and a Wayford clerk's son, and when the glass was turned he turned his sheet over with the rest and did not look up again until it had run out.

Orrin marked them himself, all two hundred and sixty-one, in the week after, at his desk under the high window, without knowing whose any of them was, because Ferra's clerks had put a number on every sheet at the door and kept the names in a sealed book. The steward's boy's was a good paper. The sums were right. The map was right; he had seen the mill. The answer to the last question said: What the stones do, and why the priests say not to ask.

Orrin read that one twice.

When the clerks opened the sealed book and put the names back on the numbers, the boy had passed eighth, between the cooper's daughter and the Wayford clerk's son, in the order he had come; and the list was printed, three hundred copies, as all the barony's papers were printed, and one was pinned at the cross, and one went into the school's book, and one went into Ferra's, and one went north to Esterby with the steward's receipt for the first quarter's fee, and the rest went where the barony's papers went. Aubrey, second son of the lord of Esterby, north riding. Eighth. Honest paper. Every word of it true. The first child who was not Kaldo's own, nor from any of Kaldo's baronies, whose name was written down in Kaldo's hand as coming to live on the island and drink its water.

Orrin did not think about that. He thought about the answer to the last question, and whether the boy would be the kind who went on asking, and hoped he was.

The school was the island. That was what nobody outside Kaldo understood yet, and a good many people in Kaldo had not quite understood, and Orrin was not sure, even now, walking round it, that he did.

It was not a building. It was streets. The old exchange at the heart of it, where it had always been, with its high windows and its long room and its benches, which were his, and had been since he had first walked into them as a prisoner with a guard behind him and been asked what he could teach. Round it, in the lanes behind the old market, the houses the barony had closed as their people crossed the water to New Kaldo, a street at a time, a house at a time, each with the barony's mark on the door and an inventory on a nail inside it, handed to his list or Armand's in the order the prince had drawn two winters ago. His list had been long. It was getting longer every quarter day, as the last of the island went over the bridge to the new houses, and nothing came down; nothing ever came down; the houses were only opened, and swept, and given new doors, and filled with benches and tables and slates.

There was a drawing hall now, in what had been the cooper's long shed behind the market, with its north side knocked out and glazed and a row of tables under the glass, where Bram's draughtsman taught the children to draw a thing so that another man could make it from the drawing without asking. There was the surveying room over it, the loft, where the Tarrants came in from the coast twice a week, uncle and nephew, with their chain and their level and the brass instrument with the little telescope on it that the prince had given them, and taught the older children to measure a field and draw it and find out, from the drawing, how much seed it wanted. There was a room for the printing, in the old tannery, where the children who were good with their hands set type for the primer's next printing, and argued about spacing. There was Bram's workshop by the water, on the old boat hard, where the apprentices worked in the mornings and came up to the exchange for their letters in the afternoons, and where young Edwin, who had a new pink scar across the knuckles of his right hand that he would not talk about, taught the smaller boys how to hold a file.

And there was the ward. Imani's ward, which had been on the island since the plague, in the building it had been in for years now, would stay on the island. New Kaldo had its hospital now, with its long bright rooms and its piped water and its drains, and the sick went there. But the teaching stayed in the old ward, on the island, in the old rooms, where Imani taught the ones who wanted to learn to do what she did, with a skeleton on a stand that had come from nobody would say where, and the plates she had had the prince print for her, and a rule over the door, painted, that said Wash.

The mages were up the hill, in the keep, which was the tower now: Armand's tower, with Armand's students in it, forty of them, across a street and up a lane from the exchange and not in the same rooms. Orrin went up there once a week to eat with Armand, which was a thing he had done for years, since before either of them had had a title. He did not teach up there. They had their teaching and he had his. It had been settled a long time ago, and not by anybody saying so.

He stood in the middle of it on the last evening of the summer, in the lane behind the exchange, with the children gone and the list in his hand, and looked round him at the closed doors with the barony's mark on them and the open doors with the benches inside them, and the lamps coming on in the ward's windows, and the keep on the hill with its lamps coming on too, and the old market square below him that was a green now, where a few of the Kaldo children who had sat the paper were still kicking a bladder about in the dusk and would not go home. He thought that it was a school the size of a town, and that it would never be finished, and that he would be dead before it was. He found he did not mind that as much as he would have expected.

Then it was the autumn, and the first morning.

He had pinned the prince's drawing up on the wall of the old exchange the night before, himself, on a board behind his desk, where he could turn round and see it. It was the one the prince had drawn two winters ago at the table, the island as it would be: the lanes behind the market turned into long halls, the market square a green, two new bridges on the east side, the keep, the church, the old exchange drawn exactly as it was; and beyond them, at the far end of the island, a row of long buildings roofed in glass, bright even in pencil, like a row of greenhouses the size of churches. The prince had sent it down to him in the spring, rolled, by Hobb, with a note in his own hand that said For the wall, when there is a wall. E., and he had kept it rolled in his desk all summer, because he had not known which wall, and had known this morning.

The children came in at the first bell.

There were more of them than there were benches. He had known there would be. A hundred and forty had passed, and he had benches for a hundred and ten, because the carpenter was behind. They came in through the three doors, in their good clothes and their bad boots and their cleaned faces, and stood about in the long room under the high windows, looking up at the windows and at each other and at him, and he let them stand. The Grayrock girl was at the front, already, sitting, with her hands folded on her slate. Hal Dunmore was at the back with his cap in his hand. The boy who wanted to know why the lamp went blue was on the end of the third bench, sharing it with two girls who were not pleased about it. The Wayford boy had brought his father's list of questions and was reading it. The boy from Esterby was sitting very straight in the middle of the middle bench, as if he had been told where to sit, and had not been.

A small girl near the front, one of the first quarter's, with a gap in her teeth, was looking past him at the drawing on the board. She put her hand up. Nobody had told her to put her hand up; he had not told them anything yet. She put it up anyway.

"Master," she said. "What are the big glass ones for?"

He turned and looked at the drawing. The long bright roofs, at the far end of the island.

"I don't know," he said. It was the truth. The prince had never told him. He turned back. "For you, I should think. When you're older."

The girl considered that, and seemed to find it fair, and put her hand down.

He had the primer on the desk in front of him, the new printing, the one the children in the tannery had set the type for. He opened it at the last page, the way he had opened it at the last page on the first morning of every school year since there had been a primer, and the room saw him do it and went quiet, all at once, the Kaldo children first, because they knew; and then the others, because the Kaldo children had.

It was the short psalm. The one about the lamp in the window. Every child in Kaldo who could read could say it by heart, and most of the ones who could not, and Doran had proofed it in his book with his initial the first time it was printed, and it had been at the back of every primer since. Orrin did not need the page. He read it from the page anyway, because the page was the point: the printed words, the same on every copy, the same in every child's hands in every school in the barony that morning.

He read the first line, and the room said it with him. The Kaldo children, all of them, loud; and then, halfway through the second line, the Grayrock girl, who had learned it from somewhere, very clearly, at the front. And then the Wayford boy, who had looked it up in his primer and was reading it, a word behind. Hal Dunmore at the back did not say it. He stood with his cap in his hands and his mouth shut and listened to it, all of it, to the end, the way a man listens to a song in a language he has decided to learn.

Then it was finished, and the room was quiet, and there were a hundred and forty of them looking at him, under the high windows, with the light coming down, and more of them standing than sitting.

Orrin closed the primer. He looked at them for a moment longer. He had been a prisoner in this room once, with a guard at his back, and been asked what he could teach. He had been talking in it, more or less, ever since.

"Right," he said. "Where does the warm go?"

And he started talking, and he did not stop.
