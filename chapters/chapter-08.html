Chapter 8 - Door to Door

On the first morning Emeric knocked on four doors and changed nobody's mind.

He did everything right, or what he'd have called right in his old life. He explained. He was clear and patient and accurate. He described the slope, and the rows, and the wells, and the thing too small to see that went down through the soil and into the water. At each door a face listened to him with polite, total blankness, waited for him to finish, and thanked His Highness for his concern.

Imani did eleven doors that morning and turned eleven households.

Bram did more than that, and Emeric never did learn how many, because Bram didn't explain anything. He stood in a doorway with his arms folded and said, more or less, Boil your water. All of it. Every day. I'll tell you why later, and moved on. It took him about ninety seconds a door. People did what he said.

It took Emeric two more days to see what he was doing wrong.

He was explaining. People didn't want a prince explaining things to them on their own doorsteps. Imani had something he didn't: she'd sat up with half these families' dead. Bram had something else: thirty years of mending their pots and their hinges for whatever they could pay. When a prince knocked on your door and told you your well was killing you, the first thing you wondered was what he wanted from you.

So he stopped. He let Imani talk. He carried water.

Rylan thought this was the funniest thing he had ever seen. Eight days later he was still laughing about it at odd moments, for no reason Emeric could see.



The real obstacles were money and time.

Boiling three times a day took fuel, and fuel cost. The woods along the road were the baron's, and the baron fined anyone caught cutting there, so wood came in by cart and was sold by the bundle, and the lower ward couldn't afford three fires a day. Clean water meant a walk up the hill to the keep's cistern and back, and the cistern hardly held enough for the keep. The church had said nothing either way, and in Kaldo a silence from the church was half a no. And some doors shut when they saw who was standing there, because the last time the crown had come knocking it had taken their sons.

It was Bram who fixed the fuel.

"One fire," he said, on the fourth day, standing in the middle of a lane with his thumbs in his belt. "Not one a house. One a street. A big kettle, a rota for who tends it, and the wood split between all of them." He counted the doors. "Nine families. Nine families can keep one fire going."

Emeric stared at him. Then he said, loudly enough for the lane to hear, that he had never once in his life worried about the cost of firewood, and that it hadn't occurred to him, and that Bram was right.

That did more than anything else he did that month. By the end of the week the forge district had a fire on every street.



Imani kept the trials in her own register. It was her book, and her terms. She wrote down which houses boiled and which didn't, who got sick and who didn't, every day for a fortnight, in a small careful hand that let nothing through.

Two households on the same low street were told together, the same morning, by the same people.

One of them boiled.

The other one didn't. The woman of that house heard them out in her doorway with her arms crossed and said no. The cistern was a long walk up the hill and she had bad knees. The street fire was a share of wood she couldn't spare. And her mother had drunk from that well for fifty-one years and been none the worse for it.

Emeric started to answer. Imani's hand closed on his sleeve.

She'd told him the night before, flatly, so that he'd remember it. A yes said to a prince isn't a yes to the truth. A yes to a prince is just a yes to a prince, and it lasts as long as the prince is standing there. And she would still be living in this street long after he'd gone back to wherever princes go.

So he offered twice: the share of the fire paid for, help with the walk. The woman said no twice. He thanked her and stopped.

Three weeks later the grandfather in that house died of the sickness. The daughter caught it too, and Imani sat with her two nights, and she lived.

Nobody told the street what it meant. Nobody had to. By the end of that week there was a fire going before dawn on Cooper's Row, and on the next street after it.

"You let it happen," Ward said, that night, standing at the window of Emeric's room while Emeric sat on the bed and looked at his hands. "You didn't do it. Different ledger."

Emeric started to answer him.

"No," Ward said. And never spoke of it again.



The north well boiled as a whole street. A woman organized it, and organized it well, and it was a fortnight before Emeric learned who she was.

In the fourth week the sickness got into Sela's youngest.

He was perhaps eleven, a thin, quick boy who ran errands for the fishmongers, and he went down in a single afternoon. By night he was emptying out faster than anyone could pour water into him.

Imani sat with him. Emeric went to her the first night and stood in the lane outside, because he knew Sela would rather the house burned than let him into it, and when Imani came out for air he told her one thing.

Boiled water, with a measure of salt and a measure of honey, and he gave her the exact amounts. Small sips, all night, all the next day, more than seemed possible, more than the boy would want to keep down. The body was losing salt and water faster than it could make them, and you had to put back exactly what it lost, in that proportion.

Imani listened, and asked him two sharp questions, and he answered them. Then she went back in without saying whether she'd use it.

She sat three nights. The boy lived.

Emeric didn't ask her what she'd given him. She never told him. Two weeks later, he heard that the woman running the north well's fire was Sela. She still wouldn't look at him when they passed in the street.

At the service at the end of the fifth week, Doran spoke from the pulpit. He didn't mention the prince. He didn't mention the dead. He said, in a few sentences, that the work being done in the lower town was not against the faith, and that boiling water before drinking it harmed nobody.

That was all. The number of fires in the lower town doubled overnight.

Doran still hadn't spoken a word to Emeric since the night of the map.



In the sixth week Wexler was waiting for him in the entry hall of the keep.

"Hello, Emeric."

No title. He'd dropped it the way you'd drop a glove, to see who'd pick it up.

Emeric didn't pick it up. He said good morning, walked Wexler courteously into the small side room off the hall, shut the door, and offered him a chair. It was Wexler's house. Both of them knew it. It was also the first time in six weeks that Emeric had decided where a conversation would take place.

He laid it out flat. The map. The book. The wedge. The trials, house by house, from Imani's register, with the dates. He gave Imani credit, by name, and Bram, by name. He didn't raise his voice and he didn't argue. He let the numbers sit on the table between them.

He watched Wexler read it. He watched the contempt go out of the old man's face, slowly, like water out of sand, and something more careful come in to replace it.

Then he asked for the cheaper thing.

He didn't ask for the rows to be moved. That was the right answer, and it was a year of work and a fight with the church, and he wasn't going to win it today. He asked for the latrines to be moved downstream of the wells, or for new wells to be cut above the rows, where the ground was clean. One or the other. Whichever the barony could bear.

It took eleven days.

Every morning for eleven days they fought it out for twenty minutes in that side room. Wexler was never once wrong on a fact. There was no money in the treasury. It was a year's work. There were no men to do it, because the war had taken the men and the plague had taken their sons. Every fact was true, and every one was aimed.

On the twelfth morning Wexler signed an order. It said that the ground was set aside and permission was given, for wells to be cut above the burial rows and latrines to be moved below the wells, at such time as the funds for it existed.

He signed it and slid it across the table and sat back.

"You'll never dig it," he said.

"Probably not," said Emeric, and folded it, and put it inside his coat. "Permission. It keeps."

Later, alone, he did the arithmetic. He'd spent eleven days winning the right to do something he couldn't pay for.



Somewhere in the seventh week the town went cold on him.

It happened all at once. Doors that had opened stayed shut. Women who'd nodded to him at the street fires looked past him. Even the forge district was quieter, and when he asked Bram, Bram looked at him oddly and said he'd better ask Imani.

He didn't ask Imani. He decided he knew. Two days before, he'd made a speech at the north well. It had gone badly; he'd said too much, too fast, in front of too many people, and used words they didn't use. He'd heard himself doing it. Clearly that was it.

He was wrong.

What had happened was a rumor. It had started as a remark the steward made in the kitchen, been repeated at a well by a guard's cousin, and been carried down the hill by a woman who cleaned in the keep and had a mother on Foulard Street. By the time it reached the lower town it was simple. The crown's boy meant to dig up the graves.

It hadn't come from Wexler. Emeric didn't know it existed.



At the end of the month the summer came in hard, and the river dropped.

It went down a foot, then two, and the drains along the low streets stopped running and lay still in the heat. The smell that had met them two miles out on the road came back thicker than ever and sat on the lower town like a lid.

He was carrying water up from the cistern that evening, two buckets, the way he did most evenings now, with his shoulders still aching from Ward's drill at dawn. Halfway up the lane he realized he wasn't thinking about the buckets at all. He'd been working the coin in his head, the shape of the lift, and when he'd tried it the night before he'd held it at hand height for a count of forty. Six weeks ago it had been twelve.

It was a strange thing to be proud of, standing in a lane in a dying town with two buckets of water. He couldn't have told anyone why it mattered. He didn't try.

Near the bottom of the lane there was one house still drinking from its own well, an old couple who'd heard everyone out and said no. He stopped at their door. He had the parish book's numbers in his head, the names, the dates. He could tell them exactly how many people on this street had died since the rows began.

Imani was there before he'd knocked. She'd come down the lane behind him, and she put her hand flat on the door, not on him, and shook her head.

"Not like that," she said.

"It's the truth."

"It's who died." She took her hand off the door. "It isn't what killed them. You tell an old woman how many of her neighbors are dead, and all she hears is that she's next." She looked at him. "Go home, Emeric."

He went home.
