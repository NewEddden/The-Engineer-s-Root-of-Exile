Chapter 8 - The Walled Barony

The road went down off the rise and into the edge of the wood, and ran along the inside of it for most of the morning, trees dark and close-set on the left hand and open fields on the right. Rylan named it over his shoulder. The Whispering Woods. Nearly there.

Halfway along, Emeric felt looked at.

It was the same as the gallery above the kitchens: plain and physical, the pressure between the shoulder blades that comes from a stare across a room before you have found it. It came from the trees on the left, and it was steady, and it was in no hurry at all.

He turned in the saddle and looked. Trunks, and the shadow between trunks, and a jay going off somewhere deeper in. Ward, on the far side of the carriage, was watching the road ahead. Nothing in the wood moved that had any business not moving.

He faced front. It stayed on him until the trees thinned, and then it was gone, the way it had gone at the end of the gallery.

Kaldo announced itself two miles out, by smell.

Woodsmoke on top, which was fine, which was every town. Under it something slickly sweet that got into the back of the throat and stayed there and did not improve with familiarity.

He had read about that smell. In the past tense. In a language nobody within six hundred miles of here had ever spoken, in books that treated it as a problem solved several centuries before he was born.

Standing downwind of it as a lived condition turned out to be a different experience from reading the paragraph.

He had the diagnosis before he had the town, which was the worst possible order to get it in.

The road came out of the trees through farmland, and then it crested, and the whole thing arrived at once.

A wall first, because a wall is what the eye takes first. A ring of grey stone, very nearly a circle, with towers set along it at intervals and a moat lying all the way round the outside. Inside it a town packed so tight that from here the roofs were one roof, thatch and old timber in every shade of brown there is. At the center the ground rose to a second, smaller wall, a raised inner ward, and on it the keep: square towers and smaller round ones with conical roofs of a blue that had no business surviving in that much brown. Around the foot of the inner ward ran a ring of water, a canal inside the town, with little arched bridges stepping over it.

East of the wall, a river. A big one, wide and brown and moving, with a stone bridge thrown across it to a straggle of wharves and huts on the far bank.

Fields all round, patched green and brown, a good many of them empty.

And coming in from the north across all of it, a line of stone arches, striding over the fields from a lake that shone at the far edge of what he could see. He followed it in, arch by arch, and near the middle of its run one arch was not there. The channel ended in the air on one side of the gap and began again on the other.

Past everything, to the south, the grey line he had been looking at since morning.

One bridge, over the moat on the south side, to the one gate. The road took the long way round the west wall to reach it, so that the town turned slowly in front of him for most of a mile.

He read it three times in ninety seconds and got three answers.

Defensively superb. One gate, one bridge, water all the way round and a river on the east for good measure, and a keep on its raised ward positioned by somebody who understood what a keep was for, with sightlines down every lane in the place. Built to be held by a small number of men and it could still be held by a small number of men, if there were any.

Economically dead. Those wharves across the river had been cut for real traffic, coast trade coming up from the sea and barges coming down. The mooring posts were worn in the places that only use wears, and three quarters of them stood empty in the middle of a working day.

Sanitarily a bucket. Three thousand people packed inside one wall, drinking from wells dug into the same ground they were burying in, if the smell meant what he knew it meant, with clean water standing in a lake a few miles off and a channel built to carry it that stopped, in the middle of a field, at a missing arch.

Then a fourth reading, quieter, at the river.

Force. Continuous, free, running past a dead town every second of every day since before anybody here was born, and driving a handful of mill wheels and nothing else at all.

He looked at it slightly too long and made himself stop.



Nobody met them.

Every noble in the kingdom knows the etiquette when somebody higher up the ladder comes through your walls. You meet the road. You do it whether you like the man or not, and the liking is not the point of the custom.

The bridge was empty.

Either the baron had chosen not to come, or nobody had told him they were coming. He turned both of those over for the length of the bridge and could not decide which was worse.

Then he decided that either one told him exactly what this house thought a prince was worth, which was the useful piece of information, and that he should stop enjoying having thought of that.

The walls at the gate were old and patched with stone that did not match, repair work done by people who could not afford to do it properly and had stopped waiting for anybody who could.

The gate guard was nineteen at the outside, with a bad shave.

He had a spear because somebody had handed him one and told him where to stand. Ward gave their names and their business, and the boy looked past Ward at the carriage, and at Emeric, and then at Emeric's eyes, and went red to the ears. After that his face did something complicated that did not resolve into anything at all, and he looked over his shoulder for an adult who was not there.

Nobody had taught him the protocol for a prince because nobody had expected to need it.

He pointed them up toward the inner ward and forgot to bow until they were past him.

They crossed the canal ring by one of the little arched bridges, over water that was green and did not move.

The keep had been built for a barony twice this size, back when Kaldo had a working port and men who were not dead or conscripted.

The drawbridge chain had been replaced with rope. Thick enough to do the job, ugly enough that nobody had planned on it lasting this long. A section of the outer wall sagged where water had gotten in behind the stone and never dried.

Patched. Not fixed.

Inside was worse in the quiet way. An entry hall sized for a household that no longer existed. Half the torch brackets empty. Footsteps carrying in a manner that told him more about the payroll than an account book would have.

A thin tired steward named Hale, doing the work of three people and not apologizing for any of it, because the apology was already written into the walls.

A corridor that had plainly held art on both sides at some point and now held brackets.

He had the decision made before he reached the office door, and it was not a careless one.

The wastrel was the only armor he had. He was not taking it off in the first five minutes inside a house he did not trust.

"Baron Kaldo, I presume."

He knew the name. He had known it since his father whispered it to him in a small office in the capital with a man listening at the door.

The man behind the desk did not stand. Past sixty, heavy through the shoulders, grey, with the face of a man who had been handed bad news for thirty years and had stopped flinching at it.

He also did not correct him quickly enough for the pause to read as anything but deliberate, and in that pause he let a prince stand in a doorway being wrong for a beat longer than manners allow, and he watched while it happened.

What Wexler concluded from it was what Emeric had wanted him to conclude, which was that they had sent him a fool, and that a fool is disposable.

What Wexler also learned, which Emeric had not intended and did not see land, was that a prince who does not know the name of the family holding the land he has been sent to is a prince with nobody feeding him anything. No secretary. No steward of his own. No letters. Nobody's ear anywhere in the kingdom.

One silence bought a complete inventory of Emeric's resources, and the inventory came back empty.

"Kaldo is the land," the old man said at last, still seated, no hand offered. "Not the name. It has not run blood to blood since Kal himself, who took land over a captaincy and then could not get a son on anybody. He left it to the man he trusted most instead of a boy he never had. We kept the name we came with. The land kept hers."

He had made that correction many times, by the sound of it, and had never once minded making it.

Then he supplied the timing himself, because he wanted the boy to know how it had been done to him.

"Word came a week ago. Rider from the capital, one line. Keep it running for him." A pause with nothing performed in it. "Not a question in it anywhere."

Four generations. Through the war and the plague after it. And a man past sixty had been told rather than asked.

Emeric did the arithmetic silently, because he could. A rider with remounts does that road in five or six days. The exile had been pronounced two evenings before he left the capital, and he had spent twelve days on the road. Every part of it was consistent, and none of it was an insult that anybody had actually chosen.

He did not say so.

The contempt across the desk was real and it was the surface. Underneath it was a man watching the only thing his family had ever had get handed to a royal embarrassment as a convenient place to put him.

The old Emeric would have taken the insolence personally as a form of self-respect.

He discovered that he did take it personally.

The flush went up the back of his neck. He let it, and went on with the conversation, and it came down again in its own time.

He noticed something else at the same time, which was that the empty bridge had gone onto the list, and that it had gone on provisionally, without a determination, in a column he had not known the list had.

That bothered him considerably more than the flush did, and there was an old man across a desk waiting for him to say something.

He could not remove this man and they both knew it.

The steward was Wexler's. The gate guards were Wexler's. Four generations of every debt, marriage, grudge and favor in a town of three thousand ran through the chair the old man had not gotten out of.

Emeric had a piece of paper, three healthy guards, one wounded one, and a prisoner.

Removing Wexler was affordable in precisely the sense that setting fire to your own house is affordable.

They spent a quarter of an hour lying at each other competently. He gave the man nothing to sharpen and took nothing away.

He waited until the pleasantries were finished before he spent his one coin.

He had a prisoner. The man would be held in the room nearest his own, third door along the east corridor, with his own men on it, in rotation, day and night. He would not be needing an escort from the house for his own movements either. His guards would do.

Wexler looked at him properly for the first time since the doorway.

A man who does not trust the house he is standing in either says so out loud or quietly keeps his own hands on the keys. The second is worth noticing, because it means he has thought about it.

Emeric had told him, in two sentences and without one word of accusation, that he had counted the ways a prisoner can die in a cell and did not intend to find out which one Kaldo used.

"As you like, Your Highness."

No offense taken at all. Which was worse. He had not been insulted. He had been informed.



In the corridor afterward, alone with Ward, he let his shoulders down half an inch.

"How do you think I did?"

"Spectacular, Your Highness."

Same word. Same flatness. Nothing whatsoever the same about it, and both of them knew exactly what it had cost, and neither of them said a word about it the whole way up the stairs.

By dark everything was up from the carriage. Petram was two doors down with Declan sitting outside it, and Emeric lay in a cold room making his list for the morning.

Petram was not on it.

The reasons were ready and the reasons were sound, which was the trouble with them. A man left alone in an unfamiliar room for a week talks better than a man questioned the night he arrives, because fear needs time to do its work, and because silence tells a prisoner nothing about what you already know. Rush him now and he learns the shape of your ignorance.

All of that was true. He checked it twice and it held.

It was also true that he did not want to be in a room with Petram, and that the not-wanting had arrived first and the argument second.

He noticed the order. He used the argument anyway.

Ward found him in the corridor.

"You haven't been down to see him."

"Tomorrow, maybe. A man talks better after a week alone than after a night."

"That's true."

"It is true."

"Yes." Ward moved off toward his post. "Which is why it works."

Emeric stood in the corridor for some time working out what had just been said to him, and arrived, eventually, at all of it.

Two floors below, a tired old man behind a desk had already finished doing his own arithmetic.
