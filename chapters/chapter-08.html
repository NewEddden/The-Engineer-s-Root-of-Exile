Chapter 8 - What the Loop Kept

Emeric didn't call for anyone. Word found him anyway, through walls he'd told everyone not to cross.

A plate left outside his door and collected untouched. A message from Bram, relayed through somebody else, asking if he wanted to talk. A knock from Ward that lasted exactly as long as good judgment allowed and then stopped. He had asked not to be disturbed unless something was outrageously urgent, and the castle had taken him at his word more thoroughly than he'd expected, which he understood was its own kind of message.

He did one useful thing in those two days, and he did it in the first hour, before the grief had properly landed.

He pulled every local man off Petram's door. Not because he suspected any of them, and he told them so, and he suspected all of them, and they knew that too. Ward, Rylan, Declan, Osric, in eight-hour turns, which meant nobody in his service was going to be rested for as long as this took. Then he moved Petram to a different room and told nobody which one except the four.

It should have occurred to him a week earlier that a man who had just been killed for overhearing a name was standing next to a man who had said it, and that the second one was worth considerably more than the first.

His hand found the stone before his mind went looking for it, the habit of three months in a pocket and a childhood before that. He turned it over once, twice. Not thinking. Needing something in his hand that wasn't the memory of a name on a duty roster he had approved without reading twice.

The stone warmed. He let it.

He hadn't meant to delve. He wasn't chasing the loop or the branch, wasn't trying to be useful about any of it. He wanted the noise in his head to have somewhere else to go, and the workspace had always been good for that. Structure where a moment before there had been none. The closest thing to quiet his brain still seemed able to produce.

He drifted back to the loop anyway. Whatever passed for hands in there kept circling the same structure, the way a tongue keeps finding a broken tooth, and somewhere on the third or fourth pass he noticed something that hadn't been there before, or that he hadn't been steady enough to see.

The loop wasn't resetting.

Every time he came back through it, some piece of what he had touched on the last pass was still sitting where he'd left it. Unchanged. Holding its shape whether or not he was holding it.

Data that persisted without him watching it. Not the branch. Not the loop. Something underneath both, staying true with nobody looking.

He came out of it entirely then, on purpose, and sat on the edge of the bed with the stone loose in his hand and no part of himself down there at all, and counted slowly to two hundred, and went back in.

It was still there. Not the memory of it. The thing itself, in the same place, in the shape he had left it in.

So it was not his attention holding it open, and it was not being held open by anything else in that cold room either, which left one object. Whatever he built down there went somewhere while he was not looking, and there was exactly one thing in the arrangement it could be going into. He turned the crystal over under the candle and it looked the way it had looked in the capital.

He had no name for it. He filed it anyway, out of the same reflex that sorted ledgers and mapped wells, because filing something new was easier than sitting with the thing he actually didn't want to look at.

He noted, and did not follow anywhere, that a thing which keeps what you put in it is also a thing that can be dropped.

It did not escape him, somewhere in the surfacing, that he had spent the week staring at a page where somebody had scraped a word off vellum and written a lie into the gap, and had come down here and found a thing that held what was put in it and could not be scraped.

He turned that over for a while, and got nowhere with it, and let it go. Not every rhyme means anything. Sometimes a man in a bad week just wants the world to be saying something to him.

He surfaced an hour later no lighter than he'd gone in, Tam's name exactly where he'd left it, and one more unearned thing sitting next to it that he hadn't done anything to deserve.

He wasn't sure that felt like winning either.

He found Ward before Ward could find a reason not to be found.

The practice yard, alone, running drills nobody had asked him to run, well past the hour any sane man would have called a night. The forearm wasn't wrapped the way it should have been. He didn't stop when Emeric came into view. He finished the pass first, discipline being the only thing left he seemed to trust.

"You've taken every shift since."

Not a question.

"Someone has to."

"That's not what I asked."

Ward set the practice blade down with more care than it needed. "I built the rotation. I put him on that door."

His voice stayed level, which was worse than if it hadn't.

"You vetted him yourself. You told me why."

"I did." A pause that went on a beat too long. "Second son. Poor house. Took the post in spring for a half copper over the docks. No cousin on the castle payroll, no debt to the steward's office, no brother in the garrison. His mother's the woman the whole north end likes and nobody in this castle has anything on."

He picked the blade back up and looked at it instead of at Emeric.

"I went through forty men to find four I'd put on that door. He was the cleanest name on the list. That's why he was there."

Emeric had a sentence ready about how that wasn't his fault, and heard, before it got out, exactly what it was going to sound like. It was going to sound like a man saying it to feel better himself.

"It wasn't yours to put anywhere," he said anyway.

He heard how thin it landed the second it left his mouth. He wasn't people-sharp enough to fix it in the moment. Only sharp enough to know it had gone wrong.

"I mean it. But I know saying it doesn't move it."

Ward almost smiled. It came nowhere near humor.

"No. It doesn't."

There was a version of the next minute in which Emeric told him about the three routes the name could have taken out of that corridor, and about the stone, and about a loop that kept what you put in it. He had the whole thing sorted and ready. He did not say a word of it, and could not afford to examine why, and the not-examining was the same shape as everything else he had learned in this castle in three months.

Neither of them said anything else for a while. Emeric didn't leave and Ward didn't ask him to, and eventually that was the whole conversation, two men standing in a cold yard because leaving first would have meant admitting something neither of them wanted to admit yet.

It wasn't fixed. Emeric was starting to understand that certain things didn't get fixed so much as carried differently, by more than one person at a time.

There was one thing that did get fixed, or came nearest to it, and it wasn't for him or for Ward.

He counted the coin out himself, late, by one lamp, after the castle had gone quiet enough that nobody would ask. Not a large sum and not a small one. The size a man makes a thing when he has done the arithmetic on what a house loses when it loses a grown son, and hates the number for existing, because a number is a thing you can pay and the thing that was taken is not.

He thought about carrying it up himself. He got as far as the door of the idea and stopped, and made himself be honest about why.

Not the road, though the road wasn't safe. It was that a prince at the door of a woman who had just buried her second son wasn't a comfort. It was the crown come to look at what it had cost, and she had paid too much already to owe him the courtesy of pretending the visit was a kindness.

So he didn't go. He told himself it was for her sake and knew it was at least partly for his, and let both be true, because a reason doesn't stop being real for having a coward's half folded inside it.

He wrote the letter three times. The first two he burned, because they were a prince's letters, careful and shaped, full of the kind of grace a woman who had buried her boys could smell across a room. The third he left plain, because plain was the only register that wouldn't insult her.

Sela,

I'm sending this coin in the hope it helps, and for no other reason. It isn't a payment. Nothing pays for what you've lost, and I won't insult you by pretending a purse could. It's only that a house short a working hand goes short of a great many things through no fault of its own, and I have coin, and you shouldn't go without for want of my having the sense to send it.

I would have brought it myself. I didn't, because I don't know if you'd want me at your door, and I couldn't bring myself to stand there and find out. I know what my family has been to this barony, and I know what it's cost you in particular, twice now, in a fight that never once asked your leave to take what it took. You don't owe me the burden of my face on top of everything else. So I've kept it away and sent the coin instead, which is the smaller and safer thing.

If there's anything you need that coin won't answer, Rylan knows how to reach me. You don't have to ask kindly. You don't have to ask at all, if it's easier to have Rylan ask for you.

I'm sorry. I know how little that's worth from where you sit. I'm sending it anyway.

Emeric

He sealed it around the coin and gave it to Rylan, who was the only man he'd trust to put it in her hands and say nothing while she read it. Rylan took it without a word and put it inside his coat, close, the way a man carries a thing he doesn't mean to lose.

At the door he stopped, and didn't turn all the way round.

"She'll not thank you for it." A pause. "But she'll know you meant it. She's had enough of the other kind to tell the difference."

Then he went out into the cold to walk it up the hill himself, and Emeric sat with the empty place on the desk where the coin had been, no lighter than before, and hadn't expected to be.

Ferra had the pages spread over the whole table by the time he made it to Bram's, corners weighted with whatever had been in reach. A hammer. A cup. A folded rag.

"Three more pages touched, not one," she said, without looking up. "Same hand doing the covering. Different hand underneath. Whoever wrote the originals wasn't hiding anything. Whoever came back later was."

"The merchant house name. What was actually there."

"Wasn't a name at all." She turned the page and angled it into the light. Under the scraped ink, fainter, sat the ghost of a different hand. "It was a route. A dispatch waypoint marker, the sort riders use. Not the sort merchants use, because merchants have names and want them written down."

She let it sit a second.

"This wasn't a debt owed to a house. It was owed to whoever ran that route."

"And?"

"Cobb runs that route."

It still wasn't the baron's name. But it was Petram's word and Ferra's ledger agreeing with each other, arrived at from opposite ends by two people who had never met, neither of whom had known what the other was going to find.

That was the thing he had been waiting three months for. Not a bigger piece of evidence. A second one that couldn't have been shaped to fit the first.

"Not enough yet," Ferra said, reading his face before he got there himself. "Enough to know where to point next."

"That's more than I had walking in."

She almost smiled, the way her father did when a piece came off the anvil closer to right than he'd expected. "Everything's more than you had walking in, lately."

Word came off the capital road two days later, unrelated on its face.

Baron Wexler had petitioned for an audience with the crown's judiciary. Unprompted. Over a matter he described only as irregularities requiring clarification, before misunderstanding hardened into accusation.

Nobody had accused him of anything. Not on paper. Not anywhere he could point to.

Emeric read the report twice, the way he'd read the discharged debt's date twice, hoping it would come apart into something less certain on a second pass.

He knows.

The conclusion arrived whole and comfortable and he made himself take it apart anyway, because comfortable was what the last one had felt like too.

A guilty man petitions the judiciary to choose the room the fight happens in, to be the one asking rather than the one answering, and to be on record as having come forward before anybody came for him. All true.

An innocent man with a reputation and no allies petitions the judiciary for exactly the same reasons. Also all true.

Both men do precisely the same thing. Which meant the petition told him nothing whatsoever about which one Wexler was, and he sat with that a while, and disliked it, because he had wanted it to tell him something and had almost let it.

What it did tell him was when. Wexler had moved within days of a boy dying on the mill road. Not within days of the ledgers going up the hill, which had been three weeks earlier and would have been the obvious moment to panic. Days of Tam.

So Wexler had learned something from that death, or about it, and either way he had learned it fast, and either way the corridor was not Emeric's.

There was one thing he could not make fit, and he stayed up with it well past any hour that was going to do him good.

If the man was guilty, why. Not the ledgers. The ledgers were skimming, and skimming was a thing men did out of appetite and habit, and appetite did not explain paying to have a prince killed on a public road, which was an enormously expensive way to solve a problem that a set of quietly thin accounts did not actually pose. A prince who found the skim would report it and Wexler would be ruined. A prince who died on the road would bring the crown's whole attention down on the barony inside a fortnight.

Nobody sane makes that trade to protect a skim. So either the skim was not what he was protecting, or the man had not been thinking about protection at all, and Emeric had no way to tell those apart from the inside of a cold room three months into knowing him.

He wrote none of it down. He put the report on the desk next to the ledger page with the scraped word on it, and the two of them sat there in the lamplight saying nothing to each other.
