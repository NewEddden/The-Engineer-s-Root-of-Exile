Chapter 315 - Talking in Cipher

Bram rewrote stations nineteen and twenty himself, in the delve, against her keel, on the night of the tenth; and he did not let anybody else touch them, and he did not speak to anybody while he did it, and when he came up out of the delve at two in the morning on the shed floor he went and stood in front of the spirit level on the cradle beam, looking at it, and then took it off its pegs and put it in the scrap.

The K Amara went up for the third time on the thirteenth of October, with her crew aboard and clipped on, and the shed's great doors open at both ends to the morning.

She came up off her cradle level. A foot. Four. Ten, with the shed floor dropping away under her and the men on her deck very still and the crew on the floor stiller. At ten feet she was where she had rolled twice, and she did not roll. She hung. Her black length hung in the middle of the shed from nothing, the whole four hundred and twenty feet of her, the way the K Discovery hung, the way every ship Emeric had ever drawn hung, from a gravity matrix high in her hull that pushed only up and down and so let her hang straight under it whatever she carried; and Fletcher, on the tower, took her a little higher, and a little higher, and then, very slowly, forward, out through the shed's river doors into the light.

The yard was full. Nobody had told it to be. Every hand who had ever put a weld on her was in it, and their families behind the fence, and half the works from across the river; and when her prow came out of the dark of the shed into the sun, black, with the light running along it like water, the noise they made went up the river so far that the lightermen at the Wayford wharf, a mile down, stopped work to look.

She went up. She cleared the shed roof and the yard cranes and went on up over the lake, slow and level and quiet, a black ship rising out of a river valley on a clear morning with her flags out; and at a thousand feet Fletcher held her, and she hung there over the lake, perfectly still, not a degree off level end to end or side to side, like a bucket from its handle.

Bram watched her go from the shed floor with his arms folded. He did not cheer. When she was at a thousand feet and holding he turned round and went back into the shed and picked his spirit level up out of the scrap, and wiped it on his apron, and put it back on its pegs on the cradle beam; and then he took a stick of chalk out of his pocket and wrote on the beam under it, in large letters, THIS IS NOT THE SKY.



They tried the shell two days later, out over the sea, where nobody could see or hear but gulls.

The shell was Emeric's. He had coded it himself, from the second stone in the bucket, over the summer, in the cellar under the study, a little every night from July to the middle of September: the register of every soul on the K Amara's rolls, so the shell would know them; the lattice, faceted round the inside of her hull like the threads of a net, so that it could be thrown out a yard beyond her skin all round her at once; and the dial, from a slow soft give to a stop. It was the stasis stone. It stopped what came at the ship, the faster the harder, the way a trampoline stops a fall; and it was the second of its kind in Kaldo, and the second of its kind that anybody knew of outside the old audience hall in the capital where one sat under the king's chair and had never once been seen to do anything.

They did not know, any of them, if it would hold. It had held on the bench. A bench was not a ship.

Ward fired the first bolt. He stood on the K Discovery's open deck a hundred yards off the K Amara's beam, the two ships hanging over a flat grey sea at two thousand feet, with a crossbow he had kept since before Emeric was born; and Emeric, on the K Amara's tower, put the shell up, and felt it go out from the stone in the keel through every facet of the lattice and stand a yard off her skin all round her like a held breath; and Ward set the bolt, and aimed at the after-castle's black flank, and loosed.

The bolt stopped a yard from the steel.

It did not hit anything. It did not bounce. It went from the speed of a crossbow bolt to nothing in a yard of air as if the air had thickened round it into syrup, and hung there, in the air, quivering, a yard off the hull, with its point toward the ship; and then the shell let go of it and it dropped, turning over, into the sea two thousand feet below.

And there was the thump.

Every mage aboard either ship felt it. Emeric had known they would; he had read it in the old quarrymen's lines in Armand's books, and felt it on the bench, small: but he had not felt it on a ship. It went out from the K Amara in a ring like the sound of a great drum struck once, not a sound at all but a push in the mana itself, so that on the K Discovery's deck Ward's note jumped, and on the K Amara's apron the knights' notes all jumped together like a flock of birds put up off a field, and somewhere in Kaldo, two hundred miles north-west, Emeric thought, a mage at a window would have looked up from his book and not known why.

"That'll carry," said Ward, from the other ship, across the hundred yards of air. He had to shout.

"It'll carry," said Emeric.

"Then it stays down when we're running quiet."

"It stays down."

Ward nodded, once, and set another bolt.

They did the rest of it as Emeric had written it on the slate in July, one thing at a time. Ward fired six more bolts, at six speeds, from the crossbow and from a rifle on air, and the shell stopped each one, harder the faster it came; and at the rifle's ball, which came in at a speed no crossbow could give a thing, the thump was so hard that one of the apron knights sat down on the deck and said a word that Ferra, who was on the K Amara's tower beside Emeric with the stone book open and a pencil going, wrote down. Then Rylan.

Rylan was on the register. That was the point. He stood on the K Discovery's rail, in his flight suit, with his hearts lit, clipped to her jackline with a long lanyard that Ward held in one fist; and when Emeric lifted his hand on the K Amara's tower Rylan stepped off the rail and put his suit's whole push behind him at once, every node, flat out, straight at the K Amara's flank, faster than anybody in the yard had ever been let fly a suit and faster than Imani's list would have allowed if Imani had been there to read it.

Rylan came across the gap like a stone out of a sling, flat, with his arms out and his face screwed up and a long yell going behind him on the wind; and he went through the shell without a check, as if it were not there, because to the shell he was Rylan and Rylan was let through at any speed; and came down on the K Amara's after apron with his nodes reversed too late, rolling, in a heap, in front of the apron knights, with the lanyard paying out behind him back across the gap to Ward's fist.

He lay on the apron on his back for a moment looking at the sky.

"Passed," he said.

And then the stranger.

The stranger was the thing Emeric had been most curious to see, and least sure of. A shell that knew its own people, and stopped everybody else, could still be got through, the old lines said, by a man who did not know he was meant to be stopped: by anybody who simply walked into it, at a walk, at the pace a man walks into a room, because a shell judged by speed, and a walk was slower than anything that meant harm had ever moved. He had tried to think of a stranger who would walk up the K Amara's gangway without being told it was a test. In the end the stranger had found himself.

The K Amara had come down to a hundred feet over the beach at the island's north point for the last of it, and run out her stern gangway onto the sand, with the shell up; and up the beach toward her, at ten in the morning, came Maren's bread boy.

He was twelve. He had been sent out on the Hod's morning run with the island's bread and a basket of Maren's buns for the K Amara's crew, because Maren did not trust anybody else to feed a crew she had not fed herself, and nobody had told him the K Amara was doing anything. He came along the sand under the black hull with the basket on his hip, whistling, and looked up at her as anybody looks up at a ship, and turned at the foot of her gangway and walked up it.

Everybody on the stern apron stopped what they were doing. Emeric, on the tower, had the shell up full; he felt the boy come into it at the gangway's head, a yard from the hull, a small cold unknown note coming in out of the morning at an ordinary pace, a boy's pace, with a basket.

The shell did not stop him. It felt him. Emeric felt it feel him, as a net feels a fish go by it in a current too slow to snag. And let him by.

The boy came up onto the apron with his basket and stopped, because forty knights in flight suits and Rylan flat on his back and the duke on the tower above them were all staring at him.

"Buns," he said, uncertainly, and held up the basket.

"Ferra," said Emeric.

"I've written it," said Ferra. "Hole, at a walk. Shown once. Buns." She looked down at the boy over the tower's rail. "Bring them up here, please. The duke hasn't eaten."



And then, in the afternoon, Emeric asked the question he had been waiting since July to ask, and Ferra gave him a look over the stone book that meant she had known all morning he was going to.

The shell stopped what moved. It stopped it by gripping the air round it, the mana in the air, which did not move; the mana that lay in the world the way water lies in a sea, still, while ships and arrows and weather went through it. That was why it could stop a bolt dead in a yard. It held the bolt to the world.

So what if it held the ship?

Not the whole ship; the whole ship was what the shell was guarding. One facet of the lattice, at the stern, the facet Emeric had cut separate in the cellar in August without telling anybody but Ferra what it was for. Set to grip, not the bolt, but the hull itself, to the still mana round it. An anchor in the air.

The K Amara went out to sea again, ten miles off the island, over open water, and hung at three thousand feet. Every soul aboard was clipped on. Emeric had made Rylan go round twice.

Below her, on a raft that the Hod had towed out in the morning and anchored, there was a target: a lattice tower of old timber, forty feet high, whitewashed, with a mitre painted on it, because the knights had insisted.

"She can't fire sideways," said Fletcher, on the tower, for the record, because he had said it every day for a year. "Not her big guns. Not in the air. Every gun I ever heard of kicks, and a ship hanging off a gravity matrix is a bucket on a handle, and you can't fire a forty-centimetre gun out of the side of a bucket. She'll swing. She'll swing like a bell and every man on her deck'll go off it, boots or no boots."

"Then she fires from the water," said Emeric. "Or straight down. That's what we said."

"That's what we said," Fletcher agreed, and looked at him, and waited.

Emeric went into the stone, in the keel, and found the stern facet, and set it.

The K Amara stopped.

She had been hanging still. Now she was still in a different way, that every mage aboard felt at once and every man who was not a mage felt a breath later, in his feet, as if the deck under him had stopped being a deck on a ship and become a floor in a house: as if somebody had driven a nail through the stern of her into the sky. The thump went out from her stern, low and long and heavy, not a drum this time but a door shut in a great house; and from deep in her hull, from the keel spine that Bram had drawn and redrawn and argued over with Fletcher for a winter, came a sound that Emeric did not like at all, a long grinding groan of steel taking a load it had been built for and did not enjoy.

"She's pinned," he said.

"Then fire," said Fletcher.

The K Amara's after turret came round. Two of its three long barrels came down, slowly, toward the white tower on the raft three thousand feet below and a mile off her beam; and Ruan, her gun captain, a square silent man from the coal quay who had laid the swivels on the river barges for twenty years and come up to the K Amara because he said he wanted to fire something that hit what it was pointed at, laid them with his own hands on the wheels and looked at Emeric on the tower and waited.

Emeric nodded.

The K Amara fired her first broadside in the air.

It was louder than anything Emeric had heard since the cellar. It went through the deck and up his legs and into his teeth, two of the forty-centimetre guns going together, and the ship kicked; he felt her kick, the whole black length of her, a great shove sideways from the turret at her stern, the shove that would have sent a bucket swinging on its handle and every man off its deck. She did not swing. The pin held her. It held her the way the shell held a bolt, by the still mana round her stern, and took the whole of the kick into itself and through itself into the sky; and the thump of it went out from her stern so hard that Emeric felt it in his chest like a blow, and the keel spine groaned again, longer, and Ferra's pencil stopped.

And the crew stayed put. That was the thing he would remember. The deck under them kicked, and the deck hold held them to it, and the harnesses took their weight, and the boots held, locked, and the line held; and not one man of three hundred on her deck went anywhere at all.

Except Tolliver.

Tolliver was clipped on. He had been clipped on for six days without missing once, and Rylan had said so, at mess, aloud, which had made Tolliver go red to the hair. But Tolliver was on the forward gun deck, a loader, and between rounds he had unclipped to carry a charge from the hoist to the breech, as the drill said a loader might, for three steps; and he was on the second of the three steps when the after turret fired, and the kick went through the deck, and the boots held his feet and the rest of him went over sideways from the knees exactly as it had in the shed.

Ruan was there. Ruan had seen Tolliver unclip, as Ruan saw everything on a gun deck, and had moved before the round went. He took Tolliver by the back of the harness with one square hand as he went over, and held him, and with the other hand clipped Tolliver's lanyard back onto the line, and let go of him; and went back to his gun without a word.

Tolliver stood on the forward gun deck with the charge in his arms and his lanyard clipped and looked at the back of Ruan's head.

"Thank you," he said.

Ruan said nothing. He was watching the raft.

The white tower on the raft was not there any more. There was a raft, and a great deal of white water round it settling, and a smoke of whitewash and splinters drifting off downwind over the grey sea.

"Again," said Fletcher, from the tower, very quietly, in a voice Emeric had not heard him use.

They fired three more. The pin held for all of them, and the thump went out for all of them, and on the fourth the keel spine's groan was a little longer, and Emeric let the pin go and felt the K Amara come free of the sky and swing, a little, gently, under her matrix, like a bucket set down; and Ferra wrote in the stone book, while he watched her, in small upright figures: Pin, four rounds. Budget: a third of the stone's day. Spine: Bram to look. Thump: they'll feel it in Kesrath.

"Not in Kesrath," said Emeric.

"Somewhere," said Ferra. "I'm writing Kesrath so you remember there's a somewhere."

That evening, in the yard, before the K Amara was even in her shed, Ruan went to the gun stores and drew a hooked bar for every gun station on both her turrets and both her broadside decks, a plain iron bar with a hook on the end of it and an edge on the inside of the hook: a hook-cutter, the yard called it by morning. When the yard's storeman asked what they were for, Ruan said grapple lines. When the storeman asked whose grapple lines, Ruan looked at him until he went away.



The radio trial was on the twentieth, in the Band, and it was the thing Kaldo had been working toward since the night the first spark jumped a gap in Tess's room over the works.

Not the spark. The voice. They had had the spark for two years, the dots and dashes going out from the line room's mast to the ships in the Band and back, slow and certain; they had had the voice on the ground since the spring, Tess's valves glowing in their row in the Motion Shop like a row of small hot moons, and a man in one room talking and a man in another room hearing him. But a voice from the Band, from a ship four miles up, to the ground and to another ship, the whole of the fleet talking at once in the air as men talk round a table: that was the war's. That was what nobody else had.

And anybody who had a receiver could listen.

So every word in it was Ferra's. Ferra's cipher, the one she had made years ago and grown every year since; the one Keller's letters came south in, in groups of five; the one nobody but the six and the line room's clerks could read, and the line room's clerks only on a key Ferra changed every month and kept in her book. On the ship the radio clerk would read the cipher into the mouthpiece as numbers, and on the ground the line room's clerk would write the numbers down and turn them back into words off the key, and nobody listening in between would hear anything but a voice reading out numbers; which, Ferra said, was what most of the kingdom's business sounded like anyway.

Ambrose had built the sets. Tess had built the valves. They were at the Kaldo end, in the line room, side by side at the big set on the trestle under the window, with the line room's clerks at their elbows and Ferra in the corner with the key; and Emeric was on the K Amara, at four miles, with the K Discovery a mile off her bow and the Hod a mile off her stern and the sea under all three of them the colour of slate.

It did not work.

That was the first hour. The voices went out and came back, and came back wrong. The K Amara's clerk read a group into his mouthpiece and the K Discovery's clerk heard it, and so did the Hod's, and so did the line room, all of them at once, every set hearing every other set, the voices running into each other in the sets' horns like a room in which everybody was talking and nobody could tell who; and the sets drifted. Emeric could hear them drift. A voice would come in clear on the K Amara's horn and then slide, slowly, as he listened, into the howl between two stations, and come out the other side a different voice, from a different ship. Every set had its note, the note it was built to talk on; and every set's note wandered, with the heat of its valves and the cold of the Band and the shake of the ship, so that ten minutes after you set it you were talking on somebody else's.

"It's the coils," said Tess, in his horn, from four miles below, faint and furious and perfectly clear for a breath before somebody else's voice slid over her. "The coils and the... it's everything that's warm. Warm things move. Ambrose, tell him."

"She's right," said Ambrose. "Every set's on its own note and none of them will stay on it."

Emeric stood in the K Amara's radio room, which was a cupboard under the tower with two clerks in it and the set and a porthole, and listened to the voices slide over each other in the horn, and knew what it was, and what fixed it; and thought about walking out, as he had walked out of the shed.

He did not walk out. He could not walk out of a cupboard at four miles. And this one was not a thing anybody in Kaldo could work out by the end of the week, however good they were. This was a stone that rang.

"Ambrose," he said, into the mouthpiece. "Is Ferra there?"

"She's here."

"Tell her I want quartz. Grayrock's. The clear kind, out of the veins in the north drift that the miners throw away. Ottoline will have a note of where. As much as she can find." He thought. "And tell Bram I want it cut thin. Flat. Plates. Very flat, and very exactly thin, and every plate a different thickness. Thin as a fingernail. The Forge can do it."

There was a pause, full of other ships' voices sliding.

"What does quartz do?" said Ambrose.

"It rings," said Emeric. "Squeeze it and it makes a current. Put a current through it and it moves. And it rings at one note, its own, a note set by how thick it is, and it holds that note better than anything you can wind or solder." He looked out of the porthole at the K Discovery hanging a mile off in the blue. "Build it into each set. One plate to a set. The set talks on the plate's note, and the plate won't drift, because it's a stone."

Another pause.

"A stone that rings," said Tess's voice, faint, and then a man's from the Hod slid over it, and then hers again, clear: "I'll want to see it ring before I believe you."

It took the Forge eleven days to cut the plates and three more to anneal them, floating, in a bath of oil on the Forge's bed, so that not one of them carried a strain in it that would put its note off; and Ambrose and Tess built them into the sets, one plate to a set, each set on its own note, in a little brass case they called the crystal because Emeric did, and the line room's clerks called the oscillator because Tess wrote crystal oscillator on the drawings and they thought it sounded more expensive. And on the fourth of November the K Amara spoke to the ground from four miles up, and the K Discovery and the Hod spoke to her, and every voice stayed where it was put.

But that was November. On the twentieth of October, in the Band, with the sets drifting, they did the trial anyway, because the fleet would have to talk in the air whether its sets were perfect or not; and they did it as the line room had drilled it for a fortnight, a voice at a time, slowly, with the clerks reading their groups twice and the other ships keeping off the air until they were called. It was slow. It was clumsy. It worked.

And at a quarter past three the line room called the K Amara, and the K Amara's clerk, a thin boy from the records hall with ink on his cuffs, put his pencil to the pad and wrote down what the line room read him, and turned it, at the end, off Ferra's key into words; and stopped; and looked up at Emeric, standing in the cupboard's door.

"It's the Chancellor, Your Grace," he said. "For you. A figure."

"What figure?"

"Just a figure. She's read it as her own group. Her voice. Not the clerk's." The boy looked at his pad. "Eleven thousand four hundred and six. And then her mark."

Emeric had been in the radio room for three hours. He had heard his own voice and Tess's and Ambrose's and Fletcher's and the Hod's clerk's and the K Discovery's clerk's, sliding over each other and out of the horn, all afternoon, until they had stopped sounding like voices at all. He had not heard Ferra's. She had been in the corner of the line room with the key, the whole time, and had not once come to the mouthpiece.

"Read it again," he said.

The clerk looked at him.

"The figure," said Emeric. "Read it to me again."

"Eleven thousand four hundred and six, Your Grace."

"Again."

The clerk read it again. The second clerk, at the set, had turned round on his stool. Behind Emeric in the passage, Rylan, who had been passing with a coil of line on his shoulder and had stopped, put the coil down on the deck very gently, so as to hear better.

It was the stone book's figure. He knew it. It was the K Amara's lift budget for the day, in Ferra's units, the number she had worked out that morning and given him on a slip at breakfast, which he had put in his coat; and she had read it into a mouthpiece four miles below him, in her own voice, in her own cipher, for no reason whatever that a radio needed. She had read it so that he would hear her voice in a cupboard four miles up in the sky. That was all. That was the whole of the message. Eleven thousand four hundred and six.

He knew the clerk had heard her voice. He would have liked very much to hear it himself.

"Thank you," he said.

The clerk went back to his pad. The second clerk turned back to the set. Rylan picked up his coil, and went on along the passage, whistling, and said to the first man he met on the deck, loud enough for the whole of the after apron to hear, that the duke had just had a love letter read to him three times by a boy with ink on his cuffs and it was eleven thousand four hundred and six; and by supper the whole ship knew, and by the next morning the whole yard, and by the end of the week there was not a soul in Kaldo who could hear a large number said aloud without somebody in earshot saying, read it again.

Ferra, told, said that it had been a perfectly ordinary figure and that she had read it herself because the line room's clerks were busy.

Imani, told, laughed until she had to sit down on the infirmary's steps, and then went straight to the line room and asked Ambrose, very seriously, how long it would take to teach a nurse to send.



The Well's crew came to Ward's yard on the twenty-second.

There were twenty-one of them. Bram had picked eight from the works, the men and women who had built the condensing plant at the secret dock and run it since, who knew the column and the tank and the pumps the way they knew their own stairs. Ned had given four of the Hod's Band hands, who had been going up to the Band every week for three years and had stopped noticing it. Two radio operators from the line room. One of Imani's nurses, a big calm woman called Hesketh who had been on the ward through the plague and said nothing in Emeric's hearing, ever, that was not about a patient. One of Maren's cooks. The rest, one each: a machinist, an electrician, a weather girl from Linus's school with a notebook full of the Band's winds, a stores clerk of Ferra's. And the chief.

Orla Garran had been the Hod's bosun since the Hod's first year in the air, when Ned was a lighterman who had never been higher than a warehouse roof. She was not tall. She was perhaps fifty, broad in the shoulder and short in the leg, with grey hair cropped close to her skull because, she said, it got in the way of a helmet and she was not going to plait it at her age for anybody; and a face like the side of a boat that had been out in the weather a long time, brown and seamed and tight round the eyes from squinting into wind. The top joint of her left little finger was gone. She had lost it to a line on the Hod's first winter in the Band, and gone on with the job, and come down that night and held the hand out to Imani with the stump wrapped in her neckerchief and said, Sew that, I'm on again at six. Her voice was low and very rough, as if she had been shouting into wind most of her life, which she had; and she used it as little as she could.

"You'll want soldiers," she said to Ward, on the yard's steps, by way of good morning.

"I'll want you able to hold a door," said Ward. "Two doors. An airlock and a dock arm. Four miles up, with nobody coming to help you for a day, and something on the other side that wants in."

"There's twenty-one of us. There'll be two of yours."

"Two of mine won't sleep," said Ward. "You will." He looked along the twenty-one, standing in a ragged line on his cobbles in their work clothes, with the yard's knights at the forms beyond them pretending very hard not to look. "So everybody holds a door."

Orla Garran looked at him for a long moment out of the seamed brown face. Then she nodded, once, the way Ned nodded at a weather report that he did not like but believed.

"Right," she said, and took off her coat.

Ward put them through all of it. The run, up the wall stair and along the wall walk and down and round, twice, as his knights ran it every morning; and the run with the sack, sixty pounds of sand on the back, up the same stair. The rifle, in the butts behind the cart shed, the bolt-action, five rounds and then five more, lying and kneeling and standing, until every one of them could put five in a man's chest at a hundred yards. The square in the gravity hall at the knights' base, where the floor pulled at a man at twice his weight and he had to throw another man down on it and keep him there. And the door.

Fletcher had built the door. It stood in the middle of the yard on a frame: a mock airlock, two steel doors on hinges with the space of a small room between them, exactly the size and shape of the one Bram had drawn for the station's house, with a wheel on each door and a bar across the inner one. Ward put four of the crew inside it and four of his knights outside it and told the knights to get in, and the crew not to let them; and the knights got in, every time, for the first two days, through the outer door and the inner, in less than a count of thirty, and the crew picked themselves up off the cobbles, and Ward wrote it all down and said nothing.

He did not say anything for most of the first week. He watched. He watched them run and fall and get up, and shoot badly and then less badly, and be thrown down in the gravity hall by knights twice their weight; and Emeric, who came most mornings to stand at the rack for an hour, could read nothing on his face at all but a kind of patient unimpressed attention, like a man watching a pot that he does not believe will boil.

On the sixth day the four inside the airlock held it.

Orla Garran was one of the four. Emeric had watched her all week; he had watched her come last up the wall stair with the sack on the first morning, purple in the face and swearing steadily under her breath in the Hod's own language, and on the third morning come fifth, and on the fifth come third; and he had watched her in the gravity hall, thrown down on the pulling floor by a young knight of Ward's who was at least twice her weight, get up, and get up, and get up, and on the fourth time put the young knight down instead, by his own weight, with her hip, and sit on him, and stay there until Ward called it. In the airlock on the sixth day she did not do anything that Emeric could see from the rack. She stood at the inner door with the bar in her hands and said three words to the three behind her, low, in the Hod's voice; and when the knights came through the outer door, the crew were not where the knights had been told they would be, and the knights stood in the little steel room between the doors for a count of five, ten, looking for them, and the outer door swung shut behind them, and the wheel went round.

The knights were in the airlock. The crew were outside it, on the wheel.

"Trapped," said Orla Garran, through the door, to the knights inside. "That's what it's for."

Ward was quiet a while. Then he wrote something in the book, and Emeric, at the rack, saw that it was more than one word.

"Less," said Ward to Emeric, afterward, at the trough, when Emeric asked him. "Less unimpressed. That's all."

One of them did not pass. A man from the works, a good fitter, one of Bram's eight, who had built half the condensing plant's pipework with his own hands and knew the column better than anybody but Bram and Tess. He could do everything but the door. He could run and he could shoot and he could throw a man in the gravity hall; and in the airlock, with the knights coming through the outer door, he froze, every time, with his hands up in front of his face, and could not make himself move until it was done.

Ward told him on the last morning, by the rack, alone. Emeric was not near enough to hear. He saw the fitter's face, and Ward's hand go once on the man's shoulder and come away; and the fitter nodded, and nodded again, and went to the cart shed for his coat.

Bram met him at the yard gate. Emeric did see that. Bram put out his hand and the fitter shook it, and Bram said something to him, short; and the fitter laughed, surprised, the way a man laughs who has been braced for something and not got it; and the two of them went off down the hill to the works together, with Bram talking, about the column, about something on it that wanted changing, as if the man had been away for a week and come back.

"What did he say to him?" Emeric asked Bram that evening.

"That I'd want him on the ground end," said Bram. "Somebody's got to fill the K Endeavor's tanks when she brings the stuff down. Somebody's got to know what the column's doing when the ones up there radio down and say it's doing something it shouldn't." He shrugged. "He's the best fitter I've got. He just can't stand in a door." He looked at Emeric. "Not everybody can. That's not a thing wrong with a man. That's a thing about a door."

And that same afternoon, on the yard steps, Orla Garran met the two Kalden knights who would go up with the crew to the Well and sleep at its airlock for four weeks at a time. Ward had picked them. A tall fair young man from Milford, Brann Ashby, who flew better than he fought and knew it; and a woman of thirty from the port, Tamsin Reeve, a full-traced knight of the old rite with a long silver crack down the side of her neck where it had tried to kill her, who fought better than she flew and did not care who knew it. Orla looked at the two of them, up and down, the way she would have looked at two new lines to see whether they would hold.

"You'll eat with the crew," she said. "You'll stand watch with the crew. You'll sleep by the door, because that's what you're for. And if either of you ever tells one of mine what to do in my house, I'll put you out of the airlock in your suit and you can fly home." She held out her hand, the one with the short finger. "Orla."

Tamsin Reeve took it, and looked at the finger, and grinned.

"Tamsin," she said. "What took it?"

"A line." Orla took her hand back. "I've still got the line."



Merrin made the record on the twenty-fifth, and nobody in Kaldo ever let Maren forget it.

It was an accident; or Merrin said it was, and stuck to it. She had been building the thing for the pictures since the summer: the sound camera, the second box that had sat on the records hall steps at Commencement and hummed and done nothing, which was meant, one day, to turn a voice into light and put the light on the film beside the pictures as a stripe that a projector could turn back into a voice. It did not work yet. Its eye did not work yet; Merrin could not yet make a valve that turned light into current well enough to read the stripe back. But the other half of it worked. The half that took the voice in through the horn and made it strong, the amplifier, worked very well; and Merrin wanted to know how well, without the stripe in the way.

So she did it Emeric's way. He had told her once, in the spring, idly, that you did not need light to keep a voice. You needed a turning plate of something soft enough to cut and hard enough to keep the cut, and a needle on the end of the amplifier's arm that shook with the voice; and the needle cut the voice into the plate as a groove that wandered as the voice did, round and round; and afterward another needle, running in the groove, shook as the first had shaken, and gave the voice back. Merrin had written it down. She had found the soft-hard stuff in the Isle sap that Bram's floor had been boiling into hard rubber for a year for the hoses and the gaskets, and pressed it into a disc on a hot plate, and set it on a turntable from a clock-maker's movement that Ottoline had lent her; and on the twenty-fifth, in the afternoon, she set it turning on the bench in the little room off the kitchen passage that the castle had given her for the sound work, with the horn on its pole in the doorway, and the needle down, to see whether a voice would cut.

The kitchen hatch was open.

Maren was singing. She sang every afternoon, between the dinner and the supper, at the big table with the bread, when the kitchen girls had gone out to the yard for their hour and she thought nobody could hear; and she was not, by anybody's account, a good singer. She sang an old song from the river country, about a lighterman's girl and a drowned man, loudly, and somewhat flat, and with great feeling, all the way through, four verses; and the horn on its pole in the doorway of Merrin's room took it in through the open hatch, and the amplifier made it strong, and the needle cut it, round and round, into the turning disc.

Merrin listened to it back that evening, on her own. Then she fetched Emeric.

He stood in her doorway while she lifted the needle onto the edge of the disc and set the turntable going, and out of the horn, thin and small and crackling as if from the bottom of a well, but unmistakably, came Maren's voice, singing about the lighterman's girl.

He stood very still and listened to all four verses.

"It's a gramophone," he said, at the end, before he had decided to say anything.

"A what?"

"That's what it's called." He did not know how to explain that; he did not try. "A gramophone. And that's a record."

"It's Maren," said Merrin.

"It's Maren," he agreed. "Does she know?"

Merrin looked at the disc.

"No," she said.

Maren found out the next morning, because Merrin, who was very honest, went and told her. Emeric heard the rest from the yard. The whole castle heard the rest from the yard. Maren came down the kitchen passage to Merrin's room at a pace nobody had seen her use since the plague year, with a ladle, and demanded to hear it; and heard it; and stood in Merrin's doorway through all four verses with the ladle in her fist and her face going from red to white to red; and at the end of the fourth she said, in a voice that carried to the gate, that Merrin was to break it, now, this minute, in front of her, and that if she ever heard of it again, or of anybody hearing it, she would put every one of Merrin's wires in the soup.

Merrin did not break it. She said that she would not, very politely, and that it was the first one, and that the first one of anything was not a thing you broke; and Maren went away down the passage with the ladle and did not speak to her for two days.

On the third day she came back.

She stood in the doorway again, with her arms folded, and did not come in.

"My sister's in Wayford," she said, to the wall over Merrin's head. "She used to sing that one. When we were girls. She sang it better. She can't get about now." She did not unfold her arms. "If a person was to make another one. Of that. Could they? From the first one?"

"I can try," said Merrin.

"Then try," said Maren, and went away again, fast, before Merrin could say anything else; and Merrin, who was very honest, did not tell anybody about that part at all, except Emeric, and him only because he asked her how many discs of the hard rubber she would need for the pictures, and she said three hundred, and one.



On the twenty-seventh Bram came in from the island with a plaster on Corin's face in his pocket, as it were, and the rest of it in his book.

He told it at the table that night. Tess had been in Kaldo all month for the radio, and the Dust House had been Corin's and Wren's while she was away; and Corin, who was still taller than Emeric and still unrepentant about it, had been working down Tess's grid in her absence with her bench book open beside him, a square at a time, as she had told him to. Fire over lightning was her warm cell; it was in the book with the kettle and E. made the tea. Fire over water was next along the row.

"He put a fire glaze on a water tile," said Bram. "Keyed both. Set them on the bench. And they went." He made a short gesture with one big hand, open. "Burst. Not loud. Like a cup dropped. The tile flew to bits, and a bit of it went across his face, here." He touched his own cheekbone. "Wren put a stitch in it. He'll have a mark."

"What did it do?" said Armand.

"Before it burst?" Bram took his book out. "Corin had the meter on it. Tess's rule. It went right over. Right off the end, past where the warm cell sends it, past anything." He found the page. "So it's a cell too. Fire over water. A strong one. Stronger than Tess's, for its size, a good deal. It just doesn't like to stay in one piece." He looked round the table. "Corin wrote it in red in her book. The first red one. He says she told him to mark it red if it hurt anybody."

"It hurt Corin," said Imani.

"It did."

"Then it should be red."

"It's red." Bram shut the book. He looked at Emeric, and Emeric could see him turning something over, the way he turned a bad casting over in his hands to find where it had gone wrong. "It was too thick. That's what I think. Corin poured the glaze thick because Tess's warm cell was thin and he thought thicker would be stronger. It was. It was too strong for the tile." He put his hands flat on the table. "Thin. Every seam its own, and thin. Then put another on top of it, and another, each with its own key, each one thin. Like a pour. Like the dockhand's walls." He nodded to himself. "Lifts."

Tess was at the end of the table. She had not said anything; she was listening with her chin on her hand.

"That's what I'd have done," she said.

"I know," said Bram. "I'm telling them before you get back out there and do it, so they know it was mine first."



And on the twenty-eighth of October, very late, in the round tower that Armand had taken for his teaching on the dome's west side, Armand cast a light, and it came out wrong.

Emeric was not there when it happened. He was there a quarter of an hour after, because Armand sent for him, by the boy who slept by the tower's door; and he came up the tower stair in the dark to the round room at the top where Armand had taught his stone students since the spring, and found him alone in it, standing in the middle of the floor in his long dark coat with the sleeves that reached his wrists, with no lamp lit, looking at his own right hand.

"Shut the door," said Armand.

Emeric shut it.

"I was showing them dimming," said Armand. "The four. They'd asked. I've been putting it off; it's the hardest thing the tower teaches and I didn't want to teach it badly. Tonight I thought, well, you're thirty-two again, old man, you've the hands for it, show them." He did not look up from his hand. "So I drew a light. A plain light, a fire glaze for a lamp. Full, first, so they could see the shape of it. And then dimmed, as the tower teaches: the same glyph, smaller and fainter, squeezed down until it's barely there, all the lines of it still in it but tight, like a word whispered instead of spoken. It costs more. Everybody knows it costs more. It's the ceiling. It's as far as anybody goes." He turned the hand over. "And I did it, and it was dim. And they clapped, the four of them, very politely. And I sent them to bed."

"And?"

"And then I did it again, by myself, to see how dim I could go. These hands." He flexed the long fingers. "I went very dim. And at the end of it, Emeric, the glyph came up as a ring. A faint ring of light in the air in front of my hand, the size of a coin. And nothing in it. No lines at all. The light came anyway." He looked up then, at last. "It came anyway. Full. Out of nothing."

Emeric did not say anything.

Armand looked at him in the dark through the plain glass of his spectacles, the young broad face under the dark curls, the heavy brows drawn down; and Emeric felt the old man's note behind it, the thousand-room library of it, going over something very fast.

"Do it," said Armand. "You. Show me."

Emeric lifted his hand and put a light in the air over it, a small one, the size of a candle flame, without drawing anything.

He had done it for years. He drew a glyph when anybody but the six was watching, because a mage without one would have been noticed in any room in the kingdom; alone, he had not drawn one for his own fire or his gravity in a very long time, and he had always known why. It was the delve. It was the hybrid in him, whatever that was, the knight's inwardness and the mage's reach together, a thing nobody else had because nobody else was what he was. Armand knew he did it. The six knew. They had always taken it as one more strange thing about him, like his recall and his hands, and so had he.

Armand looked at the little flame hanging over his palm.

"I'm not a hybrid," he said.

"No."

"I'm a tower mage who ate a stone. A very good tower mage. I have been dimming for sixty years. I have never once in sixty years dimmed past the ceiling." He held up his own hand and looked at it. "And tonight I did. And I don't think it was the stone that did it. I think I've always been able to. I think every man who ever sat in the tower's long room could, if anybody had ever let him try." His voice did not change at all. "I think the glyph's a crutch."

Emeric let the flame go out.

"Prove it," he said. It was what Armand had said to him a hundred times across the long room table, and Armand heard it, and very nearly smiled.

"I can't. Not by myself." Armand turned and went to the shelves on the room's far side, in the dark, without a lamp, and came back with a book under one arm and a flat leather case under the other. "But you brought me the proof two years ago and neither of us looked at it properly."

The book was Ansel Vey's. The case held Ivo's prints of the mural from the cave under the Isle: the great wall in the dark under the sea, photographed by battery lamp in eight plates and printed in Ivo's yard, the old races at their work along it, the tall ones and the short broad ones and the others; and among their work, here and there, over a hand, over a pot, over a forge, the signs of magic being done.

Armand lit the lamp at last. He laid the prints out on the floor of the round room in their order, and opened Vey's book beside them at the plates Vey had copied from the same wall eighty years before, and knelt over them with the lamp; and Emeric knelt beside him.

"There," said Armand. "Fire."

It was a forge, on the third plate. A short broad figure at an anvil, and over the hearth, drawn in the old way in the rock, a sign: the fire's sign, the thing that hung in the air when the work was done. Emeric had seen it a dozen times. It was nothing like the tower's fire glyph. It was a different shape altogether, a spiral where the tower's had a star.

"Different people," he said. "Different glyph."

"Yes. That's what I thought. That's what Vey thought; he says so, there, in the margin. A different school. A different tongue." Armand moved the lamp. "Now look here."

The sixth plate. The same short broad people, the same kind of forge, the same work; and over the hearth, a sign. Not the spiral. A thing like three linked rings.

"Same people," said Armand. "Same work. Same wall. Fifty feet along it." He moved the lamp again, to Vey's book, to a plate Vey had copied from somewhere else on the wall. "And here, a third. A tall one, this time, at a fire, for cooking. And here's her sign." It was not the spiral, and not the rings. It was a single curved stroke, like a hook. "The same work. Three signs. On one wall. Made by people who were there at the same time, side by side, and did not, so far as anybody can tell, quarrel about it."

Emeric looked at the three signs in the lamplight.

"If it can be three things," said Armand, very quietly, "it isn't the thing. If a fire can be drawn as a spiral and a ring and a hook and burn the same, then the drawing doesn't make it burn. The drawing is just the drawing. A picture of what you want, made in the air to help you see it. And the seeing makes it work. And because it works, you think the drawing did it." He sat back on his heels. "So you teach the drawing. You teach children the drawing until their hands draw it before they've finished thinking. And every one of them grows up certain that the drawing is the spell, and is slow by the length of a drawing for the rest of his life, and pays for the drawing every time out of what he's got, because a glyph's made of mana too." His long hands lay open on his knees. "And the tower teaches dimming as the ceiling. The half-step. So nobody pushes past it."

"Somebody must have," said Emeric. "In all that time."

"Somebody must have." Armand looked at the prints. "And either the Church found him first, or the tower's old men did." He was quiet a moment. "I sat in that long room for forty years, Emeric. I sat with the elders. There were things the elders knew that they did not say even to a High mage, and I always thought they were things about stones." He shook his head slowly. "I think one of them was this."

Emeric was quiet for a long time.

He had always known why he could do it. He had been certain. It was the one strange thing about him that he had never questioned, because it had always seemed to him the least strange: he was what he was, and so he could do what he could. And now an old man in a young man's body was kneeling on a floor in the lamplight telling him that he could do it for the same reason anybody could, and that the only thing he had that nobody else in the kingdom had was that nobody had ever drilled him out of it.

It was a long time since anybody had told him he was less special than he thought. He found that he minded it a little, and then that he did not mind it at all.

"What do we do?" he said.

"Do?" Armand began to gather up the prints. "We do what Ferra would do. We count." He laid them in the case one by one, in order. "I'll teach the four. Tomorrow. The truth, and the drawing as well, because they'll need to read other men's glyphs to cut them, and other men will be drawing them for a long time yet. They'll drill. They'll be bad at it. You were bad at it, I expect, once."

"I don't remember."

"No. You wouldn't." Armand shut the case. "And nobody outside this tower hears it. Not this winter. Not until the war's done and there's a crown that can stand being told it's been teaching children to limp for five hundred years." He stood up, all at once, the long new legs unfolding under him, and held a hand down to Emeric. "And it will be you that shows them, when they're shown. Not me. Not some boy of mine in a yard. You, in front of everybody, when it matters."

"Why me?"

"Because you'll do it without meaning to," said Armand, "the way you do everything," and blew out the lamp.
