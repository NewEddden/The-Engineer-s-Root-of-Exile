Chapter 98 - The November Report

The bell went at a little before three in the morning on the twelfth, and did not stop.

Emeric heard it through his door, as he heard it every time, and was on his feet with his boots in his hand before he was properly awake. It was not the short hard ring of a body coming and going. It went on, and on, a long unbroken clatter from the top of the keep, and by the time he had his boots on and was out on the stair it had not changed, and he could hear Rylan's voice above it, shouting down the stair for the captain.

The line room when he came into it was all lamp and bell. Rylan was at the board. Ward was coming in at the other door with his sword belt in his hand. The shutter for the second drain, the middle one of the three under the narrow walk, was down and showing white, and the needle beside it was over at about the mark the board gave a grown man, and stayed there, and wandered a little, a hair over and a hair back, and stayed there, while the bell rang and rang.

"It came on and it hasn't gone off," Rylan said, without turning round. "I sent the pair. That's on the card. It's still there." He put his hand over the bell and it stopped, and the silence after it was very loud. "It's not coming in. It's not going away. It's a man's mark and it's sitting still in the mouth of a drain at three in the morning." He looked at Ward. "That's not on the card. So I woke you."

Ward looked at the needle.

"Too steady for a man," he said.

"Too big for a dog."

Ward buckled his belt. "Four crossbows," he said, to the stair, and went down it.

They went out by the water gate, Ward first, then the four, then Emeric, who had not been asked and had not asked, with two lanterns hooded, along the strip of mud and stones that ran under the wall between its foot and the river. It was narrow and slippery and very dark, and the river was up and loud on their right, and the wall went up on their left into nothing, and the drain mouths came one after another out of the foot of it, three low black arches with the water from the lower ward running out of them into the reeds.

At the second, Ward put his hand up. Everybody stopped.

There was something in the reeds below the arch. Something big. They could hear it before they could see it: a long wet tearing, and a pause, and a deep slow chewing, and the tearing again. Ward took the hood off his lantern and held it up.

It was a bullock.

A big red bullock, up to its knees in the reeds at the drain mouth, with a mouthful of them hanging out of the side of its jaw. It looked at the lantern. It looked at Ward, and the four crossbows behind Ward, all pointing at it, and at Emeric. Then it went on chewing.

Nobody said anything for some time.

"Do I shoot it?" said Osric, at last, who was one of the four.

"It is Hollis's," said Ward.

They drove it back along the foot of the wall to the gap in the hedge at the end of the river meadow, where it had come through, which took most of an hour, because it did not want to go and it was very much bigger than any of them. Osric fell in the river. The bullock did not.

Hollis came up to the keep at nine to say that the garrison had frightened his bullock off its feed, and that it had not eaten a mouthful since, and that it was a good bullock and he wanted it written down. Ferra wrote it down.



Emeric did the sum that morning in the line room, with the slate.

The board had never rung for a beast since September. Not a cat, not a heron, not the dog. A beast carried less than a person, for its weight; a good deal less. The big dog that weighed more than the smallest man on the gate crew had moved the needle half as far. He had drawn the line under the least a person carried, and Finn had come in over it and the dog had not, and he had thought that was the whole of it.

But a bullock was not a dog. A bullock weighed what four men weighed, or five, and a beast that carried a quarter of a man's worth for every pound of itself, and had five men's worth of pounds, came to a man, near enough. The line could not tell a bullock from a man. It could only tell how much. Anything big enough would come over it. A horse would. An ox would.

They did not come onto the wall. Nothing that size could get up onto the wall walk, and most of the stones were on the walk. But the three drains opened at the foot of the wall into the reeds, at the water's edge, and the river stair went down to it, and anything that could wade could get there.

Bram drove the stakes that afternoon.

Oak, from the saw's offcuts, as thick as a man's arm and pointed, driven into the mud at each of the three drain mouths in a half ring two lengths out from the arch, close enough together that a bullock could not get between them and far enough out that nothing behind them could come within a stone's reach. A stone felt a few paces and no more. Bram did not ask why it had to be two lengths. Bram had heard about the bullock, as had all of Kaldo by noon.

The line went on the card that evening, under the others, in Ward's own hand, which nobody had ever seen on anything in the line room before.

A mark at the drains that stays and does not come in. Look before you shoot. It may be Hollis's.

The garrison read it in turns all that week, in the guardroom, as if it were a letter from home. It was the only joke anybody in Kaldo had ever known Ward to write down.



He wrote the November report on the fourteenth, in an afternoon, in his own room, at the table under the window.

It was the quarter's account to the crown, the fourth since the twin year began, and it went the way the others had gone. The harvest, every sack, as Ferra had reckoned it in August. The fifth, and what it had bought. The engine, which was running. The wall, and the board, in a sentence, without the stones. Grayrock's ore and Grayrock's coal and what Kaldo paid for them. The soap and the spirit and the coast. The House's factor's line, accounts sound, security in good condition, copied out exactly as she had written it. The cost of everything, in Ferra's figures. It was true, all of it, and dull, and the dullness was the point; it was meant to be a report a clerk in the capital would file without reading past the first page, if a clerk in the capital ever had it in his hands, which nobody in Kaldo now believed.

Then he came to Milford, and stopped.

He got up and went to the drawer in the wall by the bed, the one with the lock, and took the key from the cord round his neck and opened it.

The letter was where it had been for a year and a half, on top of everything else, folded the way it had come out of the bale of hides in April of the year before. He took it to the table and unfolded it and read it again, though he knew it. Renner meant to come against Kaldo before midsummer. Renner had asked Milford for its levy and Milford would give it. The writer had no quarrel with Kaldo. The writer had no wall. Signed Paxton, of Milford. Nothing else.

He had kept it out of the war report. He had kept it out of the account he had carried north himself and put into the chancery clerk's hands last December. He had kept it in this drawer with the key on his own neck, because a letter like this was a man's life, and the man had trusted it to a cask of spirit and a slow carter.

And in August, in the gate yard, with his hands behind his back, the man had asked him to go on keeping it.

He sat and looked at it.

The law said that a baron who took the field in a private war forfeited his land. Paxton had taken the field. He had stood at the back and never drawn, and it would not matter, because he had stood there in his colours with his levy behind him, on a field where everybody could see him, and the law did not care where on the field. Grayrock's widow had written to the crown and her writ had come back inside a season. Wayford's baron had written to the crown about Kaldo's war in the summer before last, and the chancery clerk had let slip, in December, that Wayford's letter was on the file, and told it the other way. Grayrock's account of that war was on the crown's file, and Wayford's, and Kaldo's own, which he had carried north himself with the warning left out of it. Nobody had written a word for Milford. And when the crown came at last to rule on it, as Saban said it would, as Sethe told every table in the south it would, the crown would rule on what was on the file.

A defence that arrived after the ruling was worth nothing. A defence that was on the file first, with a date on it, was worth a great deal.

It would most likely never be read. He knew that. This report would go into the post house bag with the gate clerk's slip, and go north, and somewhere on the road it would go wherever the others had gone, into a thief's fire or a bad clerk's stove, and nobody would read a word of it. But the slip would exist. The twin would exist, in the House's coast house, with its date. If the day ever came when Keller asked what Kaldo had told the crown about Milford, and when, Kaldo would be able to put a slip on the table with a date on it, and Paxton would have been defended before anyone accused him.

And it would cut Sethe's string. That was the part he kept coming back to. Sethe held Paxton by the forfeit. Sethe told every hall in the south that Milford would want friends who could write to the crown. Well, Milford had one, and the friend had written.

I'd thank you not to spend it for me.

He had said that he would not.

He thought about it honestly, for as long as he could bear to. What Paxton owned, he had said, was that Sethe thought Milford was his. That was the thing nobody had priced. And the crown was not Sethe. Telling the crown was not telling Sethe. Paxton had meant: don't let Sethe know. He could not have meant: don't tell the king's own court the one thing that would save your land.

He knew, while he thought it, that he was telling himself what Paxton had meant.

He wrote it anyway. Two pages. That Paxton of Milford had written to Kaldo on the tenth of April in the year of the war, by his own hand, to warn it that Renner of Grayrock meant to attack, and had asked nothing for the warning. That Milford's levy had come to the field at Renner's demand and stood at the back of it by Paxton's understanding with Kaldo, and had not drawn, and had gone home. That the crown, when it came to consider the war, should read Milford as having stood with Kaldo, under compulsion, and not with Grayrock, and not with Wayford. He did not soften it and he did not dress it. He put the date of the letter, and where it was kept, and that the prince would produce it to any court that asked.

Then he folded Paxton's letter back the way it had come and put it back in the drawer, and locked it, and put the key back round his neck.



Hale wrote it out twice in his clerk's hand that evening, the crown's copy and the twin, word for word, and first thing in the morning Ferra checked them.

She did it as she had done every pair since January, at her trestle, with Emeric's own sheets in front of her and the two copies on either side, a ruler under the line she was reading, going down all three at once with her lips pressed together, a word at a time. When a page was right on both copies she initialled the foot of it, small, on each. She initialled the harvest, and the fifth, and the engine, and the wall, and the ore, and the coast. She turned the page.

She read the first Milford page. She did not initial it.

She read it again. She read the second page. Then she put the ruler down, and sat back, and looked at the wall of the back room for a while, and not at him.

"Does Paxton know?" she said.

"No."

She nodded, as if he had told her a figure she had already worked out.

"He asked you not to," she said.

"He asked me not to let Sethe know. This goes to the crown."

"He asked you not to spend it for him." She said it in Paxton's own words, which Emeric had told her in August and which she had evidently kept. "He didn't say to whom."

He gave her his reasons. All of them, in order, as he had given them to himself the afternoon before, and they sounded, said aloud across her trestle, very nearly as good as they had sounded in his head. The forfeit. Wayford's letter on the file. The date. The slip. Sethe's string. The likelihood that nobody would ever read it. He said that it was a kindness, and believed it, and heard as he said it that he believed it.

Ferra heard him to the end. She did not interrupt once.

"You're deciding for him," she said, when he had finished, "what he'd want."

He could have said a great many things. He had several of them ready.

"Yes," he said.

She looked at him for some time across the trestle. Then she picked up her pen, and initialled the two Milford pages on both copies, small, at the foot, as she had initialled every other page, because they were correct copies and it was her work to say so, and she did not do her work by halves because she disliked what was in it.

Then she drew her own book toward her, the year's book, and turned to the page for November, and wrote on it, in her own hand, under the date of the report.

Milford pages. Paxton asked that it not be spent. Said so. Overruled.

And the date. And blotted it.

He read it upside down, as he had read it the first time, about a dead man's retainer and a man called Cobb. She had been right then. There had been another time, over the mill, when he had sat and waited for the line and she had written the date and the price and nothing under it, and he had never asked her why. He knew now. She had not thought the mill was worth it.

She thought this was.

She closed the book and gave him the two copies, folded and initialled, and went back to her column, and did not look up again while he was in the room.



On the fifteenth he walked up to the post house on the north road with Hale.

He did not have to. Hale had carried every report since the first one and would have carried this one, and looked, when Emeric fell in beside him at the South Gate, as if he would very much have preferred to. But Emeric walked with him anyway, the half mile up the north road in the cold, with Hale holding the crown's copy against his chest in its oilcloth as he would have held a baby, and neither of them said anything all the way.

The post house's gate clerk was the same one as in January, a thin grey patient man with ink on his cuffs who had never once asked why the barony of Kaldo wanted a receipt for its letters and would not have been interested in the answer. He took the packet. He weighed it on his little brass scale. He held it to the window and looked at the seal, Bram's tower, with the nick in its shoulder that he did not know was there, and at the fold, which he did not know was anything but a fold. He wrote on his slip the weight and the seal and the day and the hour, the fifteenth of November, a little before ten in the morning, and signed it, and took his two coppers, and gave Hale the slip, and put the packet in the crown's bag on the hook behind him with the others.

Emeric looked at the bag on its hook a breath longer than he needed to. Then they walked back.



Dunnel was at the exchange window when they came down to it, paying his bill.

It was his turn. He had come in from the western circuit the night before with half a cart of fleece and the other half of a story about a ram, and he was settling his account with Ferra through the window, as he settled it every time he came through, in the same month it had been raised, to the copper, talking the whole while. He paid it in silver and copper counted out on the sill, and Ferra wrote it in, and he told her it was a pleasure to do business with a woman who never once let a man off a copper, and she said that was not what he had said in March, and he laughed so loudly that a woman crossing the square stopped to see what was funny.

Then he saw Emeric, and lit up.

"Highness. Highness. I have a thing for you." He went to his cart, where his boy was standing at the horse's head, and came back with something wrapped in a cloth, round and heavy, and put it into Emeric's hands. "From my sister. She's heard you eat bread. She says that's not enough for a prince and she won't have it said in Milford that we let a prince go short." He beamed. "It's a cheese."

It was a cheese. A whole one, the size of a small wheel, pale and hard and smelling of grass.

"Tell her thank you," Emeric said.

"I will tell her you wept," said Dunnel. "She'll like that better."

Ferra had the twin ready. She brought it out of the back room herself, the second copy, folded her way, with the strip through the slit and Bram's tower across the strip and the fold together, wrapped in oilcloth and tied, and Dunnel took it from her through the window with both hands, more carefully than he did anything else, and carried it to the cart, and opened the bale he kept at the front for it, and put it into the middle, deep, among the fleece.

"Round by the hill farms for the last of the wool," he said, tying the bale. "Then the coast. Ten days. Eleven if the ram's still there." He winked at the boy. "Make room."

The boy was fifteen or so, thin, in a coat too big for him, and he had stood at the horse's head the whole time without a word, holding the bridle, as he had stood the first time Emeric saw him in February, and the time in August. Emeric had never heard him speak. He spoke now, to Emeric, without looking quite at him.

"Is it true it does fourteen horses?"

It took Emeric a breath to understand him. The engine. It had been running for less than a fortnight, and the whole of the western circuit apparently knew what Tess's slate said.

"Fourteen."

The boy thought about that. He thought about it seriously, with his hand on the horse's neck, a big plain cart horse that was one horse and no more.

"I'd like to see that," he said.

"Come in the spring. I'll show you."

The boy nodded, and looked pleased, and said nothing else at all, and Dunnel told Emeric that the boy's name was Davy, that his mother kept the inn at the coast end of the circuit, and that he had not said so many words together since harvest, and that this would be all over the coast inside a week, and climbed up onto the cart.

They went out by the South Gate a little after noon, Davy driving and Dunnel beside him with his red scarf wound twice round his neck and the bale at his back. Emeric went up onto the wall above the gate with the cheese still under his arm, because nobody had taken it from him, and watched them go down the road and take the turn west between the bare fields toward the hills.

At the turn of the road, where it went down behind the first rise, Dunnel stood up on the moving cart, which a man should not do, and turned round, and waved the end of his scarf.

Emeric lifted his hand. Then the road went down behind the rise, and the cart with it, and there was only the road.
