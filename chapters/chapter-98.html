Chapter 98 - Below Weight

Fletcher's bill came up from the lower water in the middle of the spring, with the carter who brought the salt, and Ferra carried it up to the long room herself and laid it on the table in front of him.

It was a single sheet in the square careful hand, and it was a bill and nothing else. Frame iron, one length, cut and bent to the station. Rivets, fourteen, and six over for the ones that would not close. Coal for the rivet fire. Six days, himself. Six days, two men. And on a line of its own at the foot, above the sum, a charge Emeric had never seen on a bill from any yard in four baronies: One day, truing her with a line.

One Gold Rand, four Silver and seven Copper.

"He's charged you for measuring the twist," Ferra said.

"He measured it."

"That's what the line is for." She had her book open along her forearm, the way she carried it on the stair. "It's the fairest bill I've had off the lower water. He could have put the day into the six and you'd never have known it was there. He wanted it on its own line."

Emeric looked at it for a while. A man who had asked what and not why had gone and stretched a line along her from bulkhead to bulkhead, to see exactly how far she had been wrung, and then charged a day for the looking and set it down where the man who paid would have to read it. It was a question with a price on it, which was the only kind of question Fletcher had left himself.

"Pay it," he said. "All of it."

"I was going to pay it."

There was a second sheet folded inside the first, shorter still. Frames up to the twelfth station. Plating when the iron comes. Nothing under that. Fletcher had asked once, and he was not a man who asked a thing twice. He had gone back to his stocks.

Emeric had owed him an answer since the summer. He wrote it now, while Ferra waited, on a plain sheet, and it came to one line.

When there is something true to tell you, I will come down and tell it to you myself.

He signed it and turned it round and pushed it across to her, because every letter that left the keep went through her book, and she read it at the speed she read everything.

"That's a promise," she said.

"It's the one I can keep."

"It hasn't got a date on it."

"He'll know the date when it comes."

She considered that the way she considered a figure that was correct and not complete. Then she opened the dispatch book on the corner of the table and entered it: the day, the carter, the lower water, the shipwright. And then, in the margin, where the book had no column for it, one word more. He could read it upside down this time.

Owed.

She closed the book and took the letter and the bill down to catch the carter before he turned his mules, and Emeric sat on for a while with the line about the line. Then he went down through the works to the pattern store, where he had gone most evenings for three weeks to look at a plate.

The hold bench stood where Bram had built it, on its four short stands, with the scorch in the oak beside it and the four numbers pinned on the wall above. The plate sat on its stands. The last set had spent itself against Bram's iron fingers on the night the head was made, and nobody had set it since, because nothing else in the barony yet needed a bar held still.

He knew now what the first failure on this bench had in common with a hull section lying twisted in a foot of water. He had known it since the morning on the shelf.

The window corner had gone down. It had gone a hair at a time under the push of his work and not come back, and nothing on the bench could bring it up again, or bring the other three down to meet it. Every stone under the plate did one thing. It carried. You could ask it to carry more or less, once, on the day you set it, and after that it pushed up at exactly that until it died. And on the shelf below the foundry, two stones off the new face had gone on pushing up their two corners of a split section for fifty counts after it stopped mattering, because nobody could tell them to stop, and nothing in them could pull the other way.

Every stone he had ever set had been asked to lift.

But a man who wants a table level does not only lengthen the short leg. He can shorten the long one. A hold that could only push up was half a hold, and the other half would have to press.

In the yard Armand had taught him to put his work into a stone and leave the weight where it was. Nobody had ever taught him to add to it.

He wrote it up that night as a plan, one sheet, fair. Since the section, every trial went to Ferra on a sheet before anybody else saw it, however small, and this one was very small. The place: Armand's bench. The stone: one blank, an ounce, cut in the pattern store and entered. What he meant to ask of it, in a line. What could go wrong, which was the thing that had gone wrong in the autumn: eight years of the slab drill taking hold of him in the middle of the working, so that he set the blank above its weight instead of below it, and it rose. And who would stand where. Ward at the door. Armand at the bench, to read, and if it rose, to take it.

Ferra read it at her table and gave it back to him without a word, which he took for yes.

Imani came down to the room off the foundry office the next morning, before the blank was cut, with his plan in one hand and a sheet of her own in the other.

The plan was not his copy. It was in Ferra's hand, line for line, and he understood that Ferra had copied it before she gave it back, and sent the copy up the long street, and said nothing to him about it. It was not a stay. It was not nothing, either.

Armand was at the bench with the week's tracings from Nevin, and Ward was on the stool by the door. Imani laid both sheets in front of the old man and put one finger on the line with his name in it.

"If it rises, Armand takes it," she read. "No."

Armand looked at the finger, and then at her.

"Twice," Imani said, "I've had two fingers on your wrist on that bank. The first time you'd held a hull for four minutes and your legs sat you down. The second time you'd held a falling one for fewer than ten counts, and I couldn't get you off the settle by the fire until dark." She did not raise her voice. "I'm not writing up a third."

She turned her own sheet to face him. It was short. Her hand was quick and flat and made no allowance for anybody reading it.

From this day the Secretary of the Arcane catches nothing. No load, no stone, no man. Nothing that is falling.

Under it was a line for a name.

Armand read it twice, as he read everything.

"It would have floated," Imani said, before he could say anything. "The section. The plan said so. Ferra read the plan twice and came down to watch it float. It was going to fall a hand into a foot of water and lie there, and you caught it."

"My hands were up."

"That's the finding. A man with his hands up will catch. So you don't stand there."

Emeric, at the end of the bench, did not say anything. He had written the plan for the section. It had said the water would take her if she dropped, and then it had put Armand on the lip with his hands out, because if she dropped he could lift her. He had built a floor, and stood a man in front of it, and the man had gone first. Of course he had. That was what a man was for, in a plan like that. Emeric had only never written it down that way.

Armand took a pen from the tray of forty.

"Falling will not do," he said.

Imani waited.

"Everything I lift, I let down again. For the last part of the way, it is falling." He did not look up from the sheet. "As you have written it, I may lift a thing and never set it down. I will be standing on the bank with the Wayford hull over my head until one of us dies."

"Then write what you mean."

He wrote it in the crabbed hand, small, above the line: save what he has himself lifted. Then he signed under it, and blotted it, and turned it back to her.

Imani read the amendment the way she had once read Emeric's signature on a hospital date, as though it were a dosage, and found nothing in it to object to. She folded the sheet and put it away inside her coat.

Then she looked down the bench at him.

"And you," she said.

"I didn't catch anything."

"You were grey on that bank too. Ferra didn't send for me for you, because you were standing up." She looked at him the way she looked at a man who was telling her the cut had happened by itself. "You'd have tried."

He did not answer that. She was right, and she knew she was right, and anything he said to it would only have been a way of not saying so. She nodded once, as she did at the end of a history when she had what she needed out of it, and went out. Ward stood aside for her in the door, and then went back to his stool and was careful to be looking at nothing.

Emeric drew a line through Armand's name on the plan.

Then he took out the notebook and opened it at the second column. Under the rule for the matched set there was one word, which had sat there alone for three weeks, and he wrote the first line under it now.

Every trial stands on a floor that is not a man. Sand, a line, or water.

He looked at it for some time. He did not write an exception for himself. Imani's order was Imani's, and she had written it about one man. This one was his, and it was about all of them, and he was one of them.

It changed the bench by two things, and Bram made both in an afternoon without asking what either was for.

The first was a sheet of lead off the roll he kept for flashing, cut to the size of the pan and beaten flat on the surface plate, as thick as a thumbnail. That was Armand's condition, and the only one he made. "I have one bench," he said, and nothing else, and nobody needed him to say anything else. Whatever the blank did, it would do it to the lead first.

The second was a strap of iron bent over the pan like a staple, with its feet bolted to the frame and its crown a finger above the place where the blank would sit. If Emeric's body went to the weight the way it had in the autumn, and the blank went up, it would go up a finger and meet iron. If it went down, it would meet lead. There was nowhere in between for a hand.

Nevin cut the blank in the pattern store with Armand standing over him, off one of the black pieces in the chest: a cube the size of the end of a thumb, with six faces so flat it would not rock on anything. He swept up after himself into the lidded pail without being asked, and the pail went on the shelf by the door to go across to the etching floor on the seventh day with the others. Armand entered it against the stock in the strongroom ledger. One ounce, for a weight. At the Crown's price it was a Gold Rand and a quarter, a little under what Fletcher had charged for a week of three men and a new frame.

They did it that night, with the door shut. Armand sat on his stool with a clean sheet on his knee, ruled across under the four headings. Bram stood at the far end of the bench with his arms folded. Ward stood at the door.

"If it goes up," Emeric said to him, "it goes into the iron."

"I heard her," said Ward.

Armand had spent a quarter of an hour setting the needle to nothing with the lead on the pan, and with the blank on the lead it read an ounce and a hair. Emeric put his hands out over it and opened the medium, and the blank was there in it, a thumb of dense strange heat in a room of plain cold iron.

He put his work in, and his whole body went to the weight.

He felt it go as it went, eight years of catching things before they fell, and the needle came back off its mark toward nothing and the blank stirred on the lead as if it meant to go and find the iron. He was still on the thread. He drew the work back up it, all of it, cleanly, the way Armand had taught him in the yard, and the blank lay still and the needle went back to an ounce and a hair.

Armand wrote something on the sheet. He did not say again. He did not need to.

The second time Emeric did not reach for the weight at all. He thought of the blank as a thing already being pressed into the pan by a thumb, and he gave it the thumb.

The stone took it. It took it as readily as it had ever taken lift, which he had not expected, and in the medium it felt no different at all. Whatever the black stone was, it did not seem to care which way it was asked. The needle came off its mark the other way. It went past the ounce and did not stop. Two ounces. Four. Armand's bench had been built to read stones, not anvils, and the arc of its dial ended not very far past a pound. The needle ran to the end of it and lay against the pin, and the pan went down onto its stop with a small hard tick.

At the tick Ward came a step off the door with his hand out and open, the way he had come off it in the autumn for a stone going the other way. He stopped with it out. He looked at it. Then he put it behind his back and went back to the door.

Emeric kept on. Past the end of the needle there was nothing left to read it by but the lead, which was what the lead was for, as much as it was for the pan. He gave it more, and more again, until the blank was heavier than anything he had ever asked of a stone the size of a thumb, and then he let go of the thread and drew nothing back.

It was set. There was no way back.

For a breath nothing happened. Then the lead made a sound, very small, like a thumbnail pressed into wax, and the blank went into it.

It did not go fast. It settled the way a stone settles into cold clay, a hair and then a straw, and the lead came up round its foot in a bright little lip. Ward was counting under his breath without seeming to know he did it. Emeric had the blank in the medium the whole way down, and it was thinning faster than any stone he had ever set, the way a lamp turned up full burns its oil. That was the other half of the arithmetic from the autumn, and it held going down as well as up. The harder a stone was asked, the sooner it was spent.

At Ward's count of thirty-one the flecks in it began to go out, a few and then a great many together, and the blank stopped. It lay sunk to its shoulders in the lead, with its top face a little proud of the sheet, grey and dull, a thumb of dead rock.

Bram prised it out with the point of a knife. It came up with a small sucking sound and left behind it a square hole in the lead, with straight walls and a floor so flat that the lamp lay on it like water. Bram looked into it for a long time.

"Nevin cut that true," he said. It was the most Bram ever said about anything, and he had said it about a hole.

Armand wrote the ledger first, because the ledger came first. One ounce, spent. And under it, because he was a man who wrote down what was true, the cause. Then he turned back two leaves to the autumn, and looked from one entry to the other for a while, and Emeric, reading over his shoulder, saw the two lines in the same crabbed hand.

Set above weight. No way back.

Set below weight. Presses.

"Nobody," the old man said, "has ever asked a blank to be heavier."

He capped the pen.

"The tower carves anchors. A few, for moorings and the feet of cranes, cut to press at one weight and never any other, and it sells perhaps one a year. Every other coin it has been paid since the founding, it was paid to make a thing lighter. A carriage. A hull. The king's barge on a feast day. Why would anyone ask a raw stone for heavy? A mule is heavy, and a mule does not need a tower."

"To hold a thing still," Emeric said.

Armand looked at him over the ledger. It was not an answer he could write down, and he did not write it.

Emeric kept the rest to himself. It was lying on the bench in front of them with a square hole in it. The blank had held nothing still. It had pressed on the lead, and the lead on the pan, and the pan on its stop, and the stop on the bench, and the bench on the floor it was let into, and the floor on the island, and the island had not noticed. A weight with no frame was a press, and the floor carried it. Put the same weight at one corner of a plate, with lift at the other corners of the same plate, and it would press on nothing but the plate. That was a hold. He said none of it, because half an idea said aloud to Armand became a question he would be asked every morning until the other half arrived.

Armand was still looking at the two lines.

"The tower asks a stone for nothing it cannot sell," he said, "and it asks nowhere but the bench room. The bench room is at the foot of the tower, because the floor there is bedrock and does not move." He shut the ledger. "Mages who have crossed the northern passes will tell you they come to the top of the road with more in them than they started with. The tower has always said it is the thin air going to their heads."

"Has anybody gone up with a bench?"

"Up a mountain," said Armand, in exactly the voice he used for the constant, and that was all the answer the question got.

Emeric put it where he put the other things the tower had never looked at, which had become a long shelf, and picked up the lead.

Saban came back up from Wayford at the end of the week and brought the lake with him.

He told it at the table in the long room that evening, with Ferra at her end and Armand at his, in the voice he used for a comparison of prices when one of the prices had surprised him.

"Wayford has it," he said.

"Which one?"

"The barge. It was always going to be the barge." He spread his hands on the wood. "It's a grain barge now, and loaded. The old tower mage raised it off the bottom of the lake at dawn, grain and all, and set it down on the far bank, and there's a man in the inn by the ford who'll swear the grain came out dry."

"Dry," said Ferra, without looking up.

"In Wayford it's dry."

"In Wayford," said Armand, "I am a great deal stronger than I am in Kaldo."

Saban did not know what the old man had signed that week. He looked from Armand to Emeric, and saw that there was something there, and let it go.

"Do you want it corrected?"

"No."

"Helped along, then. It's a good story. It's got a mage in it, and nobody in Wayford has ever been surprised by a mage." Saban said it reasonably, the way a man points out that a cart is already going the right way. "Nobody in the capital either."

That was the best thing about the barge, and Emeric had seen it the night Saban first laid the four versions out on this table. A mage lifting a sunk barge was a thing the capital already knew how to think about. It would hear it, and put it on the shelf where it kept such things, and never once look under it. The fourth version was the one nobody believed, and it was the only one that needed a new shelf.

It was also why the barge would go further than any of the others, and why he did not want a hand on it.

"Leave it where it goes," he said. "Don't carry it and don't stop it. Anything we do to it now makes it ours."

Saban looked at him for a moment, and then let it go the way he let go a price he had decided was not worth arguing over.

"It'll be in Milford by the quarter," he said. "Past Milford I can't tell you where it goes."

"Nobody can," said Emeric. He had thought it on the walk back up from the shelf, and it was no more comfortable said aloud.

He sat up after they had gone, with the notebook open at the second column.

Flight. The floor. And under the floor, now, a third line, which he wrote slowly: Heavy. Set once, like light.

It was one step, and he knew exactly how long it was. He had the other half of a hold now, and it was precisely as stubborn as the first half. A stone told to press would press at that and nothing else until it died, the way a stone told to lift would lift. A plate held by stones that were told once would stay level until something moved on it, and then the low corner would stay low and the high corner high, and every stone under it would go on doing faithfully what it had been told on the day, all the way into the wrong answer.

He turned to the column for things he could not answer himself and wrote the question there.

Who tells it twice?

He looked at it for a long time. He did not have an answer, and he did not cross it out.

Down in the room off the foundry office, the lead lay in the drawer under Armand's bench, on top of the strongroom ledger, with a square hole in it the size of the end of a thumb. Armand had put it there himself, and had not said why, and nobody had asked him.
