Chapter 81 - Seven Carriers

The House's copy came back down the stair in the morning, in the hands of the boy who carried Vantic's wood up, with the fold and the strip and the cracked tower exactly as Emeric had left them.

Pinned to it was a half sheet in the House's form. The date. The hour. Under them, in her hand: The House receives the borrower's copies at its coast house. The House does not carry them.

Ferra read it at the trestle and laid it down.

"She's right," she said.

He had known that before he reached the end of the second line, and it had not made him like it. "Her courier goes to the coast house every week."

"Her courier is hers. If the copy goes in her bag, it goes through her hands." Ferra squared the half sheet against the edge of the trestle. "And her hands know what it's for now. You told her."

It was not a reproach. Ferra did not spend reproaches on things that were finished. She said it the way she would have said that lamp oil had gone up a copper, and that it was his lamp.

"Keller would ask who held it," Emeric said.

"Keller would ask who held it." She turned to a clean page in her book and wrote a heading on it. "So we need carriers."



She had five by the Friday.

Three were Saban's: the men who drove his carts to the coast and back in turn, and who had carried Armand's forty-one letters in December without losing one. Two were merchants who went to the coast on their own business every few weeks, and whom Saban said he would trust with his purse, which from Saban was a great deal more than a reference. Ferra had seen all five in the exchange, had their names in her book and their dealings beside their names, and had written a rule at the head of the page and underlined it. No man would know his turn until the morning it came. No man would carry twice running. Each would be paid the same fee, at the exchange door, on the day he set out.

"It's a good list," Emeric said.

"It's a bad list." She turned the book so that it faced him. "Look where they go."

He looked. He did not need to look long.

"The western road," he said.

"All five. Every one of them." She laid her pen along the page, across the names. "It doesn't matter what order I put them in. If anyone ever thinks to stand on the western road and watch what leaves Kaldo, he sees all five, and the order with them, inside a season. I want seven, and I want the other two to be the kind he doesn't see."

"One that doesn't use the road at all."

"There's nothing goes to the coast from here by water but salt."

"There's Fletcher," Emeric said.

Ferra looked at him for a little. Then she wrote the name at the foot of the page, under the five, with a question mark after it, which was a thing he had never once seen her put in that book.



Emeric found Abloh at the second vat, with his sleeves up past the elbow and the steam coming off the lye into the cold in a long grey sheet. The soap yard smelled the way it always smelled, of fat and ash and something underneath that bit the inside of the nose. Two girls were cutting bars at the long bench. Nobody looked up.

"What's Fletcher like now?" Emeric said.

Abloh went on stirring.

"Not much," he said.

Emeric waited. The paddle went round twice more.

"He was a lot, once." Abloh lifted it out and watched what ran off it, and put it back. "Ask Rylan. Rylan remembers him when he was a lot."

"Come down with me."

"No." Abloh said it to the vat. "If I come, he'll think I asked you to. Then he'll say no, so I'll know he didn't need asking." He tapped the paddle on the lip of the vat. "He's like that. He was like that before the war, and the war didn't help."

Emeric was at the gate of the yard when Abloh called after him.

"Tell him the soap's good."



They went down on the salt boatman's skiff, because it was going down anyway.

The boatman still made the salt run every month, and still thought he was paid badly, and said so at the first bend and not again. Rylan sat in the bow with his collar up. Ward sat in the stern beside the boatman, with his back to the town, and watched the banks go by as if he expected them to do something. The river was low and brown and slow, and at the edges in the early mornings there was a rind of ice in the reeds that the skiff broke going past, with a sound like somebody tearing paper.

"You didn't walk it last time," Rylan said, on the first afternoon.

"I had nothing last time."

"You had Abloh's wage."

"That was Abloh's."

Rylan thought about that for a while, watching a heron that had been standing in the shallows since before they came round the bend and had no plans to stop.

"Nessa said you'd go back for him," he said. "The night you came home with Abloh. She said a man who won't walk three streets is a man who's counting them."

"Nessa's a hard woman."

"She is," said Rylan comfortably. "I married her for it."

They slept the night at a reed-cutter's landing, in a shed with the cutters' bundles stacked to the roof, and Ward sat up most of it by the door with his sword across his knees, and in the morning the river widened and the banks went flat and brown on both sides as far as anyone could see, and the reeds stood higher than a man on a horse. The village came up out of them at noon on the second day the way it had the first time: smoke first, then roofs, then a landing of black piles and a row of punts drawn up on the mud.

Abloh's old house had somebody else's washing in front of it.

Emeric stood at the end of the lane for a little, where he had sat on Abloh's bench nearly three years ago and not walked it. Then he walked it.

It was three streets. It had always been three streets. The first was the lane, and the second was a crooked run of fences with nets drying on them, and the third ended at a gate of old grey planks that somebody had hung very well a long time ago and nobody had painted since.



The half hull was the first thing he saw.

She sat on her blocks at the top of the yard where the ground sloped to the water, a coaster by her lines, broad in the bow and full in the quarter. She had her keel and her stem and her sternpost and every frame, and planking on her up to the turn of the bilge, and above that nothing, so that the ribs stood up grey and bare against the sky on both sides like the fingers of two hands held up together. There was moss on the blocks. There was moss on her keel. A gull was sitting on the top of her stem, and it did not get up when they came in.

The man under the punt did not get up either.

He had the punt turned over on two trestles at the bottom of the yard and was caulking her seams from underneath, on his back on a sack, with an iron in one hand and a mallet in the other, and the mallet went on. Tap, and tap, and the iron moved along, and tap. He was a big man gone heavy with age, in a leather apron, with forearms like a smith's and a beard cut short with a knife.

"You took your time," he said, to the bottom of the punt.

"I had nothing to bring," said Emeric.

"So I heard." Tap. "And now you've got something."

"Yes."

The mallet stopped. Fletcher came out from under the punt in two movements, neither of them quick, and stood up, and he was taller than Emeric by a hand and broader than Ward. He looked at Emeric for exactly as long as a man looks at a plank he has been offered and does not expect to buy. Then he looked past him at Rylan.

"You're the boy that went in off the black hull," he said. "In March."

"I was pushed."

"I saw. I was planking on the next mooring." Fletcher wiped the iron on his apron. "I thought you'd drown. You hadn't the sense to."

"No," said Rylan. "I've been short of it since."

Something happened in Fletcher's beard that might have been the beginning of a smile, and stopped there, and he turned back to Emeric.

"I heard you had the boats up," he said. "Off the moorings."

"The iron out of them."

"Half of those were mine. I drove those bolts. I drove them through two inches of oak, green, by hand, the year I took my own slip, and I'd have told you they'd see me out." He did not raise his voice. "Where are they now?"

"Working," said Emeric.

"They were working."

"They were on the bottom."

"I know where they were." Fletcher looked down the yard at the river, which went by at the foot of it brown and wide and slow, and was not Kaldo's river any more, though it was the same water. "You want to hear something about your port, prince? Nobody killed it. That's the thing. Nobody did anything to it at all. A river's always trying to fill a port. Every winter it brings the hills down and lays them in your harbor a finger deep, and a port's only a port for as long as somebody pays to dig the finger out again. Your old baron stopped paying. One winter the coasters were touching at low water. The next they were waiting on the tide. The one after that they went round to the coast harbors and didn't come back, and I sat on my slip with nothing on it for a summer, and then I came down here." He jerked his chin at the half hull. "And laid her. On my own money. Because a coaster is the one thing every coast always wants, and somebody would come."

The gull on her stem shifted its feet.

"Nobody came," Fletcher said. "That's what a hull with no use looks like. Half of one. Bear it in mind."



Emeric told him what he had, and what he did not.

He did it standing in the yard with his hands empty, which was the only way he could think of to do it. He could not give Fletcher the port. Not this year, not the next. He did not know when, and he said so. He did not say why the barony wanted its copies carried, or who it was hiding them from, and Fletcher, who had been listening with his arms folded and his head a little on one side, like a man listening to a hull for rot, noticed what he had not said and let it lie.

What he had was a run. The barony's copies to the House's coast house, by water, down the river and along the coast, in Fletcher's own boat, when Ferra sent word that it was his turn and not before. The same fee as the carts.

"That's now," Emeric said. "This month."

"And the something."

"A yard. A slip, under a roof, on the far bank of the river above the east bridge, where the water still runs deep enough. Before next winter is out."

Fletcher looked at him.

"What for?"

"For you to build in."

"Build what?"

"A hull." He kept his eyes on Fletcher's. "I'll tell you what for when you're standing on the slip."

Fletcher said nothing to that for long enough that the mallet in his hand began to look like something he might put down. He did not put it down. He looked instead at Rylan, which Emeric had half expected, and which he minded less than he would have a year ago.

"Is he good for it?"

Rylan took his time. He had his hands in his armpits against the cold, as he always did, and he looked at the half hull and not at either of them.

"He paid Abloh every week since the morning he took him up the river," he said. "He paid two old men for the iron out of your boats, by the weight, on Bram's beam, in front of the market. He paid the gate crew to go into the river in November to get it. He's slow, and he's odd, and he keeps a list of everything, and he's never once told me a thing would happen that didn't." He considered. "He's good for it."

Fletcher grunted.

"Abloh?" he said.

"He wouldn't come," said Emeric. "He said to tell you the soap's good."

For the first time since they had come through the gate, Fletcher looked as if something had got past him.

"He sends me a bar of it," he said. "Every midwinter. Since the first year. Wrapped in a bit of sailcloth, with nothing written on it." He turned the caulking iron over in his fingers. "I told him it'd never sell. To his face, the night before he went. Tell him I know."

He went back to the punt then, and got down on the sack, and got himself under her in the same two slow movements, and the mallet started again. Tap, and tap.

"The run I'll do," he said, from underneath. "It's a wage. A man who's offered a wage takes it."

"Abloh told me you said that."

"Abloh talks." Tap. "The slip I'll believe when I'm standing on it. And when you've got it, you come down here and tell me so yourself, to my face. Don't write." The iron moved along the seam. "Letters out of Kaldo don't get anywhere, these days. Everybody says."

Ward, at the gate, turned his head and looked at the river.



It was three days back up, with the current against them and a wind off the land the whole way, and Emeric spent most of them in the bottom of the skiff with his back against the salt sacks, and did not talk, and nobody made him.

Ferra wrote Fletcher's name sixth on the page. She did not ask how it had gone. She asked how long the run would take, and he told her what Fletcher had told him, and she wrote that down beside the name, and it was the longest by most of a week.

"It's slow," he said.

"It's slow and it never touches a road." She drew the line under it. "I'll take slow."

The seventh he chose himself, and it took him two days to do it.

He did it from her book. He sat at the end of the trestle with the exchange's ledger for the last year open in front of him, and went down the market dues, page after page, every stall and every cart that had paid to stand in the square, looking for a man who went west every week and had never once been late. There were not many. There was one who was never late at all, who paid his due on the morning it was raised, the same morning, every time, for a year, and whose name was in more columns than anybody's but the barony's own.

He watched him on the market day, from the exchange door.

Dunnel was fifty, or near it, and broad in the middle, in a good brown coat that had been a better one, with a red scarf wound twice round his neck and a voice that reached the back of the square without his raising it. He bought fleece. He bought it a sack at a time from the hill farms, and argued over every sack, and won most of the arguments and was cheerful about the ones he lost. He sold whatever he happened to have in his cart to whoever stood still. He knew everybody, and told them so. He had a boy of fifteen or so to drive the cart and hold the horse, who looked as if he had heard all of it before. Every market day Dunnel came in from the west, bought, sold, talked, paid his due at the exchange window on his way out, and went back west, to Milford, where he was the wool factor, and on round the western circuit to every market between Milford and the sea.

Nobody looked at him. Everybody saw him, and nobody looked.

"He's Milford's," Ferra said, when he told her.

"Everyone knows he's Milford's. Everyone knows where he goes, and when, and what's in his cart, and what he paid for it, because he tells them."

"He'll tell them about us."

"He'll tell them he carries the barony's copies to the House's coast house," Emeric said. "Which is true. And dull. By the third time anyone hears it they'll be looking for a way out of the conversation."

Ferra looked at the name in her book. She looked at the column of dues beside it, paid on the day, every month, without a mark against one of them.

"He pays the month it's raised," she said.

"I know. I read your book."

She wrote him in, seventh, and drew the line.

Dunnel, when they put it to him at the exchange window that same afternoon, was delighted. He was delighted to be asked, and more delighted to be asked by a prince, and said so, twice, to the window and to the boy and to a woman buying candles who had not asked. He would carry anything for the barony at any time. He had carried for better men than a prince and for a great many worse. He named a few of each. He asked what the fee was, and Ferra told him, and he said it was very fair, and then said it again in a slightly different voice, and Ferra told him the same figure again, and he laughed and said that he could see he would get nothing out of her, and seemed very pleased about that too.

"My sister won't believe it," he said, turning his scarf round once more against the cold. "The barony's post. She'll say I've made it up. I make up a great deal, but I've never made up a prince." He winked at the boy. "Make room in the bale."



The first pair went out in the second week of February, on a Tuesday, on the same morning, because Ferra said that if they did not go on the same morning they were not a pair.

Hale walked the crown's copy to the post house on the north road himself, in its fold, with its strip, with Bram's tower in the wax and the crack in the shoulder of it, and stood at the gate while the clerk put it on his scale. The clerk weighed it, and turned it over and looked at the seal, and wrote the weight and the seal and the day and the hour on a slip, and signed the slip, and asked for two coppers. Hale paid him two coppers. Ferra had said he would want two.

The twin went west in the satchel of whoever's name stood first in the order Ferra had written at the back of her book. She had shown the order to nobody.

"Not even me," said Emeric.

"Especially not you." She did not look up. "You talk to people."

The roof went on Harl's mill the same week. It was the last of the work Bram's irons had been drawn for, back in the autumn, and the new rafters went up into the old walls on the straps and knees and bolts he had made, and the men put the last course on in a thin rain on the Thursday and came down. Harl stood in his yard with flour on his sleeves and looked up at it for some time without saying anything. Then he went back in to his stones.

On the Friday, Ferra brought two boxes into the back room of the exchange and put them on the trestle side by side.

They were tithe-house boxes, oak, with lids that lifted, and Bram had planed the old marks off them and not put any new ones on. She set the one on the left at her elbow and laid the gate clerk's slip in it, flat, face up, by itself on the bottom. Eight days later, the same as the House had taken over the amendment in the autumn, the carrier came back from the west with a line in the coast house's hand: one packet received from the barony of Kaldo, the borrower's copy of its quarter account, on such a day, by such a carrier, entered. It went into the left-hand box on top of the slip.

The box on the right she left open, with its lid back against the wall.

"Crown replies," she said, when he asked.

"There won't be any."

"No." She dipped her pen. "And in a year somebody is going to ask what the crown sent back. I'd like to be able to hand him the box."

He looked at it a while. Bare oak, and the light from the window lying across the bottom of it, and nothing else.

Then he went back to work, and so did she, and the box stayed where it was, open, at the right hand of the trestle, where anyone who came in could see it.
