Chapter 81 - The Shape of the Water

The first sheet came out of the mill in the second week of autumn, and it came out wrong in three places, and it came out.

The mill stood in a lean-to against the foundry wall on the downstream side, where the wheel's second shaft came through the stone. It was the smallest machine Bram had ever been asked to build that did not fit on a bench. Two iron rolls, each as thick as a man's thigh and a little under two feet long, set one above the other in a heavy iron stand, with a pair of screws on top that brought the upper roll down onto the lower a hair at a time. Jorrin had turned the rolls on the second lathe over most of the summer, a pass and then a night's cooling and then a pass, because Bram had not forgotten the spindle and would not let anybody else forget it either. The gears that turned them came off the wheel shaft through a clutch Bram could throw with his foot.

Neta brought the bar out of the forge at a yellow heat on a pair of long tongs, and Bram fed it in, and the rolls took it.

It went through with a sound like a cart going over a bridge and came out the far side longer and flatter and still glowing, and Wick caught it on a second pair of tongs and walked it round the stand, and Bram screwed the top roll down a quarter turn, and it went through again. Nine passes. By the seventh the iron had gone dull red and was fighting the rolls, and it went back into the forge, and came out, and went through twice more. When Wick laid it down on the flagstones it was a sheet as long as a man and a little under a forearm wide, thin enough to flex, and it lay there ticking as it cooled.

Nobody said anything for a while. It was flat. Nobody in four baronies had ever seen iron that shape that had not been hammered into it by a man over a week.

Then Bram knelt beside it and ran his thumb along the edges, and it was not flat. The edges had split in a dozen places where the iron had spread faster than it could hold together, a ragged fringe like the edge of a torn loaf. The sheet was wavy along its length where one end had cooled before the other. And when Jorrin cut two squares from it, one off the near side and one off the far, and set them on the scale against each other, the near square went down.

That was the other thing the summer had made, and nobody had thought it worth remarking. The master foot hung in its case in the yard, twelve divisions, never to be redefined, and in the early summer Emeric had had Jorrin turn a brass cup whose inside was cut from the foot, three divisions each way, and fill it with rainwater. What the water in the cup weighed was the pound. Bram had cast an iron pound to balance it, and then the halves and quarters and ounces off the pound, each checked against the one above and against nothing else, and they lived in a felt-lined box beside the rods on the finishing shop's shelf. The rag clerk at the foot of the keep stair had been paying for rags by them at the end of the summer, to the complaint of every woman on the island, who had been selling rags by the old pound since before the plague and found that the new one was slightly heavier.

The near square outweighed the far one by a quarter of an ounce. The sheet was thicker on the side nearer the wheel.

"Rolls aren't parallel," Bram said.

"The rolls are true," said Jorrin, who had turned them.

"Then the stand isn't." Bram stood, with a hand in the small of his back. "Or the screws don't come down together. Or both."

It was both, as it turned out, and it took the rest of the week to find out how much of each. But the sheet was a sheet, and on the morning after it was rolled Emeric had the ragged edges sheared off it and a piece a yard long cut from the middle, where it was most nearly even, and wrapped in sacking. He did not say what it was for. Bram did not ask, and then watched him carry it up the yard, and then went back to the stand.

Ferra priced it before he left.

She did it at her table, with the pen, while he stood. He had told her the shape of it: a hull, of iron, for the river first and then the coast, in sections that joined at set places so that one could be taken out and another put in. Fletcher to build it at the lower water. Kaldo to roll and cast and ship every piece of iron in her. Fletcher's men, Fletcher's yard, Fletcher's price for his time and his skill and whatever timber a hull made of iron still needed to stand up while it was being made.

"You don't know his price," she said.

"No."

"Then I'll give you mine." She wrote a figure on a slip and looked at it, and he watched her look at it. It was the same look she had given the Wayford road at the reading of the Order, a number written down and then attended to, as though it might do something if she took her eyes off it. "Ninety Gold Rand. The whole of it. That's what the column will stand."

"This year's column."

"Next year's." She turned the slip round so he could read it, and under the figure she had written, small, the first line of a year that had not started yet. "This year has had its two, and a trial, and a thing on the ladder you say doesn't count. You'll sign it this autumn and he'll lay the keel after midwinter. His first payment falls in the new year. That's the only way I'll enter it."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's the only way I'll enter it." She put the slip in the back of the book, where the entries lived that had not happened yet. "Ninety. Not ninety-one."

He went down the water with Ward on the second morning, with the iron across the back of a pack horse like a door.

Two days. The reeds on the first, and the long flat light of the estuary on the second, and late in the afternoon the smell of the sea arriving before the sea did. He had ridden this road twice in ten years, the first time with nothing to offer at the end of it and the second with three streets, and he noticed that he was riding it faster.

The streets were done.

He had not been down since he had walked them at the end of the last autumn, with the covenant a fresh line through a ledger, and named them to the old man on the beach before the old man had to. Now they were drained and bedded and dry, three streets of packed stone with a gutter down either side, running from the quay to the beach, and a cart was standing in the middle one with a load of timber on it, and the timber was not sinking. Forty men had been paid through a winter for that. He knew the number because he had signed it, in a column Imani had watched him fill instead of hers. He looked at the carts and thought about her register, and then looked at the carts again, and rode on down to the sand.

Fletcher was on the beach, where he always was, and he looked at the pack horse before he looked at the prince.

"That's iron," he said.

"It is."

"You've come a long way to show me iron."

"I've come a long way to put it in water."

Fletcher considered this. He was past sixty and had spent all of it bent over hulls, and he had a way of standing with his weight back and his hands hanging, as though the ground might at any moment want something from him and he was prepared to find out what. He had a hull on the frames behind him, a small coasting boat of the kind he had been building since the city port died, and two men planking it, and he did not stop them.

"It'll sink," he said.

"Yes," Emeric said. "Have you got a basin?"

He had a trough. It stood behind the steam box where the planks were softened, a long wooden trough half full of seawater, for soaking oak, and they walked up to it with Ward and the pack horse and the two planking men, who had found a reason to stop. Emeric unwrapped the iron and took the tin snips from his saddle and cut a square off one end the size of a hand, and dropped it in.

It went straight to the bottom. It did not even wobble.

"There," Fletcher said, and it was not unkind. "That's iron."

"Is there a smith down here?"

There was. The fishermen's smith had a shed at the head of the second street, and the fishermen's smith was a broad woman called Merrit with burn scars up both forearms, who looked at the prince and the old man and the square of iron with exactly equal interest and lent him her anvil and a round hammer without being asked why. He fished the square out of the trough, carried it up, and beat it into a bowl. It took a quarter of an hour and he did not do it well. It came out dented and lopsided, a shallow thing with a crimped rim, like a pie dish somebody had sat on. He carried it back down to the trough and set it on the water.

It floated.

It sat there, rocking a little in the ripple from the steam box, and rode, a grey iron dish on grey water. The planking men made a noise. Ward, who had seen Emeric do this sort of thing before and had long since stopped making noises, looked at the horse.

Fletcher did not make any noise at all. He looked at the dish for a long time.

Emeric took a handful of the long brown fastening nails from an old keg by the steam box, the soft coast iron Fletcher had complained of five years ago and had not driven into a hull since, and held one out to the old man. Fletcher took it and turned it over. Then, without being asked, he leaned down and laid it in the dish.

The dish went down a little. Not much. He put another in. It went down a little more.

He did it again, and again, one at a time, slowly, watching the rim, and around the eighth nail he began to talk, not to Emeric, not to anyone, in the low even voice of a man working a sum at a bench.

"Sits lower," he said. "Same as a loaded boat. Same as a hold when you've put the stone in her." Another nail. "She's not floating because she's light. The bowl's heavier than the square, it's the same iron. She's floating because she's pushed out that much water, and that much water weighs more than she does." Another. "Put in more, she goes down till she's pushed out enough to match it. Same as anything. Same as oak." Another. The rim was a finger's width off the water. "And when she's pushed out all she can, when the water's at the rim." He laid the next nail in, very gently, and a thread of seawater came over the crimped edge, and then a sheet of it, and the dish tipped and went to the bottom of the trough and lay there next to the square, with its nails spilled round it.

"She goes down," Fletcher said. "Same as a swamped boat."

He straightened up.

"It's how much water she moves," he said. "It always was. Oak or iron."

"Yes."

"Forty years." Fletcher looked at the trough. "Forty years I've trimmed boats to the inch. I can tell you where a hull will sit with a ton of salt fish in her aft, and I can tell you by the look of her. I know that. I know it better than you." He was not angry, exactly. He was something that sat next to angry and was mostly directed at himself. "And every stick of wood I ever put in water floated, and every bit of iron I ever dropped went down, and I thought that was a thing about wood and iron."

"It was a good rule," Emeric said. "It was right every time you tested it."

"It was right for the wrong reason."

"That's the worst kind. You can't find it by being careful."

Fletcher gave him the look from five years ago, not quite approval and not far from it, and turned round and walked off up the beach, and stood by the hull on the frames with his back to all of them for about a minute, and came back.

"Right," he said. "Seams."

It went on until the light went, and then by a lamp in Fletcher's house, which was a stone cottage behind the yard with a table too big for the room and drawings pinned all over one wall of hulls that had never been built. The old man had got over being annoyed and had gone straight on to being a shipwright, and a shipwright was a man who had spent his life thinking about every way water could get into a thing and kill the men inside it.

Seams first. A wooden hull was caulked, oakum hammered into the gaps and pitched over, and swelled tight when it was wet. Iron would not swell. Emeric drew him a lap, one plate over the next, and a double row of rivets through both, driven hot so that they shrank as they cooled and pulled the plates together harder than any man could hammer them. Fletcher asked what went between the plates. A strip of canvas soaked in tar and red lead, Emeric said, and then caulked along the edge with iron as well, a blunt chisel driven along the lap to close it. Fletcher asked how he knew that. Emeric said he did not know it for certain and would find out on the first section, which was true, and which Fletcher accepted, because it was the kind of answer a shipwright gives.

Holing. A wooden boat holed on a rock could be patched by a man with a plank and nails. What did you do with iron? This was the one Emeric had been waiting for, and he drew it: not a hull but a row of rooms. Iron walls across her inside, at set places along her length, watertight, so that a hole let the sea into one room and no further. She would settle by the head and swear about it and float home.

"And then?"

"And then you cut the room out."

He drew that too. The hull made in sections, each built to the same stations, the same frames at the same places, joined to the next with a heavy flanged frame and a double row of rivets. A holed section came off at the flanges. A new one went on.

Fletcher looked at the drawing for a long time. "You want me to build her in pieces."

"I want you to build her so she can be taken to pieces."

"That's heavier."

"It's heavier," Emeric agreed. "Every station frame has to carry a join. They'll be the heaviest frames in her."

"Heavier than they need to be for a join." Fletcher put a thick finger on one. "You've drawn these twice the scantling."

"I'd like them to be."

It was not an answer, and Fletcher knew it was not an answer, and he looked at the prince across the lamp for a moment. Then he let it go, the way he had let the sand and the salt go five years ago, and Emeric did not add anything.

Rust at the waterline, where iron met air and water both and suffered most. Paint, and a lot of it, red lead and oil, renewed every season, and plates there rolled thicker than the rest. Fletcher said a boat nobody painted was a boat nobody was going to keep, whatever she was made of, and wrote it down.

Cost. Plate was dear. Fletcher knew what a keg of the soft coast nails cost, and what a hull's worth of plate would cost at that rate made him laugh out loud. Emeric told him what Kaldo's foundry charged Kaldo for iron. Fletcher stopped laughing.

Repair, three days from a forge. That was a real one. A wooden boat could be mended by her own crew on a beach with an axe. An iron one needed a smith, and a fire, and rivets.

"She carries a smith," Emeric said. "And a box of rivets, and a small forge. Every boat of hers will. That's a cost."

"It's a man's wages you don't have to pay on a wooden boat."

"It is."

Fletcher wrote that down too, and looked at it.

"Weed," he said.

Emeric waited.

"Anything that sits in salt water grows a beard. Weed, and shell, and worm. Worm we'd not have with iron, I'll give you that, that's worth a lot. But the rest grows on anything. On oak you sheathe her in copper and it stays clean, mostly, because nothing likes to live on copper. You can't put copper on iron. The water eats one or the other, everybody knows that." He had not known everybody knew it until he said it. "So what do you do with her bottom?"

Emeric had nothing.

He knew it was a problem. He could see it, the grey fur of weed on a grey hull, a knot slower every month. He could not remember, from anywhere, what the answer was, and he looked for it, sitting at Fletcher's table in the lamplight, with the old man watching him, and found the place where it should have been and nothing in it.

"I don't know," he said.

Fletcher looked at him with an expression Emeric could not immediately read, and then realised he had seen it before, on Bram, in a hot shop, the first time Emeric had got something wrong in front of him. It was relief. It was a craftsman discovering that the man across the table had a bottom to him.

"Haul her and scrape her," Fletcher said. "Every season. Same as we do now." He wrote it down. "Put it on the list with the smith."

They got to money when the lamp needed filling.

Fletcher's price was his men's time, and his, and the staging and the timber for building a thing of iron that would not stand up on its own until it was nearly finished, and the use of his beach for a year when he could have had two small hulls on it instead. He worked it out on the back of a drawing of a boat that had never been built, and said ninety gold, half when the keel was down and half when she floated, and Emeric had the strong impression that he had arrived at it honestly and without the least idea what anybody in the keep had written on a slip of paper.

"And a section," Emeric said. "A spare. One midships section, to the same stations as the ones in her, built and finished and kept by. For the repair stock."

"That's your argument for the pieces." Fletcher nodded slowly. "A holed one comes out, that one goes in, she's back on the water in a week." It was the modularity working exactly as it had been sold to him, and he liked it, because it was good sense, and because it was the kind of thing a man with a yard should have thought of. "Eight more. It's the dearest section, midships, she's fullest there."

"Ninety-eight."

"Ninety-eight."

Emeric signed it. He thought of the slip in the back of Ferra's book and signed it anyway, and wrote the eight on the next line by itself so that she could see exactly where it had come from.

Fletcher sat back. The cottage creaked round them in the wind off the bar.

"How's Abloh?" he said.

"Well."

It came out a word too short, and he heard it come out, and so did Fletcher. The old man waited a moment for the rest of the sentence, and it did not arrive, because there was not a great deal more to it. Abloh ran the soap house vats and ran them well, and had come up the river eight years ago on the strength of something he had never asked him, and had vouched for him to this man in a way Emeric had never been told about and could only guess at. He saw Abloh twice a month, across a vat, about lye. He could not have said what Abloh did on a Sunday.

"He says you're straight," Fletcher said. "Says you don't know half what you think, and you know it, and you'll say so." He looked at the drawings on the wall. "I thought that was Abloh being kind. He's never been kind in his life."

Emeric did not have anything to say to that either, and Fletcher did not seem to need him to.

"Will she want a sail?"

"Not for now," Emeric said. "I'm working on something."

Fletcher waited. Nothing else came. He was a man who had made his living on knowing when a customer was going to tell him more and when he was not, and he nodded as though the price of rope had been named, and got up and filled the lamp.

In the morning Emeric went down to say goodbye, and found the old man already on the beach at the far end, away from the hull on the frames, where the sand was flat and empty and ran down clean into the water.

Fletcher had a stake in one hand and a mallet in the other, and a coil of line over his shoulder. He drove the stake in. He tied the line to it and walked away from it, counting, with his head down, paying the line out behind him, his lips moving. At thirty-one paces he stopped and looked back at the stake and then at the water, the way a man looks at a thing that is already there.

He drove the second stake in, and stood between them for a while with the mallet hanging from his hand, and did not look round.
