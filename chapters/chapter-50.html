Chapter 50 - Grain for Stone

Emeric learned that Grayrock had a holder from a carter at the Left Bridge, in one sentence, while the man was waiting to be waved through.

"The hill's got its writ," the carter said, to Ward, not to him. "Crown's put the widow in, to hold it for the girl."

That was all. There was no document. Nobody at Grayrock had written to Kaldo to say so, and nobody in the capital had either. The crown had found the time to confirm the widow of a man who had marched on a prince of the blood, and had not found the time to mention it to the prince. Emeric filed that with everything else the capital had not found the time to write to him about, and it fitted there without any trouble at all.

He rode that week.

He took Ward and Saban and a cart, and nobody else, and no banners. He had decided in the first week after the war that the body was going home, and then he had not sent it, because for more than a year there had been nobody on the hill with the standing to receive a baron, and nobody in Kaldo with any business handing one to a steward. Ferra had written to Grayrock twice in that time and had nothing back. Now there was somebody on the hill with the right to answer.

Renner had lain in the crypt under Doran's church since the summer before last.

Emeric had done the work on him himself, on the second evening after the field, and it had held. He had gone down every season since to see that it was still holding, alone, with a lamp, and it always had been. What it cost him he had told nobody, and nobody had asked, and he preferred it that way.

They loaded the coffin before light. Doran came down to the crypt without being sent for and said the words over it that he would have said over anybody. Then he stood back while Ward and Saban lifted it, and did not ask what Emeric meant to do, because he knew.

The road up to the hill was a day of bad going. It had been bad the year before and it was worse now, cut into ruts by the autumn carts and then frozen into them, and every mile of it was a mile the covenant said Emeric could not mend. The cart jolted and the coffin shifted, and Saban stopped twice to rope it again. Nobody spoke much.

They came up onto Grayrock in the last of the afternoon.

It was a hard place. A stone hall on a stone shoulder, with the workings below it scarring the slope and the spoil running down in long grey fans toward the river. The wind came straight off the high ground and did not stop. There were men at the gate, and Emeric knew two of them before they knew him. The last time he had seen them, they had been lying in Imani's ward with their forearms in splints. They placed him a moment later, and did not know what to do with their faces.

He got down in the yard.

A steward came out, and behind the steward a woman in dark grey, and Emeric did not wait for anybody to offer him a chair or a fire or a name.

"The cart has Lord Renner in it," he said. "I've brought him home. He's yours, whatever you decide about anything else I've come to say. That doesn't change. He stays yours."

He meant it entirely.

And he heard, half a second after the last word, what it looked like. A dead husband in a cart in the yard, delivered by the man who had killed him, in the first minute of a visit about business. It looked like a bargaining chip laid on a table, which was the one thing it was not, and there was no way to take the words back and say them in a better order.

The woman looked at the cart for a long moment. Then she looked at him.

"Bring him into the chapel," she said to the steward. And to Emeric, "You'd better come in."

Bryda Renner was forty or a little past it, straight-backed, with a ring of household keys at her belt that she had plainly not stopped carrying when the household became a barony. The room she took him into was a working room, not a hall. A fire. Three chairs at a table, and a fourth by the window.

The table was covered in papers, and the papers were in two kinds of order. Some of the piles were squared and tied and labelled in a small, even hand. The rest were in a heavier hand that sloped to the right, and those bristled with slips of paper, each one standing up at a place where somebody had stopped reading and meant to come back.

There was a girl in the fourth chair. Fifteen, perhaps, with her mother's straight back and her hands folded in her lap. Nobody introduced her. The steward glanced at her as he came in, as a man glances at a thing he expects to be told to move. Bryda did not look at him, and the girl stayed where she was.

She had been put in that chair to hear everything said in that room, and within a minute Emeric could see that she meant to. Her eyes went to whoever was speaking and stayed there until the speaker finished. Whenever anybody named a figure, they went to her mother.

He had not finished sitting down when Bryda put it on the table.

"You killed my husband."

She said it like a careful clerk reading out the first line of an account, because it was the first line, and nothing under it could be read until it had been.

"Yes," Emeric said. "My ground. My plan. My orders."

She nodded as if he had confirmed a figure.

She was neither hostile nor gracious. He had come up the road braced for one or the other, and after a few minutes he understood what she was instead. She had run a household on this hill for twenty years without a single thing in it going wrong that anybody had heard about. She had been permitted to run the household. She had not been permitted to understand the hill. Now she held the hill, in trust, by a writ, and she was learning it as fast as a person can learn a thing, in front of the man who had made the vacancy.

He could not read her face. It did not seem to matter much. She said what she thought, in the order she thought it, and left him nothing to guess at.

He did not apologise.

He had thought about it all the way up the road, and he had decided, and he said the reason out loud, because he did not think she would respect a reason she had to guess.

"I'm not going to say I'm sorry," he said. "You'd take it as a courtesy. You'd price it as one. And you'd think less of me for spending it in this room."

Bryda looked at him for some time. She did not tell him whether that was the best thing he had said since he got down in the yard or the worst, and her face did not tell him either.

By the window, the girl's eyes moved from him to her mother and back. Nothing else about her moved at all.

He put the proposal plainly, because there was no other way to put it.

Grayrock's high-grade veins, the crystal-bearing stone and the good iron, sold to Kaldo at a fixed standing price, and to Kaldo only. Paid for in grain, at a fixed standing rate against the stone, carted up to Grayrock every season. Three years, and renewable for as long as both sides wanted it.

"I'm not doing you a favour," he said. "You're not doing me one. I need the stone. You need to eat. Nobody else within reach of this hill will sell you grain at a price that doesn't move when your harvest fails, and nobody else will buy your best stone at a price that doesn't move when the market does."

"And now," Bryda said. "This winter. We're short."

"The first carts come out of my cellars. They can be at your gate inside a week."

The cellars were where Kaldo's grain had gone in the spring before the war, when Emeric had scattered the stores across the island so that nothing a torch could reach would starve the town. The thing he had kept from the fire was the thing he was now offering to the widow of the man who had come to light it.

He did not point that out. Neither did she. One of them had certainly noticed.

Her objection was honest, and it was the best argument anybody made at that table.

"The men who buy from this hill," she said, "have been squeezing it for as long as I've been alive. I know them. I've sat at the foot of the table in the hall for twenty years and poured their wine while they did it, and I listened. I know what they pay, and when they lie, and how far." She folded her hands on the papers. "They're bad, and I know how bad, and I've lived inside that my whole life. You, I don't know. You might be better. You might be worse in a better coat. I've no way to price you, Highness, and every reason not to guess."

It was a good argument. He had no answer to it that asked her to believe anything, so he did not ask her to.

"Your husband's men came up my road on a picture," he said. "A broke plague town. A mad prince. Forty-odd men and half of them sulking. Every part of it was wrong, and they found that out on my ground."

He let that stand for a moment.

"You are, right now, the best-informed person in this kingdom about what I am. Better than my father. Better than any house in the capital. You paid for that in the field, in your husband and in the sixty men he took down that road. You don't have to trust me. Nobody else in Randillia has what you have. I'm asking you to use it."

Bryda heard him out. Then she said no.

She did not say it angrily, and she did not soften it or leave a door in it. She said that she would not tie the hill to Kaldo on those terms or any others, not this year, and that she was grateful for her husband, and that the steward would see to the cart.

She was not bluffing. Emeric had sat across enough tables to know the difference, and there was nothing in her face that was waiting to be pushed.

He did not argue.

"The terms stay open," he said, and stood. "As long as you want them. They don't lapse. And your husband stays yours, whatever you decide about any of this. He was never part of it."

He picked up his gloves and went to the door.

He had his hand on it when she spoke.

"Sit down, Highness."

He turned.

She did not tell him what had happened in her in the ten steps between the table and the door.

She had watched him lose. He had come up a bad road with the one thing she could not refuse lying in a cart in her yard, and when she said no, he had not reached for it. He had not said then bury him yourself. He had not so much as glanced toward the chapel. He had stood up, told her the dead man was hers regardless, and gone to the door. She had sat twenty years at a table where men used whatever they held against one another, her husband as readily as his guests, and she could not remember one of them putting down the thing that would have worked, at the moment it would have worked, and walking away.

And there was a girl in the fourth chair. Since the writ came, Bryda had been turning over what kind of man the hill could safely be tied to if she was not there to watch him. She had just been shown one.

That was what changed.

What she said was something else, and it was also true.

"I've run it again," she said, when he was sitting. "Your grain at a fixed rate is worth more to this hill than their coin at theirs. It's worth more every year the harvest fails, and up here it fails one year in three. As long as you're alive to ship it, the sum favours you." She turned a sheet over. "That's all it is. It isn't forgiveness. Don't take it for any."

Emeric believed the sum completely. It was a good sum. He would have come to it himself, and he was pleased with her for coming to it, and it did not cross his mind that the sum was not the whole of it.

Saban wrote out the heads of terms that evening, at the end of the table, in his square merchant's hand, and Bryda read them before the ink was dry.

She stopped at the second line.

"It says the holder of Grayrock," she said. "Not my name."

"Holders change," Emeric said. "The grain shouldn't have to."

He had told Saban to write it that way on the road up. The woman across the table held the hill in trust, and a trust was a chair somebody minded until its owner was old enough to sit in it, or until some man married into it. A contract made with whoever was minding the chair could die with her. He wanted one that would not care who sat there next.

"So if I'm in the ground before the three years are out," Bryda said, "the carts still come up."

"They still come up."

She read the line again, and nodded, and turned the page.

By the window, the girl's eyes had gone to her mother at in the ground, and they stayed on her a moment longer than they had stayed on anything else.

Saban and the mine-master settled the weights and grades the next afternoon.

They did it in a cold shed by the ore bins, the two of them, with a set of scales and a bucket of samples, and they did it happily, the way two competent men settle anything when nobody above them is in the room. By dark they had a table of grades, a table of weights, and a rate for each, and each had decided that the other was the first honest man he had met in that line of trade in a year.

The mine-master was short and broad, with grey grit ground so deep into the backs of his hands that it would not wash out. When the tables were done he walked Emeric along the upper workings in the last of the light, because Emeric asked to see them.

The shafts were shallow, and fewer than they should have been, and half of them were short of men.

"There's better below," the mine-master said. "There always was. The good stone runs down, not along. The old lord never put the crews on it, and never told anybody why. Kept the hill in his own head." He shrugged. "Put the money into the men who went down your road instead."

Emeric asked how far down the better stone ran. The mine-master said he did not know, because nobody had been let dig to find out.

"The lady's been down here more this last year than in the twenty before it," he added, and spat, at nothing in particular. "Asks good questions. Writes the answers down."

On the way back they passed a working on the lower slope that had been boarded shut, the timbers behind the boards gone black with rot. The men coming off shift walked wide of it without looking at it, as men walk wide of a grave.

"That one's closed," the mine-master said, before Emeric could ask. "Years back. Bad air. A lamp won't stay lit in it." He did not slow down. "The men will tell you worse, if you let them."

Emeric did not let them. Bad air in an old working needed nothing worse to explain it.

The roads did not settle.

That argument went on for three days, in the working room, with the steward and the mine-master and Saban and a great deal of paper, and at the end of the three days it had not finished. The road up the hill was bad. Everybody agreed it was bad. A good road would halve the cost of every load that ever went over it, in either direction, and everybody agreed about that too.

And a good road up a hill was a new project, and Kaldo was under a covenant that said Kaldo could not start one.

So they agreed a schedule that assumed the road would stay bad. Smaller loads, more of them, slower. Carts in the dry months and fewer in the wet. It was an expensive schedule. Everybody at the table could see how expensive, and nobody at the table could do anything about it.

Bryda sat through all three days. Twice she asked the steward to explain something he had said, and the second time he could not, and she wrote that down as well. The girl sat through them too, and wrote nothing down, and did not need to.

On the second evening Emeric asked Bryda about the deeper stone.

"Your mine-master says the good veins run down, and nobody's dug to find where they end. Why didn't your husband work them?"

"My husband didn't discuss the hill with me," Bryda said.

She said it like everything else she had said at that table, as a line that belonged in the account. Then she laid her hand flat on the nearest pile in the heavy, sloping hand.

"Those are his," she said. "The hill's books. He kept them himself. I've been reading them since he died, and they don't say." She took her hand away. "He talked the household over with me. The stores, the linen, the maids' wages, whether the chapel roof would see another winter. I did those well, and he said so, often, in front of guests. He was never unkind about it."

She looked at the slips of paper standing up out of the pile.

"The hill was his business," she said. "Every man who ate at that table thought so, and so did their wives, and I suppose I did too, until it was mine. If there's better stone down there, I'll know where before your carts have run a year."

Emeric looked at the two kinds of paper on the table, and then at the girl by the window, who had heard every word, and understood why nobody had sent her out. Bryda Renner was not going to have her daughter put out of any room where the hill was being discussed.

He did not ask about the deeper stone again. There was nothing more she could tell him. The answer was somewhere under the hill, and she had already started digging for it.

They buried Renner on the fourth morning.

He went into the ground in the chapel yard at Grayrock with his own priest, and his widow, and his daughter, and the men who were left. There were not many. There were the few who had come home from the field, some of them on legs and arms that had been set in Kaldo, and there were the ten who had been left behind to hold the hill while the rest marched. They stood in a close group in the wind with their hats in their hands. Some of them wept, and none of them looked ashamed of it.

Emeric attended. He stood at the back, by the chapel wall, with Ward beside him and Saban beside Ward.

Nobody invited him to speak. He had not expected to be invited, and he would have refused, and he was glad he did not have to.

The priest said the words, very nearly the ones Doran had said in the crypt, in a different voice. The girl stood at her mother's side, and when the first earth went in, Bryda's hand came to rest on her daughter's shoulder and stayed there until it was done. Then the widow went in with the girl beside her, and the men followed in ones and twos. The two who had lain in Imani's ward looked at Emeric as they passed, and still did not know what to do with their faces.

The girl spoke to him on the last afternoon.

Her name was Ilsa. He had learned it from the steward on the second day, because in all the hours at that table nobody had used it in front of him.

He was in the yard, checking the cart for the road down, and she came out of the hall alone and stood a little way off until he looked up. She had not said one word to him since he arrived. She had been in the room for all of it.

"What happens to me," she said, "if my mother dies?"

She said it flatly, the way her mother put a figure on a table, and for a moment the yard went cold around him in a way that had nothing to do with the wind.

In the palace where Emeric grew up, there had been four attempts on the life of a boy of eight inside fourteen months, and the boy had been him. He had learned there, younger than anyone should, that a calm question about what follows a death is not always an idle one, and that the calmest people asking it are the ones to watch. So for one bad moment he heard this question as that house would have heard it. A girl of fifteen, alone in a yard with the most dangerous man on the hill, asking what her mother's death would mean for her. He wondered, coldly and clearly, whether he was looking at a girl who had begun to weigh her mother's life against the hill.

He answered her anyway, and he told her the truth, because the truth served either way. If he was wrong about her, she was owed it. If he was right, it was the one thing that would end the idea.

"The writ makes your mother holder in trust for you," he said. "The trust is hers. It doesn't pass. If she dies, it ends, and the crown names someone else to hold the hill until you come of age. That will be a man. It will be a man chosen by people in the capital who have never met you and never will. And whoever holds you holds the hill, which means he decides a great deal about who you marry, and most of the men who want the hill will want it through you. There's law that's meant to stop the worst of that. It doesn't stop very much of it."

It was an unpleasant amount of law, with no comfort anywhere in it. He knew that as he said it, and he did not add any.

She listened to all of it without looking away, and none of it disappointed her.

That was what he saw, and it undid the rest. A girl doing the sum he had feared would have been listening for a number worth the risk, and would have minded when none came. Ilsa took each piece as her mother had taken his terms, as something she had already worked most of the way through on her own and had come outside to have checked. When he got to the man the capital would choose, she nodded once, as if he had confirmed a line she had written down before she left the hall.

"Thank you," she said, and went back inside.

Emeric stood by the cart with a length of rope in his hands and did not tie it.

She had not been asking what she would gain. She had been asking what she stood to lose, and how soon, and whether anybody would tell her plainly before it happened. And it had not been her question first. Her mother had asked it across the table on the first evening, about the grain. So if I'm in the ground before the three years are out. The same flat voice. The same sum, worked from the other end.

Her mother's daughter. Bryda had been handed a hill she was never allowed to understand, by a death nobody on it had planned for, and had spent a year learning it with the whole district watching. The girl had watched her do it and did not mean to be caught the same way. They were farsighted, the pair of them. They looked a long way down the road, and they started walking before it arrived.

He was ashamed of his first thought, briefly and thoroughly. Then he tied the rope.

They rode down the hill the next morning with an empty cart, and the road was as bad going down as it had been coming up.

Saban was in good spirits. He had the tables in his coat, and he had liked the mine-master, and he said twice that it was the cleanest ore contract he had seen in twenty years of trade. Ward said nothing, which was ordinary.

Emeric thought about Grayrock the whole way down.

He had the stone. He had the contract, at a fixed rate, exclusive, binding whoever held the hill. He had got it by losing, and he did not know that he had got it by losing. He believed he had got it by arithmetic, and the arithmetic was sound.

There was better stone in that hill than anybody had been let dig for, and a road up it he was not allowed to mend, and a woman in the hall who would know the place better inside a year than her husband had troubled to in twenty, for as long as it was hers to know.

Ferra stated the price that night, in the small room, after he had told her the terms.

She did not argue with them. She agreed before he had finished, because she had been trying to buy that ore for a year and knew what it was worth.

"It's the right decision," she said. "And it costs the Duvrenne line."

"I know."

"You don't have the grain for both. Exclusive to the hill means the harvest is spoken for. The old man in Duvrenne takes grain, and his house takes the iron behind the grain. Without the grain, I don't think the iron keeps going for long."

"I know."

"So we've traded the only money coming into this barony from outside it," Ferra said, "for the one thing the foundry can't run without." She wrote it down. "It's right. It's what it costs."

She kept writing for a while. Then, without looking up, she asked what the widow was like.

Emeric thought about the table, and the two kinds of paper on it.

"She's working through his books with nobody to show her," he said. "A slip of paper in every page she couldn't follow. There were fewer of them by the fourth day." He paused. "She reads a ledger the way you do."

Ferra did not answer that. She underlined the Duvrenne figure at the bottom of the sheet, and then sat looking at the underline for a little while before she went on.

Vantic's rights under the covenant ran to any decision that touched the security, and she used them. She read the contract in the small room the next morning, standing, all the way through, and then she wrote her note in the file.

It was two lines. Emeric read them over her shoulder, because she did not stop him.

Security in grain reallocated from sale at Duvrenne to exchange in kind at Grayrock, at a fixed standing rate, exclusive. Borrower to notify the Duvrenne counterparty.

There was no opinion in either line. She initialled it, closed the file, and went back to her room.

Emeric wrote to the old man in Duvrenne that night, in his own hand. It was a short letter, and it was the hardest he had written since the war. He said that Kaldo's grain was committed elsewhere from the next season, and that he regretted it, and that he would not forget which house had been the first to take Kaldo's name on trust, even if the trust had arrived in somebody else's letter.

He sealed it and sat with it for some time before he sent it.

He had the ore Kaldo needed, at a price that would not move. He had lost the only outside buyer Kaldo had. And a day's bad road away, in a hall on a stone shoulder, a girl of fifteen had asked him the one question he had no good answer for, and all he had been able to give her was the law.
