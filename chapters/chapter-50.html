Chapter 50 - The Other Ways

Three baronies do not organise themselves.

Emeric had believed that for a year. Renner had wanted the ore money and a barony that paid without haggling. Sethe had wanted his junction to stay the only thing in the south that mattered. Paxton had been told Kaldo was weak and could not afford to be the last man to believe otherwise. Every one of those was a reason. None of them was a reason for three men who had never agreed on anything in their lives to move in the same season, in the same order, on the same picture.

So he went looking for the fourth.

He ran it the way he ran everything. Saban's network, which by now reached most of the holdings within a week's ride and a few beyond. Ferra's sheets, every order and payment and cancellation in the valley for the eighteen months before the march, laid side by side, month against month, on the long table in the room over the threshing floor. The shape of who had moved when. Whose couriers had been on which roads. Which houses had bought iron, or men, or grain, in the season before the war, and from whom, and with whose money.

It was good work. He knew it was good work while he was doing it, and so did Ferra, who did most of it and did not complain once, because it was the kind of work she liked best, which was the kind with an answer at the bottom.

It took most of the summer.

There was one week when he thought he had it. Saban came back from the west road with a courier nobody could place, a young man with no colours on his coat who had ridden between Milford and the hill three times in the spring before the march, carrying something sealed, and paying for his beds in capital silver. Emeric sat up with that for two nights. He drew the rides on the tithe map. He worked out what could have been in the bag. On the third day Saban's man in Milford found the courier himself, married now and running cattle, and the sealed thing had been letters between two families arranging his wedding, and the capital silver had been his future father-in-law's.

Emeric crossed him off. Then he crossed off the next one, a buyer of iron up the coast who turned out to be a shipwright's guild, and the one after that, a clerk who had asked questions about Kaldo at a Wayford inn and turned out to be a clerk who asked questions about everything.

There was nothing at the bottom.

It was not an absence of evidence. An absence of evidence would have told him something. It was an absence of anywhere for evidence to be. Every line he pulled ended at one of the three. Every courier Saban could name was accounted for. Every coin that had moved in the valley that year had moved between men who already had their own reasons to move it.

At the end of the summer he sat with the sheets for a whole evening, and then he concluded, reasonably, that he had been wrong. There had only ever been three.

He wrote that down, in the book, under the date. He did not enjoy being wrong. He enjoyed a wrong idea left standing a good deal less.

What Saban found in those same months was not a conspiracy. It was a list of prices.

He brought it in the autumn, and he brought it as a price and not as a warning, because a price was what it was.

"Grain first," he said, and put the sheet down.

Grain moving in the valley was trading at worse terms than a good season accounted for. It was worst for Kaldo's grain, the little of it that went north by cart instead of down the water. The buyers who took it had all found, in the same month, a reason to pay less.

"Ore," said Saban, and put down the second.

The factors who would still sell ore south were quoting high. They were quoting high to Kaldo in particular. Saban had asked one factor for the same lot twice, once in his own name for Kaldo's account and once through a friend who dealt in tin, and the friend had been quoted a fifth less.

"And the road," said Saban.

The waypoint on the northern road, at the junction, had put on a toll. It was new. It was modest. It was entirely within a baron's rights to lay. It was charged on every cart that used the road, without exception, and it was charged fairly, and it was charged to everyone.

That year, most of the carts on that road had been Kaldo's.

Emeric went down to the yard the next morning when the north carts came in, because he wanted to hear it from somebody who had paid it.

The carter was a Kaldo man who had driven that road for a decade, and he was not angry. That was the first thing Emeric noticed. He described it the way a man describes the weather on a crossing.

"Polite as you like," he said. "A clerk with a little table by the waypoint, and a board behind him with the rate painted on it, big, so nobody can say they weren't told. Same rate for every cart. I watched him charge a Wayford wool wagon the same as me, to the coin. He even gave me a receipt."

He took it out of his coat and handed it over. It was a neat slip in a narrow, exact hand, dated, with the rate and the cart and the load, and a line at the bottom for the clerk's mark.

"He charges the flitting carts the same," the carter said. "Families coming south with a bed and a pot and a goat on a string. Same rate as me, to the coin. And there's a second man at the table since the harvest, from the steward's hall, with the rent rolls. Anybody he's got a name for, he asks what they owe before they go by." He shrugged. "Most of them pay it and come on. I saw two turned back."

It was the only thing Emeric heard that week that was aimed at Wayford rather than at Kaldo. A baron who sets a man with the rent rolls at his own crossing has started counting his own people going past it.

Emeric looked at the board in his mind. A rate painted large so that nobody could say they had not been told. It was his own trick, from his own square, and somebody had watched it work and had taken it home.

He kept the receipt. When Vantic saw it on his desk, two days later, she asked whether she might have it, and he gave it to her, and she put it in her book.

"I want to be plain," Saban said. "None of this is an attack. Every one of these is legal. I'd charge any of them myself, if I were sitting where they're sitting. It's only that when you put them together, they come to a number."

"And the number is the attack," said Emeric.

"The number is the attack."

Bram took the ore sheet worse than anybody. He read it standing in the foundry with his arms folded and then asked who he was supposed to hit. Emeric told him nobody. Bram said that was the trouble with the whole business, and went back to the furnace, and ran it that month harder and more carefully than Emeric had ever seen him run anything, as if the only answer he had to a man charging too much for ore was to waste less of it.

Emeric read the three sheets through, slowly, twice.

"They learned," he said. "The field taught them that this place can't be taken cheaply by force. So they've gone looking for the other ways there are to take a thing."

He had never seen his own position so clearly. He had also never been so aware that seeing it did him no good whatsoever.

There is no answer to a legal toll. You cannot fight a price. You cannot go to law against a baron for charging what a baron is entitled to charge. You can pay it, or you can stop using the road, and Kaldo had one road north.

Vantic had been reading the sheets over the end of the table, because the review was a spending review and she had the right to sit in it, and she used every right she had.

"Every one of those is an operating expense," she said.

"Yes."

"Every one of them reduces the security."

"Yes."

"Then on this question, the house's interest and the barony's interest are the same." She said it as she said everything, as a fact entered in a column, and there was no sympathy in it at all.

And then she did nothing about it, because there was nothing to be done about it, and she was not a woman who pretended otherwise.

What she did instead, which nobody had asked her to do and which nobody in the room noticed for a long while, was open a new line in her own books. The toll went into it. The ore quotes went into it. The grain terms went into it. Every cost on Saban's three sheets, each with its date and its source, set apart from the barony's ordinary expenses in a column of its own, and every cost like them after that, week on week.

She did not say why. Emeric, when he noticed the line at all, assumed it was the practice of her house, and did not ask.

Ferra brought her the rest of that month's decisions already made, with the reasoning attached. Vantic initialled them, one after another, without looking up.

By the autumn Emeric was not sleeping much.

He did not notice it. Other people did. Imani stopped him in the corridor one night and told him, in the voice she kept for patients who had come in a week too late, that he looked like a man running a fever without the fever, and that she had no medicine for what he had, and that she recommended a bed. He thanked her and went back to the sheets. Ward said nothing about it at all. But after that, whenever Ward passed the small room after midnight, the lamp in it was somehow turned down a little lower than Emeric had left it, and neither of them ever mentioned that either.

The number was on the fourth sheet.

It was an ore lot. High-grade. Crystal-bearing stone and good iron, out of the hill upriver, sold by a mine-master with nobody above him to say otherwise. Kaldo had bid for it through Saban, at a figure Ferra had worked out to the coin. It was the most the foundry could pay and still sell what it made at any profit at all.

Kaldo had been outbid.

That was not unusual. Kaldo was outbid on something most months that year. What was unusual was the rate, and Ferra read it out.

"Whoever took that lot paid a rate no barony on this river could service," she said. "Not one. I've run all three. Not Grayrock buying back its own stone. Not Milford. Not Wayford, even with the new toll coming in. To pay that and make anything on it, you'd have to sell the iron at twice what anyone on this river gets for iron. Or you'd have to not care whether you made anything on it at all."

She said it precisely, once, standing, with the sheet in her hand. It was the way she had said the ore variance three years before, in the doorway of the small room. Then she stopped, and waited.

The name of the factor on the sheet meant nothing to anybody. Saban did not know it. As far as Saban had been able to follow the money behind it, it went back to none of the three.

Emeric heard Sethe.

He did not decide to. It was the answer that arrived first, and it arrived fully formed. Sethe had the junction and the purse and the reason. Sethe was the one man in the valley who might buy ore out from under Kaldo at a loss, if the loss was the point. And Emeric had been at the sheets since before light, and had spent the whole summer finding that there was no fourth hand in this, and had written it down.

"Sethe," he said. "Through somebody. It's the toll again with a different face."

Ferra looked at him for a moment.

She did not argue. She had said the number, and she had said it once, and she did not say things twice unless she was asked to.

"Next sheet," said Emeric.

Nobody in the room said anything else about it. The next sheet was lamp oil.

He wrote to the capital about the toll.

A new toll on a crown road was a crown matter, whatever a baron's rights on it were, and a crown matter was to be reported to the crown. He set it out properly. Where. From when. How much. On whom. Saban's figures fastened to the back. He sealed it and sent it in the ordinary packet on the quarter, by crown post, the way everything from Kaldo to the crown had always gone. It went north with the quarter's post, in the last rider's bag, like every packet before it.

Nothing came back.

It did not surprise him. Nothing had ever come back. He had been writing north since his first summer, reports and requests and accounts and, once, a letter to his father that had taken him a week, and in four years the capital had not answered him once.

He had a perfectly good explanation for that, and he had had it for years. A third son sent south to a dying barony is not a man the capital answers. He is a man the capital has arranged to forget, and it forgets well, and on purpose, and it does not write to tell him so.

It was a reasonable explanation. It fitted every fact he had. For the first three years of it, it was very probably true.

For the last one, it was wrong.

Ferra read him the year once the harvest was in, in the small room, with the lamp between them and the factor's door shut down the corridor.

"The Duvrenne line is holding," she said. "The old man takes everything we send and pays on the day, and his house takes the iron behind the grain. That's one column."

"And the other."

"Everything else." She turned the sheet. "Everything else costs more than it did a year ago. The toll. The ore. The grain that goes north. None of it is large. All of it is there every week."

"And between the two."

Ferra did not answer at once. She put her finger on the last line of the sheet, where the two columns met, and left it there.

"It's thin," she said. "One bad season closes it. A wet harvest. A boat on the bar. The old man dying, and his son not liking our name. Any one of those, and we are back where we were last winter, with a debt and a covenant and nothing going out."

Emeric looked at the line under her finger for a long time.

He did not have an answer. There was nothing to build that would widen it, because he was not allowed to build. There was nothing to cut that he was willing to cut. There was the Duvrenne line and there was the road, and the road was getting dearer by the week, and he could not say who was making it so except the answer he already had, which was Sethe.

"Keep reading it to me," he said. "Every month."

"I will."

She closed the book. Then, when he had gone, she opened it again at the back, where she kept a column he had never asked about, and wrote in the rate from the fourth sheet, and the date, and the name of the factor that nobody knew.

It was the column where she had put the ore variance, three years before. It was the column for things she had said out loud once and had not been asked to say again.

It was not a long column. She remembered every line in it.
