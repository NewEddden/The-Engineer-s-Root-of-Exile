Chapter 50 - Grain for Stone

Emeric learned that Grayrock had a holder from a carter at the Left Bridge, in one sentence, while the man was waiting to be waved through.

"The hill's got its writ," the carter said, to Ward, not to him. "Crown's put the widow in, to hold it for the girl."

That was all. There was no document. Nobody at Grayrock had written to Kaldo to say so, and nobody in the capital had either. The crown had found the time to confirm the widow of a man who had marched on a prince of the blood. It had not found the time to mention it to the prince. Emeric filed that with everything else the capital had not found the time to write to him about, and it fitted there without any trouble at all.

He rode that week.

He took Ward and Saban and a cart, and nobody else, and no banners. Three men and a cart. He had decided in the first week after the war that the body was going home, and then he had not sent it, because for more than a year there had been nobody on the hill with the standing to receive a baron and nobody in Kaldo with any business handing him to a steward. Ferra had written twice and had nothing back. Now there was somebody.

Renner had lain in the crypt under Doran's church since the summer before last.

Emeric had done the work on him himself, on the second evening after the field, and it had held. He had gone down every season since to see that it was still holding, alone, with a lamp, and it always had been. What that had cost him, he had never told anybody, and nobody had asked, and he preferred it that way.

They loaded the coffin before light. Doran came down to the crypt without being sent for and said the words over it that he would have said over anybody, and then stood back while Ward and Saban lifted it, and did not ask what Emeric intended, because he knew.

The road up the hill was a day of bad going. It had been bad the year before and it was worse now, rutted by the ore carts and then frozen into the ruts, and every mile of it was a mile the covenant said Emeric could not mend. The cart jolted and the coffin shifted and Saban stopped twice to rope it again. Nobody spoke much.

They came up onto Grayrock in the last of the afternoon.

It was a hard place. A stone hall on a stone shoulder, with the workings below it scarring the slope and the spoil running down in long grey fans toward the river. The wind came straight off the high ground and did not stop. There were men at the gate, and two of them had forearms that had been set in splints, and Emeric recognised the splints before he recognised the men. They had been set in Imani's ward. The men recognised him about a second later, and did not know what to do with their faces.

He got down in the yard.

A steward came out, and behind the steward a woman, and he did not wait for anybody to offer him a chair or a fire or a name.

"The cart has Lord Renner in it," he said. "I've brought him home. He's yours, whatever you decide about anything else I've come to say. That doesn't change. He stays yours."

He meant it entirely.

And he heard, about half a second after the last word, exactly what it looked like. A dead husband in a cart in the yard, delivered by the man who had killed him, in the first minute of a visit about business. It looked like a bargaining chip laid on a table. It was the thing it most resembled and the thing it least was, and there was no way to take the words back and say them in a better order.

The woman looked at the cart for a long moment. Then she looked at him.

"Bring him into the chapel," she said to the steward. And to Emeric, "You'd better come in."

Bryda Renner was forty or a little more, straight-backed, in dark grey, with a ring of household keys at her belt that she had plainly not stopped carrying when the household became a barony. The room she took him into was a working room, not a hall. A table with papers on it in careful piles. A fire. Three chairs, and a fourth by the window.

There was a girl in the fourth chair. Fifteen, perhaps, with her mother's straight back and her hands in her lap. Nobody introduced her and nobody sent her out. She did not say anything. Emeric noticed within a minute that she was not missing anything either.

He had not finished sitting down when Bryda put it on the table.

"You killed my husband."

It was not an accusation. It was not a question. It was the first item on the page, and she had read it out first, because it was first.

"Yes," Emeric said. "My ground. My plan. My orders."

She nodded as if he had confirmed a figure.

She was not hostile. She was not gracious either. He had been braced for both, and she was neither, and he understood after a few minutes what she was instead. She had run a household on this hill for twenty years without a single thing in it going wrong that anybody had heard about. She had been permitted to run the household. She had not been permitted to understand the hill. Now she held the hill, in trust, by a writ, and she was learning it as fast as a person can learn a thing, and she was doing it in front of the man who had made the vacancy.

He did not apologise.

He had thought about it all the way up the road, and he had decided, and he said the reason out loud, because he did not think she would respect a reason she had to guess.

"I'm not going to say I'm sorry," he said. "You'd take it as a courtesy. You'd price it as one. And you'd think less of me for spending it in this room."

Bryda looked at him for some time.

She did not tell him whether that was the best thing he said all day or the worst. He was never sure, afterward, which it had been.

The girl by the window moved her eyes from him to her mother and back, and did not move anything else.

He put the proposal plainly, because there was no other way to put it.

Grayrock's high-grade veins, the crystal-bearing stone and the good iron, sold to Kaldo at a fixed standing price, and to Kaldo only. Paid for in grain. Grain at a fixed standing rate against the stone, delivered to the foot of the hill, every season, for as long as the contract ran.

"I'm not doing you a favour," he said. "You're not doing me one. I need the stone. You need to eat. Nobody else within reach of this hill will sell you grain at a price that doesn't move when your harvest fails, and nobody else will buy your best stone at a price that doesn't move when the market does."

"And now," said Bryda. "This winter. We're short."

"The first shipment comes out of my cellars. It can be at the foot of the hill in a week."

The cellars were where the grain had gone, in the spring before the war, when Emeric had scattered it across the island so that nothing a torch could reach would starve the town. The thing he had saved from the fire was the thing he was now offering to the widow of the man who had come to light it.

He did not point that out. Neither did she. One of them had certainly noticed.

Her objection was honest, and it was the best argument anybody made that week.

"The men who buy from this hill," she said, "have been squeezing it for as long as I've been alive. I know them. I know what they pay and when they'll lie and how far. They're bad, and I know exactly how bad, and I've lived inside that my whole life." She folded her hands on the table. "You, I don't know. You might be better. You might be worse in a better coat. I've no way to price you, Highness, and every reason not to guess."

It was a good argument. He had no answer to it that asked her to believe anything, and he did not try to make her believe anything.

"Your husband's men came up my road on a picture," he said. "A broke plague town. A mad prince. Forty-odd men and half of them sulking. Every word of it was wrong, and they found out on my ground."

He let that stand for a moment.

"You are, right now, the best-informed person in this kingdom about what I actually am. Better than my father. Better than any house in the capital. You paid for that information in the field, in your husband, and in the sixty men he took down that road. You don't have to trust me. Nobody else in Randillia has the data you have. I'm asking you to use it."

Bryda heard him out. Then she said no.

She did not say it angrily. She did not dress it or soften it or leave a door in it. She said that she would not tie the hill to Kaldo on those terms or any others, not this year, and that she was grateful for her husband, and that the steward would see to the cart.

She was not bluffing. Emeric had sat across enough tables to know the difference, and there was nothing in her face that was waiting to be pushed.

He did not argue.

"The terms stay open," he said, and stood. "As long as you want them. They don't expire. And the body stands, whatever you decide about any of this. It was never part of it."

He picked up his gloves and went to the door.

He had his hand on it when she said his name.

"Sit down, Highness."

He turned.

What had happened in her, in the ten steps between the table and the door, she never told him, and he never guessed.

She had watched him lose. He had come up a bad road with the one thing she could not refuse lying in a cart in her yard, and when she said no to him, he had not reached for it. He had not said then bury him yourself. He had not said think what the town will say. He had not so much as glanced toward the chapel. He had stood up, and told her the dead man was hers regardless, and gone to the door. She had spent her whole life among men who held things over other people, her husband first among them, and she had never once seen a man put down the one thing that would have worked, at the moment it would have worked, and walk away from it.

That was what changed.

What she said was something else, and it was also true.

"I've run it again," she said, when he was sitting. "Your grain at a fixed rate is worth more to this hill than their coin at theirs. It's worth more every year the harvest fails, and it fails one year in three up here. For as long as you're alive to ship it, the arithmetic favours you." She turned a sheet over. "That's all it is. Don't mistake it for anything else."

Emeric believed the arithmetic completely. It was good arithmetic. He would have come to the same sum himself, and he was pleased with her for coming to it, and he never once suspected that the sum was not the reason.

Saban and the mine-master settled the weights and grades in an afternoon.

They did it in a cold shed by the ore bins, the two of them, with a set of scales and a bucket of samples, and they did it happily, the way two competent men settle anything when nobody above them is in the room. By dark they had a table of grades, a table of weights, and a rate for each, and they had each decided that the other was the first honest man he had met in that line of trade in a year.

The mine-master was short and broad, with grey grit ground so deep into the backs of his hands that it would never wash out. When the tables were done he walked Emeric along the upper workings in the last of the light, because Emeric asked to see them.

The shafts were shallow, and fewer than they should have been, and half of them were short of men.

"There's better below," the mine-master said. "There always was. The good stone runs down, not along. The old lord never put the crews on it." He shrugged. "He put the money into the men who went down your road instead."

Emeric asked him how far down the better stone ran. The mine-master said he did not know, because nobody had been let dig to find out.

The roads did not settle.

That argument went on for three days, in the working room, with the steward and the mine-master and Saban and a great deal of paper, and at the end of the three days it had not finished. The road up the hill was bad. Everybody agreed it was bad. A good road would halve the cost of every cartload that ever came down it, and everybody agreed about that too.

And a good road up a hill was a new project, and Kaldo was under a covenant that said Kaldo could not start one.

So they agreed a schedule that assumed the road would stay bad. Smaller loads, more of them, slower. Carts in the dry months and fewer in the wet. It was an expensive schedule, and everybody at the table could see exactly how expensive, and nobody at the table could do anything about it.

On the second evening Emeric asked Bryda about the deeper stone.

"Your mine-master says the good veins run down, and nobody's dug to find where they end. Why didn't your husband work them?"

"My husband didn't discuss the hill with me," Bryda said.

She said it evenly, and looked at him while she said it, and he could not read her at all.

He could not read her that evening, or on any of the others. He tried. He was good at it and he tried for six hours across three days, and she gave him nothing. No tell. No pause in the wrong place. Nothing that a man could hold up to the light. And a person who gives you nothing at all, for six hours, about the one question that matters most, is either hiding nothing or hiding it very well, and Emeric had met a great many more people in his life who were the second kind.

He decided she was holding something back. He did not say so. He did not ask again.

They buried Renner on the fourth morning.

He went into the ground in the chapel yard at Grayrock with his own priest, and his widow, and his daughter, and the men who were left. There were not many. There were the few who had come home from the field, some of them on legs and arms that had been set in Kaldo, and there were the ten who had been left behind to hold the hill while the rest marched. They stood in a close group in the wind with their hats in their hands.

Emeric attended. He stood at the back, by the chapel wall, with Ward beside him and Saban beside Ward.

Nobody invited him to speak. He did not expect to be invited, and he would have refused, and he was glad that he did not have to.

The priest said the words. They were the same words Doran had said in the crypt, very nearly, in a different voice. The earth went in. The widow stood without moving until it was done, and then turned and went in, and the men went after her in ones and twos, and the two with the splints looked at Emeric as they passed and did not know, still, what to do with their faces.

The girl spoke to him on the last afternoon.

Her name was Ilsa. He had learned it from the steward on the second day, because nobody else had used it in front of him.

He was in the yard, checking the cart for the road down, and she came out of the hall alone and stood a little way off until he looked up. She had not said one word to him in four days. She had been in the room for all of it.

"What happens to me," she said, "if my mother dies?"

She said it flatly, the way her mother put a figure on a table, and she did not look frightened while she said it. She looked like somebody who had been thinking about the question for some time and had decided to ask the one person on the hill who would not lie about it.

Emeric told her the truth.

"The writ makes your mother holder in trust for you," he said. "The trust is hers. It doesn't pass. If she dies, it ends, and the crown names someone else to hold the hill until you come of age. That will be a man. It will be a man chosen by people in the capital who have never met you and never will. And whoever holds you holds the hill, which means he decides a great deal about who you marry, and most of the men who want the hill will want it through you. There's law that's meant to stop the worst of that. It doesn't stop very much of it."

It was an unpleasant amount of law and there was no comfort anywhere in it. He knew that as he said it. He did not add any.

The girl listened to all of it without looking away.

"Thank you," she said, and went back inside.

They rode down the hill the next morning with an empty cart, and the road was as bad going down as it had been coming up.

Saban was in good spirits. He had the tables in his coat and he had liked the mine-master and he said, twice, that it was the cleanest ore contract he had seen in twenty years. Ward said nothing, which was ordinary.

Emeric thought about Bryda Renner the whole way down.

He had the stone. He had the contract, at a fixed rate, exclusive, for as long as it ran. He had got it by losing, and he did not know that he had got it by losing, and he believed he had got it by arithmetic, and the arithmetic was sound.

And he had sat across a table from the woman for six hours and not read her once. There was deeper stone in that hill that nobody had dug for. There was a dead man who had not dug for it and had spent the money on soldiers. And there was a widow who had looked him in the eye and said her husband had never discussed the hill with her, in a voice with nothing in it at all.

He decided, somewhere on the bad road, that she knew more than she had said. He carried that decision down the hill with him and into Kaldo, and he did not put it down.

Nobody ever confronted him about it. Nothing that anybody could see went wrong because of it. It sat in him quietly, and it coloured every letter she ever sent him, and he did not notice it doing either.

Ferra stated the price that night, in the small room, after he had told her the terms.

She did not argue with them. She agreed with them before he had finished, because she had been trying to buy that ore for a year and knew exactly what it was worth.

"It's the right decision," she said. "And it costs the Duvrenne line."

"I know."

"You don't have the grain for both. Exclusive to the hill means the harvest is spoken for. The old man in Duvrenne takes grain, and the iron goes to him behind the grain. Without the grain, I don't think the iron keeps going for long."

"I know."

"So we've traded the only money coming into this barony from outside it," Ferra said, "for the one thing the foundry can't run without." She wrote it down. "It's right. It's what it costs."

Vantic had the right to the decision and she used it. She read the contract in the small room the next morning, standing, all the way through, and then she wrote her note in the file.

It was two lines. Emeric read them over her shoulder, because she did not stop him.

Security in grain reallocated from sale at Duvrenne to exchange in kind at Grayrock, at a fixed standing rate, exclusive. Borrower to notify the Duvrenne counterparty.

Neither line was an opinion. She initialled it, closed the file, and went back to her room.

Emeric wrote to the old man in Duvrenne that night, in his own hand. It was a short letter, and it was the hardest one he wrote that year. He said that Kaldo's grain was committed elsewhere from the next season, and that he regretted it, and that he would not forget which house had been the first to take Kaldo's name on trust, even if the trust had arrived in somebody else's letter.

He sealed it and sat with it for some time before he sent it.

He had the ore Kaldo needed, at a price that would not move. He had lost the only market Kaldo had ever had. And he was quite certain that the woman who had given him the first had kept something back from him while she did it.
