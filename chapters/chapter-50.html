Chapter 50 - Dead Iron

They ran it for the first time on the fifteenth of December, two hours after dark, when the stones had been still since the middle of the afternoon and the dust in the stone room had settled on every ledge.

Harl had shut the door between the stone room and the back room himself, and hung the wet sacking over it himself, and stood back and looked at it, and then come and stood at the foot of his own stair with a closed lantern in his hand, where he could see everything and be near the door. He did not say anything. He had not said anything since he came down.

Tess had stretched a length of thin iron wire between two nails on a board, and the board stood on a stool with its two ends wired to the machine through a great loose coil of the line's iron wire on a frame, to take what the thin wire could not, and beside it, in its box, one of the line's needles. If the machine made what it was meant to make, the wire would glow. If it made a little, the needle would swing. Bram had said he would come down to see it go, and had come, and now leaned in the outer doorway with his arms folded in the cold, as he had leaned in the dyehouse door.

The belt ran from the wheel's shaft to a pair of pulleys on a short post, one fast to the shaft and one loose beside it, and from there to the machine. The carpenter had made a lever that slid the belt from the loose pulley onto the fast one. Emeric put his hand on it.

"Go on, then," said Rylan, who had come down with them uninvited and been told twice already to stand somewhere else.

Emeric pulled the lever.

The belt slapped across. The ring between the iron arms jerked, and turned, and turned faster, and kept on gathering until it was a blur in the lamplight with a sound coming off it like a swarm of bees in a barrel. The whole frame shook on its oak beams. Harl's father's floor took it and did not complain. The springs on the copper drum chattered, and then settled to a steady hiss.

The wire on the board stayed grey.

Emeric looked at the needle. It lay where it had lain in its box all evening.

He let it run. He let it run long enough for the ring to have turned some thousands of times, while the noise filled the back room and the lamp shook on its hook and Rylan, who had been grinning, stopped. Then he pushed the lever back, and the belt slid onto the loose pulley, and the ring ran down slowly in a long falling hum to nothing, and in the quiet the wheel could be heard outside, going round, as it always went round.

"Well," said Rylan.

Harl turned and went up his stair without a word, and they heard him overhead, and then nothing.

Emeric already knew. He had known before the ring stopped, the way a man knows, as the step goes out from under him, which stair he has missed.



"How does it start?" Tess said.

It was the next morning, in the dyehouse, and she had been waiting to ask it since the night before, by the look of her. She had her bench book open on her knee and nothing written on the page.

"It starts itself. That's the idea." He sat down on Abloh's stool. "The ring turns between the arms. If there's any magnet in the arms at all, the turning makes a little current. The little current goes round the field coils, your coils, and makes the arms a little stronger. Stronger arms make more current. More current makes stronger arms. Round and round, up and up, until it's running full."

"Like a fire from a coal."

"Like a fire from a coal. And it needs the coal." He held up his hand, finger and thumb nearly closed. "Iron that's been a magnet keeps a little of it afterward. Not much. Enough. A pan keeps its heat after it comes off the fire. That's the coal."

Tess looked at him without writing anything. Then she said, slowly, as if she did not want it to be true:

"The river iron."

"The river iron."

He had picked it because it was soft. He had stood on the pier with a broken link in his hand and been pleased with it, because the softest iron takes a magnet most easily and lets it go most easily, and he had wanted both of those things for the arms of the machine. He had got both. The anchors and the chains had been hammered and folded until there was almost nothing in them but iron, and they had come out of the fire under Bram's trip hammer and cooled and been bolted together, and whatever magnet any of it had ever had in it, the heat had taken out. There was nothing left in the arms to start from, and the smaller field on the three sheets had no margin to spare. He had built a fire without a coal, out of the best iron in Kaldo.

"Dead iron," Tess said.

"Dead iron."

"So you give it a coal."

"So you give it a coal." He looked at the bench, at the jars standing along it in their brown pots. "You put current through the field coils from something else, once, for a count. That makes the arms a magnet. You take it off, and the arms keep a little. Then you turn it, and it starts from that."

Tess wrote that down. Then she looked up.

"Why didn't we do that last night?"

There was no good answer to that, so he gave her the true one, which was that he had hoped the forging would have left something in the iron and had not wanted to carry a crate of jars down a hill in the dark if it had. She wrote that down too.

Bram had come up from the forge with a question about the drum and stopped in the doorway to listen, and had been there long enough to hear all of it.

"Iron forgets in the fire," he said.

Emeric looked round at him.

"My father kept a lodestone, for the boatmen. You stroked a needle on it and it pointed north, and they paid a copper for it. Put the needle in the fire after, and it pointed nowhere." Bram came in and sat on the edge of a vat. "We used to do it to the new boys' needles, for a joke." He considered the brown pots along the bench. "I'd have told you."



They carried the jars down the hill in the dark two nights later, on a handcart, in straw, with Rylan pushing and Tess walking alongside with her hand on the nearest crate, and they did not spill one.

"If anybody asks," Rylan said, at the bottom of the high street, "we're smuggling."

"Nobody's going to ask."

"Then I'll tell them."

Harl was waiting at the foot of his stair in his nightshirt and an old coat, with his lantern, as before, and tonight there were two full buckets of water on the floor beside him. He watched them carry the crates into the back room and set the jars out in a row on the floor, with the copper strips between them, and he did not ask what they were.

Emeric did it carefully. That was the part he went over afterward. He lifted both copper springs off the drum and wedged them up with slips of wood, so that the current from the jars would go round the field coils and nowhere else, and not into the ring. He put the end of the jars' wire to one end of the field and the other end to the other, and counted twenty, slowly, out loud, while Tess held the lamp, and took the wires away. He tried an iron nail against the face of one of the arms. It stuck. He pulled it off, and it came away with a small reluctance it had not had in the morning.

"There's your coal," he said.

He took the wedges out, and let the springs back down onto the drum, and put his hand on the lever, and pulled.

The belt slapped across, and the ring jerked, and turned, and gathered, and the sound came up off it again, and on the stool the thin wire on its board stayed grey, and the needle in its box swung right over and lay hard against its pin.

Rylan shouted.

It held. It held for perhaps three breaths, the needle hard over, and Emeric had time to feel his whole chest open, and then the needle came off its pin and sank slowly back toward the middle of the box, and lay there.

Then the springs on the copper drum began to scream.

It was a high tearing shriek, metal on metal, and with it came sparks, a spray of small blue-white sparks off the drum where the springs bit into it, one after another, faster, and a smell of hot wax and hot copper. Behind him Emeric heard water slop, and Harl was at his shoulder with a full bucket in each hand, ready, and his face in the spark-light was a face Emeric did not want to see again.

Emeric shoved the lever back. The belt slid off onto the loose pulley. The ring ran down, and the screaming slowed, and stopped, and the sparks stopped with it, and there was nothing but the smell, and the water in Harl's buckets still slopping from side to side.

"It's going the wrong way," Tess said.

She was the only one of them who had been watching the ring instead of the needle. She was pointing at the frame, at the arrow Emeric had painted on the yoke himself in white lead, on the day they bolted it down, to show which way the ring was meant to turn.

The ring had been turning against it.



It took him a quarter of an hour on his knees with the lamp to see all of it, and none of it was complicated.

The belt that ran to the machine was the old belt from the wire drum, the one the carpenter had made last winter. The drum had needed to turn against the wheel, so the carpenter had put a half twist in the belt, as any carpenter would, and the half twist was still in it. Nobody had taken it out because nobody had been told to. Emeric had painted an arrow on the frame and never once stood and watched which way the ring went the first night, because he had been watching the wire.

Running backward, the machine had done the thing it was built to do, and done it backward. It had made current out of the coal they had just given it, and the current had gone round the field coils the wrong way, against the magnet in the arms, and in three breaths it had undone what the jars had put there. He tried the nail against the arm again. It fell off.

Dead iron, again.

And the springs. They had been bent to lie along the copper drum trailing, the way a man drags his fingers along a fence, which was right for one direction and very wrong for the other. Turned against them, the drum had caught their ends at every bar and driven them in like chisels. Four of the copper bars were gouged and lifted at their edges. The waxed linen between them, which kept each bar from the next, had been torn out in strings and burned, and lay in little black curls on the floor among the shavings.

Bram had come in from the outer door at some point. He crouched beside Emeric and looked at the drum, and picked up a curl of burned linen and rubbed it between his finger and thumb.

"That's a week," he said.

"It's a week."

"Over midwinter."

"Yes."

Bram dropped the curl of linen, and stood up, and his knees cracked, and he said nothing else, which was not kindness exactly but was close to it.

Harl had not moved from where he stood. He had put the buckets down. He stood in his nightshirt in the cold with flour still in the creases of his knuckles from the day, looking at the black curls on his father's floor, and at the scorch on the board under the drum where the sparks had landed and gone out.

"You said small ones," he said.

"I did."

"Those weren't small."

"No. It was running backward. It won't do it again." Emeric heard himself say it, and heard how it would sound to a man who had been told twice already what the machine would and would not do. "That was my mistake. The belt, and not watching it. Not yours, not the carpenter's."

Harl looked at him, and did not answer.

Then he went through the door into the stone room, and came back with a stool, and set it down at the foot of the stair, inside the back room, beside the buckets.

"Every night it runs," he said, "I sit with it."

"You don't have to do that."

"It's my mill." Harl sat down on the stool, in his nightshirt, with his hands on his knees. "Go home. It's not running tonight."



They rebuilt the drum in the dyehouse over the shortest week of the year. Bram took the bent bars out and drew new ones under the hammer in bay three from the scrap of the old, and filed them true. Abloh cut new slips of linen and waxed them himself and would not let Tess do it. Tess took the half twist out of the belt with her own hands, on the third morning, at the mill, and relaced it, and then painted a second arrow on the post beside the pulleys, in white, pointing the same way as the first, so that whoever stood at the lever would have it in front of his face.

Emeric bent the copper springs again, trailing, and then stood with one in each hand and thought about the next man to have to do it, who would not be him. He bent a third spring, a spare, and wired it to the frame beside the drum with a tag on it in Tess's hand: this way, and an arrow.

None of it would be ready before the shortest day.

And every evening that week, whether the machine ran or not, Harl filled two buckets at the race and set them down inside the back-room door, one on each side of his stool, and in the morning, when the machine had not run, he emptied them back into the river and filled them again.
