Chapter 291 - Right of Entry

Martin Corley had been told it was a village.

He had been told so by the senior clerk who handed him the inspector's spare case, and who had been south once and not liked it. A castle on a marsh, and mud, and a duke who had been a drunk and was now something nobody in the chancery could agree on. Count everything twice.

Nobody had told him about the north. He saw it from the train on the long run down, and did not know what he was looking at until one of the Church's four, the one with the ledger, leaned across him to the window and said that this was the judgment and it had been earned. For a day the hedges were sticks and the trees bare to the twig, in September, and the fields brown, with long dark strips of turned earth where people were digging. At one halt there was a queue at the church door with sacks; and Martin watched a boy go in at the front with his sack and come out at the side with two coppers and the same sack, go round the back wall whistling, and join the queue at its tail.

At Wayford the church had a clerk at its door as well as a priest, a thin woman with an iron punch on a cord round her neck, and every sack that came out came out with a hole through its neck the size of a thumb. Over her head a grey sheet was pinned to the door, much handled, with a drawing on it like a grain of rice in a little case, and at its foot a line Martin read twice: In the four baronies, every sack paid at the church is punched by the parish clerk. A punched sack is paid once.

South of Wayford the fields were green. Low and new and very green against the stubble round them, in rows so straight they might have been ruled, turnips and something that climbed on sticks; and Martin sat with his knees together and the case between them and said nothing all the way to the end of the line, which was Kaldo, in the dark.



On the first morning after the party came in, the inspector went up the hill to his seals and the Chancellor's powder book, and Martin, who was not wanted for books, was sent with the Church's four on what the Chancellor's office called the walk.

A young woman from the office took them down the river road toward the sea; and Martin, who had seen the palace and the cathedral close, was unimpressed for about a quarter of a mile, until the road came out from between the warehouses onto the quay and he stopped, and the young one of the four walked into his back.

The capital's cranes were timber, with a man walking in a treadwheel like a squirrel in a cage. These were iron, a row of them along the quay's edge on four legs so tall and wide that a laden cart went under one while he watched; and the whole top of each, an iron house with a man in its window and a long arm over the water, turned on its legs without a sound he could hear over the gulls, and swung a net of sacks out of a hold and down onto the quay as gently as a woman putting down eggs.

A bell rang on the nearest. Its arm stopped dead with the net hanging. A boy was crossing the plank walkway under it.

"It won't turn with the hook over the walkway," said the young woman. "It rings and waits." The boy crossed; the bell stopped; the arm went on. "Portal cranes, the duke calls them, because you can drive a cart through the door. The Motion Shop makes the gear in the top that turns them."

The ledger man wrote it down.

At the quay's end, at the deep berth, stood a thing too big to be a crane that was one: an iron frame astride the berth on rails, taller than the cathedral porch, its arm reaching across a coaster. Out of the hold on four chains came a green steel box the size of a cottage. It ran along under the arm and came down at a man's flag onto a railway flatcar so exactly that its four corners dropped onto four iron pegs; a man went round the corners with a lever; the arm went back for another. Behind the quay there were hundreds of them, stacked three and four high, each with a number on its end and a lead seal on its door.

"Kaldo boxes," said the young woman. "One size, so any crane takes one, and any car, and any barge. One box, one line in the book, one seal. And that's the gantry crane." She looked at the stacks with something like affection. "The Chancellor says it's the best thing he's ever done for her."

The quiet one of the four did not look at the gantry. Martin noticed because everyone else had stopped to stare, and because the quiet one had said almost nothing since the capital. He stood at the back with his hands behind him in the skirts of his grey coat, a man of middle years with a face Martin could not have described by evening, and looked at the men under the gantry instead, one after another, the way a man looks along a shelf for a book he has been told is there.



The Port Town had been printed. That was the young woman's word, and Martin did not understand it until she showed him.

Three streets ran back from the water toward the heavy works' chimneys, every house in them less than a year old, two storeys and three, and their walls grey and smooth from the ground to the eaves, without a joint or a course anywhere. Martin put his hand on one. It was cold and very hard and faintly rough, like the inside of a cup.

At the top of the third street the machine that had done it stood over a row only half there: a steel frame on rails, three storeys high and four houses wide, and hanging from it an iron nozzle with a flat blade either side like a mason's trowel, going slowly along the top of a wall while grey paste as thick as a man's arm came out of it and the blades pressed it flat. In a shed at the street's head a woman moved a pointed stick along a drawing, and the nozzle, three storeys up, moved with her.

"The long printer," said the young woman. "Bram's shop built it. A terrace of four in a week, the walls. The trades do the rest."

"By what art," said the ledger man.

"By a pump," said the young woman cheerfully, "and a great deal of cement."

The walls were all alike. The doors were not: every one was oak or ash or elm, and carved, a ship, a sheaf of wheat, a fish, a woman's face, leaves so fine they looked as if they would move. On every south roof lay long flat boxes of glass tilted to the sky, something black under the glass and a small tank above.

"For the bath water," said the young woman, when the young one asked. "The sun heats it. Solar water heaters, the duke calls them. Everybody else calls them the sun boxes. They're good till about November."

At the corner of the third street a man was on his knees at a doorstep.

It was a long step of dressed stone, the first hand-cut stone Martin had seen since the quay, under a door that had no carving yet; and the man was taking a little off its front edge with a chisel, and then a little more, and then putting his flat hand on it and looking at his palm. He was past sixty: a square, heavy, grey man in a leather apron, with forearms like a carter's and a face that did not look much used for talking.

"Morning, Master Marl," said the young woman, as she might have said it to an uncle.

"Morning." He did not look up.

"The gentlemen from the capital. On the walk."

"I know who they are." He took off a sliver. They stood on the corner in the sun and he worked as if they were not there; and at last he spoke, not to them, Martin thought afterward, and not to her, but to the step.

"I set the feet of the first trough in this town. I turned the first tap. Twelve years gone. When there was no stone cut in Kaldo for anything but graves." A flake fell. "There was a span down on the old water line. I told him spring. Twenty men and a frame, and a season's work. And it was a season, and we did it." He looked up the street at the steel frame with its nozzle going along the wall. "That thing lays in a week what my crew laid in a season."

Nobody said anything.

"They pay me more than I ever had. I'll say that. There's families under a roof this winter that'd be in the marsh huts without it, and I know it." He turned back to the step. "They've asked me to set the steps. They've asked me to cut the doors. They've asked me to teach the boys the old way a morning a month." He put his hand flat on the stone and looked at his palm. "Nobody's asked me to sit at the machine."

The young woman opened her mouth, and Martin saw her think better of it; because there was nothing to say, and the old man was right, and she knew it.

"Good day, Master Marl."

"Good day."

Martin looked back from the next corner. The mason was still on his knees. And the quiet one was still at the corner, a little apart, looking at the old man's back; and then he came after them without hurry, with his hands in the skirts of his coat.



They saw the rest from the ring road, which ran round the outside of the town wall, wide and black and so smooth that the young one asked if it had been printed too.

A train came in from Grayrock while they walked, green and black, with a tall chimney; and out of the chimney, nothing. Martin still had the Wayford train's soot in his collar. This one came round the curve with its pistons going and white steam whistling from its sides and its chimney as clean as a pipe that had never been lit.

"The stone-fired engine," said the young woman. "She burns stones, not coal. There's coal in her tender in case, and she hasn't used a shovelful since August. The Grayrock men call her the Parson, because she doesn't smoke."

A train went out on the Milford line with nothing on its flatcars but glass, great sheets of it crated on edge, flashing on the curve: for the tropical house at Milford's steadings, she said, built that summer: a glass house taller than any built, warm all winter, for plants from across the sea. Coffee. Martin did not know what coffee was. To the south-west, between the wall and the woods, lay a whole town of new houses on a grid, with lawns, and young trees along the streets too small to shade anything, every wall the same smooth grey and every south roof flashing with sun boxes and every door, as far as he could see, carved. The garden suburb. Five hundred houses. Beyond its last street the green rows of turnips and beans ran away to the woods.

"That was all wheat," said the young woman, "before the swarm." She walked on.

From the ring road's south side Martin could see the whole town, the roofs going up the hill to the old keep on its raised ward, square, with its two square towers patched and lichened; and round the keep on every side, higher already than the towers, a great round cage of timber and iron curving inward like the ribs of a basket, with tiny figures on it and glass where some of it was glazed: as if somebody meant to put the old castle under a bowl.

"What is that," said Martin. It was the first question he had asked all morning.

"The dome. It'll be finished in the spring." She looked up at it. "Then the towers come down. Not before."

The ledger man wrote that down too.



In the afternoon the four went to buy paper, which was what they had come for. At the printing works a man at a counter sold them, at the price on the board and without asking why, one of everything printed that year and the last, the Almanacs, the notices, the hopper sheets, the school primer, the ward rules, and gave them a receipt and a sack, and then another sack. Then they went up the hill to the records hall, which had a sheet pinned on its door in a square hand: Nobody whose name is on the register of Kaldo shall be taken out of Kaldo, or held, or punished, without a hearing in an open court. Any person may read the record of it.

Inside, any person could. A clerk brought them the year's open court in bound books, and the ledger man and the young one sat down to copy. The quiet one sat at the end of a table by himself with a book open that he did not read.

At four o'clock the duke came through.

Anybody would have known him. He came in at the far door with papers under his arm and a woman with a magnifying glass on a ribbon talking at his elbow, and his eyes were gold, the colour of a new Gold Rand, so that Martin, who had seen the king's once across a hall, was on his feet before he knew it. He was younger than Martin had expected, and thinner, with his hair in long ropes tied back at the neck and pink marks on one hand like an old burn; and he went down the room nodding to the clerks and did not look at the Church's four at all.

He passed the end of the quiet one's table. The quiet one did not look up.

At the far door the duke stopped with his hand on the latch, his back to the room. Only for a breath. Then he went through.

That night the four took their supper at the long table by the river inn's fire, and Martin, who was not one of them and had been reminded of it twice on the train, took his pot to the counter.

The innkeeper was a big soft man with flour on his apron. He drew the ale, and then, without being asked, turned a brass tap at the counter's end and filled a jug with water, and set it by the pot for the dust of the road.

Martin looked at the tap.

He had seen taps. There were two in the palace kitchens and one in the cathedral close, where the canons' servants queued for it with their buckets. He had never seen one in an inn. Through the kitchen door a girl was rinsing pots under another, and leaving it running while she did, as if water cost nothing.

"Where does it come from?"

"The water?" The innkeeper looked at the tap as if it might answer for him. "Off the hill. Down the aqueduct, through the sand beds, and into the pipes. There's a pipe into every house in Kaldo. You turn it, it comes." He considered. "You turn it back, it stops. That's the bit folk off the Wayford train forget. I had a cattle dealer flood the back passage in August."

"There's no well?"

"There's the old one in the square. The children drop things down it."

In the capital the water came by the bucket, a copper the pair at the conduit, or free from the river for anybody who would rather not spend the copper and could stand the taste. Martin had grown up in a house that boiled it. He drank from the jug. It tasted of nothing at all.

"And at night," he said. "The dark. In the lanes."

The innkeeper pointed upward with his chin. Over the counter hung three lamps in glass bells, giving a hard white light with no smell to it and no smoke, and they had not so much as flickered when the street door opened behind him.

"Lamp stones," said Martin, who had read of them.

"Some. Them in the passage are stones. Them," the chin again, "are off the wire. Lightbulbs, the duke calls them. A glass, with a hair of something in it that glows. The works make them down the river road."

"Who lights them?"

"Nobody lights them." The innkeeper reached past a shelf of pots to a little brass knob by the door frame and turned it. The taproom went black. Somebody groaned, a bench went over, a woman said "Oh, Dunnock, not again," in the voice of one who had said it before; and the lamps came back, and the innkeeper turned to Martin with the face of a man who has done something useful.

"Leave off showing people," said a carter on the stool beside Martin, who had a plate of mutton in front of him and gravy on his fist. "Every one off the train, he does it. I lost a potato."

"He asked."

"He asked who lights them. He didn't ask to see it go out."

The woman who had said Dunnock was next along the counter: thin, brisk, her sleeves still rolled from the laundry and her forearms red to the elbow. Martin would have put her at forty, until she mentioned in passing a granddaughter in the second class at the school; and then he looked at her again, and at the carter, and down the room. He had been looking at faces since the quay without knowing what he was looking for, and he saw it now. Good teeth. Clear skins. Grey heads on straight backs. Nobody coughing. In a taproom in the capital in October a third of the room would be coughing.

On the post by the door hung a printed sheet, two men reading it with their pots in their hands.

"What's that?"

"Hall notice," said the woman. "Whatever's done at the Assembly goes up on every door by morning. That one's the hopper money for the farms up the Milford road, and the new times for the Grayrock trains."

"And they read it? The men?"

She looked at him as if he had asked whether the men had feet. "Them as can. Which is most. Them as can't get it read them by their children, who can, and then they go to the evening class out of shame." She jerked her head at the carter. "He went."

"I went for the soup," said the carter.

"And there's the Almanac, once a year," said the innkeeper. "Everything's in the Almanac."

"Everything?"

"What the duchy took in and what it spent, to the copper. How many children at school, how many can read. The waterworks. The trains. The Chancellor's office counts it all and prints it."

"Who pays for the school?"

"Nobody," said the carter.

"All of us," said the woman.

The carter put down his fork, which Martin was beginning to understand meant something was coming. "We pay tax. Same as anywhere, same as always. Only he don't keep it, see. It goes back in. It's in the book what it goes on. So your lad goes to school for next to nothing, and the school pays his mam what he'd have earned at the nets, so she don't miss it. And in ten years he's up a crane on the quay, paid, and paying tax, and that pays for the next lad." He picked the fork up and pointed it at Martin. "And he was the last lad. So he's paying for himself. Backwards."

"You've had three," said the innkeeper.

"I've had two, and it still works."

"And not on the hearth," said the woman. "My mother paid hearth tax on one room and a smoke hole, the same as the old baron paid on his hall. It's on what you make, now. And what you own." She drank. "There's nobody owns much. There's nobody owns nothing, either."

"And the lords stand for it?"

"What lords?"

"There's him," said the innkeeper.

"He's the duke," said the carter, as if that were a different thing entirely.

"You'll see him tomorrow," said the woman, "if you stand in the square long enough. Walking. With the Chancellor and the healer, generally, all three arguing about something with drawings in it."

"With what guard?"

The carter shrugged. "The commander's got men about, I expect. Never seen one. Don't suppose you're meant to."

Martin thought of the king's eyes across a hall, which he had seen from behind three rows of other men's backs, and said nothing.

"Ask him about the healer," said the innkeeper, who was enjoying himself.

"Don't," said the woman.

"She's the nearest thing to a saint we've got," said the carter, "that the Church didn't make." The woman kicked his ankle under the counter, hard, and turned her eyes very slightly toward the long table and the black coats round it; and the carter, who had forgotten them, went red, and then said stubbornly, "Well, she is. Nobody in my house has had the winter cough in three years."

"That's the water," said the woman.

"It's her water."

"It's the stones in the water. She explained it at the Assembly, and one of the knights after her, and I followed about half of it."

"Less," said the innkeeper.

"It's good for you is the short of it. It's in the Almanac. The dose and all."

At the long table the ledger man had stopped eating. Martin saw him take out the little book and write a line in it, and then another under it, without looking up; and at the end of the bench, the quiet one, who had not turned his head all day for cranes or towers or the duke himself, had turned it now, a little, toward the counter, and turned it back.

Martin watched the pencil go. Something in it made him ask, though it was none of his business. "And all this. The pipes, the lamps. Doesn't he mind it getting out? How it's done?"

"Getting out where?" said the carter.

"Up the line. To anybody."

"It's not in," said the woman. "There's nothing to get out. What we know, he don't hide. How the lamps work, how the water's cleaned, how the trains go. You want to read it, you go down the Works Office and ask, and they give you the book. The printing works sells it by the pound."

"There's things you can't have," said the innkeeper.

"Well, there's things you can't have." The carter shrugged. "The dangerous stuff, I reckon. And the military."

"The military," said Martin.

"The garrison. The knights." The carter wiped his fist on his trouser leg. "He says army. And soldiers, for the knights. He mixes words up, sometimes, the duke. Says things nobody else says."

"He's a bit of an odd one," said the innkeeper comfortably, as he might have said it of a nephew.

"Like he's from somewhere else," said the woman. "Not off this earth, my mother says, but she says that about the Grayrock men." She drank. "It's the royal side, I suppose. Learning we never had. Who'd know? There's nobody to hold him up against."

"He calls the register a character sheet," said the carter.

"A what?"

"Everybody's on it. Every name in Kaldo. Your number, your trade, your year, your blood, whether you had the pox. The Chancellor's office keeps it. The duke calls it a character sheet. Nobody knows why."

Martin, who copied names into the chancery's books for a living, felt the hair move on his arms. "And you stand for that? Your name in a book in the castle?"

"They told us," said the woman. "Read it out at the Assembly, what's on it and why. You can go up and look at your own whenever you like. I went." She sniffed. "I was expecting worse. It's all things my neighbours know already. Even if it got a bit nosy, I'd not mind much. Everything's been better since."

"There's never been much thieving in Kaldo," said the innkeeper.

"There was never anything to thieve," said the carter.

"There is now." The innkeeper looked round his taproom, at the lamps and the tap and the pots on the shelf, with mild surprise, as if he had only just noticed. "And there still isn't much."

Martin looked into his pot. "You're very fond of him," he said. He had meant it to come out drier than it did.

"What's not to be fond of?" said the woman.

"He might be a right bastard for all I know," said the carter. "Never seen it. If he is, and this is what a bastard does," and he waved his fork at the lamps and the tap and the notice on the door and his own plate, "then we could do with a few more of them up north."

The counter laughed, and the tables nearest; and the laugh went down the room the way a good one does, and stopped at the bench under the window, where an old man in a leather apron sat alone over a pot with his back to all of it. Martin knew the set of the shoulders. It was the mason from the step.

A little after nine the quiet one got up from the fire, crossed to the bench under the window, and called for a jug. Once, when a door opened and the room went quiet, he heard the mason say, quite clearly, "Graves. I dug graves, for want of stone," and the quiet one fill the old man's pot. They sat till past ten. When the jug was done the quiet one paid for it, and put his hand a moment on the old man's shoulder, and went up to bed.

A man by the door with a pot of ale he had not touched since nine left at the same time. Martin noticed only because the pot was still full.



Emeric had known the four were in the room before he came in. He had known since the quay that morning, as he knew everything within half a mile of him now without trying. Outsiders were easy to pick out in Kaldo. Every note in the town had come up a little year on year, in its air and its water and its food, so that the whole of Kaldo sat a shade denser than anywhere in the kingdom and nobody in it knew; and a man fresh off the Wayford line read beside them like a cold room beside a warm one. Three of the four were thin and ordinary. One was a mage. He had noted it on the quay and gone on with his morning, because there were mages in Kaldo's streets every day of the week and half of them were Armand's.

Then he passed the end of the mage's table, and felt it.

Between the shoulder blades. Plain and physical. The pressure of a stare across a room before you have found it.

He stopped at the door with his hand on the latch, and did not turn round.

He had felt it twice before. In the long gallery over the kitchens in the capital, on the afternoon his father sent for him, with two maids arguing over linen and nobody looking at him when he turned; and on the road, at a fire, on the fifth night, with something at the treeline he had put down to a fox. Two stray numbers he had filed nowhere, twelve years ago, and not thought of since. He knew this one the moment it touched him, the way he would have known a voice.

Only now he had something to read it with.

So he read it, with his back to the room, while Ottoline told him the horn sleeves on the cards were cracking. A thread of mana, laid on him from twenty yards behind. Very fine, finer than Imani's hollow view, and not pitched to his note to be let in, as hers was, but laid along the outside of it, the way a man lays his palm along a wall to learn how thick it is. It was not pushing at anything. It was measuring. It had all the time in the world. It was the gallery's patience and the treeline's, the same hand; he was as sure of that as he was of Bram's note in a crowd, and could not have defended it to Ferra for a moment.

And what had there been to read, in the gallery?

Nothing. The answer came at the door, cold, the way a sum comes out wrong at the foot of a long column when you have known for three lines that it would. A prince with nothing open in him. A drunk with his father's eyes and his dead mother's blood and not a door in him that had ever been tried. Somebody had wanted to be sure of that, in the capital, and again on the road, out of his guards' sight. Somebody had wanted to know that the line had died in him unopened.

He was not unopened now.

And there was no way to make it less. He had never found one; nobody had, in any book of Armand's. He could pull his own sense down to nothing and his note would go on being what it was, as a lamp goes on burning when you shut your eyes. What it was, in a town that sat a shade denser than the kingdom, was a thing that sat denser than all of it together. Twelve years of Kaldo's air. The tree. The Band. The pod. The man behind him would not have to look for it. It was the loudest thing in the room.

Whatever he wrote down tonight would be true.

The Church had been watching him since the day he left the capital. And it had just been given the answer.

"...and when the horn goes, the chip goes with it," said Ottoline, "and then the card doesn't know anybody. Are you listening?"

"Yes," said Emeric, and opened the door, and went through it.



He told Saban that night, at the study window, with his back to the room. He told him one thing.

"One of the four is a mage."

Saban put down his glass. "Which?"

"I didn't look."

Saban looked at the back of his head for some time.

"That's all?"

"That's all. I want to know where he goes."

Saban brought it up in the morning on the back of a docket, in the pencil of a lighterman's cousin who could sit over one pot for three hours and be remembered by nobody: Supped with the party. Went across at nine and stood an old fellow a jug, a mason, going on about the printer. Listened. Paid. Bed ten past ten. Nothing else.

"Nothing," said Saban. "He likes a drink and he likes to be told about the old days by somebody who'll talk. So do I." He folded the docket. "The mason was Gideon Marl. Who set the trough."

"Gideon's been sore about the printer since the spring."

"Gideon's been sore about everything since the plague, and he's earned it." Saban took out his own small book and opened it near the back. "One more, and this one's mine. The one with the ledger, who does the talking. He was here last year on Hollow Night, at the corner of the river road under the last lamp, in a Wayford traveller's coat, from nine o'clock, watching the children. When Doran called across the square about the banns, he went off to the inn." He shut the book. "Different coat. Same man. I wrote him down then and I've written him down now. He came back for the rest of the page." He put the book away. "That one I understand. That's a clerk. It's the other one you aren't telling me about."

Emeric did not answer, and Saban did not ask again.

The four went north a week later with the inspector and eleven sacks of Kaldo's paper. Emeric was not at the station. He felt the quiet one go from the study window, up the line, until he was out of reach somewhere past the brickfield; and found that he had been standing with his hands flat on the sill for most of an hour, and took them off it.



Corbin's letter came on the sixteenth of October by the crown's courier, under the Lieutenant's seal; Saban's man's line came on the seventeenth in a laundry bag; and the table sat on both that evening in the wine store, with the lamp over the letter.

It was a good letter: one sheet in the old clerk's careful hand, signed in Corbin's own, small and upright and very even, a hand that had never once been allowed to blot.

That the settlement of Harrow Bridge, sealed under His Highness's own hand as Lieutenant, gave the crown's inspector leave to see the stores of the house of Kaldo at any hour. That the inspector could report only what he had been shown. That His Highness was therefore minded to come south in the inspector's company before midwinter, to see with his own eyes what his own seal had opened.

That certain questions had been put at court touching the duchy's practices: its schools, its census, its knights, its physic and its printing. That His Highness would wish his brother to answer them before the council in person; and would, while he was in the south, bring his brother up himself, gently, as a brother, so that they might be answered among family, and not otherwise.

Your loving brother, Corbin.

And Saban's man's line, in pencil on the back of a list of the prince's household linen: that there was a paper in the chancery's cabinet in the close's hand, a summons to the Duke of Kaldo to answer before the Church on matters of the faith, fair-copied and waiting unsealed since the week the inspector's party came home; and that it was understood among the clerks it would be sealed if the prince's letter were refused.

"Gently," said Imani.

"And not otherwise," said Ferra. She had her pen in her hand and had written nothing. "That's the whole letter. He's telling you there's an otherwise. In his best hand."

Armand had read it three times with his glass. "He's found a door in his own seal," he said, "and he's walking through it before anybody can tell him it isn't one. The clause opens the stores to the inspector. It doesn't open the duke."

"He knows that," said Saban. "The inspector's his excuse for the road."

Bram sat at the table's end with his arms folded, looking at the letter as if it were a weld he did not like.

Emeric looked at the pencil line, and at the week the summons had been written in, and thought of a man reading him from twenty yards with a patience that had waited twelve years. He did not say so. He thought instead about the simplest thing: that a summons waiting for a refusal needed a refusal, and if he went north with his brother, gently, there would be nothing to seal.

"If I go up with him," he said, "there's no otherwise. There's nothing for them to seal. I answer the council, which I can, and..."

"No," said Ward.

He said it across the table, flatly and not loudly, in front of all of them, before Emeric had finished; and Emeric stopped.

Ward did not say it again. He looked round the table at each of them, the way he looked along a rank.

"It is in his hand," said Ward. "It is not his letter." He put one finger on the laundry list. "There is a man who goes up the stair to Corbin's rooms twice a month with a portfolio. A cardinal's man. Saban's man has watched him go up it since the spring of last year." His finger did not move. "Schools, census, knights, physic, printing. That is not a list a prince makes. That is a list a priest makes. The summons waits for you to say no to your brother, so that it can say you would not come. And if you say yes, you are in the capital in winter, out of Kaldo, and the summons does not need a reason. It needs a door. You will have walked through it."

"You don't know that."

"I do not know whose it is. I know it is the Church's. I know it is not his." He took his finger off the list. "And I know you are not going."

Nobody said anything against it. Bram had put both hands flat on the table, which was Bram's yes. Imani had her chin on her hand and the look of a woman who would have said it herself, less politely.

"Ferra," said Ward.

"I'll write it. In my hand, as Chancellor. You'll sign under me."

She wrote for twenty minutes and read it to them. It thanked His Highness. It said the inspector was welcome at any hour, as the settlement provided, and His Highness with him, to see any store he liked. It said the clause gave the inspector leave to see the duchy's powder and its books and named no other thing; and that the duchy knew of no clause anywhere that gave any person leave to see the duke.

And it said that the Duke of Kaldo's name was on the register of Kaldo, as every name in the duchy was; that the Assembly of Kaldo had made it law, and the Standing Order had assented to it by name and in the open, that nobody on that register should be taken out of Kaldo, or held, or punished, without a hearing in an open court, the record to be read by any person; that the duke was bound by it as any carter was; that any question the council cared to put he would answer gladly and fully, in writing by return, or in open court in Kaldo before any magistrate the crown cared to send, Keller for choice; and that he would come north whenever his king summoned him, under the king's seal, in the king's name.

Ferra put the sheet down.

"It's my rule," she said. "I wrote it for Hob, and for a fisherman's neighbour. I didn't write it for you." She looked at him across the lamp. "It doesn't care. That's what it's for."

He signed under her name. Ward signed under his, and then all the others, though nobody had asked them to; and it went north on the morning express under the duchy's seal.

That night he lay on the warm floor of Ferra's room with his arm over his eyes, and neither of them asked him anything.

"You were going to say yes," said Imani at last, from the bed. She did not sound angry. She sounded as she did at the ward when a man told her he had been lifting with a rupture for a month. "You'd have gone up to the capital in December with Corbin holding your elbow. And I'd have been on the express after you with my bag, and Ferra after me with her book, and Ward after her with three hundred men under arms."

"Two hundred and ninety-nine," said Ferra, from her table. "He'd leave one to mind the gate."



Ward's answer in stone began on the twenty-first.

Saban called it that, at the table, the night the letter went north, when Ward unrolled a drawing he had plainly had in his coat for some time: the town wall all the way round, and at every turn of it a bastion, a great low arrowhead of stone pushing out from the wall's face into the ditch so that a man on its point could see along the wall's foot both ways. Six of them. And at the South Gate, in place of the two old round towers and their arch, a gatehouse: two towers either side, as tall again as the wall, with the arch run deep between them, a door at each end and room for a cart and team between.

"The settlement counts men under arms," Ward had said. "Three hundred, in time of peace. I have read it." He smoothed the drawing flat. "It does not count stones."

"He'll call it a fortress," said Saban.

"It is a wall. Kaldo has always had a wall. I am mending it."

On the twenty-first the masons went out to the South Gate, thirty of them, with the long printer's crew to follow for the bastions' cores, and Ward and Emeric with them; and they stood in the gate yard in a thin rain while Gale, who had laid the north well's apron, walked round the old towers with his hand on their stone and his face growing longer.

"These want to come down to the footings," said Gale. "They were poor work new. There's a crack in the east one you could put your fist in. Build on clean footings or build twice."

"They stay," said Ward. "You build round them. The new towers go up on new footings outside the old, and the old ones stand inside the new walls, whole. They are the cores." He took a lump of chalk from his pocket and walked round the east tower drawing a line on it at the height of his head, as far round as he could reach, and then the west. "Nothing below that line is touched. Not a stone, not a pin. Any man who needs to drill below it comes to me first."

"Why?" It was a mason's why, not a rude one: wanting to know what the stone was for.

"Because I said so," said Ward. "And it is my wall."

Gale looked at him, and at Emeric, who said nothing; and shrugged, and went to tell his gang.

Emeric stood in the rain and looked at the chalk line. Below it, in the feet of both towers, behind iron, lay what the Forge had shaped for the gate five years ago, after the Church's Visitor had gone home: the anchored set, planted in the old stone, that nobody in Kaldo had ever seen work and that he hoped nobody ever would. The old towers stayed because they were its cases. The gatehouse would go up round them like a coat round a man, and the set would lie deeper in stone than ever; and the masons would say in the river inn that the commander was sentimental about old towers, and that would be the end of it.

The masons' petition came in that week: eighty-one marks on two sheets, asking that the gatehouse's faces be dressed by hand and not printed, and that the masons of Kaldo be told in writing what their trade was to be now there was a machine for walls. Ferra answered it that night, two sides in her own hand, and had a fair copy run off for every man whose mark was on it.

Gideon Marl's was the fourth mark on the first sheet: square and heavy, pressed hard enough to dent the paper.



Rowena came on the twenty-eighth, to argue, as she had said she would.

She came along the sea lane in one of her grey sloops with her captain and four knights, with the grey in her ringed tail and her one blue half-cloak; and she got down in the castle's gate yard and looked up at the cage round the keep, which had climbed again that week, and said, "Good gods, Emmy. What are you doing to it," with the scar pulling at her mouth on the name, as it always did.

"Putting it under glass," said Ferra, who had come down with Ward to meet her. "Your Grace. Welcome to Kaldo."

"Ferra. You paid me too much."

"I paid you cost."

"You paid me cost, and then you paid me freight. For my grain in my ships on my own sea lane." She drew off her gloves. "I wrote cost and not a copper more, in my own hand. I said if he argued, I'd come and argue back. You've argued. Here I am."

"Your ships would have come back light. They came back full. Somebody loaded them at Emberfell and unloaded them here, and that's men and days, and I have the figures, and they're cost."

"Then give me back the freight."

"Then I'll enter it as a gift from the sovereign of Emberfell to the duchy of Kaldo," said Ferra, "in ink, in my book, where every clerk in the capital who reads my books will read that Emberfell gives Kaldo money."

Rowena looked at her a long moment. "You'd do it, too."

"Yes."

They argued at the table for two days, in company, with figures. In the end the freight went back to Emberfell in the sloop's strongbox, and the same sum, to the copper, came back the same night as Rowena's gift to the ward, for beds, in Imani's name; which Ferra could not enter as anything but Imani's, and at which she sat looking for some time, and then laughed.

Rowena read Corbin's letter at the table the first night, and Ferra's answer, twice each, and laid them side by side.

"He was a careful boy," she said. "He counted the spoons on his side of the table at dinner, under his breath, and if one was missing he wouldn't eat till it was found." She pushed the letter back to Emeric. "Somebody's handed him a list of spoons. Your Ward's right. That isn't his."

"Ward's usually right," said Imani.

"I know. He's been on the ground in front of me." She did not explain, and Ward, at the table's end, did not either.



She stayed for Hollow Night.

She had meant to sail on the thirtieth. On the twenty-ninth Saban mentioned the turnips, and Rowena asked what turnips, and was told by four people at once.

"Children dressed as monsters," she said. "Going round doors. For sweets."

"For stories," said Ferra. "Sweet or story. They have to know what they are."

"And this is his."

"It is his," said Ward.

"I'm staying," said Rowena, and told her captain that evening, and her captain did not even look surprised.

The second was a better night than the first, everybody said, because this year everybody knew what they were doing. Abloh had pinned a notice on its door in his own hand, THE SAILMAKER IS MAKING SAILS; and somebody, probably Tess, had added underneath, AND TAILS.

And it had gone up the line. Nobody had sent it; Grayrock's children had heard about it off the trains, the way children hear anything, and decided without asking anybody that Grayrock would do it too. On the twenty-sixth Grayrock's station master wired to ask for two wagons of Milford turnips, because the children had cut every turnip in Grayrock and the cattle were complaining. On the thirty-first the five o'clock from Grayrock came in with a carriage on its end full of small ghosts and smaller miners with lamps on their caps and a dragon made of a bedsheet on sticks, come to see how it was done properly, and taken up the hill by Kaldo's children like visiting cousins.

Ward had a bowl.

The year before he had opened his door to the first knock with nothing in his hands, and looked at the children on the step until they went away, and none had come back. This year he stood in his door in his black, with the lamp lit in the window as the notice said, and one of Maren's big blue kitchen bowls of caramels in the crook of his arm; and when the children came across the yard toward him in a crowd, pushing each other forward, he looked at them.

"Story," said Ward.

They told him, every one, one at a time, standing very straight in front of him as they would have stood in his yard; and he listened to each with his face doing nothing, and gave each one caramel exactly, and said "Next"; and by seven there was a queue across the yard to the gate of children who had been told by other children that the commander made you tell it properly, and wanted to.

Rowena kept the postern, with a bowl on her knee, and asked every child its story and then searching questions about it, in her court voice, until the story fell apart or rose to the occasion. A small Grayrock miner, asked how deep he went, said a mile; asked what was at the bottom, said after some thought, "More mine," and got three.

"She's terrifying," said Imani at Emeric's elbow, delighted. "Was she like that when you were small?"

"Yes."

"Then I like her. Go and get your mask."



The masks were Imani's idea, made by Tess in the Motion Shop out of paper and glue and Isle rubber, with Wren painting. Ferra had said for a week that she was the Chancellor and not a child; at half past seven, in the keep passage, she put hers on.

It was a fox.

"I don't know why it's a fox," said Ferra, through it.

"Because you're red," said Imani, "and clever, and you get into the henhouse." Hers was an owl, white and grey, with great round eyes painted round her own. "And I'm up all night."

Emeric's was a wolf, grey felt with a mouth of white paper teeth. He put it on, and Imani began to laugh and had to lean on the wall.

"What?"

"Your eyes. It's a wolf with gold eyes. There isn't another pair in the duchy." She wiped hers under the owl. "Every child in the town will know you from the end of the lane."

"Then he'll tell stories like everybody else," said Ferra, and took his arm through the fox, which she never did in the street; and Imani took the other; and they went out by the kitchen yard and down the hill into the lanes.

Every child in the town knew him from the end of the lane.

The first was a girl of about six in a sheet, who stopped dead in front of them in the smiths' lane and looked up at the wolf's eyes and said, accusingly, "You're the duke."

"I'm a wolf."

"You're the duke in a wolf."

"Then what's your story?" said Imani.

The ghost decided this was fair. She was the ghost of a girl who fell in the canal ring when it was all mud, before the duke came, and now she lived in the lock and pulled at the boats. Emeric had no sweets; Ferra, who had known he would have none, took caramels out of the fox's pocket and gave her one, and the ghost looked at the others in Ferra's hand and said, "And his story."

"His?"

"He's in a costume," said the ghost reasonably. "He has to say."

So the duke of the south told a ghost in a sheet that he was a wolf from the north who had come south for the winter because he had heard the sheep in Kaldo were very fat; and found when he got here that a fox had counted them all, and painted a number on every one, and punched a hole in every one's ear, so that a wolf who ate one would be found out by morning; and that he had been a very hungry wolf ever since, and lived on stories.

"That's a good one," said the ghost, and took her caramel, and went.

Ferra had her hand over the fox's mouth. "A hole in its ear."

"It's a story."

"It's slander against foxes." But her shoulders were going; and when Imani put her owl's face against the wolf's shoulder and laughed into it, Ferra laughed too, helplessly, under the fox, in the middle of the smiths' lane, with the children going by on every side with their lanterns.

They were stopped ten more times before the square, and each story was worse than the last. By the ninth the wolf had a grandmother who had been a dragon on her mother's side, and Imani was putting questions to him in Rowena's voice, and Ferra was keeping a tally on the back of her hand in pencil, because somebody ought to.

At the top of the smiths' lane, at the forge's door, under the best turnip in the street, which was an old woman's face this year, cut so finely the candle showed every line, Imani knocked before anybody could stop her. The door opened, and Bram stood in it in his shirtsleeves with a bowl, and looked at the fox and the owl and the wolf.

"Story," said Bram.

"Father," said the fox.

"I don't know any fox. Story or nothing. It's on the notice." He looked down at her. "You wrote the notice."

Ferra stood on her father's step in a paper fox's head for some time.

"I'm a fox," she said at last, with great dignity. "I live in a castle. I count things."

"That's not a story. That's your job." He gave her two anyway. Then he looked at the wolf, and the gold eyes in it, and his face did something complicated, and he gave the wolf two as well, and said gruffly, "Mind the fourth step," and shut the door.



Saban found them under the arcade by Maren's churns at nine, with their masks pushed up and their faces red with laughing and the cold. He had a dish of ice cream he was not eating, and a slip of paper.

"From my man in the chancery. Came on the evening train." He gave it to Emeric. "I thought you'd like it for the night."

Two lines in pencil on the corner of a blotter: that on the twenty-ninth a clerk of the close, in the chancery's long room, had asked quite loudly whether anybody knew what this mask night was that they kept in Kaldo at the end of October; and that nobody had.

Emeric gave it to Ferra. She read it with her fox pushed up on her red hair under the white lamps, and folded it in half and in half again; and he knew where it would go, at the back of her book, beside a proof with three pencil strokes through its last line.

"A night for stories," said Ferra.

"That's what the notice says," said Saban.

"That's all it says. I made sure." She put the slip in her pocket.

Saban smiled, and went off across the square toward Rowena, who had given the postern to Armand at half past eight and was standing under the market hall's lamps with Ward, watching Tess spin her chair among the dancers with Osric walking round it holding her hand, and saying something that made Ward look at his boots.

Emeric let it be the cold thing for the length of a breath: a clerk in a long room asking about mask night aloud; eleven sacks of paper going up a stair; a quiet man reading him from twenty yards with all the time in the world; a summons in a cabinet, with a week written on it.

Then Imani put her owl back on, and pulled his wolf down over his face by its ears, and took his hand.

"No," she said. "Not tonight. Tonight you're a wolf who lives on stories. Ferra, tell him."

"You're a wolf who lives on stories," said Ferra, and pulled her fox down, and took his other hand. "You've told eleven. I counted. And there's a lane behind the market hall with no lamps in it."

"Work first," said Emeric. "Fun later."

"It's a holiday. There's no work. I checked the book."

The lane behind the market hall had no lamps, only somebody's turnip on a sill at its far end; and in the dark the masks came up onto their hair, all three, and the fox kissed the wolf hard and without warning, the way she did; and the owl waited, and then did not wait, and kissed him slowly with her hand inside his coat, over his heart, counting.

"A hundred and twelve," said Imani against his mouth.

"That's high."

"It's a holiday. It's allowed."

They stood in the dark with their foreheads together and laughed quietly at nothing, until a small voice at the lane's mouth said, "That's the duke. With the fox and the owl. Behind the hall," and another said, "Sweet or story," and Ferra said, "Oh, for the Light's sake," and pulled the fox down.

It was the ghost from the smiths' lane. She had brought four friends.

Emeric told them about the wolf's grandmother, the dragon.



At midnight on the keep leads, with the turnips going out in the lanes below and Rowena gone to bed declaring she had not answered so many questions in twenty years, the faceless turnip stood on the parapet where he had set it at dusk, with its ring of small shapes cut round its side and its candle low.

There were two this year.

He had not carved the second. It stood beside his, smaller and a little lopsided, cut by somebody who had never cut one before; and round its side, in a ring, very small, in a square careful hand he knew, there were no shapes. There were tally marks. Rows of them, too many to count by that light.

He looked at Ferra.

"Don't," she said. "I don't know how many. Nobody does." She pulled the rug round the three of them. "That's what the marks are for."

Imani took his hand and did not count anything; and they stood there until both candles went out.
