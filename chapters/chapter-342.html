Chapter 342 - A Rest from Ruling

The first thing was a number, and it was wrong.

"Nine hundred and seventeen," said a voice above her, slowly, the way a man reads out a sign in a language he has only half learned. "And eight hundred and eight. Which is..." A pencil tapped twice on wood. "Seventeen hundred and twenty-four."

Ferra lay with her eyes shut and let the figure sit there. It went on being wrong. There was straw under her back and a board under the straw, and a smell of turned earth and pear leaves and lamp oil, and a hum that was less a sound than a pressure behind her teeth. Her hands were heavy. Something cold and close held each wrist.

"Twenty-five," she said.

It came out as a croak. The pencil stopped.

"Say it again."

"Seventeen hundred and twenty-five. You dropped the one." She swallowed, which hurt. "You always drop the one when you do it in your head. That's why you do it on paper."

She opened her eyes. The dome went up over her in its ribs and glass, faintly violet, the tree's crown across half of it, and Emeric sat on a stool by her board with a ledger on his knee. He looked as if he had not slept since she had, and he was grinning at her with his whole face, which she had seen him do perhaps four times in fourteen years.

"Which book is that?" she said.

"The quarter. January's coal."

"That's the station's coal. That's the quay book. The quarter's in the green one."

He turned the ledger over and looked at its spine. "I've been reading you this one for two weeks."

"Then you've been reading me the wrong book for two weeks."

"Bettany said it didn't matter what. She said a voice."

"It matters what," said Ferra.

She had read him a quarter once, by a lamp, all of one night, when he was the one on his back and nobody knew whether he would come up. She had not got a single figure wrong. She had been too frightened to. He put the quay book face down on the straw and took her hand, the right one, the one she had gone under holding, and she let him, and lay and looked at the tree, and neither of them said anything for a while.

Then she tried to sit up, and sat up, and kept going.

It was like reaching for a full jug and finding it empty. She came off the straw onto her knees and would have gone over the edge of the board if he had not had her hand. The cuffs clacked together. A fringe she did not own fell into her eyes.

"Slowly," said Bettany, from the next board, with her watch and a ring of keys at her belt. "They all do that. Your father sat up into the tree."

"Into it?"

"Into the bark. Head first, same as you. He said 'Hm' and went back to sleep for an hour." Bettany unlocked the cuffs, quick and deft, and counted Ferra's pulse against the watch with her lips moving. "There. Eight of nine." She wrote in her book. "You're the last but one."

"Last but one," said Ferra.

Bettany nodded down the row toward the end board by the door, where a shape lay under a blanket with its cuffed hands folded on it. "Saban. A week yet, Imani says. Maybe more."

Somebody had set her things out on the little shelf by the board: two hairpins, her citizen's card, her pen. The column was in ink and ought to be put right in ink. She uncapped the pen and pulled the quay book toward her.

The barrel went in two between her finger and thumb with a small dry crack, like a twig.

She looked at the halves. Ink ran down the inside of her wrist.

"Ah," said Emeric. "Yes. Everybody breaks something."

"That was the pen you gave me."

"Bram'll mend it."

"Who broke what?" She held up the halves, dripping. "I want it in order."

He looked at Bettany, and Bettany looked at the roof, and Ferra understood from both of them that it was a long list.



He went to fetch Imani, because Imani had made him promise, and Bettany went round the boards with her lamp from habit, though only one needed it now. Ferra sat on the straw with ink on her wrist and the broken pen in her lap and looked across the trunk.

On the south side Amara lay on the old mattress, as still as she had lain since March, and at the foot of it Amara sat with her hands round her knees and watched the room. Ferra saw both. It was the kind of thing you saw, once you had learned the stair.

"Good morning," said Amara.

"Good morning."

"You were quick on the stair. I told him so. Quicker than he was." She tipped her head toward the door Emeric had gone out of. "Has he said it yet?"

"He's said seventeen hundred and twenty-four."

Amara considered this. "Then no," she said, and looked pleased about something, and went back to watching Saban breathe.

Ward's book hung on its nail by the inner door, with the pencil on its string. It was the list she had asked for and nobody had given her.

The top line was Ward's: 4 April. Stone cuffs, nine sets, out. W. Under it the hands changed.

10 April. E. up, before six. Cuffs off. A.

12 April. D. up. Cuffs off. Pass-box door off its top hinge (D.). A.

14 April. W. up. Cuffs off by A. Key back on its nail. W.

15 April. R. up, noon. 16 April. O. up, first light. Cuffs off, both. A.

17 April. B. up. Hinge. B.

21 April. I. up. Cuffs off. A.

Most of the A's were Armand's, loose and old-fashioned. The one in Ward's line was Arthur's, square, pressed hard enough to come through on the next page. Ferra stood looking at that. Somebody ought to have thought about initials before they started. She would speak to somebody.

She took the pencil on its string and wrote under the last line, lightly, because she did not trust her hand: 24 April. F. up. Cuffs off by Bettany. Pen broken. F. The lead snapped on the F anyway.

The pass-box door had a bright new top hinge, and across the boards at head height somebody had chalked, in letters a hand high, GENTLY. She knew the hand. She opened it gently. It swung as if it weighed nothing, which it did not, and she caught it a finger's width from the wall and stood in the outer room with her heart going and the door in her hand.

"Gently," said Emeric, coming in the other way with Imani behind him.

"I read it."



Imani sat her on the king's crate in the outer room, because it was the only seat, and read her.

Imani did not talk while she did it, which Ferra had always found worse than talking. Two fingers on the wrist; a palm between the shoulder blades; then nothing touching at all, her hand a little way off Ferra's breastbone and her eyes gone soft and inward. Ferra watched her face. Imani was looking for something and not finding it.

"Heart's good. Lungs good. The bone's all new and it's got the packing in, like the three." She sat back on her heels. "You're well. You'll be hungry in about an hour and you'll eat like Osric."

"And you?"

"I read myself the morning I came up. Twice since. I had Armand do it once, in case I was being kind to myself." Imani shrugged. "Healthy. Boringly. I've never read anything so dull in my life. I'd made a list of things to look for."

She took a hand mirror out of her bag, the little round one with the crack across its corner. She held it up without handing it over, the way she held a thing up to a patient she did not trust with it yet.

"Go on. Everyone wants to."

Ferra looked. It was her own face, which was a relief, and then it was not quite. The line between her brows had gone. The grey of old ink that had lived in the grain of her forefinger for fifteen years had gone, she saw when she lifted the hand to her cheek, and the callus with it. The hair was the worst. It had grown in the dark, all of it, red and far too much, down past her shoulders in its pins, and a fringe had come up out of nowhere and hung over one eye like a curtain somebody had forgotten to tie back.

"I look like a pony," she said.

"You look rested." Imani tilted the mirror and studied her over the top of it, critically, as she might a set bone. "Your mouth's the same. You're frowning at it. You've lost the shadows under your eyes, and you've had those since the Works Office opened, so I don't know who you are now." She lowered the mirror to her knee. "You look about twenty-five, and furious about it."

Ferra took the mirror off her knee and turned it round.

Imani had come up three days before her, and it had not helped much. Her hair had grown in the sleep the way Ferra's had, a great dark coiled mass of it that went down her back past the middle now when she let it loose, so heavy that she had tied it with a strip of bandage because nothing else she owned would go round. Her face had always been a working face, a little tired at the corners. The tired was gone and nothing had come in to take its place, and without it she looked startlingly young, and like someone you would turn round for in the street. On the inside of her left wrist, where a kettle had caught her years ago and left a shiny patch the size of a thumbprint, the skin was smooth and the same colour as the rest of her.

Ferra put a finger on the place.

"I know," said Imani. "I keep looking for it."

They sat on the king's crate and looked at each other for longer than either of them would have admitted, until Emeric, who had been in the doorway the whole time with his hands in his pockets, cleared his throat. Then they both looked at him, together, and he went out.



The ring path had turned black and smooth under her feet while she slept, and smelled faintly of a ship's bottom, and it made her think of the works and who had been running them.

"Tess," said Imani, walking with a hand not quite under Ferra's elbow. "And she's had Osric, which you'll hear from everyone. He came up at first light on the sixteenth. Bettany sent a boy to the forge house because she'd promised, and twenty minutes later Tess was at the inner ward gate on a bicycle, in her nightdress and your father's coat. Osric came out of that door in his bare feet and sat straight down on the step because his knees went."

"A bicycle."

"There are bicycles now, and a list. You'll like the list." Imani's mouth went sideways. "He had a frame three off the works by the end of the week, I didn't ask how, and he rode it into the fountain. Mira had said he would. She's been unbearable."

"What did Tess say to him?"

"I wasn't there. Arthur was on the gate." Imani thought about it. "Arthur says she didn't say anything at all. She held the railing with one hand and him with the other, and he sat on the step, and after a while Arthur went and stood somewhere else."



The forge yard was loud, which Ferra had not known she missed.

Her father was at the big anvil under the lean-to with his sleeves rolled and a bar in the fire, and Paskin the mason was standing at the yard gate with a hod on his shoulder, staring at him. Paskin had built the ramp to the forge house door in nine days one November and had never in his life stared at anything but a joint that wasn't true.

"Morning, Chancellor," said Paskin, without taking his eyes off the anvil. "You'll know. Who's that?"

"That's Bram."

"It's not."

"It is."

Paskin thought about this. "I asked Ward last week who the new smith was, and he laughed so hard he had to sit down on my wall." He shifted the hod. "Well. If it's Bram, he wants to mind his tongs."

Bram reached into the fire with the long tongs and closed them on the bar, and the jaws folded in on themselves like wet paper. He drew them out, the ends rolled in a neat curl, and looked at them. On the rack behind him Ferra counted from habit: seven hooks, four tongs, one with a kink still in it.

"Hm," said Bram.

He hung the curled pair on an empty hook and took down another, and closed it on the bar the way a man picks up a sleeping baby, and drew it out, and it held.

"There," said Paskin, as though he had done it himself, and went off up the ring path with his hod.

Ferra crossed the yard. Her father looked like her older brother. He was taller and straighter in the back, the grey gone out of him and the burn speckle off his forearms, and his eyebrows, which the ball mill had taken in February, had come back thick and dark and a good deal more surprised-looking than before. He was the father she remembered from when she was nine. She had not known she remembered it.

"You're up," said Bram.

"I'm up."

He looked at her a long time over the anvil. Then he set the tongs down and put one hand on her head, the whole palm, the way he had when she was small, very gently, as if she were the tongs. Then he took it away.

"Hm," he said.

She held up the halves of the pen.

He took them in a palm gone pink and new, with none of his calluses left, and fitted the break together between finger and thumb, and did not go to the fire. The seam glowed a dull red, no wider than a hair, and faded. He handed it back warm, with a faint ridge down one side that her thumb would run along, after, whenever she was thinking.

"New trick," she said.

"Since the seventeenth." He took his pipe off its ledge, filled it, put it in his mouth and looked at the fire, eight feet away. "Watch," he said round the stem. "It does a thing."

The bowl went orange. It was not flame. It welled up thick and slow and glowing, a thimbleful, and the tobacco went in one puff of white smoke and the rim began to char. Bram held the pipe at arm's length and regarded it with his new eyebrows very high.

"That's rock," said Ferra. "That's melted rock."

"Hm." He knocked the pipe out against the post. A bead of grey stone dropped and clicked on the cobbles and lay there smoking. "Fourth pipe."

"You meant a spark."

"I meant a spark." He considered the cobble. "Didn't get one."

The feed gauge book hung on the post by the reservoir valve under the dome's wall, and she found it on her way back up; she would have found it if it had been in the river. Most of it was Tess in pencil, the dial's reading every hour through the sleep. Halfway down the second night: 3.40. Needle 34. Union at fourth plate, by the pear. Packing gone. A. tightened, a quarter and an eighth. Holding. T. found it.

Under it, in her father's square capitals, ten days on: 17 Apr. Union drawn and repacked. B.

And under that, smaller, as if he had walked away and come back: A.'s quarter and an eighth was right. Told him. B.

It was the longest thing Ferra had ever seen him write in a book that was not his own.



Armand had her reading by supper, because she had asked for it at noon, and he had written it out on a card because he had known her a long time.

"Every one of you," he said, and slid it across the long table. "Above the towers' register. You can see it in your cards, a little, if you know how to look. The gate clerk's sent for me four times. She thinks her reader's broken."

Ferra had seen it on the way in. Her citizen's card, held to the reader at the inner ward gate, had come up a shade brighter than it should, and the clerk had frowned and tried it again. Armand's card had two columns in his loose old hand. The towers' register was the Circle's line, the one a mage cleared to wear a tower's robe, and she had never seen it next to her name. Nine names, and Tess's and his own at the foot, and every figure cleared it. Emeric's cleared it by so much that Armand had drawn an arrow off the edge of the card.

"Mine," she said, and put her finger on it.

"A strong tower mage's, on the first day. It'll climb if you use it." He took off his spectacles and folded them, for the pleasure of it; he had not needed them since the winter. "And don't light every candle in a room to see if you can, please. Bram did on the eighteenth and we sent for a bucket."

Ferra, who had been thinking of exactly that, put her hands in her lap.

Ward had his sleeves rolled to the elbow. In thirteen years of suppers at this table Ferra had never once seen his forearms; he buttoned his cuffs to eat, and in summer he sweated. Now his arms lay bare on the board, his big hands round a cup, and the skin was clean from wrist to elbow, plain as a ploughman's, without a line on it.

Arthur was at the far end of the table with two fingers of his right hand strapped together. Ferra looked at the fingers, and then at Ward's hands round the cup, which held it the way her father had held the second tongs.

"He shook my hand," said Arthur, seeing her look, "when I did his cuffs. That's all."

"I said I was sorry," said Ward.

"You did, sir. Three times."

Ward drank. Nobody said anything about his arms. Bram, beside him, glanced at them once, and away. Ferra looked once more than that, and then at her plate, because Ward had caught her.

"Warm in here," said Ward.

That was all anybody said about it.

Declan sat across from her with his own sleeve pushed up, because Ward's was; he did most things a little after Ward did them. Halfway through the soup he stopped and turned his left arm over on the table and looked at the inside of it, where the bands had been. He laid two fingers on the place, flat, and kept them there.

She had heard him say Kal's name once, years ago, on a wall at night, and never since. He had said it in the voice his fingers had now.

He took them away and pulled the sleeve down and buttoned it, and went on with his soup.



"He told me the day after I woke," said Emeric. "The eleventh. I was sitting with you, and he came in with the mask on and sat on the other side of Imani's board and said it the way he'd say it looked like rain."

They were in his room with the door shut, the three of them. Ferra was in his bed with her fourth bowl of barley soup, because Imani had been right about the hunger and was being impossible about it. Imani was on the end of the bed with a chart on her knee. Emeric was at the window. Every candle in the room was lit, the four on the sill as well, running down onto the stone; Ferra had lit them all at once, an hour ago, by trying very hard to light only one.

"He's stepping aside," said Ferra.

"When we're all up. When Saban's up. He said, 'Argue with me when you've got your people round you, you'll do it better,' and then he went and sat in the outer room till three, and he hasn't said a word about it since."

"And you're going to argue."

"Yes."

Ferra looked at Imani. Imani wrote something on the chart and did not look up.

"Then there's a charter to write," said Ferra, "because I'm not crowning anybody on what we've got."

He talked about Paskin first. "He's the first. He won't be the last. Armand looks thirty. Your father looks like your brother. There are eleven of us and my mother, and the whole town's watched most of us get old, and they're going to see it, and then they're going to want it."

"Some of them should have it," said Imani.

"I know."

"I've a ward full of people one would mend. The dyer with the hip. The lime-kiln men's lungs."

"I know. That's half of it." He rubbed his face with both hands. "The other half's what comes after. Whoever eats one comes out stronger and lives longer and thinks faster, and they can delve. Their children aren't born any different, but there's a stone kept back for them at eighteen. Two generations of that and nobody remembers it was a gift. They think they were born to it." He looked at the door, which was shut, and lowered his voice anyway. "There's a book. From home."

Ferra put the bowl down.

He told it plainly and a bit too fast, like a man reading the parts list off the back of a drawing. A book called Red Rising. A people sorted into colours, not by skin but by centuries of breeding, a colour for each job: Reds in the mines at the bottom, Golds at the top. The Golds were bred tall and strong and quick and long-lived, and where breeding fell short they had themselves cut and rebuilt. They looked down and saw proof they deserved it. The worst of it, he said, was that everybody underneath looked up and saw the same proof.

Nobody said anything for a bit. Out in the yard a door banged and somebody laughed.

"Emeric," said Ferra.

"I know."

"Your eyes are gold."

"I know, Ferra."

"Your father's are gold. Waylon's are gold. You'll have a crown to go with them by midsummer." She tilted her head. "I'm only saying. If I'd written it I'd have been less obvious."

Imani laughed into the back of her hand. Emeric's mouth went, though he tried to stop it.

"The hero's a Red," he said. "A miner. The people who want it all pulled down take him and rebuild him into a Gold so he can get inside. Months of it. Knives. They break his bones and lengthen them."

"And he lived?" said Imani, with real interest.

"It's a book."

"Hm." She tapped the chart with her pen. "We used a tree."

"Then I'll say the unpleasant part," said Ferra, "since you're both being kind. We've got your colours already. Mages over knights, nobles over everybody. This doesn't build a new ladder. It gives the people on the top rung a better body to stand in. And it's scarce. One tree, six pods of eight at its best, two seeds in the east bed that won't bear for years. I've watched grain go that way, and salt, and ore. What's scarce gets owned."

"So I keep it quiet," said Emeric, and heard it as it came out.

"The way the Church kept the stones quiet," said Imani.

He had nothing to say to that. Paskin had seen to the quiet in any case.

"Rules, then. It doesn't go with a title. Nobody inherits one. Nobody buys one."

"Everything's bought in the end," said Ferra. "If coin won't, favours will."

"Then what?"

She reached into her hair for the pen and brought it out with a curl after it, and took the chart off Imani's knee without asking, and Imani let her. She turned it over and wrote on the back, small, reading as she went.

"Not by birth. Not by coin." The pen stopped. She wrote the third. "Not by the crown."

"I'm not the crown."

"You will be." She did not look up. "You're the man with gold eyes who owns the tree. If the stones come out of your hand it won't matter how fair you are. In twenty years it's the castle's gift and people bow for it. Somebody else decides. More than one somebody, and people who can be put out."

He opened his mouth and shut it again.

"Mine goes at the top," said Imani. "Nobody under eighteen."

Ferra drew an arrow and wrote it in above the first line, and turned the chart round so he could read it. Four lines, one of them squeezed in with an arrow.

"It's four lines," he said.

"Good ones start short. Then Saban wakes up and finds eleven ways round each of them, and we write more."

He laughed properly then, and sat on the end of the bed, which dipped toward him. He had come up two inches taller and had been hitting his head on the old keep's lintels for a fortnight, and Ward laughed every time.

"Does he win?" she said. "The miner."

"In the end. It costs him."

"From the beginning, then," said Imani, and laid the chart on the side table, the rules face up, where none of them could miss it in the morning.

He began. Ferra was asleep before the boy got out of the mine. She woke once in the night with the pen gone from her fingers and found it capped and lying on the chart, and Imani's breathing on the far side of him, and went back to sleep.



She drafted the charter in four days at the long table with the seamed pen, by writing down everything anybody wanted and crossing most of it out.

The Hearing Rule went first, because it had been the Assembly's first law and she would not have a crown come before it. Nobody's land or house or tools could be taken without an open hearing, given notice in writing, where they could speak and bring someone to speak for them. What it had never had was somebody to make it hold with no duke's court behind it. She gave that to the Assembly's clerk, who already kept the hearings book; and past the clerk, appeal to Keller's court in the capital, because she had watched Keller lose every point but one, and it had been the one that mattered.

The thirty-two-hour week was a single line, and it cost a morning. Most of Kaldo's works were at it already under her rules; in the charter it would bind every master in the kingdom. Saban was still under and could not tell her what the capital's mill-owners would say, so Ward did.

"They'll say they can't pay it."

"They can pay it. I've seen their books." She wrote. "Any hour past thirty-two at half as much again, and the hour's the worker's to give or keep, not the master's to take."

"The knights don't keep thirty-two hours," said Declan.

"The knights aren't a works."

"Bram's forge doesn't. A heat doesn't stop because a clock says so."

"Then Bram pays himself half again," said Ferra, "and he can argue with himself about it." Her father, at the end of the table with a new pipe he was not lighting, said "Hm," and she wrote it down as a yes.

The stones clause went in from the back of Imani's chart as it stood, four lines, and under it the space she had promised Saban.

She wrote the last clause on the twenty-ninth, late, with nobody at the table but Emeric, and slid the sheet across and gave him the pen.

He signed at the foot where she had ruled a line for it, without reading, and held the pen out to her.

She did not take it. "Read it."

"I trust you."

"That's why you're going to read it."

He read it. The Chancellor could stay any act of the crown in writing, and the stay held until the Assembly had heard the matter openly and voted it up or down; and while a stay stood, the crown could not put the Chancellor out. It was the clause that had bound him as duke since the Assembly first sat, word for word, with the crown written in where the duke had been.

He looked up.

"Somebody has to be able to say no to you," said Ferra. "It was always going to be me."

He read it again. Then he took the pen out of her fingers and signed it a second time under the first, slowly, in full, and wrote the date beside it at the top right, small, where she always put it, and gave the pen back.



Saban came up on the second of May at twenty past ten in the morning, and the dome was full.

Nobody had arranged it. Bettany said at breakfast that his line was rising, and by ten they were all there. The six and Emeric, less the one on the board. Tess in her chair by the north pane with Osric behind it, carefully not leaning on it. Rylan with his boots off, for no reason anyone could get out of him. Armand in his cardigan. Bettany in a mask, with her watch. The king on his crate at the foot of his wife's mattress, in the mask he had refused to wear for ten minutes in April and had now worn for forty without a word. Amara sat beside him and leaned against his shoulder, which he could not feel.

Outside the glass, Wren had her nose on the north pane and Arthur was pretending not to stand behind her.

Saban opened his eyes, and shut them, and opened them again. Then he turned his head on the straw and looked along the row of faces, one at a time, the way he counted a room when he came into it in the capital, and Emeric watched him reach the end and go back to the beginning.

"Who's minding the shop?" said Saban.

"Nobody," said Ferra. "We're all here."

Saban looked along the row again. For fourteen years he had come home from the capital to suppers already eaten and decisions already taken, and read them in Ferra's minutes, and never once complained. His mouth worked.

"All of you came up the hill," he said.

"You were last," said Bram. "Hm."

From Bram it was a speech, and Saban seemed to know it. After a while he said, in his ordinary voice, that somebody had better tell him what had been said in the capital, in what order and by whom; and Ferra took a folded sheet out of her sleeve, already written. Bettany had his cuffs off by then. The sheet tore clean in half between his fingers, and he looked at the halves and began to laugh, and could not stop until Imani made him hold still and be read.

The king took his mask off in the outer room afterward, and wiped his face, and caught Emeric's eye across the crate.

"Tomorrow evening," said Harrold. "You've your people round you now."



They had it out in the king's rooms in the old keep on the evening of the third, with the shutters open and a jug between them that neither of them drank from.

Emeric had prepared, the way he would have prepared a drawing. The kingdom was not five months out of a war over who came next. The lords had taken Harrold back. The Empire was watching, and the Church. A crown changing hands now would look, from Ostrel, like a crack. And Harrold was well, and not old, and nothing was making him do it.

Harrold heard him out. He did not interrupt once, which was worse than if he had.

"Are you finished?"

"No," said Emeric. "Kaldo needs me at the works. I'm not a king. I've never wanted it."

"I know you haven't." The king leaned back, and the lamp found the grey at his temples. "That's a reason for, not against. Go on."

"That's it."

"Then I'll tell you mine." Harrold put his hands flat on the table. "A war over who came next. The way you stop the next one is a king who hands it on alive, in daylight, with his name on the paper and his son at the table, so nobody can stand up in ten years and say what the old man would have wanted. The old man's here. He's saying it." He tapped the table once. "If I wait till I'm dead to do it, I've left you exactly what my father left me, which was a room full of cousins."

"It doesn't have to be now."

"When, then? When it breaks?" Harrold looked at him. "You've spent fourteen years telling me a thing should be handed over before it breaks. Bridges. Boilers. You had the station men change every bearing in the big engine one spring when nothing was wrong with any of them. I asked you why, and you told me that was the whole point."

"That's maintenance."

"So is this."

Emeric said several things after that, each worse than the last, and could hear it. The king let him say them all.

"You want me to keep it," said Harrold at the end, quite gently, "so you don't have to carry it. That's not a reason. That's a hope." He poured two cups at last and pushed one across. "I decided on the crate. The third night. I sat in that mask looking through a door at the two of you on your backs, and Armand told me you'd both stood up out of yourselves and were walking about together, and I couldn't see either of you. I've been on the wrong side of that glass for nearly thirty years." He drank. "I'm tired, Emeric. I've been king alone a long time. I'd like a rest from ruling. I'd like my wife."

Emeric looked at the cup.

"I'm not going anywhere," said Harrold. "I'll be at the end of your table telling you you're wrong. As a king, when you want one. As an old man, when you don't. As your father, whichever." He smiled for the first time that evening. "You'll find it a great comfort. Ask Ferra how she gets on with Bram."

It was past midnight when Emeric came down the keep stair, and he hit his head on the lintel at the bottom, and stood rubbing it. Ferra was sitting on the bottom step with her shawl round her and a candle she had not lit by hand.

"Well?" she said.

"He's right."

"I know," said Ferra. "I've been sitting here an hour waiting for you to find out."



Harrold stepped aside at the long table at ten the next morning, the fourth of May, by an instrument Ferra had drawn in the night. The six signed under him as witnesses. Waylon would read it in the capital's hall, and the crowning was set for June. It took twenty minutes. Maren's coffee girl, up with a can for the guardroom, stood in the doorway through all of it and said afterward she'd thought it would be louder.

The next morning he ate his stone.

It was the one Imani had set aside for him long ago, and he had carried it against his skin since the winter. On the nights he spoke to his wife through Emeric's delve, Amara had walked him into it a little at a time, and Imani had read it the evening before and called it bonded well enough. He sat on the board by the trunk in his shirtsleeves with the stone in his palm.

"I could take you through the way we did," said Emeric. "Tonight. You needn't do this yet."

"I know I needn't." Harrold turned the stone over. "I'd like to hear my wife without my son in the room."

Amara, at the foot of her mattress, put her face in her hands.

He swallowed it like a man taking medicine, and lay back, and Imani put the cuffs on him herself and sat down beside his board with her book, and was still sitting there when the lamps were lit.

Imani sat with him every hour her ward could spare her, that week. Amara sat all of it. Emeric went in once, on the third night, and found the stair and went down it and found his father's door shut, and his mother sitting on the step outside it with her chin in her hand.

"Not tonight," said Amara. "He's learning to be small, and he's worse at it than Osric was. He's been a king a long time." She looked at the door. "He keeps standing up to his full height to see where I've gone."

"Is he all right?"

"He's being taught," said his mother, "by a woman who taught school. He's never been better."



Sorrel's second glider stalled off the west hill on the afternoon of the sixth and came down on its left wing in the gorse, and by the time Emeric heard of it, it was solved.

Wren shouted it at him from her bicycle at four without stopping. When he got to the Motion Shop's back bay both gliders were hanging from the roof beam on ropes, the second with its left wing root cracked through. Sorrel stood under them with a split lip and her arm in a sling she plainly resented, and the Seers' Table round her: Corin with a hand on the broken root, Pell on a ladder, Rue at the board with chalk. Tess watched from her stool under the Seam Table's squares.

"It's the seat," said Rue, before he could ask. "I moved it."

She had drawn the second one single-seat and longer in the wing, and moved the seat back a hand's breadth for Sorrel's knees, and nobody had put anything forward to make up for it. Pell had found it: he had hung both machines from the beam on one rope each, slid the rope until each balanced level, and chalked the place. The second balanced a hand behind the first.

"So it's tail-heavy," said Corin. "Off the cord it climbed, and slowed, and then it just stopped flying and the left wing went."

"I pushed," said Sorrel. "I pushed the stick right forward. It didn't want to come."

"It couldn't come," said Tess. "There wasn't enough in front."

Rue had written HANG POINT on the board, with the two marks under it.

"Centre of gravity," he said. "The point it balances on. If it's behind the wing's lift, the nose comes up."

Rue wrote it under HANG POINT, without rubbing anything out. "Same thing."

"Same thing."

"Then we were right," said Rue, satisfied, and went back to the drawing, where she had already sketched a bag of lead shot in the nose and a new root with a steel plate either side.

Sorrel looked up at the cracked wing hanging over her. "Next time," she said, "somebody hangs it before I fly it."

"It's going on the sheet," said Pell from the ladder. "Hang it, mark it, then fly it."

Emeric stood under the two gliders with nothing to do, and Tess, across the bay, was laughing at him.



Saban found the eleven ways round, as promised; nine of them in one afternoon at the long table, read out in a pleasant voice while Ferra wrote.

A board had cousins. So, no vote on kin: whoever's blood or marriage was in the case left the room, and the book said so. A board could hand stones to its friends in a back room. So, an open book, every decision and every refusal with its reason, in the records hall. A board could be wrong. So, appeal to the Assembly. A record of service kept somewhere private could be bought like anything else. So the record had to be published, and the board could weigh nothing that was not.

"And when two are equal?" said Armand. "Two good records. One stone."

"A lot," said Saban. "Drawn in public."

"You'd put a stone to chance."

"I'd put a tie to chance." Saban turned his cup round on the board. "Because you can't buy a lot. You can buy nearly anything I've ever met. Not that."

Imani would not have the lot anywhere near her end of it. Healing came first, before any record, judged by her board of healers on the body in front of them and not by chance. Saban did not argue. He wrote healing first, by the healers' board above his own lines and underlined it.

Ward asked what happened when the lot fell on a fool.

"Then the fool's on a board of five," said Saban, "and the other four can count."

There were to be five. Imani, for the healing. Armand, for the stones. Master Aubin from the Polytechnic, who would not sign what he could not measure. One of the holdings' stewards, who knew who held what. And one member of the Assembly, drawn by lot in public. Never the crown: Ferra wrote that as its own line, so that nobody could add it later by forgetting.

They drew the Assembly's member on the records hall steps at noon on the eighth. Ferra's clerk turned the drum, and the trough boy from the bicycle sale put his arm in to the shoulder and brought out a slip.

"Prudence Fairley. The third ward."

A woman in a tram conductor's jacket, with a ticket punch round her neck, said, "Oh, no."

"You put your name in," said the clerk.

"I put it in for the Assembly. That was a lot as well." She looked at the drum with deep suspicion. "I've a shift at two."

"The board sits on the tenth," said Ferra. "Mornings. You'll be paid the shift."

Prudence looked at the trough boy, who was waiting to see whether there was a copper in it. "Then he can go and tell the depot," she said, and he went.

The board sat on the tenth in the long room off the records hall with the doors open, and a good many people stood at the back. The book lay ruled on the table: date, name, what was asked, by whom, decided, and why. Master Aubin, small and grey with two pencils behind one ear, asked for the definitions of six words before he would start, and got four. The steward said very little and wrote everything down.

The first entry was the dyer with the hip, on the healers' board, granted. The second was a lord in the north asking a stone for his sixteen-year-old son and offering a sum Emeric heard from the back of the room and tried to forget. Refused, Master Aubin wrote: under eighteen; not by coin. Prudence Fairley leaned over and said something, and he added: Both reasons. Either would do.

Another was a carter with a bad heart whose wife's sister, it came out, was married to Prudence's brother. Prudence got up unasked and stood in the doorway with her arms folded while the four decided, and the clerk wrote that she had, and she came back and sat down.

"That's how you do it, is it," she said to nobody, and took up her pencil.

Ferra wrote the town its sentence for the News that night. She wrote it eleven times and Saban crossed out ten, and it went in on the twelfth, at the foot of the front page in a box, under the price of flour:

The stones lengthen life; they have been tested on those who ate them first, under a healer's eye; and who is given one is the Stone Board's to decide, in an open book at the records hall, which anyone may read.

Hobb ran four hundred extra. They were gone by nine.



Imani grew the rat a toe on the seventh, and it came out wrong.

The rat was a brown doe from the old colony who had lost the outer toe of her left hind foot to a cage door in March. Imani had her asleep under a drop of ether on a folded cloth in the clean room, and Emeric stood at the door with his hands washed to the elbow, and came no closer, because she had not said he could.

She woke the stump. With the sight he could see the end of the foot go busy and bright, every cell at the cut letting go of what it had been. Blastema; she said it under her breath, carefully. Then she told it what to be.

It grew, fast, a pale nub and then a longer one; and where the last joint ought to have rounded off into a claw, it went on, and kinked sideways, and hardened into a crooked little spur of bone.

Imani looked at it a long time.

"There," she said. "That's the lump."

"Imani."

"Hush. I know what I did." She took the spur off at the joint with the small knife and stopped the bleeding with two fingers. "I gave it the toe from the book. A rat's toe. Not hers." She turned the rat over and took up the right hind foot, the whole one, and read it a long time under the lamp. "Hers are shorter than the book's. And they turn in."

The second time took half an hour. The toe came up pale and short and turned in, with a claw on the end of it no bigger than a fleck of mica, and when the rat woke she licked it, and looked offended, and licked it again.

Imani wrote up both tries in her book, and at the bottom: Read the other side first. Always.



Danner had come down on the express on the second with his sleeve pinned up, and had sat a week in a clean bed at the end of Imani's ward, eating.

It was the cleanest room in Kaldo. The linen was boiled, and anyone who came in washed to the elbow at the basin outside in boiled water, with Imani's women watching, who sent back anyone who skimped, once including Ward. Over the bed hung the feeding sheet, and Danner had had it read to him so often he could say it: liver, twice a day; beans, at every meal; milk, as much as he could hold; broth from the bones, between.

"Why liver?" he had asked her on the first day. He was perhaps twenty, a private of the fourth company, with a narrow face and very steady eyes.

"Because I can't make iron," said Imani. "Or chalk. Not out of nothing, nobody can. Your new blood wants iron and your new bone wants chalk, and I'm not going to take either from the rest of you if I can help it. So you eat them."

"All of that?"

"An arm weighs more than you'd think." She considered him. "You'll get thin anyway. Everywhere but the arm."

On the ninth she began.

The old healer was in the room for it, because Imani had asked him to be. He had come down from the capital's infirmary in February to learn the washing and stayed to argue, and when she took the dressing off the stump he said it to the room, not unkindly, the way you say a thing you were taught by somebody who was taught it.

"Nobody grows a limb. You grow a lump."

"Yes," said Imani. "I grew one on the seventh."

She sat down on the stool by the bed on Danner's right and put her hand on his right arm, the whole one, below the shoulder, and closed her eyes.

Inside, it was very quiet. She had always read a body as a house with the lamps lit, and now the lamps were brighter and she could go further in before they thinned. His right arm first. The long bone and the two below it, the way they turned over each other when he turned his hand; the joint; the muscles in their sheaths and where each tied to bone; the vessels and nerves running beside them like rope beside a hawser. She did not hurry. This was his, not the book's arm: a little long in the forearm, a little thick at the wrist from pikes, a knot above the elbow where something had cracked when he was a boy and healed crooked. She would not give the new one the knot. She would give it everything else.

Then she crossed over, and put her other hand an inch off the sealed end of the left arm, a hand's breadth above where the elbow should have been, and woke it.

It woke the way the rat's had, all at once, a busy brightness at the end of the bone. Here was where the lumps came from. It wanted to go anywhere, every direction, like water at the top of a slope; and she gave it one place to go and one thing to be. Bone first. The long bone only, today, the first inch of what had been taken, the right length and the right turn, drawing what it needed out of the liver and the milk and the broth, and out of him.

When she opened her eyes two hours had gone and Danner was grey and sweating, and so was she. Under the sealed skin at the end of his arm was a new inch of bone, the stump a little longer than at breakfast, the skin over it pink and thin and closed.

The old healer came closer and put out a hand, and stopped it an inch off, and took it back.

"That's bone," he said.

"That's bone," said Imani. "Tomorrow more bone. Then the joint, and that's a week by itself. Then the rest." She stood up, and her knees told her about it. "Six weeks, if he eats."

"I'll eat," said Danner, whose voice had gone hoarse. He was looking at the end of his arm. "Is there more liver?"



Harrold woke on the twelfth, in the morning, a week to the day.

Imani had sent for Emeric. She sat at the board's side with her fingers on his father's wrist, and Amara sat on the straw at his head, where she had sat all week.

The man who opened his eyes was perhaps thirty-two. His father had always been tall, and he was taller, his feet over the end of the board; the grey had gone from his temples and the lines from round his eyes; his hair was dark and thick and needed cutting. His forearms were clean, as they always had been; he had never needed a rite. His eyes were the same clear gold, and the only thing in the face Emeric would have known straight away.

His eyes found Amara before they found anything else.

She was sitting where she had sat every night since March, and he had never once seen her there. He saw her now. It took him a while to make his mouth do anything.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello, Harry."

He reached for her, and his hand went through her wrist and came down on the straw; and he looked at the hand, and laughed, a short astonished sound like a boy's, and left it lying there next to hers.

"Can you go out?" he said. "Into the ward. The town."

"No. Not past the glass. Not yet." She looked down at the hand on the straw. "You'll have to come to me."

"Then I'll come to you," said Harrold, and seemed to think that settled it.

Amara looked over his head at Emeric, standing at the board's foot with his hands in his pockets.

"Your son's in the room," she said.

"I know he is," said Harrold, without looking round. "He can go."

He went.



That night they ended up in his bed again, the three of them, because the bed in Ferra's rooms was worse and Imani's was full of charts.

It had been a long day and a good one. They talked in the dark about Danner's inch, and about Prudence Fairley, who had sent Ferra a note asking whether the board was paid by the sitting or the hour, and if the hour, which hour; and about Harrold, who had come down to supper in a borrowed shirt of Ward's that would not go across his shoulders and told Emeric down the whole length of the long table that he was salting his soup before tasting it, which was true.

Then Ferra had stopped talking, in the middle of a sentence about the board, and kissed him; and then Imani had; and it had gone on a while, and then gone further than that, and he had stopped it. He did not quite know how. There was a good reason and he had it somewhere. It was a long way down.

"Hm," said Ferra, into his neck, exactly like her father.

"Not yet," he said.

"You said that last time," said Imani, from the other side, not crossly, and yawned, and put her head on his chest.

They were asleep in ten minutes. He was not.

The bed was too small for three, and his feet were off the end. Ferra was on his left, wound round his arm with a knee hooked over his, and she had always slept hot and the stone had made her hotter. Imani was on his right with her hair everywhere, across his chest and in his mouth whenever he breathed in, and one cold foot on his shin. He lay on his back and looked at the soot Ferra's candles had left on the ceiling in April, and wanted, very plainly, and did not, because he had said not yet and meant it; and every time he had nearly made peace with that, one of them sighed and moved, and he had to start again.

He counted the ribs of the ceiling's beams. He did the Hod's lift sums in his head and got one wrong and could not tell which. At some point Ferra said "twenty-five" quite clearly in her sleep, and he lay there shaking with silent laughter until Imani's foot found his shin again and he stopped.

He heard the castle clock strike three, and four, and the first bell of the trams.

In the morning they were both rested and pink and enormously cheerful. Ferra ate four eggs and read the News aloud with the seamed pen behind her ear, and Imani did her charts in bed and sang under her breath, which he had never heard her do. He sat on the edge of the bed with his elbows on his knees.

Ward met them on the stair. He looked at Ferra, and at Imani, and at Emeric, for rather a long time.

"You look dreadful," said Ward.

"Thank you."

"Did you sleep?"

"No," said Emeric.

Ward looked at the two women again. Imani smiled at him with great sweetness, and Ferra had her face in the News. Ward's mouth twitched, and he pressed it flat, and it twitched again, and he turned and went back up the stair, and they heard him begin to laugh somewhere around the second landing.

"He'll tell Bram," said Ferra, from behind the paper.

"Bram'll say 'Hm.'"

"Bram'll say 'Hm' at you," said Imani, "for a month." She took his arm, and Ferra took the other, and they went on down. "Tamsy Brook's has its glass doors open. Tess says they fold right back, the whole front, and you can sit outside under the pear. We're going tomorrow. All of us."

"Is that a question?"

"No," said Ferra, and turned the page.
