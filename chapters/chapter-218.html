Chapter 218 - Blankets

The snow came to the stream ground on the second of December, and after that nobody talked about going home.

It came at night, without wind, and in the morning the earthwork was a long white bank with a dark seam along its top where the sentries had walked, and the field beyond it was white to the far hedge, smooth, every hoofprint and every shallow trench and every place where the burial parties had worked filled in and gone. Emeric stood on the bank at first light with his hands in his coat and looked at it. He had walked that field on the evening of the twenty-sixth. He had walked it again, on Ward's business, on the twenty-eighth. Now it looked like a field.

"It will thaw," said Ward, beside him.

"I know."

"Then you will see it again." Ward looked out at the white for a breath longer. "Come and eat."

The levy wintered where it stood. That had been Ward's ruling, and Linus Vance's, and in the end the ruling of the letter that came down the wire from Saban on the first of the month: that Aldane was at his hall, and was not sending heralds, and was talking. To his tenants, to the knights who had come home, to the men at his table. A second host in the spring. Every house that had lost a son at the stream ground owed him a man for it; every house that had lost a knight owed him two. Saban's man in the capital said it was the first time in thirty years anybody had seen men at court in a hurry.

So the levy stayed on the stream ground, north of Harrow Bridge, behind the earthwork Linus had drawn and Ward had set, two days' hard riding from nowhere in particular and a long way from home. They cut turf and timber and built huts along the back of the bank, low and long, with the earthwork for one wall and a roof of poles and turf and Tess's tarred cloth, twelve men to a hut, a stove of sheet iron at each end that hull two had brought up in pieces.

And they were cold. That was the camp's great subject, from the second of December on. The huts were warm enough at the stove ends and cold in the middle, and the men in the middle slept in their coats, and the blankets they had marched north with in October had been sound wool for October and were not sound wool for a field in the snow. Half of them had been cut up for rifle rags. Some had gone under the wounded on the twenty-sixth and not come back.

Amos Brindle fixed it. That was how he told it afterward, and for ten days it was true.

Brindle had the camp's suttling from Sethe's office in Wayford, by a contract Ferra had read twice and approved once: bread, salt pork, lamp oil, boot grease, tobacco, the small necessaries of an army that the barony did not carry itself. He was a narrow cheerful man with a red scarf, honest as suttlers go, which Ferra said was a low bar he cleared by a hand. On the twenty-seventh of November, the day after the battle, he came up the road from Harrow Bridge with two carts instead of one, and the second was stacked to the hoops with blankets.

Army blankets. Heavy grey wool, the coarse northern weave, a broad dark stripe at either end; the sort a duke's quartermaster bought by the thousand. Four hundred of them. Some darned, some faded, a few with a name stitched in a corner in a hand that was not a soldier's. A wool dealer had come down to the Wayford market with a wagon of them, Brindle said, a northern man, from up past Lisle, selling off a quartermaster's surplus that some lord had over-bought; and he had let them go at four Copper apiece because he wanted to be home before the roads shut, and Brindle had bought the lot.

"Four Copper," said Rylan, turning one over on the cart's tail. "For that."

"Four Copper," said Brindle. "Six to you. Five to the prince, because he's paying for most of them."

Emeric paid for most of them. Ward had them counted out by hut, the stove ends last, and by the evening of the twenty-eighth every man on the stream ground who wanted one had a grey blanket with a dark stripe, and the camp's great subject changed to the food.



Osric was writing a letter.

Emeric came on him by accident on the evening of the sixth, at the end of the officers' hut, which was the same as the other huts but with a table in it. Everybody else was at the stove end, where Declan was losing at cards to a sergeant from Grayrock and saying nothing about it. Osric was at the cold end, on a crate, with the table's one spare lamp, and a sheet of the school's paper in front of him on a board across his knees, and a pencil in his fist the way he held it, and his tongue between his teeth.

He covered the sheet with his hand when Emeric's shadow came over it. Not quickly; Osric did nothing quickly that he did not have a sword in his hand for. He just put his hand flat on it, enormous, and looked up.

"Accounts," he said.

"Of course."

"Ward wants the remount figures."

"He does," said Emeric, who knew that Ward had had the remount figures from Declan on the first. He went back down the hut to the stove and sat with his back to the cold end, and watched Declan lose another hand, and did not turn round. Behind him the pencil went on, very slowly, with long stops in it.

It went south on the seventh, on hull two, in the mail bag, folded small and sealed with a blob of candle wax and a thumb, addressed in capitals to TESS, AT THE WORKS, KALDO. Emeric saw it in the bag because he was putting his own in. He did not look at it twice.



Jory Dace went to the surgeon's hut on the seventh with his back.

He was a big slow levy man from the dock quarter, a rope-walker before the war, and he had a back that hurt from his neck to his hips, so badly he could not lie on it, and a fever, and a head, and he had been sick twice in the night behind the hut. The camp surgeon was a Wayford man Imani had trained for a summer and had sent north with a box of her stores and a list. He looked at Jory, and felt his head, and asked what he had eaten, and put him on a cot by the stove and gave him willow bark and broth and called it camp fever, which was the honest name for most things that happened to men who slept twelve to a hut in the snow.

By the evening of the eighth there were seven on cots by the stove, all with the back and the fever and the head, and all from the three huts at the west end of the earthwork, and the surgeon sent a line south by the hull's wire asking for Mistress Imani at the next run, if she could be spared, and for more willow bark either way.

She came up on the night run of the eighth. She had been coming every third week since the battle, for the wounded and the latrines and the huts, and she would have come on the twelfth anyway; she came three days early, with her bag and a crate of stores and Rylan's lantern swinging down the rope ladder at Ned Tallis's landing below the bank, and went straight to the surgeon's hut without taking off her coat. Emeric went with her because it was late and he was awake. She looked at the seven men by the lamp. She felt their heads and their backs and looked at their tongues, and asked each one where he slept and what he had eaten and whether he had been near the burial pits, and wrote it down. Then she sat on the end of a cot and said she would see them by daylight.

She did not sleep. He knew, because he did not either, and at three he came out of the officers' hut to walk the bank and the surgeon's hut still had a lamp in it, and her shape against the oiled paper of the window, sitting very still, the way she sat with a page she did not like.

At first light on the ninth she lifted the lamp to Jory Dace's face, and stopped.

It was not much. A scatter of small red marks across his forehead at the hairline and down beside his ears, flat, like a flea's work, like the start of nothing. Jory said he felt better than he had in two days, and he looked it; his fever had dropped in the night. Imani put the lamp down on the stool. She took his wrist and turned it to the light, and there were three of the same marks on the inside of it, and she put her thumb on one and pressed, and rolled it, and Emeric watched her face not change at all.

"Open your mouth," she said.

Jory opened his mouth. She looked at it for a long breath. Then she let go of his wrist, and laid it down on the blanket very carefully, as if it might break; and Emeric saw what blanket it was, grey, with a dark stripe at the end.

"Get Ward," said Imani.

He got Ward. Ward came in with snow on his shoulders and looked at Imani and not at the cots, and she stood up and said it to both of them, flatly, by the door, in the voice she used for numbers.

"It's the speckled death."

"You can feel it under the skin before it's anything," said Imani. "Like shot. Like a grain of lead. In the face and the hands first, never the belly. In the mouth before that." She did not look round at Jory. "He'll be covered by tomorrow. They'll fill, then they'll crust. If he lives they'll fall off in a month and he'll be marked. The other six will go the same way in a day or two. Then whoever's slept beside them, in twelve days or so. Then whoever's slept beside them." She looked at Ward. "I want the camp shut."

"Shut," said Ward.

"Nobody out. Nobody in but me and what I send for. No carts. No Brindle. No letters out unless I've had them. Hull two lands at the stream and nowhere else, and the crew don't come up the bank." She was quiet. "And the men with it go in one hut on their own, and the men who nurse them go in the hut beside it and don't come out either, and everyone else stays in his own hut and eats with his own hut and keeps away from the next one. And I want every one of those blankets."

Ward looked at her a breath longer. Then he went out, and Emeric heard him on the bank, not shouting, because Ward did not shout; giving the order to the first sentry, and the sentry passing it, and the order going away down the earthwork from mouth to mouth in the cold.

Before noon the pickets that had faced north for a fortnight were facing inward. Nobody told the levy what that meant. Nobody needed to.



"The blankets," said Emeric.

It was afternoon, and they were in the snow behind the west huts, because Imani would not talk in the surgeon's hut and would not go into the officers' hut with what she had on her. A fire was going in a pit. Two levy men in Imani's leather aprons and gloves were carrying out the blankets from the west end on poles, at arm's length, like men carrying a drowned dog, and dropping them in.

"I don't know," said Imani. She had her arms folded and her eyes on the fire. "I don't know how it goes from a man to a man. Nobody does. My mother said breath. The priests say God. The Seers' Table says small things you can't see, and I'm half way to believing them and I'll not say so to a camp of five hundred frightened men who'll hear it as witchcraft." She watched another blanket go in. The wool caught slowly, smoking, and the stripe at the end went black first. "But all seven slept at the west end. And the west end had the blankets first. And it's never been on the stream ground before, nor in Wayford, nor anywhere south of Lisle in nine years. It came from somewhere." She looked at him. "Things that come from somewhere come on something."

"Brindle said the dealer was from the north."

"Then ask where in the north," said Imani, "and ask who died there."

He asked Saban, by the hull's wire, that night: where the dealer came from, and what his name was, and where the blankets had been before they were surplus. And whether anybody north of Lisle had the speckled death this autumn.

Saban did not answer for four days. When he did it was three lines, in his dry commercial way, as if he were quoting a price.

Dealer gone home, name given at Wayford market a false one, I think; will have the true one. Speckled death in one of Aldane's wool towns on the upper river since September; one house in particular, a dyer's, all dead in it by October, the bedding and clothes sold off by the parish for the burial. And the same wagon that sold to Brindle sold in two markets north of Lisle on the way home. Same price. Same stripe.

Emeric read it twice in the officers' hut, by the lamp, and gave it to Ward. Ward read it once and gave it back.

"He sent it," said Rylan, from the stove. He was not teasing. It was the first time in the war Emeric had heard him say anything without a joke somewhere under it. "Aldane. He bought a dead house's bedding and sent it down to us at four Copper a blanket because he knew we were cold."

"Somebody did," said Emeric.

"Somebody who sold half of it to his own people on the way home?"

"Somebody who wanted to be paid twice," said Declan, unexpectedly, from his cards.

Nobody answered that. Emeric looked at Saban's three lines on the table. A dealer who did not exist, a parish that had sold a dead family's bedding because the parish needed the money for the graves, a wagon on a public road. Every one of them could be exactly what it looked like. In a realm that put the speckled death down to God's displeasure, it was a weapon nobody could hold up in a court. Whoever had sent it had known that. Or nobody had, and it had come down the road on its own, on a cold man's errand, the way it always had.

"It does not matter who sent it," said Ward.

They looked at him.

"Not this week." He stood up and took his coat off its peg. "This week it matters who sleeps where. I am going to walk the huts."



On the twelfth Jory Dace's face was a single sore from the hairline to the jaw, and his eyes had closed under it, and he was talking to his mother, who had died when he was six.

On the thirteenth there were nineteen in the west hut, which Imani called the sick hut and the levy called the speckled hut, and the hut beside it held the eight men who nursed them: four volunteers, and the surgeon, and three men from the west end who had already slept beside the first seven for a week and had nowhere to go that would not be someone else's death. Imani put them in leather aprons and gloves that she boiled every night in a cauldron on a fire of its own, and showed them how to wash a man who could not be touched, and how to get water into a mouth that was all sores inside, and what to do with the cloths, which was burn them. She made them wash their hands in vinegar water until their knuckles split. She stood in the snow between the two huts with her list and her lantern and took every one of them through it, twice, and then watched them do it.

On the fourteenth Jory Dace died.

Emeric was not there. He was at the stream with hull two, getting Imani's crate off the hook, because she had sent for more vinegar and more linen and the whole of the hospital's stock of willow bark. Arthur came down the bank to tell him. Arthur was carrying water to the nursing hut, by Ward's order, as far as the line of stakes in the snow and no further, and setting the buckets down at the stakes and walking away, and he had been at the stakes when the surgeon came out and said it.

"He talked to his mam all morning," said Arthur. He was nineteen and looked it, red in the face from the cold, with a blanket of his own round his shoulders, an old Kaldo one, brown. He had given back the grey one on the ninth with the rest of his hut, and had said nothing about it, and Emeric had seen him look at it going into the fire. "Then he stopped. The surgeon says he stopped all at once." He shifted the yoke on his shoulders. "My mam had it. Before I was born. She's got a mark here." He touched the side of his nose. "I always thought it was a burn."



Wayford had it on the fifteenth.

Not from the camp; Brindle had come up it four times between the twenty-seventh and the ninth, with blankets for the stragglers and the sick and the men who had joined since, and he had had the lot in his storehouse behind Sethe's market for two nights before he brought it north. His storeman took it on the fifteenth, and his storeman's wife, and on the sixteenth Brindle's own boy, who was seven, and who had slept on the bales in the storehouse loft because it was warm.

And it went south, because Wayford was the road south. A carter called at the posting house on the tenth with Kaldo's paper and drove home over Harrow Bridge, paying Lisle's toll on Kaldo's marks without a word, as Emeric had paid it all autumn; and he slept two nights in Wayford and a night at a farm and a night at Kaldo's north gate with his wife and his three children, and took it into Kaldo with him on the nineteenth. Grayrock had it from a collier's son who had come home from the levy wounded on the first, before anybody knew there was anything to come home with. A drover took it down the Milford road. By the twentieth there were cases in all four of Kaldo's baronies, a handful in each, scattered along the roads like the marks on Jory Dace's wrist; and Ferra's map in the counting room in the keep, which had been a map of the war for six weeks, was a map of something else.

Emeric went home on the night of the nineteenth on hull two. Imani stayed in the camp. Ward stayed with her, which was not how either of them put it.

"Go," Imani said, on the bank, in the dark, in her apron, with the stakes behind her. She did not come within six feet of him. She had not since the ninth; she had stopped coming within six feet of anybody who was not in the nursing hut, and slept there, and ate there, and came out only to the stakes to shout her lists. "There's nothing you can do here that the surgeon can't. There's a hospital in Kaldo with four beds for fevers and a carter's family coming in through the gate, and the carter's family are going to have neighbours." She held up her list. "Read me what I've written. I want to know you've got it. I'll not have it done wrong because you were tired."

He read it to her, by her lantern, across the six feet of snow. A separate building. Not a room; a building, with its own door, its own privy, its own wash house, its own kitchen, and no wall shared with anything. Windows that opened. A room at the door where the nurses went in clean and came out clean and left their aprons. Beds with iron frames that could be boiled. No straw. No wool that could not be burned. And warm, all night, every night, with nobody carrying fuel in and ash out through the door, because every person who went through that door was another way out.

"Warm," she said again, when he had finished. "You'll find a way. You always do with the warm." Then she turned and went back between the stakes toward the nursing hut with her lantern, and did not look round, and he went down the bank to the hull.



The hospital had a garden behind it, Imani's herb garden, walled, two hundred feet by eighty, and on the twentieth of December Tess put forty men into it with mattocks and broke the frozen ground for the footings of a ward.

She had the drawing by then. Emeric had done it on the hull, on his knee, by the binnacle lamp, between Wayford and the Kaldo slip, and given it to her on the slip at dawn: Imani's list turned into a long timber ward on a raised floor, two rows of twelve beds, windows on both sides that let down from the top, a lobby at the town end and a wash house and kitchen at the garden end, and a separate privy block across a covered walk. Tess had looked at it in her chair on the slip with the snow coming down on it, and turned it over and looked at the back, and said, "Ten days," and he had said Imani wanted it in seven, and she had said Imani could come and hold a mattock, then.

It was eight. That was the timber, which Bram had in the works yard already cut for the second slip shed and which Tess took without asking and Bram let her take without being asked; and the roof, which Tess's men knew; and the floor, which they raised two feet on brick piers so the air could get under it and the boards could be scrubbed with lye and dry from below. The heat was the trouble.

Twenty-four beds in a timber ward in a Kaldo January wanted a great deal of fire. Imani's list said no fuel carried in. Stoves fed through the wall smoked the wrong way when the wind went east; charged stones wanted a charger going in and out with a box; a furnace under a timber floor full of sick people was a thing he did not want to explain to anybody afterward. He stood in the frozen garden on the twenty-first and looked at the hospital's back wall, and over it, past the roofs of the river street, at the works chimneys on the far bank, three of them, smoking day and night, because the boilers in the engine house never went cold. Four hundred and some yards.

The works had more steam than it used. It always had. The engine house ran the lathes and the blowers and the rolling mill, and it was built for the rolling mill on a full run, and the rolling mill was on a full run one day in four; the rest of the time Bram's stokers banked the fires and let the safety valves lift, and the steam went up the chimney into the Kaldo sky, and that was heat, going to waste in plain view, the way the river had been power going to waste in plain view the first week he came. Run a pipe from the boiler house across the river and up the street to the garden. Lag it in felt and tarred canvas, and bury it where they could. At the ward, run it along the walls under the windows in loops of plain iron pipe, two inches, and let the steam give up its heat into the room and turn to water, and run the water back down a second pipe to the works to go into the boilers again, already warm. No fire in the ward at all. Nobody carrying anything through the door. The stokers in the engine house would never need to know who was on the other end of it.

Bram looked at the drawing in the engine house that afternoon, with his spectacles on the end of his nose and his hands black to the wrist, and said the river was the trouble, and Emeric said he knew.

"You'll hang it off the bridge," said Bram.

"Under the bridge. Along the downstream side, on brackets, boxed in."

"It'll freeze in the box."

"Not with steam in it."

"It'll freeze the first night the stokers bank the fires and the steam stops, and then it'll split, and then you've a ward full of the speckled death and no heat in it at three in the morning, and my pipe in the river." Bram took his spectacles off. "So the stokers don't bank the fires. Not on that boiler. Not till she says." He looked at the drawing again. "And you want a drain cock at the low point on the bridge, so if it does stop, a man can let the water out before it freezes. And a man to do it."

"And a man to do it," said Emeric.

"I'll do it," said Bram. "I'm up at three anyway." He put his spectacles back on. "Everybody my age is up at three."

They laid it in six days. Bram's men cut and threaded the pipe in the works, two hundred lengths of it, and the steamfitters, who were three men and a boy and had never fitted anything longer than the engine house, carried it out across the bridge on their shoulders in the snow and hung it on Bram's brackets under the downstream parapet, and boxed it, and stuffed the box with sawdust and felt, and swore at the joints, which wept. Up the river street they buried it in a trench cut through the frozen ground with fire and picks. At the garden wall it went in through a hole Tess had left for it in the footings, and along the inside of the ward in its loops under the windows, painted black.

They opened the valve at the works on the night of the twenty-eighth. Emeric was in the ward with Tess and the boy, and the pipes knocked like a man with a hammer going down the whole length of the room, bang, bang, bang, and then hissed, and then, slowly, from the town end to the garden end, they went warm. Tess put her hand flat on the loop under the window beside her chair and held it there.

"Well," she said.

The boy put his hand on it too, and took it away fast, and laughed, and put it back with his sleeve over it.

By morning the ward was warm enough that the night nurse had hung her stockings over the pipe at the lobby end to dry, which Imani, when she heard of it, said was not allowed, and then said to leave them.



Osric's letter came back up the line on the twentieth, inside a packet of Tess's drawings for the camp's new latrine screens; and Osric took it out of the packet at the stakes, where Ward's sergeant had set the camp's mail down, and read the outside, and put it inside his coat and walked away very fast along the bank to the officers' hut, without the drawings.

Emeric was not in the camp to see it. Rylan told him later, on the wire, because Rylan had been at the stakes too, collecting his own, and Rylan had never in his life let a thing like that go past without making a note of it.

"He read it at the cold end," said Rylan, three hundred and fifty miles off. "With his back to the room. It took him the whole of the evening. I timed him on the stove clock. And then he took out his pencil." A crackle on the line. "Emmy, it was in red. The whole of the inside. It looked like a man who'd been in a knife fight."

It had been in red. Emeric knew that because Tess had shown him, on the slip, on the eighteenth, before she put it in the packet: not to share it, he thought, but because she had been sitting in her chair on the slip with it in her lap and a red pencil in her hand when he came down the ladder from the hull, and had not had time to put either away, and had decided, visibly, not to bother.

Osric's letter was nine lines long, in the hand Emeric had seen on the drafting board in October, very large, very upright, each letter made on its own as if it had been carried there separately and set down. It said, more or less, that it was cold here, and that the snow was up to a man's knees on the bank, and that the food was salt pork and that Maren would be ashamed of it, and that they had won, which she would have heard, and that he had read the order. That was all he said about the order. I red the order. I did not stumbel. And then a line he had crossed out three times so thoroughly that the pencil had gone through the paper, and under it, as if he had given up: I hope your well. Osric.

Tess had been through it in red the way she went through a drawing that had come back from the yard wrong. Every word. Red over red, it looked like Rylan's knife fight. Here, with a little h above it in a square red hand: here. Red with a line under it: read. Stumble. Knees, with the k put in and a red arrow pointing at it, and in the margin beside the arrow, very small: silent, like you ought to be. Your, struck through, and you're over it, and the apostrophe drawn very large.

And at the bottom, under Osric's name, in the square hand, in red: Again.

"She'll have him at it all winter," Emeric had said, on the slip.

Tess had folded it up, red side in. "He asked for it." She put it in the packet with the latrine screens. "On condition nobody knows." She looked at him. "So nobody knows."

"Nobody," he had said.

The second letter came south on the twenty-third, by Imani's rule: she had every letter out of the camp pressed both sides with a flat iron off the cook-house stove until it browned at the edges, and only the parts of the camp that could not be ironed, which was most of them, stayed in the camp. Osric's came out scorched brown round the edges like a piece of toast. It was fourteen lines long. Emeric did not read it. He saw it in the mail bag going over the side at the Kaldo slip, scorched, addressed to TESS AT THE WORKS in the same capitals, but with the letters closer together than the first time, and he saw it again that evening in Tess's lap, on the slip, with the red pencil, and Tess not using it.

She was reading it slowly. Then she read it again. Then she put three marks on it, three, small, and wrote something at the bottom that was more than one word, and folded it.

She did not show him. He did not ask.



Ferra came down from the counting room to the slip on the night of the twenty-sixth with a lantern, a bundle of Ottoline's sheets, and her shawl over her head against the snow, and met him at the foot of the ladder at a quarter past ten, and stood on the boards with him while Ned Tallis's crew took the crates off the hook.

"You've not eaten," she said.

"I ate on the hull."

"You ate a heel of bread on the hull." She gave him the bundle. "These are the counts to tonight, by barony, by parish, by street where I have them. Ottoline's run them twice. The camp numbers are Imani's from the wire this evening." She did not let go of the bundle when he took it. "I'll not have you read them on the slip, in the snow, by a binnacle lamp, at a quarter past ten."

"In the counting room, by the stove, with the soup I've had kept back since six." She let go. "And with me there to tell you which ones I've corrected, because I've corrected four, and I've not struck any of them through, so you'll need me."

Ned Tallis's crew went past them up the slip with the first crate on a hand barrow, and the slip lamp swung on its hook, and for the space of the barrow's going by, three steps of the crew and the rumble of the wheels on the boards, there was nobody on the slip looking at them. Ferra put her free hand flat on his chest inside his coat, where it was warm, and left it there, and looked up at him over the lantern, very straight, very grave, with snow on her eyelashes.

"There," she said. "That's all I came down for. The soup's an excuse." She took the hand back. "Don't tell Imani I said the soup was an excuse. She'll want to know what the rest was."

"She'll know."

"She'll know," Ferra agreed, and turned with the lantern, and walked up the slip ahead of him with her back very straight and her shawl over her head, and did not look round to see if he was following, because she knew that too.

He read the counts in the counting room by the stove with the soup. She sat across the table from him with her own copy and her pen and showed him the four places, each one in her small exact hand under the line it corrected, with the date beside it, and the old figure still standing above it where anybody could see what it had been. There was a draught under the door and she put her foot on his under the table to keep it warm, and kept it there, and turned her pages with the other hand, and said nothing about it.



The north had it by Christmas.

Saban sent it in pieces, the way news came down from Aldane's country now that the road over Harrow Bridge was a war road and Lisle's men in brown were gone from the toll-house and nobody was carrying Kaldo's paper north. A drover's word at a Wayford market. A priest's letter to a priest. A clerk in a lord's house who wrote, for a Silver Rand a month, to a cousin who sold rope to Saban's factor. The two market towns north of Lisle where the dealer's wagon had sold on its way home: both of them, by the middle of December. Then two villages beyond them, on the same road. Then the upper river, where it had begun, again, in houses that had buried nobody in the autumn. Then Aldane's own town, below his hall.

And on the twenty-ninth, in a letter Saban sent down the wire in full, because he said there was no shorter way to put it: Aldane's cousin's house. The house that had been in mourning since the first of December, with its hatchments up and its gates in black and its chapel full, for a boy of nineteen who had ridden at the stream ground in his father's colours because his father was too old to ride. Two of the boy's sister's children, in the nursery, the week before Christmas. The nurse. One of the grooms.

The cousin had sent his men home. Saban's clerk said so. The household knights and their men-at-arms and the levies of his manors, who had been under Aldane's banner since the summer, gone home to their own villages to see who in them was still alive. Other houses were doing the same. Saban's clerk said that Aldane had given no leave for it, and that nobody had asked for any.

"There's your second host," said Saban, on the wire. Emeric heard him turn a page. "Gone home to bury its children." A pause. "I don't say that to please you."

"I know."

"I say it because it'll be in every market from here to the capital by Lantern Night, and somebody in every one of those markets is going to say what Rylan said." Another page. "That he sent it. And the next thing they'll say is that God turned it back on him. And the thing after that, because it's always the thing after that, is that you did."

Emeric said nothing.

"I've the dealer's road," said Saban. "Not his name yet. His road. I'll have his name by the end of the month, and then I'll have who paid him, if anybody did, and then I'll have a ledger." He was quiet. "You won't like it either way."

"No."

"I'm only telling you what you're buying," said Saban, and rang off.



The isolation ward took its first patients on the twenty-ninth: the carter, his wife, and two of his three children, carried in on boards from the house by the north gate, through the lobby where the nurses stripped and washed and put on the boiled aprons, and laid in the iron beds under the black pipes.

The third child, a girl of four, did not have it. Imani's rule said she went to a house by herself with a woman who had had it, for three weeks, and was watched. Sela took her. Sela had the mark beside her nose, and a spare room over the shop, and a son on the stream ground, and she came to the hospital lobby on the evening of the twenty-ninth in her shawl and held out her arms for the child across the lobby's painted line, and the night nurse gave her over, and Sela took her home without a word to anybody about any of it.

By the first of January there were twenty-one beds full of the twenty-four, and Armand was sitting with them.

He had asked. He had said, to the night nurse, that he had had it as a boy, and that there was no reason on earth for him to be anywhere else, and that he was old and slept badly and the ward was warm. He sat on Imani's stool at the lobby end with his book on his knee and his hand an inch above a blanket, going down the beds the way he had gone down the end room's in September, and writing down what he felt, which he said was nothing that helped. The fever, he said, ran its own course, and the mana in them ran down with it, and came back up with it if they lived; and he could tell her, for what it was worth, who was going to live, about a day before she could see it. He could not tell her what to do about it.

Emeric came in on the second, at dusk, through the lobby, washed and aproned, and stood at the foot of the carter's bed. The carter was asleep. His wife, in the next bed, was not. Her face was a crust from her brow to her chin, and her hands on the blanket were the same, and she was very quiet, and looking at the black pipe under the window.

"It ticks," she said, when she saw him. Her voice came out of the crust thin and careful. "All night. Tick, tick." She was quiet. "I like it. It's like a clock."

"It's the iron," said Emeric. "Warming and cooling."

"I know what it is. The nurse said." She looked at the pipe again. "The boy from the works said it comes from the works. Across the river." She shut her eyes. "My father worked the works. When it was the old foundry. Before you." She was quiet so long he thought she had gone to sleep. "Fancy," she said.

She died on the fourth. The carter lived. The two children lived, the elder marked badly across the face and the younger hardly at all, and Imani's book said nobody knew why that was either.



Imani came home on the night of the second, because the camp had gone twenty days without a new case in the west huts and eight without one anywhere, and because the surgeon could do now what was left to be done there, and because Ward had written to Emeric, in his own hand, two lines, that she had not slept above four hours a night since the ninth and that he did not think she knew it.

The camp's count went south with her in her book: seventy-four had taken it on the stream ground, and nineteen were dead, and Jory Dace had been the first and a boy of seventeen from Milford called Long Tom by everybody, because there were four Toms in his hut, the last, on the second of January. Of the fifty-five who had lived, a dozen were marked so badly their own huts had to be told who they were. Two were blind. Osric had not taken it. Arthur had not taken it. Declan had not, Rylan had not, Ward had not. Nobody could say why any of that was either.

She came off the hull at the Kaldo slip with her bag and went straight up the river street to the hospital and into the isolation ward, through the lobby and the boiled aprons, without going home. Emeric found her there at midnight. She was standing at the garden end of the ward, by the last pair of beds, with her lists in her hand, not reading them, with her other hand flat on the black pipe under the window; and she did not take it away when he came down the ward to her.

"It works," she said.

"Bram's up at three to make sure."

"I know. I passed him on the bridge. He was sitting on a crate by his drain cock with a lantern and a pie." She looked down the ward. Twenty-two beds, now, of the twenty-four. The pipes ticked. "Nine in Wayford dead, since the fifteenth. Twenty in the four baronies, and sixty-odd sick, and more every day, two or three new ones every day in every place it's got to. Not a camp now. Streets. Houses. People who weren't anywhere near a blanket, who went to market, who went to church, who sat up with their mothers." She was quiet. "The Seers' Table have been going round with Ferra's clerks writing down who went where. Every house. Who visited who. It's in the counting room on a map with pins in it. I went past the door. It looks like the measles."

"And the north?"

"Saban says worse. Saban says Aldane's town has had it since before Christmas and the parish has stopped writing down the burials because it's run out of the book." She looked at him at last, in the lamplight. She had a cut on her lip she had bitten in, and the skin of her hands was split across every knuckle from the vinegar. "I can keep it in a hut," she said. "I did, there. I can keep it in this ward. I can't keep it out of a town. Not once it's in. Nobody ever has. You shut the gates and you burn the bedding and you put the sick ones where it's warm and you get water into them, and you wait. That's all there is. My mother waited. Her mother waited. I've spent ten years taking things away from God one at a time and I'll not have this one back from him, Emeric, I'll not, and I don't know what to do."

He did not either.

He stood by her at the end of the ward, under the black pipe, with his own hands in his apron, and went through what he had. Shut the gates: shut. The bedding: burned. The sick: warm, watered, washed, kept apart. The well: Imani's. The counting: Ferra's and Ottoline's and the Seers' Table's, house by house. He had come into this world with the medicine of the other one put into him whole, the trial's gift, and he went down through it now the way he went down a column of figures, line by line, looking for the one that would hold, for the thing that would stop a fever running from one house to the next in the middle of winter in a town with one hospital; and it was all there, all of it, the course of the disease and the crusts and the twelve days and the scarring, and none of it was an answer. None of it was a thing he could do on the third of January, with twenty-two beds full and two to spare.

"Go home," he said. "Sleep. Four hours. Five."

"You go home."

"I'll go when you go."

Imani looked at him, and then at the ward, and then down at her own split hands on the list. She did not move.

"Then we're both staying," she said, and turned to the nearest bed, and bent over it, and began.

He fetched the water.



On the fourth of January, in the afternoon, the pins on Ferra's map in the counting room went out of the four baronies along every road that had a market on it, and down the river on both banks, and up the canal to the villages at the lock; and Ottoline's sheets that evening ran to nine pages, ninety-six names on the ward's and the parishes' sick lists, and forty-eight on the other list, and she had run them twice.

Emeric stood at the counting room window afterward with the sheets in his hand and the town under him in the snow. Lamps in the hospital, all of them. Lamps in the isolation ward, a long low line of them at the bottom of Imani's garden. The works chimneys smoking on the far bank, the third one steady, not banked, never banked now; and on the bridge, small, a lantern on a crate where Bram would be at three, by his drain cock, with his pie.

Behind him Ferra's pen was going. Down in the town a church bell started, once, and stopped, and started again, slow, for somebody.

He put the sheets down on the table, very square, and went on looking out.
