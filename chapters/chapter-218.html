Chapter 218 - Street by Street

Ilsa Renner walked the island on the first week of every month, and had for two years, and by the summer she was walking it to empty it.

She did not think of it that way. She thought of it as the count. She went out on the Monday with last month's list in her left hand and a pencil in her right and a second pencil behind her ear, because the first one always broke on the Tuesday, and she walked every lane of the island from the Western Gate to the water steps and back along the wall, and knocked on every door, and asked who slept there. That was all the count was. It had been all it was when she started, the autumn she came down from Grayrock with the tally house's figures and asked Ferra for it, and it was all it was now. It was only the answers that had changed.

In the first year the answers had been more. A cousin come from Milford. A brother-in-law and his boys, for the rail. A lodger in the loft who had not been there in the spring and swore he had. She had spent that year writing names in, faster than she could walk, and finding families living under dead families' names, and a man in a lean-to on the foundry bank with five names, one for each gang he drew pay from.

Now the answers were fewer. And since the winter, when the prince had read the notice at the cross, they had been fewer by the street.

The draws went street by street because Ferra had said so, and Ferra had said so because the prince had drawn the first quarter wrong.

He had not meant to. In the first draws, before Ilsa had the census, they had drawn by household, a name out of a box, and each household that came up had gone to whichever house in the first quarter was next on the list. It had been fair. It had been exactly fair, and it had put every family in the first quarter next to strangers. Ilsa had seen what that did when she walked the first quarter the spring after: women who had lived three doors from each other for twenty years, now a street apart and not able to find the time, and women who lived next door to each other and did not know each other's names, and nobody at anybody's door when a child was sick at night. By the fourth draw the rule was street by street, and it had been ever since. In the spring Sela from the soap counter had told the prince to his face, in the long room, that he had drawn the first ones wrong, and he had not argued.

So now a draw was a street. Ilsa took the island's streets in the order the prince had drawn on his sheet of the school, the ones the school would need first at the top, and when a street's turn came she went down it with her book and told every house the same thing on the same day. Your street's gone up. The third quarter, the fourth street off the south ring, twelve houses, the same as here. You'll go in the same order as you are now. The Pells next to you on the left, the Tollers on the right. The carts come on the Monday fortnight. Nobody has to go. If you stay, you stay, and nothing changes on your door but the neighbours.

Most of them went. Not all.

The house on Tanner's Row went first, in the first draw of the summer, and asked to.

It was a tall thin house of three storeys with a crack down the front from the eaves to the step, the house the prince's knight had counted from the wall one night years ago, three and four lights to a house all down the row, and gone and told the prince what he had counted. It had held three families then and it held three families now: the Asters on the ground floor with six children, the Dunnings on the middle floor with their mother, and a young couple called Hobbe in the two rooms under the roof with a baby, where the rain came in at the corner in a wet spring and the baby slept in a drawer on the dry side.

Ilsa went in to tell them on the Monday. She did not get past the ground floor. Aster had heard from the street's baker the week before which way the draw was going, and when she came up the step he was waiting in the doorway with his hat on.

"Which cart," he said.

"The first."

"Good." He took his hat off and put it on again. "All three of us. Separate houses?"

"Three houses. Side by side. The same order as the floors, if you want it. You on the left, the Dunnings in the middle."

Aster looked at her for a moment as if she had said something in a language he did not speak. Then he turned round and shouted up the stair, and the whole house came down it, all fourteen of them and the baby, and stood in the passage and looked at her while she told them again, and nobody said anything, and then Mistress Dunning sat down on the bottom step with her apron over her face.

They went in the first cart, on the Monday fortnight, with everything they owned in the back of it and the Hobbes' baby on top of the bedding in its drawer. Ilsa watched them go from the corner of Tanner's Row with her book. None of them looked back at the house. The Aster boys were already arguing about which of them would have the room over the kitchen. When the cart was round the corner she went into the empty house and walked it, every floor, the way she walked every house after it emptied, and wrote in her book what was left in it, which was a broken chair and a picture of a ship nailed to the wall under the roof, and the drawer, which they had forgotten after all, on the dry side of the room.

She did not take the drawer. She wrote it in the inventory, and closed the house, and put the barony's lead seal on the hasp with the house's number stamped in it, the way every empty house was closed now, and went on down the row.

Old Aggett and his wife would not go.

Their house was on the lane that ran down to the water steps, three doors from Tam Brewer's, a low stone house with a lean-to on the side where Aggett had mended nets for fifty years, and a bench by the door where his wife sat in the sun in the afternoons and watched the lake. Their lane's draw came up in the second month of the summer. Ilsa knew what they would say before she knocked, and she knocked anyway, because the rule said every house on the same day and she did not break the rule for anybody.

Mistress Aggett came to the door. She was very small and very old and she had been expecting Ilsa, too.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard where yet."

"I know where. The new place. Four miles." Mistress Aggett looked past her down the lane, to the water steps and the lake beyond them. "He's mended nets on that bench fifty years. I've watched that water fifty years. We've buried two children out of this house and I'm not leaving them on the island by themselves." She looked back at Ilsa. "Tam Brewer's staying. You wrote that for him. You'll write it for us."

"It was Mistress Ferra wrote it for him."

"Then you'll write it the way she did."

Ilsa wrote it the way Ferra had. She had seen the line in Ferra's book when she took over the census, in Ferra's small square hand against Tam Brewer's name, the one word with nothing after it, and she had thought at the time that it was the best line in the whole of Ferra's book. She wrote it now against the Aggetts' names, in her own hand, which was bigger and plainer than Ferra's and leaned a little to the right.

Stays.

Mistress Aggett watched her write it, and nodded, and went back in and shut the door. The rest of the lane went on the Monday fortnight. The Aggetts' house stood among eight sealed doors with smoke coming out of its chimney, and by the next month's count there was a second bench by the door, where Tam Brewer had carried one down from his own house so that he could sit in the afternoons with Mistress Aggett and watch the lake and say nothing, and Ilsa wrote that down too, because it was a thing that had happened on her island and the count was the island.

The empty houses were closed, and inventoried, and handed on.

That was in the rule too. Every sealed house went into a second book, not Ilsa's, a long thin book Hobb kept in the counting room, with its number and its street and what was in it. Then it went on one of two lists: Orrin's, for the school, or Armand's, for the tower. The prince had drawn which houses went to which, on the sheet he had shown at the long-room table in the winter. Orrin's houses were on the low streets round the old exchange. Armand's were on the upper streets under the keep. A house went on its list in the order of the prince's drawing and waited there.

Nothing was pulled down. That was the part of the rule that people asked her about most, and she told them the same thing every time. Nothing came down while anyone lived in the street. A street that emptied went on the drawing's list whole, every house of it, and stood whole, sealed and swept, until the school needed the ground, and nobody knew when that would be. It might be years. The prince had drawn the island as it would be in twenty years, not in one.

By the end of the summer there were four whole streets standing empty on the island, with the seals on every door and the gutters swept and the windows shuttered, and they were the quietest places Ilsa had ever walked. She walked them anyway, every month, because they were on the island and the count was the island. She walked down the middle of them with her book under her arm and listened to her own boots on the stones, and once, on Cooper's Row, heard a shutter bang somewhere behind her in the wind and turned round sharply, as if somebody had come home.

The third quarter's first printed street took the draw from Salt Lane, in the last week of the summer.

She walked out to it the day before, to see it for herself, because she did not tell any family where it was going without having stood in the street. It was on the south ring, in the new quarter, where the Walker had been clicking its way along its rails since the frost came out of the ground in the spring, and it was a whole street of it: twelve houses a side, two storeys, the walls in the Walker's long grey bands, like the courses of a very neat stone wall laid by a giant who never got tired, and at the corner of every house the pale dressed quoins that Hewitt's men set ahead of the frame. The roofs were on. The windows were in. There was a tap at the end of the street on its iron post, and a drain in the middle of the road with a grating over it, and nobody living there at all.

She went into the end house and put her hand flat on the wall of the front room. It was dry, and it was faintly warm from the day's sun, all the way through, the way old stone never was.

At the corner of the end house, on the quoin at the height of her eye, somebody had cut a mark into the dressed stone with a chisel: a small ring with an arrow through it, crisp and fresh, the kind of mark she had seen on the corner stones of every old building on the island without ever wondering what it was. She looked at it for a while. It was the only thing on the whole street that had been made by a man's hand and signed.

Salt Lane had eleven families in it. Nine went.

One would not. Not for the reason the Aggetts would not. Old Pardew, the thatcher, who had thatched half the roofs in the island's lanes before they went to slate, came to the door when she knocked and listened to her with his arms folded and then asked her where the new house was, and what it was made of, and when she told him he unfolded his arms and folded them again the other way.

"The machine," he said.

"The Walker."

"I've seen it. I went out to look at it in the spring." He shook his head. "No mason built that. No man laid a stone of it. A frame on rails with a mouth like a pig, spitting mud." He looked past her at the island's lane, where every house had been built by somebody whose name was known. "I'll not live in a house nobody built."

"The masons set the corners."

"The corners," said Pardew, with contempt, and shut the door.

And one asked for it. The widow Lusk, who lived alone at the end of Salt Lane with a cough that came every winter and stayed till the spring, came and found Ilsa in the lane the next morning, having heard the whole thing from Pardew's wife, and asked whether it was true that the new houses were dry. Ilsa said it was. She said she had put her hand on the wall and it was dry right through, and warm, and that the old houses on the island had never once been dry in a wet winter since they were built.

"Then I'll have one of those," said the widow Lusk. "Whoever built it."

Ilsa wrote them both down. She wrote Pardew, stays, will not have a printed house, and the widow Lusk, goes, asked for a printed house because it is dry. She wrote them one under the other, in the same hand, with nothing to say which of them was right. That was not the count's business. The count was who slept where, and why, if they told her.

The widow Eames came back at the end of the summer, and did not come to Ilsa about it. She went to Ferra.

Ilsa only knew because Ferra sent for her. She came up the keep stair with her book on a hot still afternoon with the shutters half closed against the sun, and found Ferra at the table with the long thin book open in front of her, and the widow Eames sitting across from her in her Sunday shawl with her hands in her lap.

The widow Eames had gone out in the spring, in the last draw before the summer, with Dock Street, to the third quarter's first street, the one Hewitt's gangs had laid by hand over the winter. She had been glad to go. She had said so to Ilsa on the day, in her doorway: a house with a tap, and a privy that drained, and a floor that was not wet. She had gone in the first cart and not looked back, and Ilsa had sealed her house on Dock Street, and it was on Orrin's list, and it had stood there sealed all summer.

"She can't sleep," Ferra said.

The widow Eames looked at Ilsa, and then at her own hands.

"It's quiet," she said. "That's all. It's a good house. There's nothing wrong with it. The neighbours are the same neighbours, and they're good to me, and the water comes out of the tap. It's quiet." She turned her wedding ring on her finger. "I've slept on Dock Street sixty years, with the lake on the other side of the wall. Slapping at the stones all night. You don't hear it after a while. You don't know you're hearing it." She looked up. "Out there you can't hear it. I lie there and I can't hear it, and I can't sleep, and I've not slept a night through since Easter. I'm too old to learn to sleep somewhere new." She looked at Ferra. "I want my house back. If that's allowed. I know it's on a list."

Ferra did not look at Ilsa, and did not look at the book. She looked at the widow Eames.

"It's allowed," she said. "The rule goes both ways. Your house wasn't let. It's sealed, and it's on Orrin's list, and it'll come off Orrin's list. It's yours, at the rent you paid before. A Copper a week." She turned to Ilsa. "When can she go?"

Ilsa thought about it. "The seal's mine to break. The house wants airing and the chimney wants sweeping, it's stood three months. And her son's got the cart, and he's on the rail till the harvest's in." She looked at the widow. "After the first frost?"

The widow Eames nodded. She did not say anything else. She got up, and pulled her shawl round her, and went to the door, and at the door she said thank you to Ferra, twice, and went down the stair.

Ferra turned the long thin book round and found Dock Street and found the widow's house, and ruled a line through the entry that said Orrin and wrote beside it, in her small square hand, Returned to the tenant, at her asking, at the old rent. And the date. She did not write anything else, and she did not say anything, and Ilsa, watching her, understood that Ferra had been waiting for this since the night in the winter when she had made the prince write the promise in his own hand, and was glad it had come, and would never once say so.

"Put her back in the count," Ferra said. "When she's in."

"I will."

"And the other thing." Ferra closed the book. "You'll have heard."

Ilsa had heard. The census was moving to the palace in the autumn, ahead of everything else, because the census book was going to be the first book of the barony's that the palace's stone rooms held, and the prince wanted it in a room with no timber in it before the winter. There was a room for her on the ground floor of the east range, with a window onto the court and a stove that did not smoke and a floor that was not damp, and it had her name on the door already, painted. The room over the old weigh house was going on Orrin's list. It would be a room for the school's clerks.

"It'll be warmer," Ferra said.

"It will," said Ilsa.

She walked the island for the last time that season on the first week of the autumn, the week before she moved her books to the palace.

She walked it as she always did, from the Western Gate to the water steps and back along the wall, with last month's list in her left hand and a pencil in her right and a second behind her ear. It took her four days now, not six. She knocked on every door that did not have a seal on it, and asked who slept there, and wrote it down. At the Aggetts' she stopped and sat on Tam Brewer's bench for a while in the sun with the two of them and the lake, and Mistress Aggett gave her a cup of something hot that was not tea, and nobody said anything about anything.

On the last evening she sat in her room over the weigh house with the book open on her knees and the fire lit, because the room was damp even in the autumn and always would be, and went through it from the front.

There were more lines struck through than written now. She had not noticed it happen. It had happened a street at a time, a lane at a time, and every month she had struck the lines for the houses that had gone, and every month there had been more of them, and now she turned the pages and the book was a book of ruled lines with names under them.

She found that she did not like striking them.

She had not expected that. It was only the count. A family went, and she struck its line in the island's book and wrote it into the new quarter's, and the family was still a family and still counted, only somewhere else. It was the most ordinary work in the world. But she sat with the book on her knees in the damp room and the fire ticking, and turned the pages, and every struck line was a door she had knocked on for two years, and a woman who had come to it with flour on her hands, or a man who had not wanted to give his name and then had, or a child who had hidden behind the door and looked at her pencil. And the line was gone through, and it would not come back.

She struck the last three anyway, for the three houses on Salt Lane that had gone at the end of the summer. She had to. It was the count, and the count was only any use if it was true.

But she did it carefully. She took the ruler, and laid it just under the writing, not through it, and drew the line there, thin and straight, so that every name on the page could still be read. She went back through the book and looked at every line she had struck that summer, and found that she had been doing it that way since the spring without deciding to, every one of them, the line just under the name and never through it. Every name on the island that had ever been in her book could still be read.

She closed the book, and sat for a while longer with it on her knees and the fire going down.

Tomorrow she would carry it across the bridge, and four miles, and put it in a stone room with no timber in it and a stove that did not smoke. Somebody would sweep this room, and a clerk from Orrin's school would sit in it and be cold. And the book would go on being the island, a street at a time, until there was nobody left on the island to count but the ones who stayed; and she would go on knocking on their doors, every month, as long as there were any.
