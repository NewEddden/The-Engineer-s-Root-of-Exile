Chapter 19 - What Petram Trades

He put the name, the ledger and the date on the table in front of Petram and then said nothing.

The cell under the keep was colder than the bedroom had been and had no window, and neither of them mentioned that either.

Petram did the arithmetic out loud, which saved Emeric the trouble of building the lever.

He had been locked up three months. He had just watched a boy die on the other side of his door for hearing one name through it. Which made him the only person still alive who could testify to any part of this.

"That's not a safe thing to be."

"No."

"You know it isn't."

"I know it isn't. That's why you're going to tell me the rest of it."

And he did.

It took most of two days and it came out in the order a man gives things up when he has decided to give them up, which is not chronological and is not tidy. He started in the middle, with a stable yard, and had to be walked backward twice.

Not the paymaster. He had never met the man and said so flatly and went on saying it under pressure and it had the shape of the truth.

Everything else.

How Garrin had run him, in what order, with what signals. Where the meets were. What was said if the meet was clean and what was said if it was not. How the coin moved, in what denominations, through whose hands, and the fact that it was never once counted in front of him.

That Garrin had not liked him and had not troubled to hide it. That Garrin had been told to take somebody from the household rather than bring his own second in, and had complained about it once, in front of Petram, which was the only human thing Petram had ever seen him do.

Ferra sat in the corner of the cell writing it down, and stopped him four times to ask what day of the week a thing had happened on, and got four answers, and two of them did not agree with each other and she wrote down both and the disagreement.

Emeric had not thought to do that and said so afterward. She said that a witness who never contradicts himself is a witness who has learned his lines, and that a magistrate knows it, and that she would rather hand over two dates and an admission than one date and a story.

And then, without meaning to and without knowing what he was giving away, the detail that confirmed the recruitment predated the roster.

Emeric had known that for three months. Petram had told him on the road, on the first day, in two words. He had never had it corroborated by anything at all, and now he had it twice, from the same man, three months apart, in circumstances where the man had no reason to keep a story straight and every reason to be tired.

That was worth more than anything else in the room, and he felt himself becoming certain while it happened, and did not stop it.

The other thing came out about twenty minutes later and did not fit.

Petram had seen Cobb and Garrin together once, in a yard, from the far side of it, for perhaps a minute and a half.

"He wasn't paying him."

"You said the coin came through him."

"It did. He carried it." Petram turned his hands over, looking for the word. "Garrin stood up. Garrin doesn't stand up for anybody. I'd seen him sit through a captain."

The deference had run the wrong way for a baron's courier.

Emeric heard it. He had a use for none of it, because every part of his attention was on a ledger with a date in it, and a courier being treated well by a poisoner explained nothing and complicated something.

He almost stopped.

He let it go past.

Petram asked for one thing at the end, and it was not mercy and it was not a sentence and it was not about himself.

"Will Corliss be all right."

"I don't know."

It was the honest answer. It was also the answer of a man who had decided in private, two days earlier, on four independent confirmations, that Corliss was a thief.

Neither of them knew the other half of that sentence and neither of them ever found out.

Wexler had heard three days ago that a prince and an outside bookkeeper had been in his accounts.

He did not strike.

He went to the stables before light on the fourth morning, took two horses and a manservant, and rode for the capital to petition the crown for a hearing himself.

A man that calculating does not run toward the crown believing he is innocent. He runs toward it because he has already started deciding how to survive being guilty, and because arriving first is worth more than any argument he could make arriving second.

Financing a road ambush had been the whole of his violence. He had tried it once, it had failed, and he had switched to law inside a season.

Emeric found out from Hale.

Not from an informant. Not from an absence noticed at dinner. He asked where the baron was, and the steward told him, plainly, because Hale had never lied to him about anything and because nobody had instructed him not to.

He also volunteered, unasked, that the baron had taken three days of road food and the good saddle and had left instructions about the winter grain that assumed he would not be back before it, and Emeric stood in a cold hall and understood that he had just been handed a man's entire intention by a tired clerk who had no idea he was handing over anything.

That was the correct texture of that house all the way through. Nobody was loyal to Emeric and nobody was hiding very much either.

They went after him inside six hours.

Emeric, Ferra, Ward, and Petram in a judiciary escort with irons on and a guard's cloak over the irons, six days at a pace the carriage had not been built for and complained about from Wayford onward.

Two days lost, and no method of recovering them, and Emeric did the arithmetic on the first morning and then made himself stop doing it, because there is nothing in a road that responds to being calculated at.

Ward had now ridden this road twice with this man.

"Better direction this time."

"We're going the same way."

"We're going toward it."

It was the most Ward had volunteered about anything since the fire, and Emeric turned it over for a mile and a half and decided not to ask what it meant, because asking Ward what a thing meant was a reliable method of never being told anything again.

Somewhere past Wayford the road went into the forest, and the forest ran most of a day, and the road through it was poor and had been poor for thirty years.

His father had told him that in a small office in a voice that died at arm's length, with a man standing at the end of the corridor listening for footsteps.

Emeric looked at the treeline for most of an afternoon and did not say one word to anybody about any of it.

Ferra built the case on the road, which is the only reason a travel chapter of six days was worth anybody's time.

She was assembling a second document that did not yet exist, out of records she did not have, by writing ahead to the waypoint registries at every stop they made and paying the couriers out of her own advance because she was not going to wait for anybody's authorization.

She slept about as much as the horse did. She wrote in the carriage on a board across her knees, and when the road got bad she wrote anyway and the hand got worse and stayed readable.

At Wayford she spent two hours in a waypoint office arguing a clerk into producing eleven years of toll returns, and came out with nine, and was annoyed about the two for the next forty miles.

The toll returns mattered because a grain contract at a price no grain has ever cost still has to have had grain move under it, and grain that moves pays a toll, and a toll leaves a line in a book kept by a man with no interest in Kaldo whatsoever. She was not proving that Wexler had stolen. She was proving that the thing he had invoiced for had never existed, in a document he had never touched and could not have touched.

Emeric understood that on the third day and understood, at the same time, that he would not have thought of it.

Emeric, who had spent the whole of his time in this world being the one who saw the shape first, spent six days holding a lamp steady for somebody else's work and found that he did not mind it at all.

He wrote that down on the fourth night. It was the second time that month he had been the least useful person in a room, and he was beginning to suspect that this was the point of the entire exercise.

She had not withdrawn her objection.

She was building the case against Wexler for the theft and for the treason, because both were true and both were provable and one of them carried a sentence. She was not building the case Emeric believed in.

He referred to it once, on the fifth day, as though she were, and she corrected him. Briefly. Without heat. And then went back to the board on her knees.

He heard the correction as diligence.

The capital came up out of the plain on the last evening, ring by ring.

The outer wall first, then the second, then the inner wards stacked above them with lights already going on, and traffic in the sky over the palace quarter that a man from Kaldo had no frame of reference for at all. Ferra looked at it for a long time and did not say anything about it, then or later.

Six months ago he had ridden out of that gate as a man who had been thrown away, in a carriage, with fifty gold Rands and a chest of crystals and a sentence from his father built to travel.

He was riding back in to prosecute a baron, with a bookkeeper, a knight, and a poisoner in a cart.

He worked out somewhere on the approach that not one person behind those walls was going to be pleased to see him, and found, to his mild surprise, that it did not bother him anywhere near as much as it ought to have.

He ran the list anyway, because he could not help it. His father, who could not be seen to receive him. Corbin, who would be delighted and would take some trouble to conceal it badly. Aldane, who would not bother concealing anything. Augustine, who had prayed for him to be made into something better and would now have to look at what had turned up instead.

And Waylon, who had gone hunting rather than stand in a line, and who would hear within a day that his brother was back inside the walls with a prisoner and a case, and would have to decide something about it.

That was the only name on the list that did anything to him at all, and he put it down before it finished arriving, which he had been doing for fifteen years and had gotten very good at.

They came in through the outer gate at dusk and nobody stopped them and nobody announced them.

The man he had come to arrest had been inside for two days already, talking.
