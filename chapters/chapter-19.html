Chapter 19 - A Second Buyer

Emeric had four traders bidding for the barony's custom and no way at all to tell which of them was honest, and had come to understand over four months that a face had never once told him the truth about a man.

He had tested it, privately, and kept score. Wexler's steward had a good face. Corliss had a face like a closed door and had been the only man in the garrison who was clean. Halloway had looked at him with flat dislike for three weeks and had turned out to be the most reliable person in the barony. Whatever instrument other men were using to read a stranger across a table, Emeric had been issued a broken one, and had eventually stopped pretending otherwise and started building around it, which was what he did with everything.

So he did not interview them.

He gave all four of them a parcel of the barony's trade, a small one, nothing that mattered, seed and salt and cordage out of the ford, and he had Ferra draw up the payment for each of them, and he had Ferra overpay every single one.

Not extravagantly. That was the whole art of it and she had spent an evening getting it right. Enough over the agreed price to be unmistakable to a man who counted his own money, and small enough to pass as a tired clerk's error at the end of a long ledger. The kind of overcount a merchant could put in his pocket and never hear about again.

"You understand this costs us the money four times," Ferra said, writing it out.

"It costs us the money four times and I get it back from at least one of them, and what I'm buying is information I cannot get any other way."

"You could ask around the ford."

"I did ask around the ford. Everyone at the ford told me all four of them were good men, because everyone at the ford is related to at least one of them." He watched her total it. "How bad is it?"

"It's eleven days of a dig crew's grain wage."

"That's bad."

"It's bad and it's cheap for what you're asking, and I'd have argued if it weren't." She blotted the page. "Write down what you expect. Now, before the answers come back, so you can't be clever about it afterward."

He wrote down that he expected two to return it, and that he expected the one he liked least, a thin careful man named Errick who did not look at people when he talked, to be one of the two.

Three of the four pocketed it.

Errick pocketed it. So did the loud friendly one who had brought Emeric a gift of preserved lemons and asked after his health twice. So did the third, who was young and who Emeric had privately half-hoped for.

Saban sent it back inside the week, counted out flat on the table in front of Ferra, with the sum written on a slip in his own hand and no letter attached and no request to be thanked. Ferra reported this in about nine words and then stood there waiting, because she knew what was coming.

"He returned it," Emeric said.

"He returned it."

"And I'd written down that he wouldn't."

"You wrote down two names and neither of them was his, and I have the sheet, and I'm going to keep the sheet." She almost smiled. "You want to be careful about the next part."

"Which part?"

"The part where you decide the returned coin means he's a good man."

Emeric considered that, and found that she was, as usual, standing exactly where the error was.

Because it did not mean that. It meant Saban had looked at a mouthful of coin from a lord who had just hanged the previous administration, and had compared it against being the preferred factor of the only customer in a day's ride who was going to be spending money for the next twenty years, and had done the arithmetic correctly and quickly. Three men had chosen the mouthful. One had chosen the twenty years.

"He's clever about his own interest," Emeric said. "And his own interest is a long way out rather than a short way out. That holds under pressure. Virtue doesn't."

"Then say that in the room and don't say the other thing, because he'll hear it and think you're a fool, and a man who thinks you're a fool prices you accordingly."

He cut the three from the barony's preferential contracts, and cut the loud one loudly, in front of two other traders, on purpose, because the point of a rule nobody sees enforced is nothing at all.

Then he went and found out what Saban's name was worth, which took eleven days and a good deal of Rylan's time, and produced the most useful answer he had ever been given about anybody.

Nobody said he was honest.

"Saban cheats," said a carter at the ford, without heat, and then went back to his cargo as though the matter were closed.

"He doesn't cheat," said a cooper in Milford who Rylan rode a day and a half to find. "He bargains. There's men who can't tell the difference and it's not his fault they've never bought anything."

"He'll squeeze you if you let him," said a woman who ran an inn on the Wayford road, "and he'll go on squeezing right up to the moment you say no, and then he'll stop and shake your hand and never mention it again, which is more than I can say for the rest of them."

And then the one that decided it, from a Grayrock factor who plainly did not much like him: "He'll take the last coin off you and count it in front of you. But if he's said he'll have forty ton at the ford by the turn of the month, there's forty ton at the ford by the turn of the month, and if there's thirty-eight he'll tell you it's thirty-eight before you've got the tarpaulin off."

Emeric read the whole of it twice and felt something settle.

"That's the man I want," he said.

Ward, who had been reading over his shoulder, said, "He's four men calling him a squeezer."

"He's four men calling him a squeezer and every one of them saying he delivers. I don't need him honest against his own interest, that's a fantasy and I'd be a fool to buy it. I need him to want the same thing I want for long enough, and to tell me the truth about tonnage, and to be too clever to burn a customer who's about to become the biggest in the region." He tapped the page. "A saint would be better. There aren't any. This is the next thing, and it's a real thing, and it's checkable, which the saint wouldn't have been."

Saban came up to the long room on a hot morning at the end of the sowing, and he was not what Emeric had expected, which by that point Emeric had learned to expect.

He was about forty, thick through the middle, dressed a good deal better than anyone in Kaldo and not so well that it was rude. He looked at the boards. He looked at them for a while, and Emeric let him, and eventually he said:

"Four thousand six hundred."

"When the ring closes."

"And two thousand now."

"Two thousand and sixty."

Saban nodded slowly. Then he said something Emeric had not braced for at all.

"My mother's buried on the west side. Under the elm, if it's still standing."

Emeric put his chalk down.

"It's standing."

"I was born in the street behind the tannery and I left at nineteen because there was nothing here to sell to anybody, and I have been back four times in twenty-one years and every one of those times it was smaller." He was not sentimental about it. He said it the way he presumably said tonnages. "I've had one lord of Kaldo try to buy me before. Wexler. He wanted me to bring grain up the road at his price and carry the loss out of my own margin as a kindness to the barony of my birth."

"What did you say?"

"I said the barony of my birth had never once done me a kindness, and I went and sold to Grayrock." Saban shrugged. "I'll say plainly that I'd have said the same to you a year ago. I'm here because the boards say something different and because my own factor at the ford tells me your dig crews are being paid in grain on time every week, which is either true or it's the most expensive lie in the region."

"It's true."

"Then let's find out what you want, because I've come a long way to be pleasant and I'd rather get to the part where we argue."

What Emeric wanted took an hour to lay out and came down to two things.

The first was a second buyer. He said Ferra's word for it, the trunk, and Saban understood it before the sentence was finished and looked briefly annoyed, the way a man does when someone else has already had his best thought.

"Milford," he said.

"Milford, at a fixed price, one road, and a baron who told me twice at this table that if Grayrock and Wayford lean on him he leans with them."

"He told you that."

"Twice, unprompted, and I believed him."

"He's right to." Saban tapped the table with two fingers. "Paxton's the best of the three and it doesn't matter. Milford's a wall you could ride a cart through and everyone in four baronies knows it. Whatever he wants, he'll do what keeps his people out of a field." He sat back. "So you want somebody who can put your grain in more than one place."

"I want somebody who can put it in six places, and I want the six places not to know what the others paid."

"That part's my whole trade and I'll not be explaining it to you." A flicker of something. "The second thing?"

"When you sell it, don't bring me coin."

Saban's face did not change, but he stopped tapping.

"Say that again."

"Coin's no use to me sitting in a box. Sell my grain, take your margin off it, and spend the rest on the road before you come home. Ore first, as much as you can move. Copper. Coal where you can get it. Tin. Whatever the list says that month." Emeric slid the list across. "I don't need silver. I need the things silver buys, and I need them arriving here rather than being ordered from here, because every week between the order and the arrival is a week the furnace stands cold."

Saban read the list. He read it twice.

"You understand what you're asking," he said.

"Tell me what I'm asking."

"You're asking me to stop being your grain merchant and start being your buyer. Those are not the same man. A grain merchant sells your crop and hands you the takings and goes home. What you want is somebody carrying your money in his own purse across four baronies, deciding on the spot what ore's worth having and what's rubbish, and coming back with a cart instead of a receipt." He set the list down. "Which means every single week I could rob you, and you'd not know for a season, and by then I'd have a good story about the state of the Duvrenne road."

"Yes."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"I'm doing it anyway, because the alternative is I hold the coin and order the ore from here like a careful man and my furnace runs at half for a year, and I've done the arithmetic on both and the careful version loses." Emeric leaned on the table. "And because I'd rather say all of that out loud in the first hour than have you work out in the third month that you could have been robbing me the whole time and start wondering why I never noticed."

Saban looked at him for a long moment.

"You're a strange one," he said. "That's not flattery, and I've met a great many barons."

"I'm told."

"Right." He pulled the list back toward himself. "Then here's mine, and it's three, and the third one you'll not like."

"Go."

"My margin's mine and it's written and it doesn't move because you've had a bad month. I'll take less in a season where taking less buys something bigger later, and that'll be my choice and not yours, and you'll not ask me for it." He held up a second finger. "Everything I buy for you comes with the price I paid and the name of the man I paid it to, in writing, and your clerk can chase any of it she likes. I've nothing to hide and I'd rather be checked than trusted, because trust goes off and a ledger doesn't."

Ferra, at the end of the table, looked up for the first time.

"And the third?"

"I'll not deal exclusively for you." Saban said it without apology. "I've eleven other customers and nine of them are in the baronies you're about to make nervous. If I drop them and hang my whole trade on Kaldo, then in two years I'm your servant with a merchant's name, and the first hard season you have, I have. And more than that, if I only ever come and go from Kaldo, every factor on that road knows exactly who I am and what I'm carrying inside a month, and your ore stops arriving." He spread his hands. "I'm more use to you as a man who trades everywhere and happens to trade a great deal with you."

Emeric turned that over and found the second half of it sitting there, plain, and did not say it out loud, because Saban had chosen not to and the choosing was itself the point.

A man who traded in Grayrock and Wayford and Milford every month, and who was not visibly Kaldo's, would come home each time knowing what the grain price was, and what the barons had been buying, and which roads had gone quiet.

"Agreed," Emeric said. "All three."

"You didn't argue about the margin."

"You'd have moved it and I'd have got a worse merchant." He put out his hand. "One thing of mine, and it's not a term, it's a request."

"Go on."

"When something's wrong out there, come and tell me the day you know it, and tell it to me flat and early even when you're not sure yet. I have a garrison captain who does that and he is worth more to me than any three other men in this barony, and he's the only one I've got, and he can't ride to Wayford."

Saban shook the hand.

"That'll cost you nothing," he said, "and one day you'll wish it had."

They put it in writing that afternoon, and Ferra read it back twice, and Saban corrected her once on a figure in his own favour and once against it, and she noted both corrections in the margin with the time of day.

Emeric walked him down as far as the gate.

"There's a thing you'll hear," Saban said, stopping. "You'll hear it from me properly when I've got more than talk, and I'll not sell you a rumour at full price. But you'll hear it, so hear it from me first and thin."

"Say it thin, then."

"There's iron moving in the region that isn't going into ploughs." He looked out at the road. "It's not much. It's not enough for me to put a name to it or a number, and half of it's likely nothing, because barons buy iron and I'd be a poor merchant if I got excited every time one of them did." He put his hat on. "But it's the second month running, and I've been at this twenty-one years, and I'd not be doing my part of the bargain we just made if I let you find out from somebody else."

Emeric stood at the gate for some time after the cart had gone.

Then he went back up and got the list out and wrote, under the space:

Second buyer secured. He is checkable, which is better than honest. The trunk has a branch.

And underneath that, because he had promised himself in Ward's own words that the bad news went down on the day:

Iron that isn't going into ploughs. Two months running. Not enough to act on. Write it down anyway, so that when it is enough, I can prove to myself I knew the month it started.
