Chapter 19 - Three Farmers

The baron's farm was his now, and the first thing he did with it was decide that it was allowed to fail.

It lay on the west side below the aqueduct: forty-odd acres of tired ground, a barn with a sagging ridge, a yard, and a reeve who had worked it for Wexler and now worked it for whoever paid him, and said so. Every farmer in the barony could see it from his own fields. That was the point. Whatever Emeric tried, he'd try here first, on ground nobody's children would go hungry for. If it failed, it failed on the prince.

Bram forged the iron for it in October, between the ward's kettles and everything else.

Four plowshares. Nine scythe blades. A dozen hoe heads. He laid them out in a row on the forge floor one evening, and they looked like very little against forty acres and a barony.

"That's the iron," Ferra said, from her desk behind the half-wall, before he could ask. "All of it. Until the next barge."

The iron had come down the river the week before, on a Grayrock barge that tied up at the dock quarter's empty moorings and unloaded onto the bank because there was no wharf to unload onto. Ferra had been at the bank with her book. She'd written the weight and the price in the column where they belonged, and then, in the margin beside them, she'd written the price again. She didn't remark on it, and neither did he.



Rylan took him out to the three farmers the others watched.

He'd asked for their names in September, riding back from the west farms, and Rylan had given them without thinking: Farris, Hollis, Geddes. Every farm on the west side waited to see what those three did before it did anything. If they sowed early, everybody sowed early. If they held back a field, everybody held one back.

They met him in Farris's yard, because Farris had the biggest one and it was nearest the road. Three men stood with their arms folded in front of a prince. They had decided in advance to be polite and nothing more.

Farris was the youngest, thirty or so, with a heavy jaw and a habit of looking at the sky while other people talked. Hollis was old, small and brown and very still, with hands that had been in the ground for sixty years. Geddes was somewhere between, a big quiet man who stood a little behind the other two and watched Emeric's face the whole time and said almost nothing.

Emeric didn't explain anything. He'd learned that much. He asked them what they grew, and how, and what they'd change if they could, and he listened.

Then he told them what he wanted to try, in about four sentences, and waited.

They had objections. He'd known they would. What he hadn't expected was that every one of them would be right.

"One harvest," Farris said. "One a year. You get it wrong, it's not wrong till next year. It's a winter."

"And it's our winter," Hollis said. "Not yours."

Geddes, when they'd finished, uncrossed his arms long enough to say the only thing he said that morning.

"Last lord who had ideas about our fields," he said, "was nobody. Nobody ever came."

He folded his arms again.

Emeric thought about the side room in the keep in the summer, and Wexler, and eleven days of being told no by a man who was never once wrong on a fact. He'd learned something in that room that he hadn't wanted to learn. Ask for the cheaper thing.

"One strip each," he said. "The width of a plow team's turn, the length of the field. The rest you farm your own way, the way you always have. On the strip you try what I'm asking. If the strip fails, I pay what it would have yielded. Your way. Out of my own pocket."

Farris looked at the sky. Hollis looked at the ground.

Geddes looked at him.

"Your pocket," Geddes said.

"Mine."

Geddes didn't say yes. He didn't say no. The other two said yes, and Geddes went home.



None of it was his. He knew that better than anybody.

It was one textbook, an agronomy text he'd been made to read for a college elective in another life, by a tired lecturer who had grown up on a farm and despaired of every student in the room. Emeric could see every page of it. He could see the diagrams of the four-field rotation, wheat and turnips and barley and clover, and the table of yields, and the photograph of a steel plow turning a furrow like a wave breaking. He knew the page. He didn't know the ground.

The three farmers knew the ground. Within a week it was obvious which of them knew more, and it wasn't him.

The plow came first, because that one he could show.

The plows on the west side were scratch plows, a pointed share on a beam that cut a groove and pushed the soil aside without turning it. They worked. They'd worked for four hundred years. Emeric had Bram fit one of the new shares to a beam with a curved board behind it, timber faced with iron, shaped from the photograph in his head and corrected twice by the carpenter off Ferra's list, so that the soil came up over the share and rolled and lay down upside down, the old weeds and stubble buried under it and the dark ground on top.

Farris's ox pulled it down the length of his strip on a cold morning with the three of them watching.

The furrow turned over behind it like a page.

Nobody said anything. Hollis walked out along it, bent down, picked up a handful of the turned earth and smelled it, and crumbled it, and dropped it. Geddes, who had come back without being asked and was standing at the far hedge, watched the ox reach the end and turn.

"Again," Farris said.

The seed was harder, because there Hollis knew better.

The book said to save seed from the best plants. Pick the fullest heads from the field by hand at harvest, keep them apart, and sow them, and in a few years the whole crop would be better. Emeric said so, in the barn, with a sack of last year's seed open between them.

Hollis listened politely. Then he said that his grandmother had done that, and his mother, and he did it too, and so did half the old women on the west side, and had the prince never seen a woman walking the field before harvest with an apron?

Emeric hadn't.

"Not from the edge, though," Hollis said. "The book say that? The edge gets the wind. It lodges. The heads on the edge look fat and they're half chaff." He put his hand in the sack and let the grain run through his fingers. "From the middle. Three paces in at least. And never from a wet corner." He looked up. "Your book know that?"

It didn't. Emeric wrote it down.

The rotation, he didn't show. He couldn't. It would take three years to show. He explained it once, as simply as he could, with a stick in the dirt of Farris's yard: the bare field, the one they left to rest every other year with nothing on it, could be sown with clover, and the clover would feed the ground while it rested, and the year after it would grow more wheat than a field left bare ever had. Turnips in the year between, to feed the beasts through the winter instead of killing half of them in the autumn.

Hollis asked where the clover seed would come from. Emeric didn't know. Farris said there was a man in Milford who had some.

The seed drill he only drew, for spring: a box on wheels with a row of tubes beneath it, so that seed went into the ground in straight lines at an even depth instead of being thrown by hand and eaten by the birds. Bram built the first frame in the forge from Emeric's sketch, and Emeric couldn't make it turn. The wheels went round, and somewhere in the frame something caught, and stopped them.

It was Tess who found it, on one of her forge mornings.

She'd been sweeping. She stopped, and watched Bram push the frame across the floor and swear when it bound, and then she put the broom down and got onto her back on the forge floor and slid underneath it.

"It's the frame," she said, from under it. "When the wheel goes over anything, the frame twists. Just a hair. And when it twists, the axle rubs the cross-piece here." She knocked on it. "Square it up. Put a brace across the corner."

Bram looked at Emeric. Emeric looked at the frame.

She was right. He'd drawn it flat, on paper, where nothing ever twisted.



The water was the last thing, and the one he'd been looking at since September.

The aqueduct crossed the west side thirty feet up on its arches, carrying the Mere's water toward the town over the heads of men who watered their fields by bucket from a ditch. Now that it was running again over Bram's timber trough, the water was right there, going past.

The mason walked twice round the pier nearest Farris's strip, and then said it could be done. A hole through the floor of the channel, small, with a wooden plug you could knock out and knock back in. A spout. A trough at the foot of the pier. And from the trough, a channel dug along the edge of the field, falling the way the ground fell, so the water ran along the head of every furrow by itself, without a single bucket.

Before anybody dug anything, Emeric asked the question.

"Where does it go?"

The mason and Farris both turned to him.

"After the field," Emeric said. "What doesn't soak in. Where does it run?"

Farris thought about it. Then he walked to the bottom of his strip and showed him: the old ditch along the bottom hedge, which ran to a bigger ditch, which ran to the river, a long way below anyone's well. Emeric walked it to the end before he said yes.

He told nobody about that. But walking back up the field, he realized he'd asked it without thinking. Without Imani there. It had just been the next question.

They dug the channel in a week. The mason cut the hole on a still morning, and knocked out the plug, and the Mere came down out of the sky into the trough at the foot of the pier and over its lip and along the head of Farris's field, and ran.

Farris watched it run. Then he went and fetched his whole household to look.

The three of them took it differently, which Emeric had expected, and he let them.

Farris took all of it, on his whole field: the plow, the seed from the middle of the field, the clover in the fallow, the water. He did it the same week, without asking, and when Emeric asked him why, he looked at the sky and said that if it was going to work on the strip it was going to work on the rest, and if it wasn't, he'd rather know this year than in three.

Hollis took the strip and not an inch more. He farmed it beautifully. He farmed the rest of his ground exactly as he always had, beside it, so that anyone walking his hedge could see the two side by side.

Geddes only watched. He came to the edge of Farris's field most evenings, and stood at the hedge with his arms folded, and looked at the turned ground and the water running along the head of it, and went home. He hadn't taken a strip. He hadn't said he wouldn't.

Emeric let Farris be the one the others saw. He didn't go out to the west side when people would be about. He didn't tell anyone in the town what he'd done. When men from the other farms came to look at the water, they found Farris there, and Farris told them, and it was Farris's field and Farris's water and Farris's idea, as far as they were concerned.

Nothing was announced.



The winter wheat went into the ground in the second week of November, on the baron's farm and on Farris's whole field and Hollis's strip, behind the new plow, in rows.

Emeric stood at the edge of the baron's farm in the cold with Ward beside him and watched the last of it go in.

"The farm's in," Ward said.

"It's in."

Ward waited. The wind came across the fields off the river.

"You said the farm eats first," Ward said. "And the garrison keeps." He didn't look at Emeric. "It's kept. It won't keep forever."

Forty-one men in the keep, and nobody sure which of them would hold a door and which would open it.

"After the winter," Emeric said.

Ward didn't answer.



That evening at Ferra's table he asked for the forge book.

She turned it round for him without a word, open at the right page. She'd ruled it the way she ruled everything. The iron that had come off the barge in October, by weight. What had gone into the shares, the scythes, the hoes, the irons for the plow board, the drill frame's fittings, each in its own line. What was left.

What was left was not very much.

And at the bottom of the page, in the column where she put the price of the next lot, there was a number. It was the price Grayrock had charged for the last barge. She'd written it twice, once in the column and once in the margin, the same as on the bank.

"That's a Grayrock number," Emeric said.

"Everything at the bottom of that page is a Grayrock number," Ferra said. "Every share. Every hoe. Every nail we'll ever put in the foundry." She took the book back. "They're the only people who sell it, and they're upriver."

She didn't say anything else. He sat looking at the place on the table where the book had been.
