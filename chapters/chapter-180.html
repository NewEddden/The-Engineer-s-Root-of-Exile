Chapter 180: Court

The king's hall had been built for feasts and was used for judgments, and it had never quite agreed to the change.

Emeric had forgotten how long it was. He came in at the south door behind Keller's clerk, with Rylan a pace off his left shoulder and Saban a pace off his right, and the floor ran away from him towards the dais under a roof of black oak that the smoke of two hundred years of feasting had made blacker. He had had twelve days in a hired carriage to remember the hall and he had remembered it shorter. The windows were high and narrow and faced east, so the morning came in over the benches in long pale bars and left the floor between them in shade. Anyone who stood to speak stood in the dark and was looked at from the light.

He felt the floor before he meant to. Dressed stone laid on older stone, and under the older stone a bed of rubble and clay, and under that the round gravel of a river that had run here before anyone thought of a king. The fill had settled on the west side more than the east, a finger's width over the length of the hall. Nobody had noticed in two centuries because nobody had needed to. He closed the sense the way a man closes a book he has no time for and walked on.

There had been a banquet in this hall once. There had been a boy with a box of things he had made, set out on the cloth between the dishes, and a hall full of lords who had been kind about them. He did not look at the long tables, which were stacked against the west wall today with their trestles folded, and he did not look for the place where he had sat. There had also been a morning in the chancery across the yard when he had signed a charter that was sealed before he read it, and had been thanked for signing it, and had gone south with it as the one being given something. Every time he had come up this road, he had come up it as the one who was told.

"You're walking like a man going to his own hanging," Saban said, without moving his lips much. His left wrist was still in its sling under his coat. He carried the ledger case in his right hand and had refused to let Rylan carry it for him for the whole of the journey.

"I'm walking like a prince who drank too much last night and wants his father to notice he's sorry," Emeric said.

"Is there a difference?"

"About six inches of shoulder."

He let the six inches go. He let his chin come up and then think better of it. He had put on the blue coat that Saban had had made in Wayford for the dinners, too good for the morning and too tight across the back, and a ring he never wore, and he had let his hair be cut by the inn's barber into something that had been the fashion at court two years ago. He had practised it on the road, at the last inn, in the glass over the washstand, and it had come back as easily as it had come back at Wayford, and it had bothered him the same amount.

The hall was full. Lords on the benches under the windows, their stewards and sons behind them. The lords spiritual on the east bench nearest the dais, grey and black and one red. The household soldiers along the pillars in the king's green, standing easy. Clerks at a long table under the dais with the rolls squared to its edge. And on the dais, his father.

Harrold sat in the chair he had sat in for thirty years and said nothing while the hall looked at him. He was greyer. The beard was cut shorter than Emeric remembered, which made the face longer, and the hands on the chair's arms had the knuckles of a man who had held reins and a sword for most of his life and paid for both in the winters. He looked at his son once as he came down the floor, the look a king gives to anyone who has been summoned, and then at the clerks.

Corbin stood at his right hand. He stood easily, as he always had, a tall fair man in a dark coat with nothing on it to show what he was, because everyone in the hall knew. He smiled when he saw his brother. It was a real smile, or it was something that had been practised until there was no difference.

Keller was at the Crown's table below the dais, with the rolls. He looked up as Emeric passed and gave him the smallest nod a man can give without being seen to give it. Emeric had written to him for two years and more and met him twice. On paper Keller was a patient man with a dry turn of phrase. In a chair he was narrow and very still and his left hand never stopped squaring the edge of whatever paper was nearest it.

On the east bench Cardinal Augustine sat with his hands folded in his lap and watched the whole floor at once, the way an old man watches a card table he has no money on. Emeric had never met him. He knew the seal. He had seen it on the reply to Armand's letter, one line under it: The chamber is a liturgy, not a procedure.

In the gallery at the north end, among the ladies and with none of them, Maeve sat very straight in grey. She did not look at him. He did not look at her for longer than it took to know that she was there.

Varek stood with the household soldiers at the third pillar from the dais, where he had stood the night of the banquet. He was heavier and his hair had gone. He did not look at Emeric either, which from Varek was a courtesy.

And across the floor, on the west side where the fill had settled, the Duke of Aldane stood with his men and his file.

Emeric had seen him before only at a distance, a big shape on a big horse on the far side of a field, going away. Close, he was a heavy man in his fifties with a grey beard cut square and a face that had been handsome and had kept the habit of expecting to be looked at. He stood like a man who had sat a horse all his life and did not think much of floors. Four of his knights stood behind him. Emeric knew two of them. He had sat across a table from them in Pennock's rolls room last summer while Ferra's clerk counted their ransom, and they had promised not to bear arms against Kaldo for a year and a day. They were not bearing arms. They were bearing witness, which was not in the terms.

The file was in a case of red leather on a small table in front of the duke. Beside it stood his advocate, a thin grey man called Serle with a voice that had been trained to carry to the back of a hall and had never learned to stop.

Emeric took his place on the east side of the floor, in the dark between two bars of light, and fidgeted with his ring.

The king's steward read the summons, which everyone had heard, and the names of the parties, which everyone knew. Then he stood aside, and Harrold said the only word he said for the next two hours.

"Read."

Serle opened the case.

There were five articles, and Emeric had read all five in the glove-maker's copy Saban had bought in the spring. Hearing them read was different. On the page they had been a list. In Serle's voice, in this hall, with the bars of light moving slowly across the benches, they were a story, and it was a good one.

The first article was the bridge. A lawful toll at Harrow Bridge, held under a charter of the Crown by the lord of that land, Lisle, whose tollmen had been set upon by a company of the barony of Kaldo in the spring of the year. The toll-yard gate broken. Four of Lord Lisle's men dead in the yard. Serle read the names of the four. He did not read the names of Colin Rusk and Tad Weller, because they were not in the article. He did not say that the toll box had been found untouched in the yard with its lid still locked, because that was not in the article either. He did not say that the men in brown had ridden north the day before, or that Lisle's tollmen had been sixteen to the patrol's twelve. What he said was true. It was the shape of it that was a lie, and there is no law against a shape.

Emeric listened with his mouth a little open, as though it were news to him that people had died, and let his eyes go to the dais. His father was looking at the clerks.

The second article was the field. Serle read it slowly. That the barony of Kaldo had armed common men, labourers and weavers and the sweepings of the debtors' road, with tubes of iron that threw fire; that with these they had killed knights of the realm at a distance of a hundred paces and more, without closing, without a blow given or taken; that forty-one knights had fallen on the field below the stream, of whom Serle read every name. He read them in order of rank. He read Sir Harlow, son of the duke's cousin, knighted the spring before, aged nineteen in a voice that dropped on the age, and a sound went along the benches that was not quite a word.

Emeric counted in his head, because counting was what he had instead of praying. Forty-one knights. Two hundred and six Kaldo pieces lost or broken on the stream ground. Five hanged men at an oak. The article did not mention the oak. It did not mention the dead field either, the three hundred stones broken in a quarter of an hour by fourteen hired mages to take away everything Kaldo might have had that was not powder, or the charge that had come at the walk and then the trot and then the canter into three ranks of men who had been told that powder does not care. It did not mention that the duke's men-at-arms had never come on. Serle said without a blow given, and it was the only true thing in the article that the hall would remember, and it was the one that would hang him.

The third article was the sky.

"On the night before the field," Serle read, "Garret Oakes, scout of the duke's household horse, being posted on the hill north of the stream, saw a thing pass between him and the stars."

Emeric kept his face where it was.

"He swears that it was longer than a tithe barn. That it made no sound. That it came from the south and went down beyond the ford, low, lower than a hawk flies at the end of its stoop. That he watched it until it was gone and rode north to his captain and told him, and that his captain and the men at the fire laughed at him." Serle turned the page. "Sir Lewin Marle, captain of the duke's household horse, swears that he laughed. And that he does not laugh now."

Nobody in the hall laughed. Emeric had thought somebody would. He had thought it was the article they would laugh at, a scout's story about a barn in the sky, and he had been ready to laugh with them, a little too loudly, the way a young man laughs at a thing he does not understand to show he is not afraid of it. He had the laugh ready in his chest. He did not use it, because the hall was quiet, and a laugh in a quiet hall is a thing people remember.

He had felt Garret Oakes. He had not known his name. On the night of the thirty-one casks, with hull two's lift bank humming under his feet and Ned Tallis at the rail and the ford a black line below, his sense had caught a rider on the hill north of the stream who stopped, and sat, and watched, and rode north. He had known then what it meant and had brought her down anyway, because the casks were the war. He had written it in the war record he had sent to Keller in two nights, as the reserve came up from Kaldo by night, and Keller had not asked what the reserve came up in.

The fourth article was Wayford.

Serle did not read it as the fourth article. He read it as the second half of the third. He laid the two side by side on purpose and he was right to. That at the Staple in Wayford, on the night the barony of Kaldo took it, the late Lord Edric Dray had seen men of the prince's company go up the outer wall of the Staple as a man goes up a stair, and come down into the yard from the roof, and had cried aloud that they flew, and that they were demons. That his groom, who fled that night and is living, swears to the same. That Lord Dray repeated it in the square at Wayford until the hour of his death, before two thousand people, and that his household holds to it still.

A man in the gallery laughed at that, once, a short bark, and stopped. Dray had been laughed at in the square. Emeric remembered the sound of it, and Dray still talking of demons with the rope round his neck. It had been easy to laugh at Dray. He had been a fool about horses and a fool about wine and he had sat in an oak chair with a stone on its arm and drawn on a tied man for an hour on the usual nights. The hall laughed at Dray the way you laugh at a drunk.

It did not laugh at the scout. That was the whole of Serle's art. A hanged fool had said a thing, and nobody believed him, and then a sober man with no reason to lie had sworn to the same thing on a hill a few miles from the same square and two summers later, and his captain had sworn that he had laughed at it and stopped laughing. One story was a joke. Two was a column in a ledger.

The fifth article was the hangings. That the barony had hanged five persons of noble blood in a market square, by its own judgment, without the Crown's. Serle read the names. Placit, Hollin, Dray, Vennor, Strade. He did not read what they were hanged for. Nobody on the duke's side had ever read that aloud, not on the chancery steps, not at the upper-market fountain, not here.

That was the file. The toll, the fire-tubes, the flying things, the demons and the five. Every line of it an exhibit, and every one of them true in its way, and none of them in the shape it had happened in.

Grayrock was not in it.

Emeric had read the glove-maker's copy three times looking for it, and it was not there, and it was not there today either. A duke who wanted to show the realm that the barony of Kaldo had become a danger to it would have put Grayrock at the top of his file. Gravity stone raised in the south, more of it every season, under a covenant with the Crown that a careful assessor had already found a difference in. It was the one thing in the whole matter that would have made every man on these benches sit up, because every man on these benches had a stake in the Crown's stone. It was not there, and Emeric knew why, and he kept his eyes on the red leather case and his face on the young man in the tight coat who was beginning to look bored.

Keller stood.

He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. He had the Crown's table and the Crown's rolls, and a man speaking for the Crown's law does not stand in the dark on the floor. He stood in the light at the foot of the dais, and the bars from the east windows lay across his table and his hands.

"The first article," he said. "The toll."

He took it apart the way Bram took apart a lock, one pin at a time, laying each on the cloth. Lord Lisle's charter to toll the Wayford road at its northern bridge had been granted in the same year as the charter of the Staple of Wayford and under the same seal. It named the Staple. It granted the toll, in its own words, which Keller read, for the upkeep of the Staple's road. It had no other purpose written in it and no other warrant. A charter whose only reason is another charter is a part of that other charter, whatever name stands on the outside of the roll. The Staple's own charter had fallen within the barony of Wayford by the judgment of its lord, confirmed by the Crown's rider in the summer of that year and entered in these rolls. What depends on a thing falls with it.

"The toll was void," Keller said. "A void toll is a pole across the king's road with armed men beside it. To clear a pole from the king's road is lawful to any lord whose people use the road. The patrol took nothing. The box was found locked." He did not look at Serle. "That is in the record of the Crown's rider, who was at the bridge the next morning. It is on this table, if the duke's advocate would like to read it."

Serle did not.

"The second article." Keller turned a page. "There is no statute of this realm that forbids a lord to arm his levy with any make of weapon. There has never been one. The barony's levy was entered at the chancery as a private company in the summer of last year, lawfully, and the entry is on this table. The law does not ask what a knight died of. It asks whether he died in a lawful fight." He paused, just long enough. "Every knight named in the article died charging."

The sound went along the benches again, and it was a different sound.

"The third article. The Crown does not try the sky." Somebody in the gallery laughed and Keller waited for it to stop. "There is no law in this realm that names anything a man may or may not do in the air, because until this morning nobody thought one was wanting. A scout saw a shape between him and the stars. It is sworn. It is on this table. It is not evidence of any act the law can name, and this court cannot make a judgment on it without making a law first. That is not its office."

"The fourth article is for the lords spiritual," Keller said, and inclined his head to the east bench, "and not for this court. And the witness to it was hanged for murder, by due judgment, which this court may prefer to remember before it gives his words weight."

Augustine's folded hands did not move.

"The fifth article," Keller said, "the Crown has already reviewed." He laid a hand flat on a thick roll at his elbow. Emeric knew it. It was his own record of Wayford, written over four nights, with a hole in it the exact shape of fifteen knots of listening stone and a root in a back room. "The barony's justice was within its rights. The Crown said so at the time, in one line, by its own hand. The Crown's hand has not changed."

He sat down. The clerk under the dais had been writing all the while and went on writing a moment longer, and then set down his pen. On the record, point by point, the Crown's law had found for Kaldo on every article.

Emeric felt the hall not care.

It was a physical thing, almost. He did not need his sense for it. It was in the way the lords on the benches shifted, and the way their stewards leaned forward to murmur, and the way nobody on the floor looked at Keller when he sat down. Keller had won. He had won cleanly and on every count and it had not moved the room an inch, because the room had not been listening to the law. It had been listening to Serle read aged nineteen, and without a blow given, and longer than a tithe barn.

"If it please the court," Saban said.

He had stepped out of the dark a pace, the ledger case open on his good arm. He held a thin book bound in calf. Emeric had not told him to. Emeric had told him on the road to wait for a question and answer it, and there had been no question.

"The household book of the late Lord Placit's steward," Saban said. "Taken at the border ford with the duke's quartermaster's chests. It is entered in the Crown's copy of the war record. There are two lines in it the court may wish to see." He did not say what the lines were. He did not say against the estate of the late Lord Placit, by the Lady's authority: to the master of a lugsail, for service in the matter of the sulphur. He did not say that the widows the duke had written his condolence to had paid for the Gannet's crew to be burned in their own hold. He only held it out.

"That book is not in the summons," Serle said.

Harrold moved a finger on the arm of his chair.

"Leave it on the table," Harrold said.

A clerk came round and took the calf book from Saban's hand and laid it on the Crown's table beside Keller's rolls. Keller looked at it. He did not open it. Nobody asked to read it. It lay in a bar of light from the east windows for the rest of the morning, and the light moved off it by degrees, and it lay in the shade.

Saban stepped back into the dark and stood beside Emeric, breathing a little quickly.

"I had to," Saban said, under his breath.

"I know."

"Nobody's going to open it."

"I know that too."

The settlement was read by the chancellor, an old man with a soft voice who had to be helped to his feet, and it took less time than the file.

The toll at Harrow Bridge was void and was not to be raised again by any lord. That was one.

Between the duke and the barony there was to be peace, and each released the other from all claims for damages arising from the matter. The ransoms already paid on the old terms were to stand. That was two, and Emeric heard it with the part of his mind that kept Ferra's book, and knew exactly what it meant in Rands, which was that Kaldo had been paid for thirty-one knights and would never be paid for the Gannet, or the depot at the ford, or Owen Tarle's burns, or the dead at the oak.

The company of the barony of Kaldo, entered at the chancery as a private company, was not to exceed three hundred men under arms in any year of peace. The Crown's rider would count them at the quarter days.

No lord of the realm was to make powder, or any substance of that kind or use, within his lands, save under the Crown's licence, granted on application to the chancery. The barony of Kaldo was to apply within the year.

The duke's losses were noted with the Crown's regret. No sum was named.

The household book of the late Lord Placit's steward was to be kept with the record.

That was all. The chancellor sat down, and was helped to sit.

Emeric let the young man in the tight coat hear it. He let his face go slowly red, the way a face goes red when a boy hears that his toys are to be taken away, and he turned half round on the floor as though looking for somebody to complain to, and found nobody, and turned back.

"Three hundred," he said. Loudly. Too loudly for the hall. "My lord, I have six hundred men on the books. More. They've signed for three years. What am I supposed to do, send them home to their mothers?"

A ripple of laughter went along the benches. It was the laughter he had been waiting for all morning, and it was warm, and relieved, and every man who laughed felt better for it. There, it said. There he is. A boy with an army he does not know what to do with, and his father has taken half of it off him, and he is sulking on the floor of the hall.

His father did not laugh. His father looked at him for a moment with no expression at all, and then away.

"The Crown has ruled," the steward said.

Emeric subsided, as the young man would, with his mouth set and his ring turning on his finger.

Under the sulk, he was counting. Six hundred and eleven names in Ward's four grey books, less the dead, less the maimed, less the ones who had gone home after the stream and not come back. Four hundred and sixty-some under arms this spring by Ward's own count. Three hundred was not half. Three hundred was enough to hold a road, and not enough to hold a road and a wall and a city at once. And powder under licence meant powder under the eye of whoever read the applications, and he did not know who that would be, and he knew that whoever it was would want to know how much, and where, and made from what, and would be entitled to ask.

Keller had won the law, and the Crown had ruled on what the room was afraid of. The duke had lost a field and it had not frightened anybody. The prince had won it with weavers and iron tubes, and every lord on these benches had a son who had been knighted young and had paid the rite for it, and each of them had sat this morning and done the sum in his head. Forty-one knights. At a hundred paces. Without a blow.

He had thought, on the road north, that the worst thing that could happen in this hall was that they would believe the file. They had not needed to. Fear of what it described had been enough, and the file had been written to make them feel it.

The hall loosened. It did not end, because the king had not risen, but the steward stepped back and the lords began to talk among themselves, and the clerks began to gather their rolls, and the bars of light had moved far enough across the floor that the dark between them was narrower than it had been.

Corbin came down from the dais.

He came down the three steps as easily as he did everything, and walked out onto the floor where only parties and advocates had stood all morning, and nobody thought it strange. He stopped halfway between the duke and his brother, as a man stops between two friends who have quarrelled.

"Well," Corbin said, pleasantly, to the hall more than to either of them. "That's done, and I think we can all have our dinner." There was a murmur of laughter. He turned to Aldane and inclined his head. "Your grace. The Crown is sorry for your losses. I am sorry for your cousin's boy. I knew him a little." Aldane bowed, stiffly. Corbin turned to Emeric. "And my brother is sorry about his three hundred men." More laughter, kinder. "I'd be sorry too, if I were him. Though I confess I'd sleep better if he had fewer." He smiled at the benches. "A man who can put knights over a wall at night could reach this city in an afternoon, I suppose, if he put his mind to it. We'll have to keep the gates shut after dinner."

The hall laughed.

Emeric laughed too. He laughed in the mask, a young man's laugh, pleased to be teased by his brother in front of the court, flattered that anyone thought him capable of anything. He put his head back a little and laughed with the benches, and he heard them laughing with him, and it was a good loud warm sound in the black-roofed hall.

It stopped a little early.

Not all at once. Nobody stopped on purpose. But the laughter in a hall has a length to it, the time it takes for everyone who is laughing to finish and look round at the people next to them and settle, and this laughter did not quite reach it. It fell away from the far benches first, and then the near ones, and there was a moment, less than a breath, when the hall was quiet with the joke still in it. A lord on the second bench coughed. The man in the gallery who had barked at Dray did not bark. Augustine had not laughed at all. Neither had Harrold. Neither had Keller, who was looking at the calf book in the shade.

Emeric kept laughing a beat past the rest of them, because the young man in the tight coat would not notice when a hall stopped laughing. Then he stopped too, and grinned at his brother, and turned his ring.

Corbin grinned back.

"And since we're speaking of it," Corbin said, turning to the dais, easy, as if the thought had just come to him, "somebody ought to keep the whole of this matter together. My lord. The Wayford papers and the war's, and the duke's file, and my brother's records, and whatever the Crown's rider has. It's scattered across three offices now and it will be in six by winter." He opened his hands. "It ought to be kept by family, I think. It's not pleasant work, and it touches my brother, and it touches the Crown. I'd rather it was me than a clerk who has no stake in how it reads." A little rueful smile. "It will be a very dull winter for me. I'll do it gladly."

It was generous. Everybody in the hall could see it was generous: the heir to the throne volunteering for a winter of dusty papers so that his exiled brother's troubles should be kept by somebody who loved him and not by some chancery clerk who might make a meal of them. It was public, because it was said on the floor of the hall in front of every lord in the kingdom who had come to hear the matter settled. And it was unanswerable. Emeric could not object to it without saying, in front of all of them, that he did not trust his brother with his own papers. Keller could not object to it, because the rolls were the Crown's and Corbin was the Crown's heir. Aldane had no reason to object and did not.

Emeric looked at his father.

Harrold looked at Corbin for what seemed a long time and was probably the space of two breaths. Then he moved the finger on the arm of his chair again.

"Granted," Harrold said.

It was the third word he had said that morning.

The clerks began to gather. Keller rose, and squared his rolls, and handed them one by one to Corbin's secretary, who had appeared at his elbow from somewhere behind the dais with a leather satchel already open. Emeric's record of Wayford went in, with its hole. The war record went in, with no hole in it, and the reserve that came up from Kaldo by night. The Crown's rider's reports. The chancery entry of the private company. Serle came across the floor with the red leather case and bowed and handed it over, and the duke's file went in on top of Emeric's records. A clerk picked up the calf book from the shade on the Crown's table, looked at it, and handed that to the secretary too.

The secretary closed the satchel and gave it to Corbin, and Corbin tucked it under his arm as though it weighed nothing, and looked across the floor at his brother.

He smiled. It was the same smile as the morning, warm, a little rueful, the smile of an older brother who has just taken a dull and thankless job off a younger one's hands in front of everyone who mattered. He smiled at Emeric as though he had done him a kindness.
