Chapter 180 - The Second Reading

Vantic left for the coast on the eighth of July, in the House carriage, with a box of Ferra's copied ledgers under the seat and her own pages in her book, and Emeric watched the carriage go out of the South Gate from the counting room window and then went down to the cellar and began on Ward's gate.

He had told Ward the summer, and it was the summer. The Visitor's report would be in the chamber's hands by now, read and filed and argued over in rooms Emeric would never see, and every posting house on the capital road had gone back to looking at Kaldo the way it always had, which was a great deal, but no more than usual. Fennick's lads were at the third ridge. Paskin had his students back on the line, a mile north of the last cutting, where the road ran away from the rails behind a hill. Nobody had asked about lights over the marsh since April.

So in July the forge made the largest thing it had made since the suits.



Ward had wanted a gate that could throw a hundred men off it with a boy at a lever. That was the whole of the specification, and it was a good one, because it said what the thing had to do and nothing about how. Emeric had thought about the how all spring.

A gravity stone pushed. Free, in a thing that moved, it pushed straight up or straight down and nothing else; that was why the ship and the suits needed air for any way but up. But a stone set into something that did not move, planted, in the ground or in a wall that stood on the ground, could push any way it was told. The lattice in the forge's cage did it every night it ran, pushing in from six sides at once. A wall could do it. A gate tower could.

The South Gate had two towers, one each side of the arch, squat and old, built by the first baron against wolves and the second against his neighbours and not much altered since. Inside each tower's face, toward the road, behind the outer skin of stone, there was a hollow the masons had left when they built it, which the garrison used for storing pikes. Emeric measured both hollows himself, in June, with Bram, at night, with a rule and a lamp, and drew them; and in July he made what would go in them.

Not one stone. Eight, four to a tower, in a frame: a set, cut together, so that they would push together, outward, from the face of each tower and across the mouth of the arch, a wall of push the width of the road, from the ground to a man's height and a little over. And at the heart of the set, the thing that would tell all eight of them when to push and how hard and when to stop, which no ordinary stone could do. A Hollow Crystal. One of Kaldo's few. It had been set aside for a gate since the winter the forge was built, in its case in the cellar with the others, and every other one of them had a name on its label in Ferra's hand, and this one had said GATE for two and a half years, and nothing else.

He cut it in the room on the night of the fourteenth, and it took him until dawn.

Not because it was hard to write. Because it was hard to decide what to write. A control stone was not a trim stone with one job; it was a small locked room with many shelves, and he could put anything on them, and anything he put there would be there forever, and only he could ever change it. He sat on the floor of the chamber with the crystal in his hands and the room open, and the shelves in front of him, empty, and thought about what a gate ought to do on the worst night of Kaldo's life, when he was not there to ask.

It pushed when the lever was thrown. Only then. The lever was in the gatehouse, behind the arch, on the inner side, in a box Bram would build with a lock on it, and Ward and the knights and the garrison's sergeants would carry the key. It pushed outward, away from the town, never inward. It pushed as hard as the lever's notch said, three notches, and the first notch would knock a man down and the third would throw a cart. It stopped when the lever was let go. If the lever's wire was cut, it stopped. If the crystal felt the stones' charge falling below a quarter, it stopped, and held the quarter, so that the gate would always have one more push in it. And it would not push at all, whatever the lever said, if any of its eight stones could feel a child in the arch. He wrote that one last, and it took him longest, because a child's note and a small man's were not so very different, and he did not want the gate to fail because a short man was on the ram.

He wrote it in the end. He was not sure he had written it well. There was no way to find out without a child.

The frames went in on the night of the twentieth, behind iron. Bram had made two plates for the hollows, an inch thick, riveted, with a lattice of bars behind, and the frames went in behind the plates, and the plates were bolted over, and the masons came in the morning and faced the tower over the plates with a skin of the new cement and stone, so that from the road the South Gate looked exactly as it had looked for two hundred years. Ward stood in the road outside the arch the next day, at noon, among the carts, and looked at the towers for a long minute, and went up close and put his hand flat on the new stone of the left one, and took it away.

"I can't feel anything," he said.

"You're not meant to. Nobody is." Emeric stood beside him. "They're asleep until the lever."

"Will it work?"

"I don't know."

Ward looked at him.

"I can't try it," said Emeric. "Not here. To find out, I'd have to throw something off the South Gate on the capital road, and everybody on the road would see it, and then we wouldn't have a gate any more. We'd have a story." He looked up at the tower. "I tried the set in the cage, at a twentieth. It pushed a sack of sand across the chamber. I tried the stop and the quarter and the lever wire. I tried the child with a sack of barley the weight of a boy of six, with a lamp stone in it, charged, for a note. It stopped." He was quiet. "That's all I can do. The rest it'll have to do on its own, on the night."

Ward stood in the road with the carts going by on either side of them.

"Then it's a promise," he said at last. "Not a gate."

"Yes."

"I've had worse," said Ward. "I've been one." And he went back in under the arch, and stopped in the gatehouse, and looked at the place on the inner wall where Bram's locked box would go, and stood there a while with his arms folded, as if he could see the lever already, and the boy at it, and the night.



The sugar came down the Milford line on the twenty-fourth, in a goods wagon, twelve loaves of it, each the size of a man's head, wrapped in blue paper, with Paxton riding on top of the load.

He had grown the beets on forty acres of the Milford bottoms the year before, on Linus's drains, against the advice of every farmer in Milford, who had never heard of eating a root you did not first boil; and he had built a sugar house at the end of the Milford line out of Kaldo's brick and the boiler from a dead pump, and sliced and soaked and boiled and skimmed and boiled again all winter, with a crew of twelve and a great deal of language, until what was left in the pans would set. The first boilings had been brown and smelled of earth. The ones in the spring had been paler. The ones that came down the line in July were white.

Paxton got down off the wagon at the works siding and broke a corner off the first loaf with a hammer and held it out to Emeric on his palm.

"Taste it," he said.

Emeric tasted it. It was sugar. Plain, white, sweet, with nothing behind it at all; the sugar of the other life, of tea and cakes and every child's birthday, that in Randillia came on ships from the far south in little stone jars and cost what saffron cost.

"It's sugar," he said.

"It's Milford sugar," said Paxton, with deep satisfaction. "From a root. I've forty acres. Next year I'll have a hundred." He wrapped the loaf again in its paper. "Your cook's had first refusal, by the chancellor's order. She's been sending me letters about it since February."

Maren had been sending everybody letters about it since February. She took four of the twelve loaves the same afternoon, and by the next morning the iced cream barrows on the square and the quay were selling a cream that was sweet without being honey, and white, and cold, and the line at the quay barrow went down the river wall to the water stair. Maren stood behind the barrow herself the first day, in her apron, with a copper ladle, and took every coin, and did not smile once, which was how anybody who knew her could tell she was happier than she had been since the first stove.

Imani bought one, in the afternoon, on her way back across the bridge from the ward, and ate it standing at the parapet looking at the river, and came up the keep stair afterward and told Emeric, in passing, that she had just eaten a thing that would rot the teeth of every child in four baronies within ten years, and that she had enjoyed it very much, and that she would be writing to Paxton about the teeth.



Vantic came back on the fifth of August.

The House carriage came in at the South Gate in the evening, under the new-faced towers, and Emeric watched it from the wall walk over the gate and could not tell anything from it. It was the same dark green carriage, and the same silent driver, and the same two horses; and she got down in the gate yard with her book under her arm and her gloves on, as she always did, and nodded to Rylan, and went up the keep stair to the counting room without looking round.

Ferra sent for him a quarter of an hour later.

She was at her desk. Vantic was in the chair across from it, the one Saban used, with her book on her knees, closed. Neither of them was saying anything when he came in. He shut the door, and stood by it.

"I won," said Vantic.

She said it to the book on her knees.

"On the numbers. Every one. They went through my book line by line for two days, the senior partners, nine of them, at the long table, with my father at the head of it, and they could not find a line wrong. They tried. Tullio's man tried for a whole afternoon. And then I put Kaldo's ledgers on the table beside the House's, and showed them the column, the one I showed you, the people the House would have refused who paid." She was quiet. "Cassian understood it before I'd finished. I saw him understand it. My second brother. He looked at the column and then at me and I saw him do the sum."

"And?"

"And then they talked about the succession." Her voice did not change. "Not to me. Not about me. Across me. As if I were the table. Whether Tullio would want the seat in the capital or at the coast. Whether Cassian would accept the trade book under him or would have to be given the coast house to keep him. Whether my father's health would see another Reading. For an hour. I stood at the end of the table with my book, where the one being read stands, and they talked about which of my brothers would have the House, and nobody asked me to sit down, and nobody asked me to leave." She looked up from the book at last, at the window. "And then my father thanked me for a very thorough book, and said the partners would consider it, and the Reading was over."

Nobody in the counting room said anything.

"There is no rule," said Vantic. "That's what I spent the coast road thinking. There is no clause in the founding ledger that says a daughter cannot have the seat. I've read it four times; I could recite it to you. There is nothing to appeal, because nothing was done. Nobody refused me anything. They simply didn't see me standing there." She opened her book, and looked at the first page, and closed it again. "I've built a method to price borrowers that nobody sees. And I've found that the House can't see me either. I suppose that's a column too."

Emeric did not know what to say. He looked at Ferra, and Ferra was looking at Vantic, very steadily, with no expression, the way she looked at a column she meant to fix.

"What will you do?" he said.

"Go on," said Vantic. "There will be a third Reading. There always is. I'll be there, with a better book." She put her hand flat on the cover. "But I want something first. I have never asked Kaldo for anything that wasn't in my audit. Not once, in five years. I'm going to ask now, and I'd rather be told no than be told yes out of kindness, because I've had enough kindness from people who don't see me to last me the rest of the year."

"Ask."

"The ship," said Vantic. "The long flight. To the south, in the winter, to Emberfell. Ferra told me there would be a voyage. I want to go on it." She looked at him. "Not as the House. As Livia. Who would like, once in her life, to go somewhere fast, very high up, with people who know her name."

He opened his mouth.

"Yes," said Ferra.

They both looked at her.

"Yes," said Ferra again. She picked up her pen. "She can read a dial, she can keep a log, she can count stores, and she's the only person in this barony besides me who can look at Rowena's harbor dues and know in a breath whether we're being robbed." She wrote something in her book, a line, short. "And she's signed the oath. And she's a mage, which on a ship that runs on stones is no bad thing to have in the passage when they go quiet." She blotted the line. "I've put her on the manifest. Fletcher will complain about the weight. You can tell him."

Vantic looked at Ferra across the desk for as long as it took to read a line.

Then she looked at Emeric.

"Ferra has said yes," he said. "It's her manifest."

"I noticed," said Vantic. Something moved at the corner of her mouth, very slightly, for the first time since the carriage came in. "It seems I have been seen after all. By the wrong family." She stood, and put her book under her arm, and went to the door; and at the door she stopped, with her gloved hand on the latch, and did not turn round. "Thank you," she said, to the door. "Both of you." And went out.

Ferra went on writing.

"You decided that before she came in," said Emeric.

"I decided it in June," said Ferra, "when she asked me first whether she might see you." She did not look up. "Nobody who asks first gets told no in my office. It's one of my rules. I've never written it down." She turned the page. "Go and tell Fletcher."
