Chapter 176 - Holes, Not Marks

The board went in on the first of May, in the little room off the line room at the top of the keep that had been a linen press for two hundred years and still smelled faintly of lavender.

Tess had drawn it in the winter, between bulbs, and Bram had built it, and it was the ugliest thing in the keep. A tall panel of oak, upright, like a cupboard door, with two rows of brass-lined holes in it, one hole for every line; and above each hole a little hinged shutter of black-painted tin with a number on it, held up by a catch. On a shelf in front of the panel, a row of cords, each with a brass plug on its end and a weight on a pulley underneath to pull it back down out of the way when it was let go. A key for ringing. A pair of earpieces on a headband, for the girl at the board.

Seven lines, to start. The works across the river. The ward. The school. The gate tower. The counting room. Bram's shop. And the keep itself, which was the board.

When somebody at the works lifted their handset off its hook, a current went up the wire and let go the little catch, and the shutter for the works fell open with a click and showed its number. The girl at the board saw the number, put a plug in the works' hole, and asked who they wanted. Then she put the plug on the other end of the same cord into the hole for whoever it was, and rang them; and the two lines were joined, through her cord, and she took her earpiece out of the circuit, and the two of them could talk.

That was the theory.

The girls were Sela's. Two of them, fourteen and fifteen, the two best at sums in the top bench at the school and the two quickest with their hands, and they had been practising on the board with Tess for a week on dummy lines before the real ones were joined; and on the first afternoon, when the works' shutter fell for the first time, the fourteen-year-old put the plug into the wrong hole, and Bram, in his own shop, was rung, and lifted his handset, and was told by a voice from the works that the second boiler wanted a new gauge glass, and said that he was not the works, and was asked who he was then, and put the handset down.

By the end of the first week they could do it in their sleep. The handsets were Bakelite, a little shell like a long dark ear with a cup at each end, and they hung on hooks beside a box on the wall with a crank to ring the board; and by the end of the first week every one of the seven places had somebody who would not leave theirs alone.



The ward's line was joined on the sixth.

It had been last of the seven because the wire to the ward had to cross the river on the new bridge's rail, and the bridge's rail had not been ready; and Imani had said at the long table that she did not see why the ward should be last for anything, and that if somebody fell under a cart at the works she would very much like to be told before they arrived on a door. Ferra had moved it up the list. The bridge's rail had not cared.

The lineman came up the keep stair at six in the evening on the sixth to say it was through. Emeric was in the line room. He looked at the board through the open door, where the fifteen-year-old was on, with her earpiece on and her chin on her hand, and the ward's new shutter in the bottom row with its number painted on it in white, held up on its catch. Nobody had used it yet.

He went to the keep's own handset, on the wall by the line room door, and cranked it.

The keep's shutter fell. The girl looked up at it, and then round through the door at him, and went pink, and put in her plug. "Number, please," she said, very correct, in a voice she had plainly been practising.

"The ward."

She plugged into the ward, and rang.

It rang and rang. He could hear it from his end, faint, a small brass bell a mile away across a river, through the cup at his ear. He thought perhaps nobody at the ward knew what the bell was for. Then it stopped, and there was a clatter, and a silence, and then Imani's voice, much closer than a mile, with an edge on it:

"Yes?"

"It's me," he said.

A pause. "I know it's you. Nobody else would ring a ward at supper to see whether the bell works."

"I wanted to hear what it sounds like at your end."

"It sounds," said Imani, "like the whole of the works breathing in."

He had not thought about that. He looked round. The girl at the board had taken her earpiece out of the circuit, as she had been taught, and was sitting very straight with her eyes on her shutters and her ears visibly pricked. In the line room, at the bench behind him, Tess had a second earpiece on its long cord to her own ear, as she had for every call since the first, because the first week of any new line was when you found its faults, and she was writing in her book and not looking up. And in the doorway behind Tess, which Emeric had not noticed until now, was Bram, who had come up the stair with a coil of wire and had stopped.

"Imani, I only..."

"There's a girl on the board," said Imani, on the wire, quite clearly, at an ordinary speaking pitch, as if she were giving a ward round. "There's Tess on the second earpiece, because there always is. There's very likely Bram in the doorway. And there's my own girl at the ward table behind me, who's stopped pretending to wash a bowl." A pause. "So if you rang to say something, I'll tell you now that I'll not say anything back on this that I wouldn't say in the market on a Saturday. And neither will you, if you've any sense."

Tess, at the bench, did not look up. Her pencil had stopped.

"Is that a no?" said Emeric.

"That's a wire," said Imani. "If you want to say something to me, the ward's across the bridge, and I'm in it till ten." And then, a little lower, so that he had to press the cup to his ear to be sure he had heard it, and so did everybody else: "I'd come before ten."

Click.

He stood with the handset in his hand, listening to the hum where the ward had been.

The girl at the board pulled her plugs out of the ward and the keep, both, with great care, as if they might go off, and let the cords drop back on their weights. Tess began writing again. Bram, in the doorway, looked at the coil of wire in his hands as if he had forgotten what it was, and turned round, and went back down the stair without a word.

Emeric hung the handset on its hook.

"It's a good line," said Tess, to her book. "Very clear. You can hear every word."

He went across the bridge at nine.



By the next morning the whole keep knew, and by noon the works.

It was not the girl on the board. She swore on the school's register that she had told nobody, and Sela, who had questioned her personally, believed her. It was not Tess, who did not repeat things. It was not Bram. It was, in the end, nobody; it was a wire with seven places on it and a bell at each, and a line room with its door open, and a ward table with a girl at it who had stopped washing a bowl.

Ottoline Fesk heard it at the gate, on the ninth, when she came in off the capital road on the carrier's cart with a crate of three more adding machines for Grayrock and Milford and a box of tools under her feet.

She had come to oil the machines in the counting room, as she did every spring, and to quarrel with Ferra about carrying, as she also did; and she was shown the board on the first afternoon, because Tess wanted her to see it. She stood in the old linen press in her black stuff dress with her spectacles on her forehead and watched the fourteen-year-old take three calls, one after another, works to counting room, gate to keep, Bram to the works, plug and ring and plug and ring; and watched the girl take her earpiece out each time and sit up straight and look at her shutters and listen anyway, with every inch of her, to whatever she could hear through the cords.

"Those girls hear everything," said Ottoline.

"They're not meant to listen," said Tess.

"Nobody's meant to listen. Everybody does." Ottoline brought her spectacles down onto her nose. "When I was a girl there was a posting master's wife in Wayford who read every letter that went through her house, over a kettle, and sold what was in them to whoever across the square would pay. Nobody ever proved it. Half the square knew." She looked at the board. "I don't say this board will do that." She was quiet. "I say there's a girl in the middle of every word anybody in this barony says to anybody else, and she's fourteen."

"She's very good," said Tess, a little stiffly.

"She's very good. She'll be better at it in a year. And then she'll marry a carter, and the next girl'll be worse." Ottoline turned away from the board. "Have you a pencil?"



Emeric came up to the line room that evening and found them both at the bench, Tess in her chair and Ottoline on Tess's stool, with Tess's book pushed aside and a sheet of Ottoline's own paper between them, covered.

He did not interrupt. He stood at the door and looked at the sheet upside down from across the room. It was a wheel. A brass wheel with teeth, and a pawl on the teeth, like the escapement of a clock; and beside it an arm, a wiper, on the same shaft, that went round a curved bank of contacts, ten of them, one at a time, a notch a step. And under that, a second drawing: a disc with ten holes round its edge, for a finger, on the front of a handset.

"You turn it to your number," said Ottoline, to Tess, tapping the disc. "As far as the stop. And let go. And a spring brings it home, and as it comes home it breaks the current, once for every hole it passes. One for one. Six for six." She tapped the wheel. "And at the far end, in a box, every break pulls a magnet, and the magnet kicks the pawl, and the pawl steps the wheel on one tooth, and the wiper steps on one contact. Six breaks, six steps. And there you are, on the sixth line, and nobody's put a plug in anything."

Tess looked at the drawing. She had gone quiet, the way she went quiet when something mattered.

"And for more lines than ten?"

"Two wheels. One for the tens, one for the ones. Turn the disc twice." Ottoline sat back. "Three, for a town." She took her spectacles off and folded them. "It's a clock, girl. A clock is a thing that counts its own steps and stops at the right one. I've been mending them for thirty years. I've never built one that counted a man's finger before, that's all."

Emeric came into the room. Ottoline looked up at him without any particular welcome.

"Highness," she said.

"Mistress Fesk." He looked at the drawing the right way up. He knew what it was. He had known its name all his life in the other one, the man who made it first, the undertaker in a town on a lake who had lost his trade to a rival whose wife sat at the town's board and sent every call for an undertaker to her husband. He had not told anybody about it, ever. He had not told Tess when she built the board. He had decided in the winter, looking at Tess's drawing, that he would not; that the board would do for a few years, and the other thing could wait for somebody to want it badly enough.

Somebody had.

"Will it work?" he said.

"I don't know yet," said Ottoline. "That's what drawings are for." She looked at him with her pale shrewd eyes. "You don't seem surprised."

"I've stopped being surprised by people in this room."

"Hm," said Ottoline Fesk.

And then, because she had brought the other thing too, in the box under her feet on the cart, and because he had stood in front of it once in her shop on Wayford square two years ago and asked what it was, she reached into her bag and took out a second sheet of paper, older, much folded, soft at the creases, and spread it on the bench over the first.

It was the census. Not the census itself; a drawing of a machine for counting one. A great frame, a table, with a bed of pins over it on a lever, and a row of clock dials across its top. And beside it, a card, a stiff card the size of a hand, ruled into little squares. Each square a question. Man or woman. Under twenty, under forty, over. Married. Children. Trade. Parish. And in each square a mark, in ink, where the answer was yes.

"Your bookkeeper's census," said Ottoline. "Four baronies. She's going to want it counted every way there is to count it, and it'll take her clerks a year each time." She tapped the frame. "You'd lay the card under the pins, and bring the lever down, and wherever there's a mark the pin touches, and a dial goes on one. Every card through in a breath. Every dial counting at once." She frowned at it. "Except a pin doesn't feel ink. I've tried it. Ink's nothing. The pin goes down on the mark the same as on the paper. I've been trying to think for a year what a pin can feel."

He looked at the card. At the little squares, and the ink marks in them.

It was the one thing all year he had thought he might say. He had decided in the winter he would say it if she ever showed him this sheet, and not otherwise, and only once.

"Holes," he said. "Not marks."

Ottoline looked at him.

"Punch a hole where the answer's yes," said Emeric. "And under the card, a cup of quicksilver for every square. Where there's a hole, the pin goes through, into the cup, and closes a current. Where there's no hole, the card stops it." He put the sheet down. "The pin doesn't have to feel anything. It only has to get through."

She did not say anything. She looked at the card. Then she took a pencil out of her apron pocket and, very slowly and carefully, drew a small neat circle in the corner of one of the squares, and filled it in solid black, and looked at it.

"Hm," said Ottoline Fesk, at last. She folded the sheet up again, along its old soft creases, and put it back in her bag, and turned back to the wheel and the wiper and the disc with ten holes, as if the other thing had never been on the bench. "Well. That's next year. This is now. Tess, how many lines are you going to want, by the time you're done?"

"Forty," said Tess. "To begin."

"Forty lines." Ottoline began to write. "Two wheels each way, one at each end of every call. A bank of selectors." She wrote, and wrote, down the margin of the sheet, and stopped, and looked at the figure at the bottom, and took her spectacles off again. "Four hundred ratchet wheels. All the same. Every tooth the same as every other tooth on every other wheel, or the pawl skips." She put the pencil down. "I cut ten a day, on a good day, with my best files and my eyes in a glass. That's forty days. And then I'll have one wrong in every thirty, and I won't know which."



The hob went into the forge's book that week, as run fifteen.

A gear-cutter. Not a wheel but the tool that cut wheels: a short thick steel spindle with a thread cut round it, like a screw, and the thread broken across by a dozen grooves so that every turn of it was a row of little teeth, all the same, as alike as the forge could make them, which was more alike than anything had ever been. You turned the hob against a blank, and turned the blank with it at the right rate, geared, and the hob's teeth walked round the blank and cut it, tooth after tooth, all the same, all the way round. Every wheel it cut was the hob's shadow. If the hob was true, so was every wheel after it, forever.

He made it in a night, the twelfth. It came out of the cage hard and bright and a little warm, and he took it up to Bram's shop in the morning in a cloth and laid it on the bench.

Ottoline was there, at Bram's lathe, with Bram, because she had been there since six. She had been told that Bram's shop and the standards room had a cutter coming. She picked it up out of the cloth and held it under the window and turned it, slowly, with her spectacles down, and ran her thumbnail along the teeth, one after another, the whole way round.

"Who cut this?" she said.

"The standards room," said Bram.

Ottoline turned it once more, against the light.

"The standards room," she said, "has better files than I do." She put it down very gently in the cloth. "I'd like to meet the standards room one day."

"It doesn't come out much," said Bram.

"No," said Ottoline. "I don't suppose it does." And she did not ask again, then or later; and Emeric, by the door, understood that she had decided, as Hale had decided about the crates, that there was a door in Kaldo she was not going to knock on, and that she had filed the fact somewhere, as she filed everything, and would know exactly where to find it.

She and Bram built the machine round the hob in eight days. A bed, a spindle for the hob, an arbor for the blank, and a train of change gears between them, so that the blank turned exactly one tooth's width for every turn of the hob; with a hand crank first, and then a belt off the works' line shaft. It was not beautiful. It cut its first wheel on the twentieth of May in three minutes, and Ottoline put it under her glass for a quarter of an hour, tooth by tooth, and then put it on the bench beside one of her own filed wheels and looked at them both for some while, and then picked her own up and dropped it into the scrap box without a word.

The gear-hobbing machine cut the four hundred wheels in a fortnight, with a boy on the crank.



The exchange cut over on the fifth of June, at noon, in the old linen press.

The board came out. It went down the stair on its side, with Bram at one end and two linemen at the other, to a corner of the works where it would be kept as a spare, and the two girls watched it go from the line room door with their arms folded and expressions of deep injury. Ottoline's automatic telephone exchange went in where it had been: three tall frames of oak, and on them the selectors in rows, forty of them, brass and black, each a stack of wheels and a wiper and a bank of contacts and a magnet, each the size of a loaf. Every handset in the barony had a new disc on its front, Bakelite, with ten holes, a dial, and a number on a card underneath.

At noon Tess threw the switch at the rack.

Nothing happened. Nothing was supposed to happen; nobody had dialled anything. The racks stood in the linen press with the lavender smell, humming faintly, waiting.

Then a selector moved. Click, click, click, very fast, a wiper stepping round its bank, and stopped. Then another, the second wheel. Click, click. Stop. And a bell rang, faintly, through the floor, somewhere below in the keep.

Everybody in the line room looked at the floor.

"That's the counting room," said Tess, who knew every number. "Somebody's ringing the counting room." She looked at the rack. "From the gate tower."

Ottoline was not in the line room. She had gone down an hour before. Emeric picked up the keep's handset off its hook, and turned the disc to the counting room's number, which he had never once done, and let it come home, and heard the wheels go in the racks behind him, click, click; and was not put through, because the counting room's line was already busy. He could hear it, very faint, under the hum. Two women's voices. One of them was Ferra's.

"Ottoline," said Tess, without looking up. "From the gate. She said she'd make the first call herself."

"To Ferra?"

"She said she had a question about carrying." Tess was writing in her book. "On the third column of the Grayrock quarter. She said it was urgent."

He put the handset back on its hook.

In the corner, the two girls who had run the board for five weeks stood looking at the racks with their arms folded.

"Nobody can hear them," said the fourteen-year-old, to the fifteen-year-old, in a low voice, outraged. "Nobody at all."

"You can," said Tess. "On the test circuit."

The girls looked at her.

"But you won't," said Tess, still writing. "Because Mistress Fesk built a machine so nobody had to. And if I ever catch either of you on the test circuit when you're not testing it, I'll tell her." She turned a page. "Go and ask Sela what she wants you for. She's got forty lines' worth of numbers to teach the whole town, and you're the only two people in Kaldo who know all of them."
