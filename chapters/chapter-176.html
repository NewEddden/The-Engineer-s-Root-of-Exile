Chapter 176 - The Seam

The duke's quartermaster had left his tent standing, and his table in it, and his chests, and his books.

Saban found it at dusk on the ninth day after the fight, at the far end of the camp above the border ford where the duke's host had run to after the stream ground and stood for a week while its captains argued, and then broken up in a night and gone north in pieces. It was a big square tent of good oiled canvas with the duke's badge painted on the door flap, pitched on a rise by the horse lines, and the horse lines were empty, and the door flap was tied back, and inside there was a table and a stool and eleven chests with the lids up, and paper. Paper on the table. Paper on the ground. Paper in the chests in bundles tied with tape, and loose, and in bound books with the duke's badge stamped on the covers. Somebody had started to burn it and stopped. There was a black patch in the grass outside the door with the ash of a few quires in it and a half-burned book, and then nothing, as if the man with the torch had heard something and run.

Saban stood in the door and looked at it for a long time.

"How long have we got?" he said.

Osric was behind him with his lantern, and the troop, twenty of the garrison on tired horses. He looked north up the road into the dark, where the duke's rearguard had gone, and where, by the riders who had come in at noon, near four hundred of the duke's household and vassals had rallied at a hall six miles off and were talking about coming back for the camp's stores.

"Till morning," Osric said. "Maybe. If they come, they'll come at first light."

"And if they come?"

"Then we're twenty men in a camp built for two thousand," said Osric, "and we go."

Saban went in.

He read everything. He had always read everything. He had read Sethe's boxes, all four of them, every page, and Ferra's second box, and the lease of every relay post on the Wayford line, and a wool factor's clerk's letters for fifteen years, and his whole life had been reading what other men had not bothered to, and finding in it the one line they had not known they had written. It was the only thing he had ever been better at than anyone else. It was why Emeric had wanted him.

And since the table was bound, he could keep it.

The room kept every page a delver had seen while it was open. That was the thing none of them had understood at first and all of them understood now. Not remembered: kept. A page read with the room open went onto a shelf in the room, whole, every line of it, in the hand it was written in, and it stayed there, and you could go back and take it down and read it again in a year, and it would be the same page. Saban had kept Sethe's boxes that way. He had kept Ferra's second box. He had a shelf in his room, which was a merchant's counting house with a green-shaded lamp, that held every paper he had read with the room open for years, and he had never lost a line of any of them.

He sat down on the quartermaster's stool at the quartermaster's table and pulled the lamp close and looked at the chests.

There were eleven of them. He did a sum. The bound books alone were forty, near enough, and each of them a hundred pages or more, and the bundles in the chests were more than the books. The loose paper on the ground was more than a man could pick up in an armful. Say eight thousand pages. Say ten.

He could read a page in the time it took to breathe twice, with the room open, if he did not stop to think about it. He had timed it once in Sethe's boxes, with Hobb. Ten thousand pages was most of a night.

And he could not read them with the room open. Not most of a night. Not alone.

He had never opened his room alone. None of them had, until Bram built a boiler in his head and walked into his room through it; and then Armand, over the winter, out of a study in a tower. Saban had not. He had always opened it at the table with Emeric's hand on the stone, or since the bands with one of the others beside him, Ferra or Armand, to count and to watch and to call him out. That was the rule. They had all made it. It was a good rule.

There was nobody here to count. Osric could not do it; Osric was not of the table, and could not have seen what there was to see.

He looked at the chests.

In the morning, if the duke's four hundred came back, this tent would burn, or go north in a cart, and nobody at Kaldo would ever see a page of it. And whatever was in it that the duke's quartermaster had begun to burn and stopped would be ash, or in the duke's own hand, which was the same thing.

He put his right hand on the band on his left wrist.

It opened more easily than he had thought. That frightened him a little, and then he did not think about it. He built the counting house the way he had learned: not the room, but the thing he knew. The desk, the green lamp, the ledger with its red-ruled columns, the smell of wax and dust and ink, the particular creak of the third floorboard from the window. And then the door was there, and he was through it, and the shelf was along the wall where it always was, and he was sitting at the quartermaster's table in the duke's tent with his eyes open and the first bound book under his hand and the room open behind his eyes like a second pair.

He began.

He did not read for sense. He had learned not to. He let his eyes go down each page, top to bottom, one line after the next, and felt the page go onto the shelf, and turned it, and the next. Stores. Oats and hay by the waggon. Bread, salt beef, beer. Horseshoes. The duke's household's wages by the week, knight by knight. The vassals' contingents, man by man. Tents. Rope. Arrows. The mages' fees, which were very large. Every page went onto the shelf behind his eyes, and he felt the band on his wrist grow warm, and warmer.

He did not look at the band. He did not look at the time. He looked at the page and turned it.

He knew, somewhere, that the lamp had been refilled, because the light changed; that Osric had come in and gone out; that the troop had built a fire outside the door. He did not stop. He went through the first chest and the second. He went through the bound books one after another, the duke's badge on every cover. He took the loose papers off the ground in handfuls and went through those.

And in the fifth chest, under the stores, he found the stones.

It was a small book, not stamped. A private book, bound in plain calf, in a different hand from the quartermaster's: smaller, older, a steward's hand. He knew it was the one before he had read a line of it, because it was the one that had been in the half-burned bundle at the top of the chest, and it had been put back unburned.

Purchases of common stone, large, for his grace's service, at the capital, by the agents, the first page said. And then, down the page and the next and the next, week by week from the first frost of last winter: stones. So many, of such a size, from such a seller, at such a price. Every purchase Saban's wool factor's clerk had written to him about in the spring, and a great many more that the clerk had not found. Three hundred and forty stones, by the last page. And the total, which was a great deal of money, more than twice what they were worth.

And beside every purchase, in the right-hand column where a steward writes whose account it goes against, one of two lines.

Against the estate of the late Lord Placit, by the Lady Placit's authority.

Against the estate of the late Lord Vennor, by the Lady Vennor's authority.

He read it again. He did not need to; it was on the shelf. He read it again with his eyes.

The widows. The two women his grace of Aldane had addressed his letter to after Wayford, the letter pasted on the chancery steps and three church doors and read at the fountain in the upper market twice a day for three days. The letter of condolence to the widows of Placit and of Vennor, from their kinsman at whose table both their houses sat, grieving for their husbands, who had been hanged at Wayford by the prince at Kaldo's order, without writ. The letter that had not said what the five were hanged for.

Placit's widow and Vennor's widow had paid for the stones. Out of their husbands' estates. Out of the money their husbands had made in the Staple's cellar out of the bodies of Hew and Colley and Jem and Wat and the nineteen in the pit.

The grievance had paid for the war.

Saban sat with the book open in front of him and his hand on the band and understood, all at once and entirely, the way he sometimes understood a contract, how this would read in the capital. It would read as the grieving widows of two lords hanged without writ, paying out of their own dower for their kinsman to take up their cause against the prince who hanged them. It would read as loyalty and grief and justice. It would read, in the capital, exactly as Aldane had meant it to read from the first frost.

And nobody in the capital would read it. That was the other thing he understood. Nobody would ask for the book. Nobody would set it beside the intake page from the Staple's strongroom, which was in Keller's hands already, in the record Emeric had written over four nights. Nobody would put the two side by side, the money going in and the money coming out, because nobody in the capital would want to know that the widows' dower had come out of a cellar, and so nobody would look.

He turned the page.

Behind the stones, in the same hand, there were other accounts against the widows' estates. Smaller. Older. He went through them. A payment in the winter after the hanging, to the coast, by the Lady Placit's steward, to the master of a lugsail, for service in the matter of the sulphur, the first half. And in the summer after, the second half, the service done.

The Gannet. Six dead off the marsh. Bede Harrow and Tollin Sedge and Moss, and three more, and the cargo burned and nothing taken.

The men who boarded your boat were paid out of the same purse that paid me.

The same purse. The five's purse. Their estates, in their widows' hands, paying on.

It did not say who had stood above the purse. It did not say who the voice on the ledge had been, or who had told Marrow what to do and paid him for it. It said only where the money had come from, and that it had gone on coming after the five were dead. It was part of an answer. Saban put it on the shelf.

He knew something was wrong in the eighth chest.

The band was hot. Not warm; hot, the way a pan is hot, and he could feel it through the cuff of his sleeve. He had been feeling it for some time and not looking at it. The room behind his eyes had begun to go dim at its edges, the green lamp going smaller and further away, as if he were looking at it down a corridor. The pages were going onto the shelf more slowly. He was having to push them.

He pushed them.

He did not stop to think about why. He knew why. The band held a charged bead, and the bead held what it held, and the room took from it while it was open, and he had had it open since dusk, alone, at a rate nobody had ever run it at, and the bead was running dry. He knew that. He knew what the others had said at the table about what happened when a man kept pulling on a stone that had nothing left in it. He had heard Emeric say it about the five's cellar, the one time Emeric had talked about the cellar: that a stone runs dry through a man tied to it the way water runs out of a cracked jar.

There were three chests left. It was the fourth hour after midnight, by the lamp.

He pushed.

He went through the eighth chest and the ninth. The pages were very slow now. Each one went onto the shelf like a stone going up a hill. The corridor to the green lamp was long, and dark at the sides, and the band on his wrist had stopped being hot and started being something else, a feeling he did not have a word for, a pulling, as if something at his wrist were drinking from the inside of his arm.

He opened the tenth chest.

He remembered, afterward, the first page of it. Horseshoe nails, by the thousand. He remembered turning it. He did not remember the second.

He woke in a bed that moved.

It was a cart. It was one of the physician's carts with the red cloth, and it was moving, and it was daylight, and he was lying in it on straw under a blanket with the red cloth over the top of the cart making everything the colour of wine. His left wrist was bound in linen from the knuckles to the elbow. He could not lift his arm. He could not lift his head. He lay and looked at the red cloth, and felt the cart go over a rut, and felt it in every bone.

The room was open.

He had not opened it. It was just open, behind his eyes, dim and small and very far off, the green lamp at the end of a long dark corridor, and the shelf. He went down the corridor to it, slowly, the way a sick man goes to a window. The pages were on the shelf. All of them. The quartermaster's stores and the vassals' rolls and the mages' fees and the small calf book in the steward's hand, the stones, the widows, the lugsail. Nine chests and a page. He could see them from where he was. He did not have the strength to take any of them down.

"Don't," said Imani's voice. "Whatever you're doing, stop it."

He shut the room. It was like shutting a heavy door.

She was sitting on the tail of the cart with her bag on her knees, swaying with the cart's movement, looking at him. She looked very tired.

"Where," he said. It came out in a whisper.

"Going south. The duke's four hundred came back at first light. Osric put you over his saddle and brought you out, and the tent's burned. He says they burned it themselves. I believe him." She did not say anything for a moment. "You'd been in there since dusk. Osric came in at the fourth hour and you were sitting at the table with your eyes open and your hand on the band and a book in front of you, and you didn't know him. He shook you. You didn't know him. He picked you up and you still didn't know him. He says you were cold."

Saban lay and looked at her.

"I'll tell you what you did," Imani said, "because nobody else will, and you'll need to know. You opened your room alone and you kept it open for most of a night, and you took everything the bead on your wrist had, and when it had nothing left you kept taking. And it took from you. The way a stone runs dry through a man in the cellar. Not as far. Not near as far. But the same way." She put her hand on the linen on his wrist, lightly. "Your pulse was a child's when Osric brought you in. You were cold to the elbow. I've had you warm for six hours and you're still not warm enough."

"The pages," Saban said.

"I don't care about the pages."

"They're there. I have them."

"I know you have them," said Imani. "I don't care."

He was quiet for a while. The cart went over another rut.

"Will it mark me?" he said.

She knew what he meant. He saw that she knew. Bastian's arm on the flat. The lines on Tobe's palms, and Cade's. The tracery.

She looked at the linen on his wrist for a long time.

"I don't know," she said.

He waited for more. There was no more. She did not tell him it would be all right. She did not tell him it would not. She sat on the tail of the cart with her hand on his bound wrist, swaying, and did not know, and did not pretend to, and he found that he was grateful to her for it, more than for the six hours.

Wenna came to the cart in the afternoon, when they stopped at the ford to water the horses. Imani had sent for her from Wayford. She climbed up onto the tail with her writing case and her face very white and sat where Imani had sat.

"Imani says I'm not to let you do anything," she said.

"I'm not going to do anything."

"She says you'll try to."

"I'm going to talk," said Saban. "You're going to write. That's all. I'm not opening anything." He could not lift his head. He turned it on the straw to look at her. "It's all on the shelf. I don't need to open anything. I can see it from the door."

Wenna looked at him for a moment. Then she opened the case, and took out the pen and the ink and a quire, and set the ink in the corner of the cart where it would not spill, and dipped the pen.

He shut his eyes. He did not open the room. He did not need to; he could feel it there, dim and far off, the green lamp at the end of the corridor and the shelf under it, and on the shelf a small book bound in plain calf in a steward's hand.

"Purchases of common stone, large," he said, very quietly, "for his grace's service, at the capital, by the agents. Page one. First line. The date is the eleventh of the first frost."

Wenna wrote.

"Of Tabard the stone-seller, at the sign of the Lamp under the chancery arches: forty stones, lamp stones, the size of a fist or above, flawed, at eleven Copper the stone. Right-hand column. Against the estate of the late Lord Placit, by the Lady Placit's authority."

He heard her pen stop, and then go on.

He lay in the cart under the red cloth, too weak to lift his head from the straw, with his wrist bound to the elbow and his eyes shut, and gave it to her line by line, page by page, in the steward's hand, from memory.
