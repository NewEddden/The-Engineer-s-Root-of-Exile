Chapter 216 - The Elevation

The summons came on the crown post in the last week of the high summer, under the great seal, in a chancery hand, and said that the king would receive the lord of Kaldo in his hall at the late-summer sitting of the council. It did not say why. A summons never said why.

Ferra read it twice at the long-room table and put it down in the middle, where everybody could see it, and nobody picked it up.

"Sent for," Ward said, from his chair by the door.

That was all he said, and it was the whole of it. The order had stood since the day room with the gap under the door: the lord of Kaldo did not come up the road unless he was sent for. He had not been sent for in two years. The one time he had wanted most to go, with a foreign power digging on the Crown's own claim, he had written by Ward's road instead, and been answered with nothing, and read the nothing as leave. Now there was a sheet of paper on the table that was not nothing, and nobody at the table knew what it was.

Keller's letter came two days behind it, by the ordinary post, in Keller's own hand, and was five lines long.

You will have had the summons. It is not a favour and you should not let anyone tell you it is. The instruments for Grayrock, Milford and Wayford closed at the quarter. Three baronies taken into one holding beside the holder's own, with the instruments closed, is a duchy in law, whoever holds them and whatever anyone would prefer. The council must sit on it. I thought you would rather know the reason than guess it. K.

Ferra read that one twice as well. Then she looked up.

"Duke," she said.

"By arithmetic," said Emeric.

"It's the only way you'd ever have got it." She folded Keller's letter along its own creases and put it in the box. "I'll want the instrument when it comes. The rank changes the precedence on every roll we send north. And the rider's count. And the licence." She was already writing. "And the style on the notices. You'll hate that."

He did hate it. He hated it at once and without argument, the way he hated a badly cut thread, and he let it go, because she was right and it was a column in a book and the book did not care.

They went north on the train as far as Wayford, which took most of a day now where the road had taken five, and then by carriage, hired in Wayford, for the rest. Ward came, because the lord of Kaldo did not go to the capital with anyone else at his shoulder, and Saban came, because the capital was where Saban's clerks and debtors and friends of friends lived and it would have been a waste of a journey not to take him. Rylan stayed. There was the island, and the dark of the moon fell in the middle of the fortnight, and Ferra had already chosen two nights on either side of it and told nobody which.

It was a long road and a dull one, and he spent most of it with the shutters down, going over the settlement again in Saban's head, which was to say listening to Saban recite it, clause by clause, from the counting house he now kept open for an hour at a time without strain. Ward sat on the box with the driver for most of the way and said nothing to either of them. That was not unusual. What was unusual, Emeric thought afterwards, was that he did not sleep on the road at all. Ward slept in carriages the way soldiers did, instantly and lightly and anywhere. This time he sat up on the box through two nights of inns where they did not stop, with his hat pulled down, and when Emeric asked him once at a change of horses whether he was well, he said he was well, and got back up on the box.

The hall had been set for a sitting of the council and not for a judgment, and the difference was a woman in grey.

He saw her staff before he saw her. There were eight or nine of them, young men and two older women, all in the same dark grey with no badge on it, and they moved about the hall in the hour before the sitting with lists in their hands, setting stools, moving a bench a foot to the left, turning back a steward who had come in by the wrong door, and not one of them spoke to another. They did not need to. Each of them seemed to know where every other one of them would be. A lord came in at the south door and was met, and bowed to, and walked to a place on the second bench that he had not chosen and did not argue with. A bishop came in and was met by one of the older women, and walked to the east bench, and sat exactly where he was put.

Maeve stood at the foot of the gallery stair with a single sheet of paper in her hand, and did not move from there the whole hour, and did not look at the sheet.

He had not known she had an office. He had not known it was a thing a king's daughter could have: the ordering of rooms, who stood and who sat, who went in first and who waited, which door. He saw now that it was the most important office in the building on a day like this one, and that nobody in the hall had thought of it as an office at all, and that this was how she wanted it. One of her young men came to him at the south door and bowed and said, "Your place, my lord," and walked him to it, which was a stool on the floor below the dais, in the light this time, alone, with a clear space round it a yard wide on every side. Ward was shown to a place two paces behind it. Saban was shown to the benches, a long way back.

Maeve did not come to him. She did not look at him. He had not expected her to. When he looked for her again a quarter of an hour later she was on the gallery stair halfway up, turned half away, speaking to one of the older women in a voice that did not reach the floor, and the older woman went away and moved the bishop of somewhere two places along the east bench, and the bishop went without a word.

He did not open the sense.

He had not opened it inside the capital's walls since he was a boy, not the delve and not even the long slow reach of the other thing that had come to him on the Deep Channel. On the court visit he had felt the floor and the crowd without meaning to, the way a man hears a clock he has stopped listening to, and had shut it again as fast as it came. It was a habit and not a rule, and he had never quite asked himself where the habit came from. It was the house, he supposed. In this house nothing was ever quite private. He kept his door shut the way he had kept his voice down in the mews, and sat on his stool in the light, and looked at the hall with his eyes like anybody else.

The lords spiritual sat on the east bench. Cardinal Augustine sat at the head of it, nearest the dais, in the red, with his hands folded in his lap. He watched the floor the way he had watched it on the court visit, all of it at once, and when Emeric's eyes came round to him he found the cardinal already looking at him, at his face, pleasantly, as a man looks at somebody he has met and liked. He inclined his head. Emeric inclined his. The cardinal's eyes stayed on his face a moment longer, courteous, attentive, as if there were something in it he was interested to read, and then went back to the floor.

The lords temporal filled the long benches under the windows. Among them, on the second bench, was a man in a good coat going soft at the seams, with a red face and a cup, who had been given wine by one of Maeve's young men at the start of the hour and had had his cup filled twice since. Emeric noticed him only because he was the one man on the benches who drank before a sitting, and because each time the young man came round with the jug he held his cup out before the jug reached him.

Aldane was on the first bench, near the dais on the west side, among the old blood.

It was where he belonged, and he looked it. The same heavy man with the square grey beard, the same habit of expecting to be looked at. He sat with the dukes and the old earls whose names were older than the crown, men whose families had held their land before there was a Randillia to hold it in, and he sat in the middle of them, because in that company he was the one who had lost a field and still had the most men. And standing at his shoulder, where a son would stand, in a dark coat with nothing on it to show what he was, was Corbin.

Nobody on the benches thought it strange. Everybody in the hall knew whose sister's son Corbin was. The first queen was Aldane's sister, and her son stood at her brother's side at a sitting of the council, the heir to the crown among the old blood, where the old blood could see him and be seen with him. It was a family standing together. It was the most natural thing in the room.

The first queen did not come down. There was a chair for her in the gallery, at the front, with a cushion on it, and it stayed empty the whole morning, and nobody remarked on it, as nobody ever had.

Aldane looked across the floor at him once, early, before the council was called.

It was the look Emeric had expected: a heavy flat look that took in the stool, the space round it, the young man sitting on it in a better coat than the last time, and gave nothing back. Emeric let the young man meet it for a moment and then look away, a little too soon, the way a young man does when an older one stares at him.

Then Aldane's eyes went past him, to the two paces behind the stool, and stopped.

Emeric did not turn round. He did not need to; he knew who was standing there. Ward stood where he always stood on a floor, with his hands clasped in front of his sword belt and his weight on both feet, and his face was the face he wore for a road or a gate or a stranger in a yard, which was no face at all.

Aldane looked at him for the space of two breaths. Then he looked down, at the floor in front of his own boots, and then at Corbin beside him, and said something to Corbin that made Corbin smile, and did not look at the floor below the dais again for the rest of the sitting.

Emeric filed it where he filed Aldane, with the rest of the contempt. A duke of the old blood did not stare at another man's guard. A guard was not there to be looked at. It was a small thing, and it was of a piece with the rest of the man: he had looked at the lord of Kaldo, and then at the lord of Kaldo's hired steel, and found the second less worth his notice than the first, and said so with his eyes, the way a man of that blood would.

Behind him, he heard Ward breathe out through his nose. It was a very small sound.

The council was called by the steward and the matter read by the chancellor, the old man with the soft voice, who had to be helped to his feet again and who read the three instruments as he had read the settlement, without inflection, in the order of their dates. The attainder of Renner, and the escheat of Grayrock to the Crown, and its grant to the holder of Kaldo. The forfeiture of Milford for its lord's part in the private war, and its grant to the same holder, with its lord kept on as steward. The forfeiture of Wayford on the judgment of its baron, and its grant to the same. The closing of all three at the spring quarter, so that what had been held beside Kaldo on Kaldo's terms was now held in one holding with it. Four baronies, contiguous, under one holder, with the instruments closed.

"Which is a duchy in law," the chancellor said, and sat down.

It was put to the council. The steward called the benches by rank, and each lord stood and gave his voice, aye or no, and a clerk at the Crown's table wrote it down. Keller sat at the end of that table and did not write, and his left hand squared the edge of the roll in front of him, over and over, as the count went on.

The lords spiritual gave their voices first. Augustine gave his in one word, aye, pleasantly, as a man agrees that it looks like rain. His bench followed him, all but one old abbot, who said no and sat down and looked at nobody.

The old blood gave theirs next. A duke, aye. An old earl, aye. Another earl, aye, grudgingly. Another earl, no. Aldane stood when he was called, and said, "No," in the voice that expected to be listened to, and sat, and the man next to him said no, and the man next to that one looked at Aldane and at Corbin and said aye.

Emeric counted. He could not help it. He sat on his stool in the light with his face arranged into boredom and counted the voices as they came, and kept two columns in his head, and by the end of the second bench he knew it would be close.

The red-faced man with the cup was on the second bench. He stood when he was called, and swayed very slightly, and put his cup down on the bench beside him, and looked along the benches to his left and then to his right as if to see which way the wind was going, and then said, "Aye," rather loudly. Somebody near him laughed. He sat down and picked up his cup.

The clerk at the Crown's table wrote the last voice and added the columns and handed the sheet up to the steward, and the steward read it to the dais, and it was two parts in three and four voices over.

Four voices. Emeric kept his face bored. He did not look at Aldane.

His father came down from the dais to do it.

That was the form. The king conferred a duchy standing, on the floor, not from the chair, because a duke was a peer of the realm and was raised among his peers and not handed a thing from above. Emeric had not known that. He learned it when two of Maeve's young men appeared on either side of his stool and bowed and one of them said, "Stand, my lord, if you please, and when his grace reaches you, kneel," and he stood.

Harrold came down the three steps slowly, with a hand on the rail.

He was older. It was two summers since the day room and the mews and the hand on the back of the neck, and he had got older in a way he had not got older in the two years before that. The beard was white now, not grey. He came down the steps like a man who was thinking about each one, and at the foot of them he stopped for a moment with his hand still on the rail before he let go of it. Then he let go of it and walked out onto the floor, and his back was as straight as it had ever been, and his face was the face he had worn in the hall on the day of the file, which was nothing at all.

A clerk put the instrument into his hands, and he read it.

He read it loudly and dryly, in the voice he used for rolls, and every word of it went to the back of the hall and up into the gallery and out, Emeric had no doubt, under every door in the building. Whereas the baronies of Grayrock, Milford and Wayford lie contiguous with the barony of Kaldo and are held with it by one holder, and whereas the instruments of their holding are closed, and whereas the law of this realm is that three baronies so taken into one holding make a duchy, now therefore. He read it as a man reads a thing he has been told to read, with nothing in it of his own. He did not say my son. He did not look up from the sheet. It was the loud voice, the voice for the door, and what it said to the door was that the king was doing what the law required of him and nothing whatever besides, and that anybody who thought it was a reward had not been listening.

Aldane thought it was a reward. Emeric could see it without looking at him. It was in the way the old blood on the first bench sat, and the way nobody on that bench looked at anybody else. The third son, the wastrel, the one who had been sent south with a thin purse for insulting a foreign power at a banquet; the one who had hanged five of their kind in a market square and killed forty-one knights at a hundred paces with weavers; raised to their own rank on the floor of this hall by his father's own hand. It did not matter what the instrument said. It did not matter that the chancellor had read the law out in words of one syllable. A king had come down his own steps to make his son a duke, and the old blood saw a father and a son.

Emeric knelt when Harrold reached him.

He knelt on the stone in the light, and his father put the instrument into his left hand and a clerk put the coronet into his father's hands, a plain gold circlet with the eight low points of a duke's rank, and his father set it on his head, not gently, the way a man puts a lid on a pot. For a moment Harrold's hands were on both sides of his head, settling it.

"You kneel like her," his father said. "Too quick."

It was under the breath. It was lower than a whisper, at the edge of the ear, in the space between setting the coronet and taking his hands away. It was not in the voice he used for rolls and it was not the voice from the day room either. It was another voice altogether, dry and fond and a little amused, and Emeric had heard it before, a very long time ago, from somebody else. His father had borrowed it. For the length of five words he had spoken in his dead wife's way, the way she had spoken to both of them across a table when she was laughing at them, and then he took his hands away and stepped back, and it was gone.

"Rise, your grace," said Harrold, loudly, "and take your oath."

The oath was read to him a phrase at a time by the chancellor, and he said it back a phrase at a time, kneeling, with his hands between his father's hands. He had read it on the road. It was long and old and most of it was about land. He would hold the duchy of the Crown and in the Crown's name. He would bring the Crown his counsel when it was asked for and his arms when they were called. He would keep the king's peace in his lands and the king's law, and do justice in the king's name and in no other.

And keep the faith, the chancellor read, in his soft voice.

"And keep the faith," said Emeric.

On the east bench the cardinal's folded hands did not move. He was still looking at Emeric's face, courteously, as he had looked at it all morning, and when Emeric said the words he gave the smallest nod, as a man nods at a thing said correctly.

Keller caught him on the stair afterwards, on the way down to the yard, with Ward two steps below and Saban somewhere behind in the crowd.

It was the west stair, stone, turning, with a window at every turn, and the council was coming down it all around them, and nobody was listening to anybody. Keller fell in beside him on the inside of the turn, where the steps were narrowest, and walked down with him at his pace, with a roll under his arm, as though they had happened to leave at the same moment.

"Four voices," Keller said. He said it at the level of ordinary talk, which on a crowded stair was as good as a whisper.

"I counted."

"I know you did. I watched you do it." Keller squared the roll under his arm against his side. "You should know why it was two parts in three and not a plain majority. It isn't a courtesy. A duke sits in council for life and can only be unmade by the same count that made him, so the council does not make one lightly. It takes two parts in three to raise a duke. It takes two parts in three to unmake one." He went down two steps. "And it takes two parts in three for the council to do anything at all to the king's own person. It's the same rule. It's the oldest rule in the rolls. The council can advise a king by a plain majority. It can only act against one by two in three."

"Has it ever?"

"Twice, in four hundred years." Keller did not look at him. "Once to confine a king who had lost his wits, until he died. His lieutenant ruled for eleven years."

"Who was his lieutenant?"

"His heir. It's always the heir. It isn't written; it's custom, and it's never once been otherwise. If the king is absent from the realm, or ill, or confined, the council names a lieutenant of the realm to act for him, and the lieutenant is the heir. Everybody knows it. Nobody has needed to know it for a long time." They came round the last turn, into the light from the yard door. "I'm telling you because you're a peer now, and a peer is supposed to know the rules he votes under, and nobody else is going to tell you."

"Thank you."

"One more thing." Keller stopped at the foot of the stair, and Emeric stopped with him, and the council went round them on both sides like water round a post. "The oath. The last clause."

"Keep the faith."

"They're the king's own words. From his crowning. The duke's oath is the coronation oath, cut down: land and arms and law and faith. The first dukes swore what their king swore, so the realm would know they were bound by the same things he was." Keller's left hand squared the roll again. "Every duke on that first bench has sworn it. Aldane swore it. Your father swore the long form of it forty years ago in the cathedral. I only mention it because it's the kind of thing I notice. It's the one clause in the oath that nobody has ever been able to tell me how a man would keep, or how he'd break it."

He nodded, once, and went away across the yard toward the chancery with his roll under his arm, a narrow still man in a black coat, and did not look back.

Waylon was in the long passage that ran from the yard door to the east court, standing at a window with his hands behind his back, and Emeric did not see him until he was nearly level with him.

His youngest brother turned round from the window. He was taller than he had been at the inn, or seemed it, and broader in the shoulder, and he had grown a short beard that made him look like somebody Emeric had not met. He wore a riding coat, not a court coat. He had not been in the hall. Emeric had looked for him on the benches and in the gallery and had not found him, and had not expected to.

Ward stopped three paces back without being asked.

"Your grace," Waylon said.

"Don't."

Waylon looked at the coronet. Emeric had forgotten it was on his head. He took it off, and then did not know what to do with it, and held it.

"I heard it from the yard," Waylon said. "The aye and the no. You can hear the whole hall from the yard if you stand by the south door. I used to stand there when I was small and listen to them." He looked back out of the window. "Four over."

"Four over."

There was a silence in the passage. A servant came through it with a tray and went out the other end. Emeric stood with the coronet in his hands and looked at the side of his brother's face, which had their mother's jaw in it and nothing else of her, and tried to find the first word of any of it, and could not.

"How's the grey mare?" he said.

Waylon's mouth moved. It was not quite a smile.

"In foal," he said. "Since the spring."

"Good."

"Yes." Waylon turned from the window. He looked at Emeric for a moment, straight, as if he were about to say something else, and Emeric waited for it. It did not come. "I have to go," Waylon said. "I'm riding out this afternoon."

"Hunting?"

"No," said Waylon. "Just riding out." He went past him down the passage toward the yard, and at the door he stopped, with his hand on the frame, and did not turn round. "She'll foal about the new year. If it's a filly I'll send word."

He went out. Emeric stood in the passage with the coronet in his hands until Ward said, quietly, "Your grace," and he said, "Don't you start," and put it back on, because there was nowhere else to put it.

Saban came to the inn after dark, having gone round every tavern in the chancery lanes where a clerk might drink after a sitting, and sat down at their table in the back parlour and asked for bread and cheese and said nothing until he had eaten half of it.

"Four," he said then.

"Four."

"I thought it would be two. I had it at two all the way up the road." He cut more cheese. "You'll have seen the man on the second bench with the cup. Red face, soft coat."

"He looked both ways before he voted."

"Grier." Saban said the name as if he were entering it in a column. "Lord Grier, of somewhere with a great deal of sheep and no river. He owes everyone." He ate the cheese. "I don't mean that he's in debt. Half the benches are in debt. I mean everyone. Three of the lending houses, the vintners' company, two of the old earls, his own steward, a man in the upper market who sells saddles. I've heard his name in four taverns tonight, and every time it was somebody he owed." He wiped his knife. "A man who owes everyone votes with whoever he owes most that week. This week, apparently, it was us." He paused. "Or it was nobody, and he voted aye because the man to his left voted no and he didn't like the man to his left. It's the same thing, with Grier."

Emeric thought about the red face looking left and right before the aye.

"Write it down," he said.

"I already have," said Saban, and tapped his temple.

They left the next morning in the hired carriage, with the instrument in its case on the seat and the coronet in a box under it.

Ward did not ride on the box this time. He sat inside, across from Emeric, with his hat on his knee and his eyes on the window, and did not sleep, and did not speak, for most of the first day. Emeric let him be. He had known Ward long enough to know the difference between a silence that wanted breaking and a silence that did not, and this was the second kind, though he could not have said how he knew.

Somewhere past the third village, with Saban asleep in his corner and the road going grey and straight through the market gardens toward the south, Emeric said, "Aldane looked at you."

"Yes," said Ward.

"I thought he'd look through you. He looked at you and then away. He couldn't be bothered with a guard." Emeric turned the thought over. "It's the old blood. He'd look through a steward the same."

Ward did not answer at once. The carriage went over a rut. Saban grunted in his sleep and settled.

"That was what it was," Ward said, at last, to the window.

It was an agreement. It was said in Ward's road voice, flat, the voice he reported in. But he had waited to say it, and Ward did not wait before an agreement, and Emeric looked at him for a moment longer than he meant to, and then at the window too, and let the road go by. He put it away with the other things he would come back to. There were a great many of them by now. It was the kind of year it had been.
