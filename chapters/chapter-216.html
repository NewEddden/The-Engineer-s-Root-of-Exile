Chapter 216 - The Stream Ground

There was frost on the earthwork at first light on the twenty-sixth of November, a thin grey fur of it along the top of the bank, and the levy had been told not to lean on it because it would soak their coats through by noon. Most of them leaned on it anyway. It was the only thing on the stream ground higher than a man's knee.

Emeric stood on the firing step behind the middle of it with his helm under his arm and looked north across what Linus had given them.

Linus had walked it with his chain in the second week of the month and come back to Sethe's counting room with a roll of paper and the face of a man who has found a coin in a drain. North of Harrow Bridge the country was open grazing, a gift to anybody with a thousand horses. Except here. Here the stream came down out of the hills and bent east, and the meadow on its north side ran down to it between two things a horse did not like: on the west a wood of old alders standing in their own black water, and on the east a beech hanger on a bank too steep for a horse, with a tongue of reed bed at its foot that looked like pasture from the saddle and was not. Linus had put his boot through it to the knee and lost the boot.

"It's a funnel," Linus had said, at the table, as if apologising for it. "Anything that comes down that meadow at speed comes down narrower than it started. It can't help it. The ground does it."

So the earthwork stood on the south bank of the stream, across the neck, three hundred and eighty yards of it from the alders to the reeds: a ditch on the stream side, the spoil thrown up behind into a bank as high as a man's chest, a firing step cut along the back. Three hundred men had dug it in five days, in the rain, and Ward had walked it every evening with a stick and made them dig again the bits he did not like. At the east end it gave out into sixty yards of soft ground before the reeds began. Ward had looked at that gap for a whole evening and said that it would do, because he had six smoke pots and the wind was in the west.

Across the meadow, on the rising ground at the hill foot, Aldane's host had been coming in since the day before.

Emeric had felt it come. A mile and a half off, a great low warm smear of notes along the hill foot like a town seen from a ridge at night; the bright hard points that were knights; and in the middle of the host, in carts, close together, a long row of thinner, brighter points that burned in his sense like coals raked into a line. Stones. Too many to count.

He knew what they were. Everybody on the earthwork knew. Saban's man had counted the closed carts going north in October, and Ward had counted them again in his head, and they had all stood in the quarry every week since and been blind together.

Ward came along the firing step from the west end, in his plate, without his helm, with Declan behind him and a sheaf of papers in his hand.

"Highness."

"Ward."

"They have been bringing up the carts since before light." Ward did not look at the hill foot. He had looked at it all night. "Fourteen men in grey went along the line of them an hour ago. I saw them with the glass."

"Fourteen," said Emeric. He had felt them. Fourteen bright thin notes, walking. Fourteen men with the faculty and no banner. "Hired."

"Yes. From the coast, Saban thinks. Men who break stones for money and do not stay for the rest." Ward held out the papers. "The order. You have read it. I want it read along the bank before they start."



Ward had written it the night before, by a lamp in the tent at the back, in his small square hand, on six sheets of the school's paper, one for each company; and Declan had copied the six again so that there would be twelve, because Ward did not trust a thing that existed only once. Emeric had read it at midnight. It was short. Ward's orders were always short, and they never said anything twice, and they never said anything a man had to work out.

It said that the stones would break and the field would go dead, theirs and Kaldo's alike, and that nobody on the bank was to look for help from a stone, or a band, or a glyph, or His Highness, because there would be none. Every order would be given by voice, and the volleys by the drum at the centre: one beat for the first rank, two for the second, three for the third, and a man who could not hear the drum was to fire when the man on his left fired. The first volley at the white stakes, three hundred paces, and no sooner. The cups at the red stakes and not before. No hand egg unless a horse was in the ditch. No man over the bank for any reason, for a friend, for a horse, for a banner, for money, unless his officer went first; and when they broke, nobody was to follow. And at the end: that every man on the bank had done this forty times in a stone hole north of Kaldo with a rock for an enemy, and that today the rock would be on a horse, and that was the only difference.

The company officers were to read it to their own men. Ward had said so the night before. And Osric had the third company, at the centre, where Emeric was, a hundred yards of bank and fifty of the levy kneeling behind it; the company with Arthur in it, and the cooper's boy from the river street, and a carter from the vat road with a beard he had been growing since August to look older.

Osric took the sheet from Declan without looking at it. He was not wearing his helm either. He had a cold, and his nose was red, and he stood on the firing step in his plate with the paper in his gauntlet and the fifty of them looking up at him from the frozen mud, and Emeric, who had stood on a dark landing outside a drafting-room window four weeks before, found that he had stopped breathing.

Osric looked at the paper.

"Third company," he said. "This is the captain's order. Listen."

And he read it.

He read it slowly, the way a man walks on ice he has walked on before, putting his weight down on each word and trusting it. But he did not stop. He did not guess. He did not say "and so on" and fold the paper and give them the gist, which was what everybody on that bank who had ever served under Osric had seen him do with a written order, and what half of them were waiting for now. When he came to "theirs and Kaldo's alike," the carter with the beard said "Ha," loudly, and somebody hit him. When he came to the drum, one beat and two and three, a boy at the end of the line counted along under his breath, the way children do in a school room.

And when he came to the line about the friend, the horse, the banner and the money, he read it, and somebody in the second rank shouted, "Not even money, Sergeant?" and Osric looked up from the paper and said, "Especially not money, Simkin, I've seen you play dice," and went back down to the paper and found his place without hunting for it, and went on.

They cheered that. Then they cheered the next line, which did not deserve it. By the end they were cheering every line as he finished it, fifty men on their knees in the frost banging rifle butts on the frozen ground, not because the lines were stirring, because Ward's lines were never stirring, but because it was cold and they were frightened and a big red-nosed man with a cold was standing over them reading out loud as steady as a priest, and it was something to shout at that was not the hill foot.

"Today the rock will be on a horse," read Osric, "and that is the only difference." He folded the paper and put it inside his breastplate. "That's the captain's. Here's mine. Don't shoot the sergeant."

They cheered that too.

Rylan came along the firing step from the fourth company, with his helm on the back of his head and his eyebrows up as high as they would go.

"Osric."

"What."

"Where in all the hells did that come from?"

Osric blew his nose on a rag. "Where did what come from?"

"That. You. Reading. All the way through, at a pace a man could follow, with nobody holding your finger on the line." Rylan looked at him with real wonder. "I've watched you take a pay sheet to Cade and ask him what the bottom figure was. In this very barony. Last spring."

"I was checking his figures." Osric put the rag away. "I've always read like that."

"You have not."

"Always," said Osric. "You never listened."

Rylan turned to appeal to somebody, and the nearest was Declan, a yard off with the copies under his arm, who had served with Osric for nine years and heard him read out loud perhaps a dozen times in all of them, every one a disaster.

Declan looked at Rylan. He looked at Osric. He looked, last and briefly, at Emeric, who had his face under very good control.

"He's always read like that," said Declan, and went off down the step to the second company with his papers.



The first stone broke a little after eight.

Emeric was watching the hill foot with his sense, because a mile and a half was too far for eyes. He felt the fourteen thin notes go out from the host in a line, each with a cart behind it, and stop, spread across the meadow at intervals of forty or fifty yards. He felt one of them take a stone out of the cart and hold it. He felt the stone fill.

The wind that moved nothing came down the meadow from the hill foot toward him. He felt it come like a draught under a door; and it did not reach him. It stopped. A mile and a half was too far for one stone, even a big one. But behind where it had stopped, a piece of the world had gone out, a round patch of blank in the far end of the meadow with a thin note standing in the middle of it, and the stones in the other carts all round it still burning.

"One," said Emeric, out loud, to nobody.

Then another, forty yards west of the first. Then three together. Then they were all at it.

It came toward him in pieces. Every crack put out a patch of the world, and the patches ran together, and the grey men walked forward across the blank ground they had made and broke more, and the edge came down the meadow toward the stream like a tide coming in over flats. He felt the hill foot go. He felt Aldane's host go dark, the knights' hard points and the horses' warm ones, going out a handful at a time like lamps along a street at closing. The white stakes. He felt the knights in the front rank go blank and could not tell whether they had felt it too.

"They knew," said Ward, beside him. He had come back along the step without Emeric feeling him come. "Every charm on their own saddles. Every charged edge. They bought the stones in October and they have known for a month that this would take it all off them as well as us." He looked out across the meadow. "They do not need charms. They have a thousand horses and men who have ridden since they could walk. We have farmers." He was quiet. "That is the bet."

The red stakes went. The ditch in front of the earthwork. The far bank of the stream.

And then the last of it came over the bank, and the wind went through Emeric, and through Ward and Osric and the fifty men of the third company kneeling in the frost, and on behind them south toward Harrow Bridge; and the world went flat.

It went flat everywhere. In the quarry there had always been the rim, with the lamps and the world waiting beyond it; on the north road there had been a road out. Here there was no rim. He pushed his sense out from habit, the way a man in the dark puts out his hand, and it went nowhere. Not as far as Ward's arm. Not as far as his own boots. Three hundred men, a thousand horses, a whole countryside, and in his head one grey flat silence with nothing in it but himself.

The hearts behind his breastbone went cold. The stones in his suit, under the plate. The bead on the band at his wrist. The thin line to Ward that he had kept open for three years without thinking about it, gone, like a rope cut at the far end.

And down the bank, along the firing step, on forty forearms under forty coat sleeves, forty glyphs went dead, and forty young men looked down at their own arms.

A quarter of an hour, Declan said afterward; he had had his watch out, an old brass one that ran on a spring and did not care. Fourteen men, a quarter of an hour, and every cart along the hill foot empty. Emeric had stopped counting the cracks at two hundred and twelve, when they began to come too fast to count, and Ward's guess, from the size of the carts and how many had come north, was three hundred. Nobody ever knew exactly. Nobody on that field could feel them any more.

He put his helm on.

It came down over his head with its smell of oil and old sweat, and the world shrank to a slot. Eyes, ears and the drill; and the thing a dead field could not reach, his own mana turned inward into his arms and legs like a knight's, quiet and warm. He let it come up a little. He did not know yet how long the day would be.

"Good," said Ward. "Now they come."



They did not come at once.

That was the first thing nobody had drilled. In the quarry the stone broke and Ward said "first rank" in the same minute. Here the stones broke and then nothing happened for the better part of an hour while Aldane's host sorted itself at the hill foot, and three hundred men knelt in the frost, blind, with nothing to do with their hands.

Somebody on his right was praying, in a whisper, the same three lines over and over. Somebody else was eating. The carter with the beard had his rifle across his knees and was taking his cartridges out of his pouch one at a time and counting them and putting them back, and then taking them out and counting them again. Arthur, three men down, was sitting very still with his left sleeve pushed up, looking at the glyph on his forearm, black and dull, with nothing in it.

"It'll come back," said the man next to him. "Rylan said."

"I know," said Arthur.

"Tomorrow. When it seeps in."

"I know." Arthur pulled his sleeve down. "I wasn't going to use it anyway. Ward said."

Along at the east end Tobe and two works boys were setting the six smoke pots out on the soft ground across the gap, squat and black, wicks covered against the damp. Fennick was with them, which he was not supposed to be; his powder cart was a hundred yards back behind the third company. He was laughing at something Tobe had said. Emeric could see his maimed hand moving.

And behind every third man on the step, leaning against the bank with the others, a rifle with a squat iron cup screwed onto the end of its muzzle like a thimble on a finger; and at each of those men's knees, in a straw-lined basket, five eggs.

The levy called them eggs. They were the size of a big man's fist and the colour of a stove, with a ring and a pin at the top, and they looked like something you would use to hold a door open. Emeric had drawn the shell, and the scoring inside that made it break where it was told to, and the cup. That was all. Fennick had looked at Tobe's smoke pots and asked what would happen with powder in one instead of smoke. Bram had cast six hundred in the open foundry in under a fortnight. Tess had made the striker out of one of her own primers and a spring and a pin, so that a man could pull the ring in the rain, in a dead field, and five counts later it would do what it did without a match or a stone. And Ward had thrown them in the quarry and written a page on when to use them, which he called doctrine and everybody else called Ward's page.

Out beyond the stream, a horn.



The horse came down off the hill foot at a walk, as horse always did, because a horse that started at a gallop a mile and a half off would be blown before it got anywhere. Emeric watched it come and could not stop watching it.

It came on a front of most of half a mile, wide as the meadow at the hill foot, in three long lines with the banners in the first. Knights in the first line, as many as could fit; the lances and men-at-arms and the hired riders behind. A walk, then nearer the middle of the meadow a trot, and a sound like thunder very far off that did not end.

Nearly a thousand, Ward had said, after the march and the remounts. From the bank it looked like the whole world. Behind it, on the hill foot, Aldane's foot stayed where it was, a long dark block of men-at-arms and baronial levies with bills, waiting to come down and finish what was left.

Emeric found Sallow's banner by eye, after a while. A grey horse's head on blue, near the left of the first line, with a big man under it on a grey. Next to it, on a bay, a slimmer man with the same banner on his pennon. Bastian. He was sure, and then not sure. It was a long way.

The front was narrowing.

He saw it begin where the alders came out on the right and the reeds on the left. The men on the far wings saw the wet ground ahead, or their horses did, and edged inward, and the ones they edged into edged inward too. Half a mile wide became a third, then a quarter. Three loose lines became five, knee to knee, the wings riding up behind the centre because there was nowhere else to go. He had seen water do that, coming into a sluice.

"Linus," said somebody on the step, in a kind of awe. Emeric thought it was Declan.

The trot became a canter. At the white stakes it would be a gallop.

"Steady," said Osric, along the line. He was walking behind the third company, up and down, with his helm on now and his sword still sheathed and his voice not loud. "Steady. Rifles up. Nobody fires till the drum. If you fire before the drum I'll make you dig the ditch again by yourself."

The rifles came up onto the bank, fifty of them in the third company, three hundred along the whole length; first rank kneeling at the bank, second rank standing behind, third rank behind them, with the cup men in all three. Emeric heard the breech blocks close, a long ragged clicking all along the earthwork like crickets. Somebody's did not close. Somebody swore. It closed.

He did not have a rifle. Ward had put him at the centre beside the drum, with the cup men of the third company to his right and left, to count. That was his place in the order. In the quarry he had always been the one who counted, because in the quarry he could not do anything else, and the cup men had learned to throw on his count; and Ward did not change a thing that worked.

The front came to the white stakes.

The drum went once.

The first rank fired. A hundred rifles, all along the bank, not together. He heard it go from the centre outward, a long tearing crack like canvas ripping, the west end a heartbeat late, and the smoke came up white along the whole top of the earthwork and lay there in front of his eyes, and through the gaps in it, three hundred paces off, he saw horses go down.

Not many. Not as many as there should have been. Three hundred paces was a long way for a farmer, even a farmer with a good rifle who had shot at a painted rock forty times. A horse here. A horse there. A man coming out of a saddle with his arms up. The front came on through them without slowing, and the horses behind went over the fallen ones or round them.

The drum went twice. The second rank fired, standing, over the heads of the first. More horses. He could hear the first rank reloading, the breech blocks dropping, brass ringing out onto the step, a hundred men doing a thing with their thumbs they had done ten thousand times in a stone hole; and some of them dropped the cartridges and scrabbled for them in the frozen mud, and Osric's voice went past behind them saying, "Leave it, take another, leave it."

Three. The wind took the smoke east, off down the line toward the reeds, as Ward had said it would, and he could see again. The front was at two hundred paces and galloping, and it was not a front any more. The meadow had squeezed it into a column three hundred yards wide and nobody could say how deep; the horses at the edges were in the reeds to their hocks or among the alders' black water, and everything left was coming down the dry middle packed so close he could not see grass between them.

One. The first rank again. He could not see what it did. There was too much smoke.

The red stakes were at a hundred and fifty paces. He had walked out with Ward and set them himself, three days before, with a measuring rope, and he knew where they were by the smoke clearing off them for a breath and a white shape of a fallen horse beside one.

"Cups," he said. And then louder, because his voice had come out small. "Cups!"

The cup men knelt with their rifles angled up over the bank, butts on the frozen ground, blanks in the breech, eggs in the cups since the white stakes. A hundred along the bank. He heard the pins come out, a hundred small dry clicks, and the primers under them going off with a snap like a dry twig, so small in all that noise that only the man holding the egg could have heard it. Then the hiss.

"One," said Emeric.

They fired. The blanks went off with a flat bang quite different from a ball, and the eggs went up out of the cups and over the bank and out into the smoke, black, turning, a hundred of them, very slowly, it seemed, too slowly, up against the grey sky and over.

"Two," said Emeric. "Three."

A cup man to his right had not fired. Emeric saw it from the side of his slot. A boy, a farmer's boy with no beard at all, kneeling with his rifle up and his hand on the trigger and his face white, staring at the egg sitting in the cup above his own head with its fuse hissing in it and smoke coming out round the rim. He had pulled the pin and then his hand had stopped.

"Four."

The man behind the boy reached over his shoulder and pulled the trigger for him. The blank went off. The egg went up, short, much too short, and came down in the ditch in front of the bank, forty feet off, in the water.

"Five," said Emeric.

The meadow blew up.

That was what it sounded like, from the bank. Not a hundred bangs. One long one, a hundred and fifty paces off, rolling up and down the length of the funnel from the alders to the reeds; and under it, in the ditch, close, one short wet one, and mud and water came up over the bank and down on the third company in a dirty rain. Something sang over Emeric's helm and struck the turf behind him.

And the sound of the horses changed.

The smoke from the eggs came back on the wind, grey and dirtier than the rifle smoke. Through it he saw the front. Where the eggs had come down, it had stopped being a crowd: horses down in heaps of three and four, horses on their sides kicking, horses standing with their heads down and their riders gone, horses running back up the meadow with the stirrups flying. And behind them the second and third lines, riding knee to knee with nowhere to go but forward.

Some of them could not stop. They went into the heaps and over. Some of them could, and pulled up, and the ones behind them could not, and rode into them.

"Cups," said Emeric. He found his voice was quite steady. He did not know why. "Second egg. Pins."

They had practised it forty times. Spent cases out, new blanks in, a second egg from the basket into the cup. The boy who had frozen had dropped his basket, and two of his eggs were rolling about the step among the brass with their pins in, and he was on his knees grabbing for them, and the man behind him had him by the collar.

"Leave them," said Emeric. "Leave them. Take one."

The boy took one. His hands were shaking so much it rattled in the cup.

"Pins," said Emeric. "One."

The second throw was ragged. Some of the cup men along the bank had had time to load and some had not; it went up in pieces, thirty, then forty, then a long straggle of the rest. He counted the centre's. The bursts came down in a long rolling uneven line, further back this time, where the second and third lines were piling into the first, and the screaming went up again.

The drum. One. Two. Three. The rifles were firing on the drum again, and he heard the drum going steadily on its own behind him, and realised that he had stopped hearing it, and that it had been going all the time.



Some of them got through.

Not many. Afterward, when they walked it, Declan said fewer than a hundred, of near a thousand; and of those, not more than thirty or forty ever came close enough to the bank to matter. But at the time, from the step, it seemed to Emeric that the smoke in front of the ditch was full of horses.

They came out of it at a gallop in twos and threes, the ones who had been at the very front when the eggs came down and had ridden on through, because they had been riding forward all their lives. Knights, most of them, with their lances down and nobody beside them, coming on as if the rest were still there.

The rifles took most of them. At a hundred paces even a farmer could hit a horse. A big bay went down at fifty paces with all four legs folding under it at once and threw its rider over its head into the stream, and the rider did not get up out of the water; he could not get up. He lay in the stream on his back in his plate with the water going over his face and his arms moving, slowly, like a beetle on its back, and he did not get up.

"Hand eggs," said Ward's voice, far down the bank to the west. "There. Horse in the ditch. Hand eggs."

A grey came out of the smoke right in front of the third company, a knight on it, gold on blue, and went down the near bank of the stream and through it, the water flying up white round its chest, and up out of it at the ditch, and the ditch was too wide and too deep and the grey went into it with its forelegs and came down and the knight came off over its neck against the face of the bank, four feet below Emeric's boots.

He was still alive. He was getting up, in the bottom of the ditch, in the mud and water, with his sword in his hand; he had his feet under him, he had his hand on the turf of the bank, he was coming up. A knight in a dead field kept what was inside him. This one had a great deal inside him. He came up the face of the bank in his plate as if it were a stair.

The carter with the beard stood up on the step and threw an egg at him from three feet away.

It hit the knight on the helm and bounced off and fell into the ditch behind him. The carter had not pulled the pin.

Emeric was already moving. He did not decide to. He was on top of the bank with his own strength up in his arms and legs, a good deal of it now, and the knight's sword came round at him and he did not see it come. He heard it. In the quarry Ward had made them do this blindfold with wooden swords until they stopped waiting to see and started listening for the scrape of a boot and the breath going in; and he heard the breath go in and the boot slip on the turf and put his arm up where the sound said the sword would be, and it was. It took him on the vambrace and drove his arm down against his own helm, and the arm went numb to the shoulder.

He took hold of the knight by the edge of his breastplate with his other hand and lifted him off the bank and threw him back down into the ditch.

"Now," said Emeric to the carter, who was standing with his mouth open. "Pull the pin. Now throw it. Not at him. Past him."

The carter pulled the pin, and stood there holding it.

"Throw it."

The carter threw it, high, over the knight and over the stream, into the meadow beyond where three more horses were coming. Emeric counted it out loud. On five it went off among them, short, under their feet, and two of the horses went down and the third turned, screaming, and went off sideways along the stream into the alders with its rider hanging off one side.

The carter sat down on the step all at once, as if his legs had been taken away, and was sick between his knees. Emeric left him there.

His arm had come back. It hurt. He found that he did not much care.



The east end went worse than the middle.

He did not see it. He pieced it together after, from Rylan and Tobe and from Ward's own account two days later, a page long with nothing in it that was not true. When the eggs broke the front, some of Aldane's right wing, the men who had been edging in from the reeds, saw the end of the bank and the open ground past it and went for it, because it was the one place on the whole front where nobody was firing at them from behind turf. Forty horse, perhaps fifty, with the fourth company firing into their flank.

Tobe lit the pots.

These were the old pots from the summer, made before Tess's strikers, and they wanted fire; a slow-match in a tin was fire as good as any in a dead field. Tobe ran along the line west to east, and Fennick, who should have been at the powder cart, ran behind him and lit the ones that did not catch. Rylan said he laughed all the way, a breath of it at each pot before it went, as if the pot had told him a joke. The six went up in a thick white wall that the west wind rolled straight out across the gap and the reed bed, and the horse that came round the end of the bank rode into it blind.

They did not know where the gap was any more. Most of them found the reeds. Rylan said you could hear them going in, horse after horse, at a canter, and stopping.

A dozen found the gap and came through it into the back of the fourth company in the smoke, and that was where most of Kaldo's dead were made. The rear rank turned and fired at ten paces and dropped four. The rest were in among the levy before they could load again.

Rylan and the twelve garrison knights at the east end went at them on foot. They had nothing on their blades and nothing in them but what was inside and Bram's plate; but neither did the men on the horses, and the men on the horses had ridden a mile and a half and been blown half off their feet already, and Rylan had been sitting on a powder keg all morning eating an apple. He said that afterward, to make it smaller. It was not small. One of the garrison died of it before dark. Six of the fourth company were killed in the smoke by men they could not see, before it was finished; one of the second company by a ball from somewhere, nobody knew where; and at the centre, the boy who had frozen with his egg, by a lance in the throat from a knight who came up the bank out of the ditch while Emeric was on the far side of the drum. The man behind the boy shot the knight through the visor from a yard away.

Nine, by evening. Nine of Kaldo's. Emeric knew all their names by nightfall. Ward read them out.



The men-at-arms never came.

He kept waiting for them all through it, because that was what foot did, and what Ward had built the bank for. When the smoke cleared enough to see the hill foot, the long dark block had not moved.

Then it moved. Backward.

He saw it begin at the edges: a few men drifting back up the slope toward the wagons, then a few more, then a whole block of somebody's levy with their bills turning round at once and walking off over the crest, not running, like men going home from a market. Men on horses rode along the front shouting with their arms up. It did not help. By the time what was left of the horse came back up the meadow, riderless horses first and then men on foot leading them, half the foot had gone, and the other half was going.

They had watched it from a mile and a half off: three hundred farmers who never stood up, a long white smoke along a bank, and then a black cloud coming down out of the sky into the best horse in the north. Nobody on that hill had seen anything like it. Emeric did not blame them for going home.

"Cease," said Ward, down the bank. "Cease fire. Third rank, stand down. First rank, keep loaded." The drum stopped. Emeric had not known the drum was still going. "Nobody goes over."

And nobody went over. Except one.



Jabari had stood at the west end all morning, in the second company, among the garrison knights, in the plate Bram had made him.

He had come south with Isolde in the middle of the month, when her father's banner went up with Aldane's, and asked to be put in the line, and Ward had looked at him and said yes. Bram had fitted him in a week: a knight's harness of forge steel, blackened, with nothing in it that needed a stone, lighter and harder than anything in the north. Jabari had tested it on the first day by taking it off and hitting the breastplate with a hammer, and said, "Good."

When the horse broke, a knot of it had come down the west side by the alders and been stopped short of the stream by the second company's volleys and a cup egg, and a grey horse with a blue saddlecloth had gone down rolling seventy yards from the bank and thrown its rider clear onto dry ground. A big man in good plate, with a grey horse's head on his shield. He got up, which most of them had not, and stood in the meadow with his sword out while the horse ran.

And Jabari had said something to Ward that nobody heard, and Ward had nodded once, and Jabari had gone over the bank.

The order said no man was to go over unless his officer went first. Ward had gone up onto the bank with him and stood on top of it, in plain sight, in the meadow's eye. That was his going first. He did not go any further. He stood up there with his arms folded and watched.

Emeric heard about it from four people and saw the end of it himself, when somebody shouted and the whole of the third company turned their heads west along the bank. So he saw it in pieces, with the smoke blowing across it: two men in the meadow among the dead horses, on foot, one in blackened plate and one in bright, fighting.

It was slow. Both of them had been up since before dawn, one of them had come off a horse at a gallop, and in the dead air neither had anything but his own body, in plate, in a meadow of frozen ruts and dead horses and egg holes. They circled. The swords rang and they came apart. Sallow was the bigger and heavier, thirty years a knight; he swung hard and wide and meant every blow, and twice Jabari gave ground. Once he slipped. Emeric saw him go down on one knee behind a dead horse and Sallow come over the horse at him with the sword high, and heard the whole third company make one noise.

Jabari was not where the sword came down. He had rolled, in full plate, round the horse's head; and when Sallow turned to find him, the big man's foot went into the mud of an egg hole, to the shin, and stuck.

Jabari had walked that meadow. Every man in Kaldo's line had walked it, in the three days before, with Linus and his stakes. He had known exactly where the soft ground was.

He put Sallow down. Not with the edge. With the pommel, on the side of the helm, once, and then with his whole weight, so that the big man went over backward into the mud with one foot still stuck and the sword gone out of his hand somewhere into the dead grass. And then Jabari stood over him, with his own sword point at the gap under Sallow's gorget, and stood there, and did nothing else.

The third company had started to cheer, and stopped.

Sallow lay on his back in his plate, in the mud. He was trying to get up. He could not get up. Nobody in that meadow who had come down in plate on soft ground had got up without help all morning; there was a knight thirty yards from him who had been trying since the first volley. He moved his arms. He moved his head. He said something, up at the man standing over him, that nobody on the bank could hear.

Jabari sheathed his sword. He bent, and took Lord Aurel Sallow by both wrists, and pulled; and when the stuck foot would not come, he put his own boot down in the mud beside it and pulled again, harder, until it came out with a sound they heard on the bank; and he hauled his wife's father up onto his feet in the middle of the meadow, among the dead horses, alive, and stood holding him up by one arm because the man could not yet stand by himself.

Then he walked him back to the bank.

It took a long time. Sallow walked like a man much older than he was. At the ditch Jabari lowered him in and climbed down after him and pushed him up the face of the bank, and two men of the second company leaned over and lifted him across. When the helm came off his face was grey, blood running out of his hair, and his eyes went from one face to the next as if looking for somebody he knew.

Jabari climbed up after him, slowly, and stood on the step with his sword arm hanging and got his breath.

"Is he hurt?" said Ward.

"His head," said Jabari. "And his foot, I think. The ankle." He got his breath again. "She'd not have wanted him dead."

"No," said Ward.

"I'd not have wanted to tell her," said Jabari, and went and sat down on a powder keg, and did not say anything else for an hour.



Somebody saw the ship at about two in the afternoon.

It was one of the cooper's boy's mates, looking south because he could not look north any more. Far off beyond Harrow Bridge, beyond the end of the dead air, a small grey shape high against the cloud, standing still where nothing that size should stand still. It had been there since morning. Ward had put it there, with Cade at the glass and a written order that it was not to come closer for any reason, not for the wounded, not for him; and Emeric had signed under Ward's name, because the rule was his before it was Ward's. No ship over a fight. Nothing dropped from one.

The levy knew what it was. Aldane's men did not. Emeric thought about that later. A great many of them, walking back over the crest of the hill foot in the early afternoon with their bills over their shoulders and their lords shouting behind them, must have looked back once at the meadow and seen, far off past the bank and the smoke, a thing hanging in the sky over the south road. Grey. Still. Watching. It had done nothing. It did not need to.



Declan had the count by dusk.

He brought it to the tent at the back with his brass watch in one hand and a sheet in the other, and he read it standing, as Ward read things, without anything in his voice.

Aldane's dead, in the meadow and the ditch and the stream and the reeds and the alders, counted by lantern by four parties of the levy: about four hundred. Declan said about; some were still in the reed bed and would be till the frost hardened it. A hundred and eighty-eight of them were knights. That number he knew exactly, because a knight could be told by his plate and his spurs and his shield, and Ward had had them counted separately for the heralds.

Taken alive, and held in the barn behind the earthwork under the garrison's guard: thirty-one knights, Lord Aurel Sallow among them, for ransom by the custom. Some hundreds of horses caught loose in the meadow. Wounded men of Aldane's on foot and in the reeds, more than Declan could count yet, being carried in to Imani's tent at Harrow Bridge on hurdles by men of the levy who had been shooting at them that morning.

Kaldo's dead, nine. Kaldo's wounded, twenty-three, of whom Imani thought two would lose a hand and one an eye. None of them had bled past what a dressing could hold. The register's camp list and the white dish stayed shut in her box, where she had sworn to keep them until her rats said otherwise.

Declan put the sheet on the table.

Nobody said anything. Rylan was sitting on the end of a cot with his helm between his feet, looking at it. Osric was in the doorway. Ward stood at the table with his hands flat on either side of the sheet, reading it again, and when he got to the bottom he read it again from the top.

"Three stones," said Ward at last. To nobody. "Next time I will drill with three."

That was all he said about it. Then he took a pen and wrote the nine names at the bottom of the sheet, in his small square hand, one under another, and gave it back to Declan to copy twice.



Emeric went out onto the field after dark.

He did not tell anybody he was going. Ward saw him take the lantern and did not stop him. His sense was still gone; it would not come back till the next day, when the mana had seeped in again from the earth and the edges, and he had stopped reaching for it, mostly. Now and then he still put it out without meaning to, like a man feeling for a step that is not there.

So he walked it with his eyes.

He went over the bank and down through the ditch and across the stream by the stepping stones, out into the meadow. The frost was coming down again. It lay on the dead horses already, white along their flanks. The levy had laid the knights out in rows near the stream with their shields on their chests, for the heralds, and the others where they could. Powder and blood and the insides of horses, and the stream running through all of it, quite loud, the same as it had run that morning.

He had thought he might feel what he had seen on Ward's face in the tent, or Rylan's: a tired flatness with a job at the end of it. He had thought, if he was honest, that he might feel it had worked. It had. Linus's ground had squeezed them, the rifles had broken them, the eggs had come down where they were meant to, and every fuse and striker had worked in a dead field in the cold. Three hundred farmers against the best horse in the north, and nine of them dead.

He did not feel any of that. He walked along the row of knights with the lantern, looking at their faces, and what he felt was the cellar.

He did not know at first why. There was nothing here like the cellar. Nobody had been strapped down; every man in this meadow had ridden into it on his own horse with a sword in his hand. But it sat in him in the same place, under the breastbone where the stones were cold. The cellar had been done by somebody else, and he had walked into it afterward and counted. This he had drawn. The shell and the scoring and the cup, on a winter evening in the long room with a cup of Maren's tea going cold, passed across the table to Fennick.

About halfway along the row, a boy.

He was in good plate, very good, too good for a boy; a squire's kit made over into a knight's by somebody with money. He had come off near the stream and the horse had come down on him, and the levy had got the horse off and laid him out with the others, and the frost was on his hair. His shield had a device Emeric did not know, a white hart on green with a bar across it, for a younger branch. He could not have been twenty.

"Harlow," said a voice behind him.

Emeric turned with the lantern. One of the taken knights stood at the edge of the light with two of the levy on either side of him, a big grey-haired man with his arm in a sling of somebody's belt. He had asked to come out, the levy said, to see to his own; Declan had let him, under guard.

"His name's Harlow," the knight said. He was not looking at Emeric. He was looking at the boy. "His father's His Grace's cousin. On the mother's side. He was nineteen in September. He wasn't supposed to be in the first line." He was quiet. "He asked. They let him. His Grace said it was safe. He said the farmers wouldn't stand."

Emeric said nothing.

"I'll take him home," said the knight, "if I'm let. When the ransom's paid. His mother'll want him home." And then, as if it had only just come to him, he looked at Emeric properly for the first time, at his face in the lantern light and the plate and the cut on his vambrace, and Emeric saw him understand who he was talking to. He did not say anything else. He did not need to.

"You'll be let," said Emeric.

He stood there a while longer. Then he went on along the row, to the end, because he had started it, and counted them, a hundred and eighty-eight, all the way to the alders. It came out the same as Declan's. He had known it would.



Saban came up from Harrow Bridge at nine, on a hired horse, with his coat buttoned to the throat and mud to his knees.

He had been at the posting house below the bridge all day, outside the dead air, with his own men and horses. He sat down on the firing step next to Emeric without being asked.

"Three riders went north from the hill foot before two o'clock that I know of," he said. "There'll be more. Two of mine went after them, to the capital and to Wayford, with Ferra's words for the gazette and Declan's count." He looked out at the lanterns moving in the meadow. "Mine won't get there first. A man running from a thing rides faster than a man carrying news of it."

"How long?"

"To the capital? Four days on the post. Three if they kill horses." Saban turned his hat round in his hands. "It won't need the gazette. Every house in the north will have it by word of mouth first, from a man who saw it from the hill foot and has had a week to make it worse. I've been sitting in that posting house all day trying to work out what today's worth." He looked at the meadow. "A hundred and eighty-eight knights. In a morning. To farmers. There isn't a man at court who'll hear that and go back to his dinner."

"No."

"They'll be frightened," said Saban. "That's what it's worth. The old ones will be frightened and want it stopped, and the young ones will be frightened and want it for themselves, and whoever's been waiting for a better year to do whatever he was going to do to us will look at the calendar and decide it's this one." He put his hat on. "I'd sooner they were frightened than brave. Brave men you can't price. But I'd not stand too near any of them for a while."

He got up, and went off along the bank to find Ward, and left Emeric sitting on the step.

Out in the meadow, the levy parties were still working. He could see their lanterns moving among the dead horses, small and yellow, stopping and bending and going on; and he could hear them calling to one another, a name now and then, and the stream. Down the bank to his left, in the dark, somebody of the third company was laughing at something, too loudly, and then stopped. Further along, a man was reading out loud, slowly, by a lamp, from a letter, to two or three others who were listening. It sounded like Osric.

The frost came down on the meadow and on the bank and on the rows by the stream.

Somewhere about midnight, without any warning, a little of the world came back into him at the edges: a horse, very faint, far off to the south, and then a man asleep in the barn, and then nothing again. He sat still and waited for the rest. It came back slowly, all night, a note at a time, the way the dead field had come down on them that morning in reverse; and every note it gave him back was somebody who had lived.
