Chapter 191 - Steel Only

The yard had been raked in the night.

He saw it from the top of the stair in the grey before dawn: the long square of sand combed smooth from wall to wall in fine straight lines, every scuff of yesterday gone, the rack of wooden swords under its roof at the far end and two blades laid out on a cloth on a bench in front of it, steel, side by side. The drop on the open side went down to the harbor in the dark, and the lamps of the anchored ships were still lit on the water, and the mountain's plume stood up grey against a sky that was only beginning to be lighter than the mountain.

Her knights were along the walls. Not forty; a dozen, in plain coats, with their arms folded, the grey knight among them. Ward came down the stair behind Emeric and went along the north wall without a word and stood with them, a few feet from the grey knight, and folded his own arms. Behind Ward the rest of the Kaldo party came down in ones and twos and stood where the duchy's people made room for them: Saban with his collar up, Fletcher, Vantic. Ferra and Imani together, at the end, by the stair foot. Ferra had her book. Of course she had.

Rowena was at the bench.

She was in a quilted jack like a common man-at-arms', grey, laced up the side, and an open helm under her arm, and plain boots, and her hair in its ringed tail tucked down inside the back of the jack where nobody could take hold of it. No plate. No cloak. She picked up one of the blades and held it out to him across the bench, hilt first.

He took it.

A war blade's weight and a war blade's length, a hand and a half, with the edges ground off round and the point rolled back on itself, so that it would break a bone and not open a vein. He had held one before. He had held one in Ward's yard every morning for two years. He had held one, a long time before that, much too heavy for him, in a yard in his father's castle, with this woman's hand on his.

"Steel only," said Rowena.

"Steel only."

"No casting. No knight's push in the legs. No sense." She put on the helm and did up its strap under her chin. "I'll know."

"I know you will."

He shut his sense.

All of it. Not the half shutting he did at night, to let a house sleep without him in it; the whole of it, down to nothing, the way he had shut it in the dead zone over the north road in November, except that this time it was his own hand on the door. The yard went flat. The people along the walls became shapes in the half dark with faces, and nothing else; no notes, no weight, nothing going on behind any of them that he could feel. Rowena became a woman in a grey jack with a sword. He stood in a yard he could only see, and it was the strangest he had felt in years, and he made himself breathe through it until it was only a yard.

He had read it last night. He still had that. He had filed it on the way up to dinner without meaning to, and the file was there, in his head, where his sense had put it: a hand's depth of sharp sand, and clay under it, and a drain corner to corner, north-west to south-east, settled in the middle, a long trough a finger lower than the rest that the rake had combed over and that nobody would see. And by the east wall, the soft place, the size of a cart bed, over the old cistern, where the clay was wet.

That was all he was going to have. He decided he would use it.

The grey knight walked to the middle of the yard and looked at them both and stepped back out of it again.

"When you like," said Rowena.



She came at him at once, and he understood in the first three strokes why Ward had stood on her yard once and been put on the ground.

She was not fast. That was the thing he would remember after. Ward was faster. Osric was much faster, and threw it away. She was not fast at all; she was exactly as fast as she needed to be at every instant and no faster, and every stroke she made began from where the last had ended, so that there was never a gap between them, never a breath where she was coming back from anything. The blade came round at his head and he took it on his own and it was not there to be taken; it had gone, while he was taking it, to his left side, and he got there by luck and Ward's footwork and nothing else, and felt the blow go up his arms into his teeth.

He gave ground. He gave it the way Ward had taught him to give it, not straight back, which was how men got pinned on walls, but round, across, a step and a turn, letting the blow have its road; the knight's Turn, the plain one, with no weight in it, because weight was casting and casting was not allowed. She followed him round. She followed him without hurrying, and her feet in the sand made almost no sound, and her face under the helm was calm and interested and gave him nothing at all.

He brought her round to the north-west corner.

He did not do it fast. He did it the way he would have brought a lathe round to a cut, a little at a time, giving ground where she wanted him to give it and then not quite where she wanted, a step to the left instead of back, a turn that went half a pace wider than it needed to. And she came after him across the yard, stroke and stroke and stroke, and he felt the place in his head where the drain began, under the north-west corner, and the line of it going away across the yard under the combed sand; and he went back along that line, giving ground, and she came down it after him.

Her back foot came down in the trough.

A finger's depth. Nothing. A finger lower than her body had been told the ground would be, on a yard she had walked every day for twenty years, raked smooth last night by her own people. He saw it. He saw her weight go into a foot that met the ground a hair late, and her hip drop the width of a thumb, and her blade come round a hair wide because of it.

He went in.

And she was not there. She had slipped; and she slipped the way a cat slips off a wall, and was already turning the slip into a step, and the step into a cut, and his blade went through the place where her ribs had been half a breath before and met nothing; and hers came round under it and stopped against his left side, flat, hard, just under the arm, where a real edge would have gone in between the ribs.

"One," said Rowena.

She stepped back. She looked down at the sand at her feet, at the place where her back foot had gone down, and the combed lines of the rake were broken there now; and then she looked at him.

She did not say anything. She lifted her blade.



The second time he did it to her by the east wall, and it was harder, because she was watching for it now and he knew she was.

He did not go near the drain. She would be ready for the drain. He gave ground the other way, east, toward the cliff side of the yard where the sand ran out to the wall and the drop beyond it, and she came after him, and he let her press him harder than he needed to be pressed, so that it looked like what it was half of: a man being driven onto a wall. Her knights along the north side shifted. He heard one of them say something. He did not hear what.

He felt the soft place in his head. The size of a cart bed. A stride from the wall. He went across the front of it, not into it, giving ground sideways along the wall, and turned, and came off his back foot and cut at her, a real cut, hard, at her head, so that she had to take it and step to take it; and the step she had to take was forward and to her left.

Her heel went into the soft clay under the sand up to the ankle bone.

It sucked. He heard it. A small wet sound in the dawn, like a boot in a farm gate. Her weight went down into it and stayed there, held, for the length of a breath; and in that breath he was inside her blade, past it, with his own coming round at her throat.

She let go of the ground.

Not with magic. He would have known magic; even with his sense shut he would have seen a glyph, and there was no glyph. She did it with her body, the way he had once seen a horse do it in a bog at Milford: she stopped fighting the clay that had her foot and threw her whole weight the other way, onto the other foot, out of it, and the clay let go of her boot with a sound like a cork, and her shoulder came into his chest as he came in and turned him; and he went past her, on his own momentum, into the place she had been; and his own front foot went into the soft patch to the shin.

He stood in her cistern up to the knee with her blade against the back of his neck.

"Two," said Rowena.

Somebody on the north wall laughed. It was not Ward. Emeric got his foot out of the clay. It took him longer than it had taken her.

The third time she did not let him take her anywhere.

She came at him in the middle of the yard, on the good sand, nowhere near the drain or the wall, and he could see in her face that she had decided the yard was hers again and he was not having any more of it; and he lasted eleven strokes, and he counted every one of them, because each of them was a little harder to answer than the last. On the twelfth she went low at his knee, and he went to meet it, and it was not at his knee. It came up off the low line under his own blade the way Ward's had come up under it in the first month at Kaldo, seven years ago, when he had held a sword like a man who had not held one in ten years; and it took his blade at the cross, and turned it, and his hand opened, and the steel went up out of it and over his shoulder and came down in the sand behind him, point first, and stood there.

Her blade was at his throat.

"Three," said Rowena.



She took off her helm.

The sun had come up over the inlet while they fought, and it lay along the yard now, low and gold, and threw their shadows the length of the sand to the drop. Her face was wet. She was breathing harder than he had expected, and less hard than he was. She looked at him for a long time, with the helm under her arm and the blade still in her other hand, its rolled point down in the sand.

"You hold it the way I showed you," she said.

He looked down at his hand, which was empty.

"The thumb along the back of the grip," said Rowena. "Not round it. The little finger doing the work. Every master in Randillia teaches the thumb round the grip, because that's how a man holds a hammer, and every man in Randillia has held a hammer before he's held a sword." She looked at his hand. "I taught it to you on a wet morning behind Harrold's stables when you were twelve. You can't have been holding it like that for seventeen years by accident."

"Ward kept it," said Emeric. "When he started me. He said whoever had taught me the grip had known what they were doing, and he wasn't going to take it off me."

Rowena looked across the yard at Ward, on the north wall with his arms folded.

"Did he," she said.

Ward did not say anything.

"And the ground?" said Rowena, to Emeric.

"I read it last night. Coming up to dinner. You said so."

"I know I said so. I felt you do it." She turned the blade in her hand. "And you didn't read it this morning. You had your sense shut so tight I couldn't feel you in the yard at all. You might have been one of my clerks." She looked at him. "So you fought me on a yard you'd walked across once, in the dark, and remembered. A drain I've forgotten is there, and a cistern my grandfather filled in." She was quiet. "Why didn't you open it? Just a crack. I'd have known. But you'd have had me on the second."

"You said steel only."

She looked at him.

Then she put the helm on the bench, and laid the blade beside it on the cloth, and walked past him across the yard to where his own blade stood point down in the sand behind him, and pulled it out, and wiped the sand off it with the flat of her hand, and brought it back, and laid it on the cloth beside hers.

"Yes," she said. "I did." She picked up her helm. "Breakfast is in the hall. Your people are welcome." She went to the stair; and at the foot of it, passing Ferra and Imani, she stopped. "He'll be stiff tonight," she said to Imani. "In the back of the left knee. From the clay." And went up.

Imani looked after her. Then she looked at Emeric, across the yard, standing in the sun with clay to his shin and his hands empty.

"She's right," said Imani. "You will."



The grain was dead by the middle of the afternoon.

It died in a long cool room under the dome with a table down the middle of it and windows down one side over the harbor, which the grey knight called the standing room, and which had a chair at its head and four chairs down each side of it, and nobody in most of them. Rowena at the head. The grey knight at her right. Two old men in dark coats with chains on them, a treasurer and a harbor-reeve, and a clerk with a book at the foot. And on the other side Emeric, and Ferra beside him with the barony's book, and Saban, and Vantic at the end with her own.

He made the offer. He made it well; he had made it in his head on the voyage, and on the island, and in the harbor house in the night, and it came out in order. Milford's second harvest, the four baronies' drained bottoms, eleven thousand bushels over what the four baronies would eat, by Ferra's count, which was never wrong. Wheat and barley. Carried by Kaldo's coasters at Kaldo's cost, landed on her quay. At a price, which he named, and which was fair, and which Saban had checked against the capital's market and the coast's.

Rowena listened to all of it.

She did not interrupt. She sat at the head of the table with her hands folded on it and her face perfectly correct, the way it had been at dinner, and let him get all the way to the end, to the price; and when he had finished, she looked at him a little longer, to be sure he had.

"I have a coast," she said.

That was all.

He waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it. She sat with her hands folded and looked at him with no unkindness in her face at all, and the treasurer beside her looked at his own hands, and the grey knight looked at the window.

"Southern grain," said Rowena, after a while, because he had not understood, and she could see he had not, and she was not cruel. "From the wheat islands south of the bay. They grow two crops a year down there and can't eat either of them. It comes up to my quay on the long boats, every month, in their own hulls, at their own cost, and I pay for it in sulphur and wine and glass. It's cheaper than yours. It was cheaper than yours before you got in your boat." She unfolded her hands. "You came six hundred miles to sell bread to a harbor."

Beside him Ferra did not move.

She did not write anything. He had expected her to write something. She sat with the barony's book open in front of her at the page for the day, and her pen in her hand over it, and did not write; and he understood, sitting there with his good offer dead on the table, that she had already written it. In October. At her desk in the counting room, in her square careful hand, on the day he had told her he would make it. Grain to Emberfell, at the holder's wish. Said so. Overruled.

"I'm sorry to have wasted your afternoon," he said.

"You haven't wasted it." Rowena looked at him. "You've told me what you think I need. That's worth an afternoon." She stood up, and the table stood with her. "You can tell me tomorrow what you think I need when you've had a night to think about it." She looked down the table at Ferra, briefly, and away. "Somebody at your table may have an opinion."



Rowena's women came for Ferra and Imani after the lamps were lit, as she had said they would.

Two of them, in grey, with lamps, at the foot of the harbor house stair; and Ferra came down it with her book under her arm and her hair pinned up very hard, and Imani behind her with nothing at all in her hands, which Emeric had never seen before. They went up the lit stairs into the city between the two grey women and their lamps, and he watched them from the balcony until the stair turned and the vines took them.

"She took the book," said Ward, beside him.

"She takes it everywhere."

"To a bath," said Ward. He looked up at the dome on its crag, with the lamplight round its foot and the steam going up beside it from the falls. "I'd like to know what she writes in it."

"So would I."

"You'll not ask her."

"No," said Emeric. "I'll not ask her."

He amused himself, as he had been told to. He went down to the ship with Fletcher and Cade and went through her with them by lamplight from bow to stern, every case and every neck, and found every stopper seated and every stone full; the Band's charge was still in her, all of it, every open stone in the ship lying at the top of its dial as if it had been filled that morning. He sat in the station with Tobe's log and read down the climb in Tobe's careful pencil, the times and the groups and the needles, and the line Tobe had written at the foot of the page in a different pencil, smaller: Nose bled 11.40. Not much. And under it, smaller still: Cade 11.38. Not much either.

He read it twice and did not say anything about it.

They came back at the eleventh hour.

He heard them on the stair. He was in the dining room with Ward and a jug of the black wine that neither of them was drinking, and he heard the two grey women say good night at the foot of the stair, and the two of them coming up it, slowly, not talking. Then Imani laughed. Once, on the landing, low, the short dry laugh through her nose; and Ferra said something to her, very fast and very quietly, and Imani laughed again.

They came into the dining room.

They had been in hot water a long time. He could see it on them. Ferra's face was pink to the hairline and her hair was damp at the temples where it had come out of its pins in the steam, and pinned back up in a way that was not her way; somebody else had pinned it. She had the book under her arm. She held it against her side the way she had held the box of the four lines in the long room, as if somebody might take it. Imani's hair was down. All of it, loose and damp and enormous round her shoulders, and her face was soft and warm and amused, and she looked, he thought, more rested than he had seen her look in a year.

Ward got up, said good night to the two of them, and went out to his chair on the landing. He did not say anything else. He did not need to; the way he went said it for him.

"Well?" said Emeric.

"No," said Ferra.

He looked at her.

"Whatever the question is," said Ferra. "No." She went pinker. She put the book down on the table, and then picked it up again, as if she had remembered something about it. "It was very hot."

"It's a volcano," said Imani. She sat down at the table and poured herself a cup of the wine, and drank half of it, and looked at him over the cup. "She's very frank."

"She's very frank," said Ferra.

Neither of them said anything else. He waited. Imani finished the cup. Ferra stood by the table with the book against her side and looked at the window, and her mouth did something at the corner that she stopped.

"She knew your mother very well," said Imani at last. She put the cup down. "Better than anybody we've met. Better than Ward, I think, in some ways." She stood up. "That's all you're getting tonight. Good night."

She went out. Ferra stayed a breath longer, by the table. She looked at him, and then away, and then at him again; and he saw her decide something, and then decide against it.

"Not tonight," she said. "I need to put it in order."

"Put what in order?"

"What she said." Ferra held the book. "She said a great many things, very quickly, in very hot water, and I couldn't write any of it down at the time because I'd have ruined the book." She looked at the window. "I wrote it on the way back. On the stair. Under the lamps. Imani held the lamp." She went to the door. "Some of it's about you. Most of it isn't. You'll get the parts that are about you when I've decided which they are." She stopped in the door. "Saban's not back."

"He went to the taverns."

"I know," said Ferra. "He'll have something. Wake me."



Saban came back after midnight, and nobody had to wake Ferra, because she had not been to bed.

He came up the stair quietly with his coat over his arm and a smell of tar and wine and fish on him, and found the three of them still in the dining room, Ferra at the table with the book closed under her hand, Imani in a chair by the dead fire with her feet tucked under her and her hair still down, Emeric at the window; and he sat down at the table and poured himself a cup of water from the jug, not the wine, and drank all of it, and poured another.

"I've had a very good evening," said Saban. "I want to say that first. I've had the best evening I've had in five years."

He had. Emeric could see it on him. At Kaldo Saban had a hundred people. Carriers and almanac men and a man at every horse fair and a cousin at every inn on the coast road, and the post, and the second post; and he sat in the middle of them in the counting room like a spider at the middle of a web and felt every thread. In Emberfell he had nobody. Not a name. Not a cousin. He had gone down to the harbor at dusk with a purse and his own two ears, and sat in three taverns in turn on the fish quay, and bought the right men the right cups at the right moment, and listened; and it had been, he said, like being twenty again.

"The brother," he said.

They waited.

"She told you the arena. That's true. Everybody on the quay tells it the same way, more or less, and they tell it with their chests out, because she's theirs." He turned the cup. "But they tell another thing after it, if you buy the second cup. There's a good many on the quay, the older ones, the fishing families, who think she should have killed him in the arena. There and then. When she had him on his back. That was the old law. The strongest has the land, and the other one's dead, and everybody goes home." He drank. "She let him up. She gave him back his sword and his chair. She said she'd only wanted to come home." He looked at the cup. "And he put a knife in her face. And there's men on that quay who think that was her fault, for letting him up; and they love her for it, and they'd die for her, and they think it was a fool thing to do. All three at once. I had it from a net-maker who's known her since she was nine."

"She doesn't think it was a fool thing," said Imani, from the chair.

"No," said Saban. "That's the other thing." He put the cup down. "The law."

It had come six months after the knife. In the first year of her chair, when she was new to it and had a healed face and a city that had watched her let her brother up and watched what came of it. She had written a law, herself, in her own hand, and stood on the steps of the dome and had it read out, and had it cut in stone on the wall of the standing room.

The old law of the strongest was gone. In its place: the Sovereign of Emberfell named her successor, in writing, under her own seal, whoever she liked, of the blood or not of it. Blood neither needed nor preferred; that was the phrase on the stone, Saban said, and every man on the quay knew it by heart. And the naming was confirmed by the standing table, which was the eight chairs down the long room under the dome: the harbor-reeve, the treasurer, the master of the works, the prior of the harbor chapel, the captain of her knights, the master of the vintners and the master of the fishing families and the master of the sulphur pits, each of them sitting by right of his office and not his name, so that whoever held the office held the chair. And any person who took up arms for the Sovereign's seat, against the naming, was thereby disqualified from it, for ever, and so were his children.

"Arms are disqualification," said Saban. "That's on the stone too. Three words." He looked at Emeric. "It's a good law. It's the best law I've ever heard of on that subject, and I've heard of most of them. It's aimed at the brother, and the next brother, and every brother after."

"And?" said Ferra.

"And it's never been used." Saban spread his hands on the table. "Twenty years. She's named nobody. The table's never confirmed anybody, because there's never been anybody to confirm. Half those eight chairs are empty most of the year; the harbor-reeve's eighty and sits in his office by the mole and sends his clerk. The master of the works is a man she appointed last spring because the last one died, and he's never sat at the table at all." He looked at his cup. "The net-maker said it to me plain. When she goes, there's a stone on a wall with a law on it, and eight chairs, and nobody in them who's ever done the thing the stone says they'll do. And her son's gone off past the edge of the map, and her daughter's at Harrold's court, and neither of them is named, and she won't name either of them, because the stone says blood isn't preferred, and she means it." He drank. "The net-maker thinks there'll be a knife in the arena again inside a month of her funeral. He'd put money on it. He's very fond of her."

Nobody said anything.

Emeric stood at the window and looked at the harbor, at the ships' lamps on the water in their long lines, and the dome on its crag above it with its foot lit, and the mountain behind it all with its plume going up into the dark.

It was all her. He had seen it in the hall at dinner and the yard and the standing room, and not understood what he was seeing. The knights on the quay were hers. The law on the stone was hers. The eight chairs had a law that said they would stand when she was gone, and they had never stood once while she was there to do it for them. Every clerk he had seen in Emberfell had been carrying something up a stair to her to sign. Everything in the duchy went up the stairs to the dome to her hand, and came down again with her seal on it; and the day her hand stopped, there would be nothing left in Emberfell that knew how to do anything without it.

She did not need grain. She had a coast. She had always had a coast.

What she did not have was anything that would stand up after her and keep standing.

"She doesn't need grain," said Ferra.

He turned round.

She was at the table with her hand on the closed book, looking at Saban's empty cup; and she said it the way she said a figure at the long table that she had worked out an hour ago and been waiting for everybody else to arrive at. Without triumph. Without anything.

"She needs a clerk who signs for her," said Ferra. "Somebody who can put her name on a thing when she isn't in the room, and the thing goes through, and nothing breaks. One clerk. Then a table that's sat. Then a school that makes the next clerk." She looked up at him. "Everything else follows. You don't start with the stone on the wall. You start with the clerk."

He looked at her for a long time.

"Go to bed," said Ferra. She stood up and put the book under her arm. "You've got the face. You'll want to be right about it by morning." She went to the door. "And I'll want to hear you say so."
