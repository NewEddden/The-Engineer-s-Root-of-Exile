Chapter 191 - Standing Water

Hollis said it at the long-room table in the last week of the frost, before the engine went up the Wayford line, before anybody had asked him.

"We bought," he said.

He had his sheet in front of him, the same sheet, or its son; he kept every one and brought the newest. He had his one good hand flat on it. The other lay in his lap, as it had since the coalition war, and nobody at the table looked at it any more than he did.

"Not much," Hollis said. "Less than I said we would, in the autumn. The lake farms came in thin, as I said they would, two hundred and twenty acres of bread corn that never saw a load of dung because the beds had it. A thin year is no famine. It wasn't. But there were two thousand men on the flat all winter and their people in the lean-tos, and the camp ate through the reserve by the new year, and Saban bought the rest on the coast at the coast's price to see us to the thaw." He did not look at Saban. "I've written it down."

"You said it would happen," said Emeric.

"I did. I said it so nobody could say nobody said. That's not the same as wanting to be right." Hollis turned the sheet round, so the figure faced up the table. "It'll happen again. More men on the flat this year, not fewer, and the fourth draw off the island and the fifth behind it. More mouths in the barony every quarter than the quarter before. The lake farms are nine divisions on the edge of a lake. I can make them better. I can't make them bigger. There isn't any more edge."

Nobody at the table argued with that. It was the most useful thing anybody had said at the table all winter, and they all knew it, and they had all known it for a year and not said it.

Emeric looked at the figure. He had spent his first year in this barony counting grain in a cellar with a candle and doing sums about who would be hungry in the spring, and he had promised himself then, not aloud, that he would never run Kaldo's bread that close again. He had run it that close this winter. He had done it with his eyes open, for the beds, and it had been the right choice, and it was still the thing he had promised himself he would not do.

"Milford," he said.

Hollis's hand stayed flat on the sheet. "Milford's Paxton's."

"Paxton keeps it for the barony," said Emeric, "and has since the judgment, and he'll want a great deal said to him before anybody puts a plough in it. It's still the ground. And Milford has what Kaldo hasn't. Ground. More good ground than all the lake farms and the river farms together, three times over, and most of it under grass because nobody could get water up onto it. The herds. And its own water." He looked at Hollis. "Milford feeds the four. Kaldo, Grayrock, Wayford and itself. That's what it's for, from now. Not the lake."

"And the lake farms?"

"Stay. Yours. They stop trying to feed a city they can't feed, and they grow what the island and the works need fresh, and they grow the seed. Clean seed, graded through the sieve, the way you've graded it since the first year, for Milford and for everybody else." He paused. "And anything new gets tried on your ground first, where you can watch it, before it goes west. If a new thing's going to fail, I want it to fail in front of you."

Hollis thought about that. He thought about most things for a long time, which was one of the reasons Emeric checked the barony against him and had never told him so.

"The trial ground," Hollis said.

"The trial ground."

"I'd want that written." He looked at Ferra. "That the lake farms are mine, and they're the trial, and nobody takes them off me to put houses on."

Ferra wrote it.

The water was the thing that made it possible, and the water was the thing everybody at the table had seen on a map and nobody had ever looked at.

Emeric had it on the long-room wall the next morning, pinned up over the old tithe map: the Tarrants' sheet of the whole south, drawn off their chains and the older surveys and the rider's reckonings, as good as anybody had. The river came down out of the hills in the north to the lake below Wayford, and out of the lake's foot it ran south, and a little way below the lake it split. One leg went on south, the leg every man in Kaldo knew, down through the hills and the reed country to Lake Kaldo, where it went round the island on both sides, and out past the lower water to the bar and the sea. The other leg went south-west. It ran through Milford, and on across Milford to the coast, in its own channel, and it never came within a day's ride of Kaldo.

He put his finger on the fork, and ran it down the western leg, and took it off at the sea.

"Nothing Milford does to its water touches ours," he said. "Nothing it takes out of it. Nothing it puts in. If every yard in Milford drains into that leg for a hundred years, not a cupful of it comes past the island."

Imani, at the table's end, had come in for this part and nothing else. She looked at the map for a while, at the fork and the two legs, and then said, "Good," and went back down to the ward. It was the whole of her part in it, and she had come up two flights of stairs for it.

The trouble with Milford's water was not where it went. It was where it lay. Hale had said it years ago, reading his old map aloud as if it were an accusation against the man who held the barony: the leg ran south-west in a cut of its own, well below the flats, so that Milford's best ground stood dry above the only river it had. Paxton's father had given up the plough and gone to grass, because grass did not need a river. Emeric had ridden out past it in his first year and written one line in the notebook at a halt. Water below, ground above. He had not read it back for a long time.

He knew what he wanted to draw. He did not know yet where to draw it, because nobody did. Nobody had ever drawn Milford's ground the way a man would need to draw it to put water on it: where it rose and where it fell, where the wet lay and where it didn't, which fields drained and which sat sour. There was no map of that anywhere. There was no way to make one, in the ordinary way, in less than a year of men walking the barony with a chain.

He had a way that was not the ordinary way. It needed the thaw.

The Tarrants went out first, in the first fortnight of the thaw, on the Milford line: the elder and the nephew, and Weekes's two chain gangs lent off the flat, and a dozen Milford men hired at the siding to carry boards, with a wagon of limewashed crosses and two levels and every chain the Tarrants owned. Four parties, working outward from the siding, one to each quarter.

They set crosses. That was the whole of their work for a fortnight, and they did it the way they did everything, better than they had been asked. Two boards nailed across each other, the height of a man's waist, limewashed white on both faces, set in the ground at every half mile on a square grid across the whole of the barony that could be walked: north to south, east to west, the lines laid off the Milford siding's corner stone and chained and levelled from it. Where a line crossed the leg the crosses stood on both banks. Where it crossed a steading they stood in the yard, and the steading's dogs had opinions about it. By the end of the fortnight there were crosses from the high flats in the north to the reed edge in the south, in rows, as far as a man on the siding's roof could see, like a field of white stitches in the grey grass.

Paxton was told it was a survey for the tithe map. It was. The tithe map was old and wrong in every parish, and the Tarrants' grid would correct it, and the barony would be glad of it. Paxton rode out on the second day and sat his horse by a cross and looked at it and then at the elder Tarrant, and said that Kaldo was very thorough about its tithes, and rode home. He did not ask anything else. Emeric had not expected him to.

The Tarrants chained the falls as well. That mattered more than the crosses, though nobody on the flats would have guessed it. Every cross had a height on it, written on its board in Weekes's figures from the siding's datum, so that when the crosses were a map the map would know which way water would run across it. From the air a man could see a great deal. He could not see a fall of a foot in a furlong, which was the whole difference between a field that drained and one that did not, and every channel that would ever be cut in Milford would be cut on the Tarrants' falls and not on anything he saw.

The elder Tarrant brought the book back to the long room at the end of the fortnight, and put it on the table, and said, "Seven hundred and eighty-three crosses. One of them's in a bull's field, and the bull's had it down twice." He looked at Emeric. "You'll want to tell me what they're for, one day."

"One day," said Emeric.

Tarrant nodded as though that were an answer, and went home.

In the south the thaw came in two parts. The low ground went first, soft and slow, a little more each day. Then, at the end of it, the snow on the hills went all at once: a warm wind off the sea for three days, and the high snow gone in a night, and every river in the four baronies brown and bank-full by the morning, and the water coming down onto flats that had already drunk all they could.

For about a week after that, the melt stood.

It stood in every low place on every flat, in sheets and threads and long still pools, because the ground under the grass was sodden to the frost line and would take no more. It lay in the hollows and ran along the bottoms and gathered where the drainage gathered and sat where the drainage sat. For a week, the whole shape of a barony's water was drawn on its ground in water, in silver, as plainly as ink on a sheet. And then the ground drained and the sun took the rest, and it was gone, and nobody had ever seen it whole, because a man standing in a field can see his own field and his neighbour's and the ditch between them, and no more.

From three miles up, a man could see all of it.

The hill snow let go that year in the fortnight after the engine came back from Wayford. Hull three went on the second night of the standing water, with Hask's heading a week into the hill two days out and the second tower standing to its first floor on the flat.

She went the way she had gone the first time. Armand's rule still stood. Nothing that flew went off the lower water while there was a mage on the flat, and there were mages on the flat again, six of them, back at the thaw for the gate towers' upper floors and the palace's first storey. So she went down the lower water at dusk on the ebb behind the Marsh Hen, a coaster towing a coaster, with no lamp lit on either, and was cast off in the dark five miles past the bar. Ward had the levers. Fletcher had the wheel, because he had built her and would not be left on the ground for any flight of hers if he could help it. Two of her own hands had the racks. Emeric stood at the forward window of the room under her roof, with a board on his knees that he would not use and a pencil he would not need, because he had not yet learned to go without them.

She went up in the dark, slowly, turning, the way she always went up, until the sea was a black floor with a faint grey edge to it far out in the east. Then she went west over the land, high, higher than any of them had taken her before on purpose, until the racks' hum changed note and Ward said, "Band," and held her there, just under it, where her ceiling was. Three miles, near enough. The cold came in round the window frames. Fletcher put his coat on over his coat.

She came over Milford an hour before first light, and waited.

Emeric had worked it out for a week, and it came down to one simple rule. The sun would come up in the east. Anybody in Milford who looked up at dawn would look up into the sun's side of the sky first, because that was where the light was. So she held in the east, with the sun coming up behind her, and anybody who looked would look into it, and see nothing but the glare, and look away. And the low sun, coming in flat across the whole barony from the east, would lay every rise and every hollow out on the ground in shadow, long, the way a low light lays out the grain of a plank.

It came up. He looked down.

He had known it would be good. He had not known it would be like that.

Milford lay under them from the hills to the sea, grey and gold in the first light, and it was drawn in water. The fork was there in the north, where the river came out of the lake and split, and the western leg went away from it across the whole of Milford in its cut, a dark line with the light on it in places, lower than everything round it, winding. Along both sides of the cut the ground stood up in long level shelves above the water, benches, dry and pale, the good ground the river had never reached in any man's lifetime. And below the benches, along the leg itself, in every bend and every flat bottom, the melt stood in sheets, silver and pink with the dawn in it, so that the bottoms were drawn in light and the benches in shadow and there was no mistaking one for the other anywhere.

Beyond the benches the high flats went on for miles, open range, grey with last year's grass, and across them the melt lay in threads and pools where the ground dipped, so that he could see, from three miles up, every one of the dips, and which way each one ran, and where each one ended in nothing.

He could see the sour ground. It was where the herds had been. Round every water hole on the flats and every ford on the leg and along every drove road the ground was darker and the water stood on it longer, in a stain, because the cattle had stood there winter after winter and trodden it into something like a potter's clay. The drove roads themselves ran away across the flats in worn bare lines, pale, three and four abreast where the herds had spread, and every one of them went to the slaughter yards at Milford's market town and stopped. Behind the slaughter yards there were heaps. He knew what the heaps were. Paxton had tried to sell them to him at seven Copper the hundredweight.

He could see the steadings, small and dark, each with its yard and its ricks and its thread of morning smoke. He could see, on the border with Kaldo's farms, a black square in a field with nothing standing on it, where a barn had burned to its footings in the year of the lost letters, full of grain, half of it Kaldo's, and whose footings were still there, black, in the grass.

And he could see, coming in from the east across the whole of it, straight as a rule where nothing else in Milford was straight, the new iron of the Milford line, with the sun on the rails.

He did not use the sense. He did not need it and it would not have helped. The sense was for the medium, for stones and the thin weather of the air and the density of living things in a room, and it had nothing to say about whether a field drained. This was a thing for eyes. He used his eyes.

Then he did it the way he had planned it, which was slowly.

The crosses were there. He had wondered whether they would be, from so high. They were. Not as crosses: as specks of white, each one alone in its own stretch of grey, and in rows. From three miles up the eye would pick out a thing about the size of a cart, and a limewashed board in a field of dun grass with the low sun full on it was near enough. Seven hundred and eighty-three of them, in their lines, and the lines made squares, and the squares were what he had come for.

He had divided Milford on paper into squares of four crosses to a side, two miles each way, and numbered them, forty-six of them, from the north-east corner to the south-west. He took them in order. He found the first square's four corner crosses and held the square in his eye, all of it, the ground between the crosses and every pool and thread and stain and field edge in it, and held it, and held it, for as long as it took to have it. Not to look at it. To have it. Then he said, "Two," and Ward moved her, a little, and he found the next four crosses and did it again.

Ward held her steady for three hours and did not say a word except to answer the numbers.

It was the hardest work Emeric had done in a year. He had not expected that. He had thought looking would be easy. It was not looking. It was the other thing, the thing he did in the room with a page he had seen once in the capital and needed again, done forty-six times over in the cold at a window, and by the twentieth square his eyes were running and by the thirtieth he was counting under his breath to hold himself to it. Fletcher put a tin cup of tea in his hand at the thirtieth and he drank it without taking his eyes off the ground. Fletcher did not say anything either. He stood at the next window with his own cup and looked down at a barony drawn in water, and after a while Emeric heard him say, to nobody, "Well," very quietly, the way a man says it in a church.

At the fortieth square a cloud came up off the sea.

It came from the south-west, low and white, the first cloud of the morning, and it came in over the reed edge and sat down on Milford's south-western corner, over the forty-fourth square, and stopped there. Everywhere else the sky was clear. He did the forty-first and the forty-second and the forty-third and the forty-fifth and the forty-sixth, round it, and came back to the forty-fourth, and it was still there, white, a lid on the ground. He waited. Ward held her. The sun climbed. The melt on the flats began to go dull as the light came round, and the long shadows began to shorten, and the lid over the forty-fourth square did not move.

"It's not going," Ward said.

"No."

"The racks will want the band before long. And the flats will be up and about under us." Ward did not look away from the levers. "Your call."

Emeric looked at the white lid a moment longer. He had forty-five squares. He had one he had not got, and he knew exactly which, and he would know it for as long as the map existed.

"Up," he said.

Ward took her up into the band, and the racks drank, and she went east for the sea with the sun in front of her, and nobody in Milford, as far as anybody ever heard, looked up.

He did not sleep when they came in. He went up to his own room in the keep, and sat in the chair with the curtain drawn, and went into the delve.

It was all there. He had known it would be, because it always was; it was the oldest thing he knew about the room. What he had seen sat on the shelf while he was in it, every page he had ever read, every face, every stone, every inch of every morning. This morning was there, on the shelf, whole. Forty-five squares of Milford as they had looked from three miles up at dawn in the week of the standing water, each as sharp as it had been through the window, and one white lid where the forty-fourth should have been.

He laid them out.

He laid them out on the floor of the room, edge to edge, by the crosses. That was what the crosses were for. The corner cross of one square was the corner cross of the next, and a square that had been looked at a hair to the east of true lined up with its neighbour at the crosses and not at its edge, so the crosses pulled every square into its place on the grid, the way pins pull a sheet straight on a board. When they were all down, Milford lay on the floor of the room from the fork to the reed edge, in one piece, in the morning's light, with the water standing in it.

He walked round it for a while. It was a thing he had never seen, and nobody had. Then he did the work.

One square to a sheet. He coded each one as the printer wanted it, the way he had learned to code a page for the printer's stone when the printer was new: the square's ground, and the water in it, and the stains and the field edges and the drove roads and the heaps and the steadings, in the printer's black, and its four corner crosses marked at the sheet's four corners with the cross's number and the Tarrants' height beside it. Forty-five sheets. Then a forty-sixth, for the forty-fourth square, with its crosses at its corners and its heights, and nothing else on it at all.

The printers ran all night. Both of them, home from the Wayford office since the net came down, side by side on their trestles in the print room. Two of Ferra's clerks fed them paper and took the sheets off and did not look at them, because Ferra had told them the sheets were survey sheets for the tithe map and they had seen enough survey sheets to know they were not interesting.

In the morning the sheets were in two stacks on the long-room table, and Ferra looked at the top one of the first stack for a long time without touching it.

Then she moved the table.

She and Emeric moved it together, to the wall, with the benches on top of it, and swept the long-room floor, and knelt on it, and began to lay the sheets out on the boards by the crosses, cross to cross, pinning each one to the next with the brass pins Ferra kept for the census rolls. It took the whole morning. They did it on their knees, in their stockings, because boots would have torn the paper, working inward from the north-east corner, and Ward stood at the door and kept the day's clerks out, and Saban came up at noon to ask about the carts and stood in the door beside Ward and forgot to ask.

When they had finished, Milford lay on the long-room floor.

It lay from the door to the hearth, in black on white, with the fork at the north end and the leg winding away across it in its cut, and the benches pale on both sides, and the bottoms black with standing water, and the flats beyond them threaded with it, every dip and every hollow, and the sour ground in stains round the water holes, and the drove roads in pale lines running to the heaps. Every field that drained, and every field that didn't. The steadings. The black square on the Kaldo border. The rail, straight across it all. There had never been a map like it in the kingdom. Ferra knew that, kneeling at its edge with a pin in her mouth, and did not say it, and did not have to.

In the south-west corner, one sheet was white. Its four crosses were at its corners, with their numbers and their heights. Between them there was nothing.

Ferra took the pin out of her mouth.

"What's there?"

"I don't know," Emeric said. "A cloud."

She looked at the white sheet for a while. Then she got up and went to the table against the wall and came back with her pen, and knelt again at the corner, and wrote across the white sheet, diagonally, corner to corner, in her small clear hand: not seen.

"A blank that says nothing lies," she said, when he looked at her. "Somebody in a year will look at that and think it's empty ground. It isn't empty ground. It's ground we don't know." She capped the pen. "That's a different thing, and it goes on the sheet."

The sheets went into Ferra's box that night, all forty-six, folded in their squares and numbered, under the stones.

What left the long room was a plan. It was drawn by hand on the Tarrants' grid, on their four hundred and six crosses and their heights, and it said where the wet ground was and where the dry was and where the water should go, and it said nothing at all about how anybody knew. Whoever read it would see a survey, done well. The Tarrants had done a survey, and done it well. Nobody outside the table would ever be told where the rest had come from, and nobody outside the table would ever need to be.

But that was afterward. That was the drawing, and the drawing had not been begun.

On the afternoon of the sheets he stood at the edge of the map with a clean sheet in his hand and a pencil, in his stockings, and looked at Milford lying on his floor. Then he began to walk round it, slowly, along its edge, the way Ferra walked round a thing before she let herself have an opinion of any part of it. At the fork he stopped and looked a long while at the benches. At the sour ground round the first ford he stopped again. At the heaps behind the slaughter yards he stopped and looked at them and at the flats beyond them, and thought about what Milford threw away every autumn and what its fields went without every spring, and how short the distance was between the two.

At the south-west corner, by the white sheet with not seen across it, he knelt down and put the clean sheet on the floor beside it, and began the other drawing.
