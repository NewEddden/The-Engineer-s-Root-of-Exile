Chapter 124 - The Island

He came down to the long room while the windows were still grey, and Ferra was already there.

She was at the end of the table with the lamp and the minute book, and her coat on, because the harbour house was cold at that hour even here. She was not writing. The book lay open at the page with the promise on it and the coal under it, and her line under that, and his answer beside her line in her hand. She looked up when he came in, and then down at the page again.

He sat down across from her. He had not shaved and he had not slept, and he knew she could see both, and she did not say anything about either.

"You were right," he said.

She waited.

"You were right in writing, two days before. I read it twice. I went ahead anyway." He put his hands flat on the table, on either side of nothing. "And I was wrong in front of a foreign table, with your name on the terms."

Ferra did not get softer. He had not expected her to and she did not. She looked at him across the lamp the way she looked at a figure somebody had brought her late, and then she put one finger on her own line on the page, where it said that nobody at their table knew what the sovereign of Emberfell lacked.

"What does she lack?" she said.

It was her question. She had written it before they sailed and he had overruled it, and she was asking it again now, in the same words, because it had not been answered and she did not let things go unanswered in her book.

He had spent the night on it. He had lain in the small room at the top of the house and watched the red go out of the mountain, and he had it, and it came out shorter than it had been in the dark.

"Something that still stands the day after she doesn't," he said.

Ferra looked at him for the length of a breath.

Then she turned the page. She turned it past the promise and the coal and the objection and his answer beside it, to a clean page, and wrote the date at the head of it, and under the date she began to write.

He had not finished. He had the rest of the night still in him: the law Saban had brought back from the taverns, and the table that sat by office, and the chair she had never wanted. He opened his mouth to give it to her in order, and saw that she was already a line ahead of him and did not need the order, and shut it again.

He read her first line upside down, because he always read her upside down.

*A clerk who signs for her.*

He did not know where she had got it. He had not been in the harbour office. She did not look up to tell him, and he did not ask, and she went on writing, and by the time the lamp needed trimming there were eleven lines on the page and he had supplied perhaps three of them.

Nothing was said about anything else. Imani came down the stair at the first bell, looked at the two of them bent over one book with the lamp between them, and went through to the kitchen without a word to either.

The knight came a little after the second bell.

It was the tall woman who had stood across the door from Ward on the first night, with her helm under her arm again. She did not come in. She stood in the doorway of the long room and said that Her Grace was walking down to the works, and would be glad of the prince's company, if the prince had nothing better to do with his morning.

It was not a question. Emeric got his coat.

He had been carrying the grey stone in the silver star since the night of the dinner, and he did not take it out now. He pinned the star at the collar of his plain coat, because the stone went where he went, and went down. Ward came down the stair behind him without being asked, and at the door he let Emeric go out first and then stood back, and came after at ten paces, which was how Ward walked behind a man in somebody else's town.

She was waiting at the foot of the slope in a plain dark coat, with the long sword at her hip and no other sign on her of what she was. Nobody bowed to her in the street. People stepped round her the way they stepped round a post they had walked past every day of their lives, and a few of them said good morning.

"You came for sulphur," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you'll want to see where it comes from."

That was all she said by way of a reason. She set off along the street toward the mountain and he fell in beside her, and her tall knight fell in a pace behind her, and Ward ten paces behind that, and the town went by.

He kept the sense shut.

It was harder than it had been on the yard. There he had been fighting and had not had room to want it. Here he was walking through a strange town beside a woman who had felt it go over her once, and every street they went down was a street he did not know, and the whole of his habit for ten years was to open the medium on any ground he did not know and take the lay of it. He did not. He walked through Emberfell with only his eyes, the way he had walked through the capital at twenty-two, and it felt like walking with one hand in his pocket.

The works were a mile off, along the foot of the mountain, and the road out to them went through the upper edge of the town and then out across black ground where nothing grew. There was steam standing up out of cracks in it here and there, in white threads, and the ground was warm through his boots. Near the works it was yellow. The crust lay on the black rock round the mouths of the vents in a thin bright skin, like frost that had gone wrong, and men with cloths tied over their mouths were raking it into baskets and carrying the baskets to carts. The smell was very strong. It got into the back of his throat and stayed there.

"You get used to it," Rowena said. "Or you move to the upper town."

The works were long sheds of black stone, as he had seen from the harbour house window, with no stacks and no smoke, and the heat that came off them shook the air. The thin man from dinner met them at the door of the first one, the head of the great works, with soot on his cuffs and a look of a man who had been told at breakfast that his sovereign was coming and had not been told why.

He showed them the furnace that had cracked.

It stood at the end of the shed, cold, with its side opened up and men inside it on their knees, chipping out the lining. The floor of the shed was hot underfoot. Under each furnace, the head of the works said, the ground had been opened to the heat below, a long time ago, by men nobody remembered, and the heat came up through a throat of stone into the furnace's belly, and all a man had to do was keep the throat clear and the lining whole. They burned nothing. They had never burned anything. They built against the mountain the way a man builds against a south wall, and the mountain did the rest.

"How did you know it would crack?" Emeric said.

The thin man looked at him as if the question had been asked in a foreign language, which in a sense it had.

"You hear it," he said. "Before. It changes its note, a day or two before it goes. My master could hear it across a shed." He shrugged. "I can hear it at twenty feet. The boy I've got now can't hear it yet. He'll learn."

"Is it written anywhere?"

"Written?"

"What the note is. What it changes to."

The head of the works thought about it honestly, which Emeric liked him for.

"No," he said. "I don't know how you'd write a sound."

Emeric thanked him and did not say anything else, and put it with the rest. A furnace nobody could read but by ear, and the ear in three men, and the third a boy. He saw Rowena watching him put it away. She did not ask what he had put away, and he did not tell her.

They went down from the works by a different road, toward the water.

It came down along the side of a stream that ran warm out of the black ground and steamed in the cold air, and at the foot of it, where the stream came out into the inlet below the harbour, there was a boatyard. It was a small one. There was a slip, and a fishing boat hauled out on it with her bottom in the air and her seams raked out, and two men working along her with a pot and a brush.

The pot was sitting on a flat stone over a vent, with steam coming up round it, and what was in the pot was black.

Emeric stopped walking.

He knew it before he had seen it properly. He knew it by the smell, which came up the slip to him on the warm air from the vent, and which was not pine tar, not the resin smell of every boatyard on the lower water, but something heavier and older, a smell like a road on a hot day. The man with the brush drew it along a seam, thick and glossy and slow, and it stayed where he put it.

In another life, men had dug that out of pits and scooped it off the tops of pools for longer than anybody had written anything down. They had paid their boats with it and set their bricks in it and smeared it on wounds, and then, a very long time afterwards, somebody had boiled it the right way and found out what else it was, and after that the world had changed shape round it.

He was standing still in the middle of the path. He knew he was, and he did not move.

Rowena had stopped a pace further on. She looked at him, and at the pot, and back at him.

"You're interested in the black stuff," she said.

"What do you call it?"

"Pitch, when it's like that. When it comes liquid, in the cask, the boatmen call it burning water." She looked at the pot. "They pay seams with it. The masons put it in the mortar on the harbour wall, down where the sea gets at it. The healers use it for a salve, for scald and for sheep's feet, and they'll tell you it's good for a great deal more than that, and it isn't." She said all of it flatly, as a list. "Are you?"

He could have said a little. He knew exactly how to say a little. He had spent eight years in a court saying less than he meant about everything, and ten more in Kaldo watching Saban buy things cheaply from men who did not know what they had, and he knew the face a man makes when he wants a thing and does not want its price to go up.

He did not make it.

"Yes," he said. "I've got uses for it. It's worth a great deal to me."

Rowena looked at him for a moment longer than she needed to.

"Most men who want a thing," she said, "tell me it's very nearly worthless."

"It isn't."

"No," said Rowena. "I don't suppose it is."

He went down the slip to the pot, because he could not help it, and the man with the brush stood back and let him look, with the face of a man who has had his work looked at by a stranger before and did not think much of it. It was thick and black and it had a skin on it where the air touched it. It was the heavy end of the thing, the part that stayed behind. What he wanted was lighter than this.

He did not make a list of it. It came to him the way things did, one after another, standing over the pot. Grease that would not cook off a bearing, for the engine and the locomotive and the forge's own axles, which Bram packed with tallow now and cursed every week. Lamp oil, if he could boil it right, which meant a still, which meant the glassworks. A road that did not wash out in the thaw, for the city he meant to build and had not told anybody about yet. And under all of those, a flame.

A flame did not know what the medium was.

He had written three lines in the notebook two miles over the sea, three days ago, about the dead air. He had been trying to answer it from the sky, with boxes of the band and a medium pressed denser than stone, and it had not occurred to him that it had an answer on the ground. Inside a dead zone every stone went out. A lamp of oil would go on burning in the middle of it as if nothing had happened, because for a lamp of oil nothing had.

He straightened up from the pot.

"Where does it come from?" he said.

"Duvrenne," said Rowena.

Then she stopped.

He saw it happen. She had said the word the way she would have said the name of any port on a bill of lading, and then something came up behind it, and she stood on the slip with her hands at her sides and looked at him, and her face did something it had not been given permission to do.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'd forgotten."

He knew what she had forgotten before she said it.

"The crystal delegation," Rowena said. "Duvrenne's. The woman with the price. Alina." She said the name carefully, as if it might break. "It came here, the story. Years ago. The King's third son drunk at a banquet, calling Duvrenne's envoy a cheat in front of the whole court, and his father sending him south for it." She looked at the pot and back at him. "I should have remembered before I said the word."

"It's old business," said Emeric.

He said it easily and he meant her to hear it easily, and she did. He waved a hand at it, and she let it go.

It was old business. It was ten years old, and it had been said by a man in a hall on the last evening that man had his own life to himself, and it was not a thing Emeric had said at all, in one sense, and in every other sense he had said it and paid for it and would go on paying. He had been right. He had never once in ten years been sorry he had been right. He had been sorry, sometimes, about how loud.

He was sorry now. He stood on the slip with the smell of it all round him and he was angry, and he could not have said whether it was with himself or with the man whose mouth he had inherited, and it did not matter which.

Duvrenne had no quarrel with Kaldo. That much he knew. He had sold Duvrenne a season of grain, once, to an old man with a slow voice who had never heard of Kaldo and had taken its name on trust in the end because of a line in somebody else's letter, and then he had written to that old man and taken the grain away again, and it had been the hardest letter he had written that year. The grain houses on that coast had never cared what a drunk prince said at a banquet. Grain was grain.

But this was not grain.

"Where in Duvrenne?" he said.

"The far north. Up past the posts." Rowena turned and looked out across the inlet, as though she could see it from here. "It comes out of the ground up there. And the ground up there belongs to houses, the old ones. The same houses that dig the crystal." She said it as a fact about a neighbour, the kind of fact a ruler keeps about everybody's ground. "The same houses that sent that woman to your father's table."

He had talked, that night, about her father's mines. He had talked about what the yield reports out of them would have to look like for her price to be honest, and about what it meant that nobody had seen them. He remembered the music stopping one instrument at a time. He remembered her going quiet.

So that was the ground. A grain buyer could not sell him ground. An old man in a counting house could not. The thing he wanted most in the world after sulphur came up out of the fields of the families whose honesty he had questioned in front of seventy people, and if Kaldo had stood well with those families it would have come to him by the shipload, and Kaldo did not stand well with them. Kaldo did not stand anywhere with them. As far as those families were concerned, Kaldo was the place they had sent a loud boy to be forgotten.

The man who kept the boatyard had come up the slip by then, wiping his hands, because his sovereign was standing in his yard. Rowena asked him, for Emeric's benefit, how the stuff came to him, and he told it with the pleasure of a man who knows a thing nobody asks him about.

It came a long way. It came out of the ground in the far north-east of Duvrenne, the man said, where the diggers broke it out of the pools through the winter and kept it in casks in the cold, and in spring it went down to the trade posts on that coast, and from the posts it went by sea, round and down into the inner water and along it and out through the strait, and so at last into this inlet, into this pot. Thousands of miles, he said, proudly, as if he had rowed them. To be painted on the bottom of a fishing boat.

"Does it ever come east?" Emeric said. "Along the coast past here?"

The man laughed. "Who'd buy it? There's no yards that way. Nothing past here but salt boats and fishermen, and they'll use pine."

That was Kaldo, Emeric thought. Nothing past here but salt boats and fishermen. That was why in ten years he had never seen a barrel of it, and why nobody on the lower water had ever put a smell like this in his way. It came the long road round the world to the last big yard on the coast, and stopped.

They walked back up.

He did the sum on the way. He did it the way he had done the ones at the back of the notebook in the autumn, in his head, with his lips not quite still. He had learned the map of the world at nine, in a schoolroom in the capital, for a tutor who never struck anybody but made you draw it again from the start if you got a coast wrong, and he could see it still: the long shape of Randillia, and the Core in the middle of the world, and Duvrenne up at the top of it, and the empty northern coast of Duvrenne going off the edge of the tutor's parchment into nothing. From here, straight, that coast was perhaps fifteen hundred miles. Perhaps more. The ship made fifty miles in a glass. Four glasses to the hour.

A night. Going round anything that needed going round, a night and a little more.

"You're counting," Rowena said.

"Yes."

She looked at him sideways as they walked, and did not ask what, and he could see her not ask it, and he could see her put it wherever she put things she meant to come back to. It was getting crowded, he thought, wherever that was.

"I'll have some sent down to your ship," she said. "Of the black stuff."

"I'll pay for it."

"No," said Rowena, "you won't."

She said it the way she had said *steel only* on the yard, and he knew better than to argue, and did not. He thanked her. She nodded once, as one ruler nods to another, and then she looked at him a moment longer, and it was not quite the same nod.

The way back to the harbour house went through her yard.

There were knights on it, as there had been the morning before, drilling in padded jackets on the worn flags. Two pairs were working at the far end with blunt steel, and a knight-captain he recognised from dinner was walking between them with his hands behind his back. Nearer, by the water butt, a woman a head shorter than Emeric was working with a man a head taller than him, and as they came along the wall she stepped inside his cut, and took his wrist, and turned, and put him down on the flags with a sound like a sack coming off a cart. She stood over him with the blade at his neck. Then she gave him her hand and pulled him up, and they went back to their marks and started again.

Emeric had not slept, and he was watching her, and he said it before he knew it was going to be said.

"She fights like her."

It was half aloud. It was to nobody. It was the kind of thing a man says to himself on the third day with no sleep, and hears himself say, and wishes back.

Rowena turned her head.

"Like who?"

"Nobody," Emeric said. "Somebody I saw once."

She looked at him, and at the woman by the butt, and back at him. Then she walked on to the end of the wall, where the path went up to the harbour house, and stopped there.

"I'll send for you tonight," she said, and went up the other way, toward the hall, with her tall knight behind her.

The black stuff came down to the ship in the afternoon.

It came on a handcart pushed by two men from the harbour office, with a third walking beside it with a paper for Fletcher to sign: two casks, iron-hooped and heavy, and a crate of the pitch in cakes, each the size of a loaf, wrapped in sacking. There was a seal on each cask and a seal on the crate, pressed into black wax, and it was her seal. There was no note.

Fletcher sent up to the harbour house for Emeric before he would let anybody lift anything, and Emeric went down.

He found Fletcher on the black quay beside the cart with his arms folded, looking at the casks the way he had looked at the scale on the beach.

"I know what that is," Fletcher said. "I've never had any. I've heard of it." He bent and put his nose near a bung and straightened up again. "Coasting men talk about it the way they talk about a woman in a port they've never been to."

"Where does it go?"

"It's decided." Fletcher did not uncross his arms. "Burning water, they call it here. It'll not go anywhere near a fire stone in her. Not by the hob and not by the boiler and not under anybody's warm floor." He looked along her. "The forepeak."

The forepeak was the narrow space in her bow, beyond the first iron wall. There was nothing in it but her cable and her spare canvas and her paint, and there never had been. It was the coldest place in her, and the farthest from any fire, and the range was aft of the crew's berths, in the galley, with the whole length of the berths between.

"Paint burns too," Fletcher said, "and it's been in there since she was launched. It can have company."

They took the casks and the crate aboard on a tackle and down into the forepeak, and Fletcher lashed them himself, against the wall, with the cable between them and the bow. He did it the way he did everything that was going into his ship, slowly, and when he had finished he stood in the scuttle with his head and shoulders out and looked down at the three seals of black wax in the lamplight.

"She didn't send a word with it," he said.

"No."

"Then she meant it," said Fletcher, and came up the ladder.

Rowena sent for him after dark.

It was the same room off the end of the hall, with the one lamp and the two chairs and the jug on the table, and the window on the mountain glowing at its top. Ward stood outside the door. Her tall knight stood across from him on the other side of it. The two of them looked at each other once and then at the wall, as they had the first night, and Rowena shut the door.

She did not go to the window this time. She sat down, and poured, and pushed his cup across, and let him drink half of it the way he drank, before she said anything.

"Who fights like her?" she said.

He had known she would ask. He had known it on the path at the end of the wall, and he had known it all afternoon, on the quay with Fletcher and in the long room with Ferra's page, and he had thought about what he would say, and he had not found anything that was true that he could say, and he had come up the slope to the hall with that in his hands.

He could not tell her. There was nobody in the world he could tell.

So he lied to her.

"A woman I saw in a dream," he said.

He did it on purpose. He heard it come out of his mouth in his own voice, even and easy, the way he had heard a great many things come out of it in the capital, and he knew exactly what it was while he was saying it. In ten years in Kaldo he had never once explained where anything came from. He had built things and let people wonder, and when they asked, he had said nothing, and let the question sit until they stopped asking. Two nights ago he had told this same woman *slowly*, and let that sit too. Maren had wondered about his recipes and filed it. Ferra had wondered about everything and filed all of it. Nobody had ever been given a reason, because a reason was a thing that could be carried away and looked at.

This was a reason. He had just given one to a foreign sovereign who was also his father's wife.

He did not feel what it cost. Not then. It felt like a door he had closed quietly, and it was a long while before he understood that it had been a door he had opened.

Rowena looked at him.

"Tell me the dream," she said.

So he told her about the island.

He told it plainly, because it was a plain story. There was an island, in the dream, far out in a warm sea, and there were only women on it. There had only ever been women on it. They had never been ruled by a man or married to one, and they trained from girlhood the way her knights trained, every morning, on flags like hers, and they were the best fighters in the world. And the best of all of them was a woman who was not quite a woman. Her mother was the island's queen, and her father was a god, a greater god than any the island prayed to, and she was the finest warrior on an island of warriors, and she knew it, and so did everybody else.

He thought it was a compliment when he started. He thought he was bringing a queen of warriors a story about a country of them.

Rowena listened to all of it without moving. When he stopped she was quiet for a moment.

"How do they keep it?" she said.

"Keep it?"

"An island. All women. For how long, in your dream? Generations?"

"Yes."

"Then how do they have children?"

He got there in his own head before he got anywhere near his mouth, and he stopped.

He knew how. There were several ways it had been told, in another life, and none of the old ones were kind, and every one of the old ones was a blade. He had brought her a country of women and the answer to her question was men taken and used and killed, and he was about to say it across a table to a woman who had killed her brother in front of her whole town, who had given the seat back before he stuck the knife in her, who had gone home and written in a night that no blade would ever again be an argument in Emberfell. He had known all of that for less than a day. He had known it since the dark. And he found that he could not put the island's answer on the table between them, and he did not know how to say that he could not.

So he said nothing.

Rowena watched him say nothing. Then she answered it herself, flatly, the way she would have priced a load of sulphur.

"They take ships," she said.

He did not say anything.

"A crew off a boat that comes too close. Pirates, most likely. Nobody sends a letter asking after a pirate." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the table. "They use them. And then they kill them."

"Something like that," Emeric said.

"Something like that." She looked at the lamp. "Then they did it the cheap way."

He had not expected that.

"A place that can only hold itself together by keeping men out of it hasn't proved anything," Rowena said. "It's proved it can keep men out. That's a wall. Anybody can build a wall." She said it without heat, as a knight gives a verdict on another knight's footwork. "I did it with men in the room. In your father's court for twenty years, and here for the rest. Plenty of my knights are men, and they'd follow me into the sea. I never had to kill a boatload of anybody to make that happen." She drank. "My law says arms are disqualification. Your island's nothing but arms."

He had brought her a story he thought she would like, and she had told him, in four sentences, that it was a poorer version of her own life. And she was right. He sat back and found that he was smiling, and that she had seen it, and that she did not mind.

"Were there others?" she said.

"Others?"

"In your dreams. Women." She lifted the jug and filled his cup again, though it did not need filling. "A man who dreams one of them dreams more than one. You said *like her* on my yard as though you'd a list."

He had a list. He had a list the length of another world, and he did not recite it to her, because she did not let him. She pulled them out of him one at a time, with questions, the way she would have drawn a report out of a scout who kept wanting to tell it in the wrong order, and she was impatient with the ones that did not serve her and she said so.

He gave her a goddess of a city by the sea, first, because she was the obvious one. Two gods had wanted the same city, and the god of the sea had struck the rock and given them a spring, and the spring was salt. And the goddess had given them an olive tree. And the city had chosen the tree.

Rowena liked that. He watched her like it.

"She offered the better thing," she said. "And the other one came at them with the sea."

"Yes."

"Good." Then: "Who was her mother?"

He told her. The goddess had no mother. She had come out of her father's head, fully grown, in armour, when his skull was split open with an axe.

Rowena was quiet for a moment.

"So the one who gave the better gift," she said, "was made entirely by a man, out of a man, with nobody else's hand in it." She set her cup down. "I expect he told everybody that at dinner."

He gave her a goddess of war, next, a plain one, of armies and of the fighting itself, and got as far as her name and her chariot.

"No," Rowena said. "That's a war with no story in it. Next."

So he gave her the sun.

It was a goddess who was the sun, in a country at the far end of the other world, and she had a brother who was the storm. And the brother had come into her realm and fouled it, and broken her looms, and killed one of her women, and she had not fought him. She had gone into a cave and shut a rock across the mouth of it. And the whole world had gone dark, and stayed dark, and every god there was had to stand outside the cave and work out between them how to get her to come out.

Rowena had sat forward somewhere in the middle of it, with her elbows on the table. When he got to the dark she went very still.

"How did they get her out?" she said.

"They made her curious. They made a great noise outside, laughing, and put a mirror at the mouth. She moved the rock a little to see what they were laughing at, and saw herself, and one of them took hold of the rock."

"And the brother?"

"They threw him out of heaven."

Rowena did not say anything for a while. Then she sat back again.

"Next," she said, and her voice was quite ordinary.

He gave her the queen who went down.

She was a queen of heaven, and she wanted the world under the world as well, which belonged to her sister. So she went down to it, and there were seven gates on the way down, and at every gate the keeper made her give up something: her crown at the first, and her jewels, and her rod of office, one thing at each gate, until she came to the bottom with nothing, and her sister looked at her once and she died. She hung on a hook for three days. And then she was brought back up, through all seven gates, and at each gate she took back what she had left there, and came out into the world with all of it, and her power, and somebody else was sent down in her place.

Rowena said nothing afterwards. She said nothing for long enough that he began to think he had said something wrong, and he did not know what, and he did not ask. She looked at the lamp. Then she looked at him and nodded, once, at nothing in particular, and he understood that he was to go on.

He gave her a lioness who had been made. A god had made her out of his own eye, to punish men who had stopped honouring him, and let her loose on them. And she had done what she was made for so well that she would not stop. They had to pour beer dyed red over a whole field, so that she would think it was blood and drink it, and fall asleep, before she could be called off.

"An instrument," Rowena said, "that did its work."

"Yes."

"And he blamed her for it afterward, I expect."

He did not know. He said so.

He gave her a goddess of the north, of a country of long winters, who took half of everybody killed in battle for her own hall. And she chose first. Before the king of the gods, who took the other half. He got his pick after hers.

Rowena laughed. It was short and surprised, and it was the first time he had heard her laugh.

"Before the king," she said.

"Before the king."

"I'd have liked her." She was still half smiling. "Go on."

Somewhere in the northern goddess, while he was looking for the next one, he realised he had been humming. It was not a tune. It was a few notes, under his breath, the way a man hums while he goes through a drawer for something. He stopped when he heard it. Rowena was looking at him, and she did not say anything, and he did not either.

He gave her the dark one next, and he gave her the same weight he had given the goddess of the city and the olive tree, because in the other world there were people who still lit lamps to her every evening, and he would not tell her as a curiosity. The gods had been losing a war against a demon they could not kill, and they had poured all their power together into a single goddess and sent her to do it, and she had done it. And out of her anger, in the middle of the fighting, had come another, black, with a necklace of heads, who drank the demons' blood before it could touch the ground, because every drop that touched the ground grew another demon. And when she had killed them all she did not stop. She went on dancing on the field, and the world shook under her, and none of the gods who had made her could make her stop. In the end her own husband lay down on the field in her way. She stepped on him before she saw who it was, and it was that which stopped her.

Rowena listened to all of it with her hands flat on the table.

"They let her out," she said, "and then found they had no way of calling her back in."

"Yes."

"Gods," said Rowena, "are very like councils."

He gave her a goddess of the forge.

She was a goddess of smiths, in a green country in the far west of the other world, and of poets, and of healing. And she was where you went for judgment, when you had a quarrel that needed settling. The same woman. The forge and the law.

"Emberfell would have built her a temple," Rowena said, and meant it, and he saw that she meant it, and filed that too.

And then, because it was late and he was tired past the point of choosing well, he gave her one who was not a goddess at all.

"She was a queen," he said. "Only a queen. She was real." He did not stop to wonder how a man knew a woman in a dream had been real. "A king from the south came to take her country, and he couldn't beat her army in the field. So he left a camp full of wine where her son's men would find it, and they found it, and drank it, and he took her son while they were asleep. Her son killed himself in his chains when he woke and understood what had happened. And she raised everything she had, and met the king, and destroyed his army. And when they found him among the dead she had his head cut off, and filled a skin with blood, and put his head in it." He turned his cup. "She'd sent him word before the battle that if he did not give her son back she'd give him his fill of blood. She kept her word."

Rowena did not say anything for a long moment.

"Again," she said.

He looked at her.

"Tell it again. From the wine."

So he told it again, from the wine, and she listened to it the second time with her chin on her hand and her eyes on the lamp, and when he got to the skin of blood she closed her eyes briefly and opened them.

"That one," she said. "That one I'd have known."

Then she looked at him properly, across the lamp, and her face went back a little toward the quay.

"What language are those names in?" she said.

He had given her some of the names. He had not meant to, and he had not thought about it while he did it; they had come with the stories, the way a name comes with a face. And she had heard every one of them and held them, the way she had held his footwork on the yard.

He had nothing. He sat there with the wine in front of him and looked for something and there was nothing, not a lie and not a truth, and she watched him not find it.

"And another thing," Rowena said. She said it without any edge at all, as a fact she was handing him because she thought he ought to have it. "You've sat at my table half the night and talked about gods. Not one. A crowd of them. Fighting, and breeding, and cutting each other's heads open, and dying, and coming back, and being lied to. You talked about them the way a man talks about weather." She tilted her head. "There isn't a priest I know who'd let a man talk like that in his hearing. Not in the capital and not here. I'm not a priest, so I don't mind. But you didn't notice you were doing it."

He had not noticed.

He sat with it for a moment. It was true, and there was nothing to say to it, and he did not say anything, and she let the silence stand for exactly as long as it needed to and then took it away again, the way she had taken her blade away from his throat.

"Your people," she said.

"My people?"

"I've watched them. I watch everybody who comes to my table. It's the whole of my work." She counted them on the table with one finger. "The big one at my door doesn't need telling anything, and never did, and he'll die in front of you without being asked. The one who makes jokes is better than he lets anybody see, and he lets them not see it on purpose. The physician looks at everybody's mouth." She stopped. "And the one with the ledger never looks at you when you talk. She looks at whoever you're talking to."

He did not say anything to that either.

Rowena looked at him a moment longer, and then at the window, where the red at the top of the mountain had gone down to a thread.

"I made you the wrong offer yesterday," Emeric said.

"You did."

"I'd like to make you another."

"Not grain."

"Not grain."

She considered it. She did not ask him what it was. He watched her not ask, and understood that she was not going to ask, because she wanted to see it the way it would be put in front of her, and not have it given to her in a room with a jug.

"Tomorrow," she said. "The same room. The same table."

"Thank you."

She stood up, and he stood with her, and she looked at him across the lamp. Her face did not go back to the quay. It stayed where it was, which was somewhere he had not seen it since he was twelve.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"No."

"I can see it. Anybody could. You've been walking round my town all day like a man with sand in his joints." She went to the door and put her hand on it, and did not open it yet. "Go to bed, Emmy."

Nobody had called him that in sixteen years.

He had not known he still had it. It came up out of somewhere under all the rest, under the capital and the mask and the ten years, and there were two people in the world who had ever called him by it, and one of them was dead, and the other was standing at the door with her hand on it, looking at him as though she were deciding what he was worth and had made up her mind a while ago.

She opened the door. Ward and her knight came off the wall together.

He went down the slope to the harbour house in the dark, with Ward ten paces behind him, and went up to the small room at the top, and lay down in his clothes, and slept.
