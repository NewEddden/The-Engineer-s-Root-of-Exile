Chapter 124 - Wayford Square

Wayford was quiet.

They came down the hill road into it on the sixteenth of August, two weeks after the hanging, on a market day, and there was no market. The square where Emeric had ridden through behind Ferra's chaise a month before, between stalls, with carts grinding through and a man selling chestnuts out of a pan where everybody had to slow down, had been swept. It was empty. A few people crossed it, quickly, on their own business, and looked at the riders coming down into it and then looked away. The inns down both sides of the south road had their doors open and nobody in them. At the top of the square, on the hill, the great house where Sethe had lived stood with its gates shut and a crown's man in grey at them with a pike, bored, leaning on the gatepost.

Wayford was the crown's, and then it was Kaldo's, and nobody in it yet knew what that meant. Emeric could see them not knowing it in every face that looked up at him and away.

"They're waiting," said Ferra, from the chaise, which had a new wheel.

"For what?"

"To see what you'll do to them." She looked at the swept square. "Sethe owned every silver Rand in this town. Every tavern, every barn, half the boats on the ferry. Everybody here owed him something, or worked for somebody who did." She shifted the book on her knee. "They don't know whether you've come to collect."

The crossing house stood at the head of the slope down to the ferry, square and stone, with the crown's arms worn smooth over the door. Its door was open. There were bags going in at it, as there had always been; the post did not stop for a hanging. But the man who stood in the doorway with the ledger under his arm, watching them go in, was not the heavy middle-aged man in his shirtsleeves who had looked up at the carriage in May. This one was young, and thin, in a clerk's black coat that was too big for him, with the look of a man who has been told to stand in a doorway and has not yet worked out why.

Ferra looked at him as they went past. She did not look at the window at the end of the house beside the back door. Emeric did. There was no lamp in it at all.



Aster was waiting for them at the posting house.

Emeric did not know him. He would not have known him in any room in the kingdom. He was a man of about thirty, neat and narrow and quiet, in a good brown coat that had been mended at both cuffs, standing in the yard of the posting house by the trough with his hat in his hands, as if he had been standing there some time. When Ward rode into the yard he stepped forward, and Ward's hand went to his sword, and the man stopped and held his hat out a little way from his body, in both hands, so that it could be seen there was nothing in them.

"Highness," he said. He did not look at Emeric's eyes. He looked past Emeric, at the chaise coming in under the arch behind, and at Ferra in it. And Emeric saw his face change.

Ferra had seen him too. She had stopped with one foot on the step of the chaise and her hand on the hood, and was looking at him across the yard, and her face had gone the way it went at a column that did not add, perfectly still, with everything behind it working.

"Aster," she said.

The man bowed. Not to Emeric. To her.

"Ferra," he said. "You've grown."

"I was a girl," said Ferra. "When you left."

It came to Emeric then, all at once, standing in the yard of the posting house with the reins in his hand: eleven years before he came. The year that ran through every page of Ferra's first finding, in the first summer, at the trestle, when she had laid out what she could not explain and written it down without guessing. The year Kaldo lost its magistrate. The year Hale became steward. The year Cobb's account began. The year Wexler's son left Kaldo for Wayford and did not come back.

Wexler's son.

"Highness," said Aster again, to Emeric this time, and made himself look at his eyes. "I'm Wexler's son. The baron that was, of Kaldo. I'd not blame you for wanting to ride on." He held his hat. "I've been a clerk in Sethe's hall for fifteen years. In his counting room. I kept the books of his barns and his boats." He paused. "Not the other books. There were other books. I wasn't given them, and I didn't ask for them, and I knew they were there." He looked down at the hat. "I've brought my own. All fifteen years. The barns and the boats. To the day. If the new holder of Wayford wants to know what his barony owns and what it owes, they're in a box in my room at the inn across the square." He looked up. "I'll carry them to the gate and go, if you'd rather. But they're true. Every line."

Nobody in the yard said anything.

Ferra came down off the step of the chaise and walked across the yard to him. She stopped in front of him, and she was shorter than he was by a head, and she looked up at him the way she had looked up at Keller.

"Did you know?" she said. "What your father did. To Kaldo."

"Not then." Aster did not look away. "I knew he was a hard man and I knew he was a greedy one and I knew I didn't want to be his steward, so I came here and asked Sethe for a clerk's place and got it, and wrote to my father twice a year and got no answer." He was quiet. "Then the plague. And then the prince came, and the ruling. I read it. Everybody in Sethe's counting room read it; Sethe had it read aloud at dinner, laughing." He turned his hat once. "I read what you'd found. The pages you scraped back with the candle. I knew his hand. I knew it from the books I learned on, as a boy, at his elbow." He looked at her. "I knew then."

"And you stayed with Sethe."

"I had nowhere else to go," said Aster simply. "And I thought somebody in that counting room ought to keep one set of books that was true."

Ferra looked at him for a long while.

Then she turned round to Emeric.

"Wayford needs a steward," she said. "Today. Not when the crown's grey men leave. Today. Somebody who knows where everything is, so the barns don't burn and the boats don't walk off down the river in the night while the town waits to see what you'll do to it." She did not look at Aster. "Under my office. Answering to my books. Weekly." She paused. "Acting. Until I say otherwise."

Emeric looked at Aster, standing by the trough with his mended cuffs and his hat, and thought of Wexler at the hall table in the first summer saying hello, and of the scraped pages in the candlelight, and of Paxton, kept on as steward of the land he had lost because he was the man who knew where everything was.

"Acting steward of Wayford," he said. "Under the Chancellor."

Aster bowed. To Ferra.



Saban's man at the posting house was a groom, a small bow-legged man with no teeth at the front, who had been taking Saban's silver for three years to tell him who came and went at Wayford, and who brought Emeric's horse back from the trough that afternoon and stood holding it longer than he needed to.

"You'll want to see the clock woman," he said, out of the side of his mouth, without looking at Emeric. "Master Saban said. If you came through. On the square. The shop with the clock over the door that's stopped."

"Why?"

The groom shrugged, and spat, and handed him the reins.

"Master Saban said you'd know," he said. "He said she's mad, and you like mad."

The shop was on the east side of the square, narrow, two windows wide, with a clock hung out over the door on an iron bracket: a big round face with a painted sun on it, whose hands stood at twenty minutes to four and had stood there, the groom said, since the clockmaker died in the plague. The windows were full of clocks. Small ones, on shelves, in wooden cases; a few that went, and many that did not. And in the middle of the left-hand window, on a square of green baize, by itself, a thing that was not a clock.

It was a box of polished brass, the size of a loaf, with a row of little wheels set into its top, eight of them, side by side, each with the numbers from nought to nine cut round its rim. Below each wheel a small window, and a brass crank on the side.

Emeric stood in front of the window and looked at it, and did not breathe for a while.

He knew what it was. He knew what it was the way he knew what a lathe was, or a dynamo, or a lens. He had seen one in a glass case in a museum on his other world, on a school trip, at the age of eleven, and had stood in front of the case until the teacher came back for him: a box of brass wheels made by a young man two hundred years before anybody ever thought of electricity, to add his father's tax accounts without mistakes.

He went in. A bell rang over the door.

The woman behind the counter was fifty or so, small and sharp and grey, with spectacles pushed up on her forehead and a jeweller's glass screwed into one eye, and she was bent over the opened back of a pocket watch with a pair of tweezers, and did not look up.

"If it's a clock, it's a Copper to look and a Silver to mend," she said. "If it's the clock outside, it's stopped and it'll stay stopped. If it's the thing in the window, it's not for sale, and if it were nobody'd buy it, so don't ask."

"It's the thing in the window," said Emeric.

She looked up. She took the glass out of her eye. She looked at him, at his face, and at his eyes, and he saw her see them, and then he saw her decide, very clearly, not to care.

"It's not for sale," she said.

"I don't want to buy it. I want to see it work."

Ottoline Fesk looked at him for some time over the top of her spectacles. Then she put down the tweezers, and came round the counter, and took the brass box out of the window, and put it on the counter between them, and turned the little wheels with her finger, one after another, to a number. Four thousand, two hundred and eighty-six. Then she turned a second set of wheels he had not seen, on the side of the box, under a little hinged lid, to another. One thousand, nine hundred and seventy-nine. Then she put her hand on the crank.

"Add," she said, and turned it.

Inside the box something clicked, and clicked, a quick neat ripple of little sounds like a fist of dice shaken in a cup, and the wheels on the top turned, by themselves, some of them one step and some of them two and one of them all the way round past nine to nought, and the one beside it, as it went past nine, turned on by one, by itself, as if somebody had nudged it. Then they stopped.

Six thousand, two hundred and sixty-five.

He had done it in his head before the crank stopped turning. It was right.

"It carries," he said.

Ottoline Fesk went quite still.

"The ones," he said. "When a wheel goes past nine, it turns the next wheel along by one. By itself. You don't have to remember to carry it." He looked at the box. "That's the hard part. That's the only hard part. Anybody can make a wheel with numbers on it. Making it carry is the whole of it."

"Nobody," said Ottoline Fesk slowly, "who has ever stood in this shop and looked at that box has ever once said the word carry."

"How long did it take you?"

"Nine years." She did not look away from him. "My husband said I was wasting his brass. My husband's brother said I was lonely. The magistrate's wife came in to see it and said it was very clever and she hoped it was a comfort to me." She put her hand flat on the top of the box. "It adds. It adds eight figures with no mistakes, every time, faster than any clerk in this town, and nobody wants it, because nobody in this town has eight figures to add."

"I've got a woman in Kaldo who adds eight figures before breakfast," said Emeric. "With a pen. And makes no mistakes either." He looked at the box. "She's about to have four baronies. She's going to have a great many more figures than eight. And she's only got one pair of hands."

Ottoline Fesk looked at him.

"People here say you're mad," Emeric said. "The groom at the posting house said so."

"They do."

"I don't think you are." He looked at the box, and then at her. "Will you come to Kaldo and work in the Chancellor's counting room? With that, and whatever you'd make next if somebody paid for the brass?"

Ottoline Fesk took her spectacles off her forehead, and folded them, and put them in her apron pocket. She looked round the shop: at the clocks on the shelves, the few that went and the many that did not, and the stopped sun over the door.

"What's she like?" she said. "Your Chancellor."

"Exact."

"Hm," said Ottoline Fesk. She did not sound as though she thought that was a recommendation. "I'll come in September. When I've sold the clocks." She picked up the brass box. "This comes with me. It's not for sale."



Ferra, when he told her that evening at the posting house, put her pen down and looked at him across the table with no expression at all.

"A clockmaker," she said.

"A clockmaker's widow. With a machine that adds."

"I add."

"You add better than anybody I've ever met. And in a year you'll have four baronies and a census of thirty thousand people and every one of them in your books." He looked at her. "You'll want somebody who can add as well as you do. There isn't anybody. So she built something that can."

Ferra looked at him for some time.

"I'll look at it," she said, and picked up her pen again, in the voice of a woman who was going to look at it very hard indeed, and find something wrong with it, and was already looking forward to doing so.



They camped on the second night out of Wayford, in the forest, by choice, because Ward wanted to see whether anybody followed, and nobody did.

Emeric sat by the fire after the others slept, with his back to a beech, and took his mother's stones out of his coat. Not the one in his pocket. The two from the faded blue velvet. He held them in his two hands in the firelight, one in each, and felt for the shape where a note had been, and it was there in each of them, very faint, like a room still warm.

Ward was awake. Ward was always awake. He was sitting across the fire with his back to a tree and his sword across his knees, and his eyes open, watching the dark beyond the firelight.

"How did you get in?" Emeric said.

He said it quietly. He did not look up from the stones.

"A garrison knight of a southern barony, with no summons and no letter. You walked into the palace in a plain coat and asked for the king, and the king saw you, the same day, alone. And gave you these, out of the treasury, from under the queen's eye, with his own hand." He turned one of the stones in his palm. "Knights of southern garrisons don't see the king. Earls wait a month to see the king. Corbin waits." He looked up, then, across the fire. "How?"

Ward looked at him across the fire and did not answer at once. The fire ticked. Somewhere in the beeches an owl went over.

"I asked," said Ward.

That was all he said. He said it the way he said everything, flat and short and true, and then he looked back at the dark beyond the firelight and did not say anything else.

Emeric sat with the two stones in his hands.

I asked. Fifteen years. Fifteen years of a knight who had stood behind him in every room of the castle in the capital, and come south with him in a plain carriage with a plan for the ninth night waiting in the forest, and stood behind him in every room in Kaldo, and never once, in all that time, been sent a letter that Emeric had seen, or written one. Who had sat in his own room at night with the door open a hand's width and his candle behind him on the floor. Who had changed the card in the line room on his own authority and said the prince does not go down alone.

Who could walk into the palace in a plain coat and ask for the king, and be seen, the same day.

He put the stones back in his coat. He did not ask anything else. He did not need to, yet. He had the shape of it, the way he had the shape of a note in a stone, and he could wait for the rest.



Vantic was at the South Gate when they came home, on the twenty-second.

She was not waiting for them. She was in the gate passage with Osric and her book, going through the toll returns for the month, as she did on the twenty-second of every month, and she looked up when the horses came under the arch, and closed the book on her finger.

"Four," she said, to Emeric, before anybody else could say anything. "The House heard on the seventeenth. By its own courier. Before the crown's rider reached Kaldo." She looked at him on the horse. "The House would like its factor to convey that it has noted the change in the security."

"I imagine it has."

"It has noted it with some care," said Vantic. "I have had three letters in five days." She looked at Ferra in the chaise, and something passed between them that Emeric did not follow, and then she looked back at him, and her eyes went to his left arm.

The sleeve was down. But the binding showed at the wrist, under the cuff, Ward's linen gone grey with the road.

She did not ask. He watched her not ask. She stood in the gate passage with her book closed on her finger and looked at the grey linen at his wrist, and he felt her look at it, a long exact look, the look she gave a column, and then she stepped forward, which she did not do, and put out her gloved hand, and touched the binding. Once. With two fingers. Lightly, at the wrist, the way a person touches a page to see whether the ink is dry.

"The House," said Vantic, "did not lend against a prince in a passage either."

She took her hand back.

"It would be obliged," she said, "if he stopped finding new places to go into it."

And she opened her book again and went back to the toll returns, and did not look up as they rode on through the gate.
