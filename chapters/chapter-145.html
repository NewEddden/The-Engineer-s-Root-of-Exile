Chapter 145 - Lantern Night

Vantic finished the count a little before noon on the sixth of March, and wrote the receipt, and sent for Ferra.

Ferra took Emeric with her. She did not say why. She came to his office door and said, "Now," and went, and he followed her across the yard and up the east tower stair, which was steep and turned the wrong way and had been built, Saban said, by a man who disliked his visitors.

The four iron boxes stood against the tower room's wall with their lids open, empty. The coin was in the House's own chests now, sealed with the House's grey wax, stacked by the door for the coast. Vantic sat at her desk in her grey dress with her hands folded on the House's book. She did not get up. She never did, for anybody.

"The House acknowledges," she said, "receipt of the last of the principal, and the interest to this day, in full." She turned the receipt round on the desk and slid it across to Ferra. Ferra read it. It was one paragraph long, in grey ink, in Vantic's small upright hand, and at the bottom it had her name and the date and the House's seal, pressed into a disc of grey wax the size of a thumbnail. "Sixty-three days of it," said Vantic, without expression, "that I'd been looking forward to writing the barony up for."

Ferra did not even blink. "I'm sure you had."

Vantic opened the House's book. She turned to the page Emeric knew, the first page, with the covenant's terms on it in her own hand and Ferra's under it, and below them, in the same grey ink, the line from December where she had struck the cap. She dipped her pen. She drew one line across the covenant, from the left margin to the right, steady, straight as a rule, and wrote the date at the end of it, and her initials, and blotted it.

That was all. There was no ceremony to it. A line in a book.

"The House's right to read every book in the barony ends with the covenant," said Vantic. She closed the House's book. "The House's right to read Grayrock's book, under the commission on the mine's stones, does not. That's a separate paper, and it runs as long as the House sells for you." She looked up at them for the first time. "So you'll have me a while yet. In the tower. Reading."

"We know," said Ferra.

"Good." Vantic looked at her a little longer. "It was well done," she said. "The whole of it. And early. I've had borrowers with ten times your coin who never paid a covenant down at all." She put the pen in its stand. "The House will say so. I'll write it."

Ferra said thank you, in the voice she used for suppliers, and turned, and went down the stair. Emeric went after her. Halfway down, on the turn where the stair went the wrong way and the light from the arrow slit fell across the steps, she stopped, and sat down on a step, quite suddenly, as if her knees had decided it without her. She put both hands over her face.

He sat down on the step below.

She did not cry. She sat with her hands over her face and breathed, slowly, for a while, and he sat on the step below her with his back against the cold wall and said nothing at all. After a while she took her hands away and looked at the arrow slit.

"It's paid," she said.

"It's paid."

"Since the loan." She looked at her hands. "I didn't think it could be done when you came. I'd have bet against it. I did, in my head, every night for the first year." She wiped her palms on her skirt. "I'd have lost."

"You did it."

"We did it," said Ferra, "and I'd thank you not to argue with me on a stair." And she got up, and went on down.



Kaldo had been folding lanterns for three weeks.

Nobody had decided it. The night the wall lamps first came on, three years ago that evening, had become a date in the town without anyone writing it down, the way the plague's first burial was a date and the night the river came into the lower ward was a date; and when the paper came in January, and went on coming, somebody in the school had asked Sela whether you could make a lantern out of it that went up. Sela had asked Tess. Tess had made one, out of four sheets of the thin and a hoop of split cane, with a cup of tallow under the open mouth of it, in the works yard one afternoon, and lit it, and let it go. It had gone up over the works roof and kept going, a little glowing bag of paper going up into the dusk on its own heat, higher than the water tower, higher than anything, until it was a spark, and then it had come down, still burning, in the middle of the dyehouse thatch.

The dyehouse had been put out. The question had been put to the table.

Ward had said no. Imani had said no, and had said why, at some length, with figures from the ward on burns. Kaldo was still half thatch. A town of thatch and paper lanterns full of fire was a town that wanted to burn down and was looking for an evening to do it.

Ferra had said: then launch them over the river.

She had a column for it, of course. She had kept the river's winds in a column for three years, because the barges went down slower or faster with them and that was money. In March, on two evenings in three, the wind in the valley turned round at dusk and ran downriver, toward the marsh and the sea, as the land cooled. Launch from the river wall on a downriver wind and every lantern went out over the water and the reeds and the empty marsh, and nowhere near a roof. If the wind was wrong, it waited a night.

And Tess had said: then make them come down cold.

She had done it in a week. A cake of wax and sawdust, pressed in a mould to a weight she set on Harl's beam, that burned for a quarter of an hour exactly and then went out, so a lantern went up on it, and rode the wind out over the marsh, and came down a long way off, dark, a bag of cold paper in the reeds. She had burned forty of them in the works yard with a sand timer to get the weight right, and drawn up a table, and every family in Kaldo who wanted a cake had come to the works for one and been given it with a card that said, in Tess's hand, DON'T BREAK IT.

Emeric had solved none of it. He had been asked nothing. He had been at the table when Ferra said river and at the works when Tess said cold, and both times he had opened his mouth to say something useful and shut it again, because they had already said it. On the afternoon of the sixth Sela came up to the keep with a lantern under her arm, folded flat, with its hoop and its cake, and gave it to him, and told him where he was to stand.



The wind turned at half past five.

Ferra watched it. She stood on the river wall at the top of the water stair with the whole town filling the wall walk behind her and the bank below, and watched the vane on top of the water tower; and the vane had been pointing up the valley all afternoon, toward the hills, and at half past five it hesitated, and swung, and came round, slowly, to point down the river toward the marsh. Ferra gave it another minute, to be sure. Then she turned round to the wall, and nodded, once, and the word went down the wall walk from one end to the other faster than any telegraph.

They had been told to light them from the wall lamps.

That had been Tess too. Every one of the wall's lamp housings had a little brass door in its side now, that Tess had cut, through which you could put a taper to the arc for a breath and draw it out burning. Lit from the light that had come on three years ago. The town thought that was the right way, and did it solemnly, queueing at each housing with tapers, the garrison men at the doors to see nobody put a hand in.

They wrote the names first.

Every lantern had one. Some had several. The plague's dead from the rows. The war's nine, and the coast man. Harl, in a dozen hands, because half the works had known him. Haddon the stoker, on a lantern the boiler crew held up between four of them. Children's names, small ones, that people wrote very carefully and then held so nobody else could see. Old names nobody on the wall but the writer remembered. The school had been folding lanterns for three weeks and had written a name on every one it folded, and Sela had told them that if they did not know anybody dead they were to write the name of somebody they had heard of who was, and the children had argued about who was the most dead, and the lantern Finn carried down the wall walk had on it, in large letters, ALL THE KINGS.

Emeric wrote two. Harl. Haddon.

And then, after a while, standing at the parapet with the pencil in his hand and the lantern on the stone in front of him, a third. Small. On the inside of the paper, near the hoop, where it would face the flame and not the town. Nobody saw him write it. He folded the paper back over it and did not look at it again.

Ferra was beside him. She was writing too. She wrote one name, and as soon as she had written it she put her hand over it, flat, so that her palm covered the whole of the word, and she held it there while she found her taper.

He did not ask. He did not look. She had not seen his third, and she had not asked either.

He knew whose it was anyway. He had asked her once, a long time ago, at her desk, who had taught her to keep a book like that, and she had not answered, and had looked at her page in a way he had not forgotten. He did not ask now. He let her keep her hand over it.

On his other side, Imani wrote her husband's name.

She wrote it in her clear plain hand, large, on the side of the lantern that would face the town, and did not cover it. When she had written it she held the lantern up a little and looked at it, the way she looked at a dressing she had just finished, to see if it was right, and it was. She did not look at Emeric. She did not need to. He had stood in her room by that man's chair and told her the most selfish true thing he had ever said to anybody, and she had sent him home, and then let him back in; and she wrote her husband's name on the side of her lantern facing the whole of Kaldo, where anybody on the wall walk who turned round could read it, because she had never once in her life been ashamed of having loved somebody.



The lower ward was full. The bank was full. The bridge was full, and the far bank, and the moorings, where the dock quarter had brought its own lanterns and its own names. Emeric kept his sense open the whole time, thin, all the way round, out of habit and because Ward had asked him to, and it was like standing in the middle of a field of fireflies. Thousands of them. Every person on the wall and the bank and the bridge a small warm note of their own, the way every body had one, crowded together in the dusk so close that he could not have told one from the next if he had tried. He did not try. He let it be one thing.

There was one note on the wall that was not small. Down the wall walk, by the north tower, a woman with short hair and a limp stood at the parapet with her hands empty and no lantern, looking up at the sky over the river. Her own note was high and clear and quick, an air mage's, and he could feel it holding very still, the way a hand holds still over a fire to see how warm it is.

Below the wall, on the quay at the foot of the water stair, there was a small commotion, and he felt that too before he saw it. Tess had come down to the quay on the motor, wheels down, lightened, the way Ward's rule said, through the crowd, and the crowd had made a lane for her the way crowds did for the girl from the powerhouse; and a big man from the dock quarter, who had been drinking since noon and did not know who she was, had come down the lane the other way, and found her in it, and taken hold of her push handles to wheel her out of his road.

The chair did not move.

Emeric felt the stones under the seat go down, hard, the way they had gone down through the dyehouse floor; Heavy, second notch, on stone. The man pushed. The chair sat on the wall walk like a rock in a stream. The man pushed harder, and swore, and took a better hold, and closed both hands round the handles; and Tess, without looking round, moved her left thumb.

The man let go of the handles with a yelp that carried along the wall from the water stair to the north tower, and stood shaking both hands, and staring at them, and at the chair. The people round him laughed. A woman nearby said, loudly, that the powerhouse girl's chair bit, everybody knew that, it was full of the works' lightning, and served him right. The man went off along the quay with his hands under his armpits. Tess moved her thumb back. Then she looked up at the wall, found Emeric at the top of the water stair, and gave him a very small nod, as if he had asked her a question about a fuse.

Nobody had seen anything. A drunk had been stung by a chair from the works, which was full of the works' lightning; everybody in Kaldo knew about the works' lightning. Ward, by the water stair, had seen all of it, and his face did nothing at all, and Emeric thought, watching him, that it was the most satisfied he had ever seen it.



Ferra let hers go first.

She had lit it from the lamp at the top of the water stair with her taper and held it while the cake caught and the paper filled, swelling and tugging in her hands like something alive. Then she lifted her hands, and opened them, and it went. Up, slowly at first and then faster, a pale bag of the barony's own paper with a light in it, going up off the wall into the dark over the river, turning a little as it went, with her mother's name on it, which nobody had seen and nobody ever would. The wind took it at the height of the tower and carried it out over the water.

She watched it go. He watched her watch it.

Then, with her face still turned up after it, without looking down, she put her hand out to her side and found his, and took it, and held it.

In front of the whole river wall. In front of half of Kaldo on the wall walk and the other half on the bank below, with the lanterns going up all round them now, one after another, and then in tens, and then in hundreds, so that the whole wall was lit from beneath by them as they rose. She did not let go. She did not look at him. She stood with her face up to the sky and her hand in his, holding it hard, as if the wall might move.

A little after that, on his other side, Imani's lantern went up with her husband's name on it toward the town. She watched it until the wind had it, and then she looked down, and saw Ferra's hand in his, and something in her face settled. She took his other hand.

So he stood there. On the river wall, in the dark, on the anniversary of First Light, with a hand held hard on each side of him and the sky over the river filling with lights.

He did not know what the town thought. He could feel it thinking, all round him, in the field of small warm notes, a ripple going out along the wall walk from the place where he stood, the way a ripple goes out from a stone in a pond. People looking. People nudging other people. Somewhere down by the north tower, somebody laughed, not unkindly. Somebody else said something, and somebody near them said shush.

Then the lanterns took all of it. There were too many of them going up for anybody to look at anything else.



Three hundred, Sela said afterward, from the wall alone. More from the bank and the bridge and the dock quarter. Nobody ever counted them all.

They went up into the dark over the river in a long slow drift, carried down the valley by the downriver wind, glowing, turning, rising, a river of small lights going up off the town and out over the water and the reeds toward the marsh. Up and up. Higher than the water tower. Higher than the ridge. Until they were a second sky, a moving one, under the real one, going out over the marsh in a great soft drifting band of light, and the town stood under it with its face turned up and did not say anything.

And a quarter of an hour after each one went up, it went out. Quietly. A light at a time, far out over the marsh, going dark, and drifting down into the reeds, cold. Tess's quarter of an hour. Emeric watched them going out, one after another, out there over the dark marsh, and thought that he had never seen anything in either of his lives that he liked better than that.

One of them did not go out over the marsh.

Arthur's. He had folded it himself, large, very large, out of six sheets of the red, and written on it the names of every knight he had ever heard of who had died in battle, which was a great many; and he had let it go at the wrong moment, in a gust that came round the water tower, and it had gone up in a long slant sideways instead of straight, and come to rest, burning merrily, wedged under the eaves of the water tower's roof.

The whole square below the tower saw it. The whole square stopped looking at the sky and looked at Arthur's lantern instead. The garrison went up the tower's outside stair with a ladder, and a long pole, and two buckets, while the square watched and called out advice; and it took them a good while, and one of the buckets went over the edge and down onto the square to great applause, and in the end Rylan went out along the eave on his stomach, with one leg hooked round a beam, and knocked the lantern loose with the pole. It fell into the square still burning. The square stamped on it. It was the loudest cheer of the night. Arthur, at the parapet, very large and sixteen and in disgrace, stood with his hands behind his back and his ears scarlet and pretended he had been watching the marsh.



Maren had a cart.

Nobody had known she was going to have a cart. It appeared at the foot of the water stair at dusk with a brazier in it and a great black pan over the brazier, and in the pan, in hot fat, rings of dough the size of a palm that she fried until they were brown and puffed and then dropped, hissing, into a crock of the castle's honey with a little of the coast boat's cinnamon in it, and sold, at a copper for two, wrapped in a twist of the dyehouse's terrible yellow paper. The queue for it went halfway up the water stair and stayed there all night. Hale, who had been sent to buy six, came back up with four, having been unable to resist two on the stair, and said that Maren had told him the price had gone up for him particularly.

She took more that night than the soap works took in a fortnight. Ferra worked it out the next morning from what Maren told her, and checked it twice, and looked at it a long while, and then wrote a line in the margin of the soap works' page that Emeric was not shown.



He saw Vantic only once, and only because Ferra did.

She was at the far end of the wall walk, by herself, in her grey, at the parapet by the east tower stair, with a lantern. He had not known she was going to come down. She held her lantern against the parapet's stone with one hand and wrote on it with the other, carefully, a few words, in what he supposed was grey ink; and then she lit it from the lamp beside her, with nobody to help her, and let it go, and stood and watched it go up out over the river with the others with her hands folded in front of her, exactly as she sat at her desk.

Ferra was looking at her.

Ferra had been beside her a minute earlier, he realised, going along the wall to see the wind vane once more; she had passed Vantic at the parapet just as the lantern was being written, and had seen it, close, and had come back. She was looking at Vantic now, down the length of the wall walk, with a face he did not know. Then she looked away, at the river. She did not say what she had seen. Not then, and not afterward, to him or to Imani, which under their terms she ought to have done, and which he understood without being told was a different thing, a thing that belonged to Vantic and not to her.

Down at the north tower, the woman with the limp had not moved. Three hundred lanterns had gone up past her, close, with nobody pouring into any of them, and her note had gone so still that he could hardly feel it. Things that flew on fire and nothing else. She stood there with her empty hands on the parapet and watched them go out, one by one, over the marsh, and did not say anything to anybody.



The fireworks began at nine.

Nobody had ordered them. That was the first thing anybody could say about them, afterward, and it was true. The lanterns had all gone, and the last of them had gone out over the marsh, and the town was beginning to come down off the wall and off the bank and go home to its suppers, when something went up from the far bank of the river, beyond the dock quarter, with a hiss like a stepped-on cat.

It burst over the river, high, in a great round flower of gold.

The town stopped coming down off the wall.

A second went up. Red, this one, and it burst and then burst again, a flower inside a flower. Then a green, that nobody had ever seen in a firework, that hung over the water and dripped. Then three together, white and gold and white. Then a thing that went up screaming and burst into a hundred small things that went on screaming, separately, all the way down. The whole of Kaldo, on the wall and the bank and the bridge, made the sound it had made at the furnace and the planes, and then went on making it, because the fireworks went on.

They went on for a quarter of an hour. Emeric had seen fireworks in the other life, a great many, on rivers, from bridges, over the heads of crowds that went on for miles. He had never seen anything like these. They were not better than the other life's. They were better than they had any right to be, with what anybody in this world had to make them out of, and they had been made, very plainly, by somebody who loved making them more than he loved anything else.

The last one was the biggest. It went up from the far bank in a long slow climb, trailing gold, and burst over the middle of the river in a great white sun that lit the whole valley from end to end as bright as noon, and the wall and the water and the faces; and then, quite slowly, as the town's breath went out of it, a single burning star came down out of the white, out of the middle of it, drifting, on the downriver wind, toward the far bank.

It came down on a hayrick.

The hayrick was in the farmyard below the dock quarter, on the far side of the river. It had been there all winter, the last of Farris's neighbour's hay, under a cap of thatch. The star landed on the thatch. For a breath nothing happened. Then the whole of the cap went up at once, in a sheet of orange, and lit the far bank as bright as the white sun had.

And somebody on the far bank, near the place where the fireworks had gone up, laughed. Emeric heard it over the water. A high, delighted laugh, that started before anybody else had understood what was happening and went on after they had.

Ward was already moving.



The rick burned down to the ground; nothing else did. The dock quarter's bucket line had it ringed before the cap had finished falling in, and the river was ten paces off, and the wind that had carried the star down onto the rick was carrying everything else away from the houses and out over the water, just as Ferra's column had said it would. Farris's neighbour lost his hay. He stood in his yard in his nightshirt and looked at the black ring where it had been and said, to nobody, that it had been very pretty.

Ward had the man in the guardroom by midnight.

He had found him on the far bank with his cart, in the middle of the field he had fired from, surrounded by the empty frames and burnt-out tubes of everything he had set off, and a great many more that he had not, in boxes, packed in straw. He had been sitting on the cart's tail when Ward and four men came up the field, eating one of Maren's honey rings out of a twist of yellow paper, and laughing. He was still laughing when they brought him across the bridge. He was laughing in the guardroom, on the bench, under the lamp, with his hands in his lap.

Emeric went down at half past twelve, because he could not sleep, and looked through the grille in the guardroom door.

A man in his thirties, thin, with a long jaw and his hair singed short at the front, in a coat that had been good once and was now scorched in a pattern of small round holes all down the front of it, like a coat that had been in a hailstorm of sparks. He was sitting on the bench with his back against the wall and his knees up, looking at the ceiling, and laughing still, quietly now, to himself, the way a man laughs at a good joke he has just remembered. He had his left hand on his knee. It had two fingers missing.

"Name?" Emeric said, to Ward.

"Fennick Crake," said Ward. "He says." He looked through the grille too, without expression. "Says he came to see the lanterns. Heard about them on the road. Brought a cart of fireworks so as not to come empty-handed." He was quiet. "Says the hayrick was the wind's fault."

"Was it?"

"It was a bit," said Ward.

The man on the bench, as though he had heard them, turned his head and looked at the grille, and at the two faces in it, and grinned, very wide, with a great many teeth.

"Did you see the green one?" he said.
