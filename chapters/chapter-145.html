Chapter 145 - The Notice

They came down behind the thorn in the dark, a long time before light, and Ferra was out of the hatch and down the ladder on the ship's flank before Osric had the legs settled, with her case in one hand and her book under her arm, and stood on the wet grass of the hill and waited for Imani.

Imani came down more slowly, because of the bags. There were four of them, and a small cask of lime that Osric carried down on his shoulder, and a box that Imani would not let anybody else carry, and she came down the ladder backward with it held against her chest and stepped off onto the grass and looked north toward the town, where there was nothing to see but the dark and a few lamps.

"Which way?" she said.

Osric pointed. He had the lamp. He did not say anything else and neither did they, the whole mile down off the hill and along the edge of Lisle's wood and up the south road into Wayford, and the square when they came into it was full of the column's fires burning low and knights sitting by them who stood up when they saw who it was, and on the west side of it the Staple, with its big doors standing open under the arches for the first time, Ferra supposed, in a very long while, and torchlight inside.

Ward met them at the doors. He looked at Imani first, and at the bags, and said, "Along the wall. Eleven. Five can't walk."

Imani went past him without a word.

Ferra stood in the doorway with her case. Ward looked at her.

"He's on the cellar stair," Ward said. "By the beam. He was." He paused. "There's blood on him. It's his own. It's stopped."

She went to find him.

She went down the stair once.

He was not on it when she came to the trap by the beam. He was at the counting house, a knight told her, with the relay. So she went down by herself, with a lamp from the hall, through the long brick vaults and the hole in the bricked arch at the end of them, into the whitewashed room on the other side, and stood in it.

She was there about as long as it would have taken her to read a page. Then she came back up the stair and through the hall past the pallets where Imani was already on her knees with her sleeves rolled, and out under the arches into the square, and stood in the dark by the Staple's pillars with her case in her hand until she was ready to go to the counting house.

She did not describe it then. She did not describe it afterward to anybody, not to the table, not in her book, which had a line for it that said only the lower rooms, seen, and the date, and her initial. There was nothing in it that a description would have added to, and she did not add things.

He was in Pennock's inner room when she came in, with Hobb at the side table under the lamp copying the relay's night log, and a sheet of paper in front of him, and a cloth in his hand that he put in his pocket when he saw her. There was a mark on his lip where he had missed some of it.

"Ferra," he said.

"Your Highness."

She put the case down on the table and opened it and took out the book and a pen and her ink, and sat down across from him, and uncapped the pen.

Before anything else, she wrote the entry. She wrote it in the form she had written it in over the river and over the coast, because the form was the point, and it had been the point on the first day she wrote it and would be on the last.

Risk accepted. The holder of the barony and the Chancellor both in Wayford, and both halves of the key. The Secretary of the Arcane holds Kaldo under the sealed letter. And the date, and the hour, which was a little before the fourth bell of the morning.

He watched her write it. He did not say anything about it. He had seen her write it before.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her. He told her in order, the way she liked, the gate and the gallery and the hall, and the stone and the servants, and Dray in the yard, and the gun, and the ball going low, and Rylan in the strongroom, and Marrow. He told her that Marrow was not the voice from the ledge, and why he knew it, which took some time, because she did not know about the ledge or the voice or the beat or any of it and he had to begin at the beginning. She wrote it all down. Then he told her about the cellar, which she had seen, and the book, which she had not, and he pushed it across the table to her, the brown calf ledger, and she opened it and read the first page and the column heads and the margin note beside the first line, and closed it again, and put her hand on it.

"You were going to send them north," she said.

"Yes."

"And now."

"Now they hang here. Tomorrow. In the square." He did not look away from her. "By my order. As lord of the barony. The barony has high justice and they are of the barony. It doesn't need the Crown and it doesn't need a writ. It needs me to say it in front of people who can hear me, and to be seen saying it, and then to do it."

She wrote that down too. She wrote it exactly, as he had said it. Then she looked at what she had written for a while.

"It will want a notice," she said.

He had the first draft of it done by the time the light came up grey in Pennock's window, and it was too long, as his first drafts always were, and she took it and read it and began to cut.

The charge was the first thing she cut. He had written the whole of it, the intake and the chairs and the stones and the ring and the pit, in plain words, a paragraph of it, and she read it twice and drew a line through all of it and wrote above it, in the margin, one line.

For holding the people of this barony in secret, unlawfully, and causing their deaths.

"That's not all of it," he said.

"It's all of it the notice needs. The rest is for the square." She did not look up. "The ones who can read will read the notice aloud to the ones who can't. By the second hand it'll be half wrong. By the third it'll be a story. Give them less and they'll get it right." She went on down the page. "They'll hear the rest tomorrow, from the people it was done to. That's what the square's for. Not a page."

He did not argue.

She left the names as they were, all five, whole, with their titles, because a notice that did not name the lords it hanged was not a notice. She left the hour. She cut by order of His Highness and put by order of the lord of Wayford, because that was the authority it rested on and the other was only who he was. And she read the last paragraph three times without cutting anything in it, and then read it aloud, quietly, to hear whether it said what it meant.

From this day, in the barony of Wayford and in every barony held by the lord of Kaldo, no person shall be held against rent. A debt may be claimed against a man's goods and against his wages. It may not be claimed against his freedom to leave. Every hold of that kind now standing is void.

"Twenty-three," she said.

"Twenty-three on the five houses' books. There'll be more on everybody else's." He rubbed his eyes. "Paxton's. Every lord in the four baronies with a tenant who owes him. They won't like it."

"No." She read it again. "Do you want it in the same notice as the hanging?"

"Yes."

"They'll say you did it to buy the crowd."

"Some of them will." He looked at her. "It has to be in the same notice. It has to be on the same page as the five names. If it's on another page on another day, it's a law. If it's on this one it's the reason. They held people against rent, and this is where holding people against rent went, and it stops, today, on the same page." He paused. "The crowd can think what it likes about why."

She read the paragraph once more, and did not change a word of it, and set the sheet down square to the edge of the table.

"The map," she said.

He drew the map himself at the table in the Ambry back room, with the printers standing silent on their trestles along the old bin wall and Wenna and Dace at the far end of the room with the pots of plain ink, and the root dark in its socket on its board.

It was the square, and the streets that came into it, and the Staple on its west side and the counting house and the inn and the church, and the pump, drawn plainly in heavy lines the way a child draws a house, so that a man who had never read a word in his life would know it for Wayford the way he knew his own hand. On the Staple he drew the ram on its rod. In the middle of the square, on the flags by the pump, he drew a gallows: two posts and a beam, and five short lines hanging. Over the top of the whole sheet he drew the sun, round, with its rays, straight up at its height, which was noon, and would mean noon to anybody who had ever worked a field. And along the bottom, under the notice, a row of small round faces with their mouths shut, which was a crowd, which was an invitation, which Ferra looked at for a long moment and did not cut.

Then he put it into the printers.

She had seen him do it at Kaldo for the primers and the well standard, with the first printer, and she watched him do it here, and it was the same, and it was not quite. He sat at the table with the notice and the map in front of him and his hand on the board where the root sat, and shut his eyes, and went wherever it was that he went, and the page went into the root a line at a time from wherever that was, and from the root into the two printers' page stones, so that each of them held it whole and would print it for as long as it was given paper and ink, without anybody writing anything. The primers at Kaldo had taken him an hour a page. The map took him most of the morning.

He did not go in at once. She noticed that. He sat at the table with his hand on the board and his eyes shut for a long time, much longer than it took him at Kaldo, before anything happened; he sat the way a man sits at the top of a ladder he has fallen off, and she could see his hand on the board, and it was quite still, and then it was not, and then it was still again. Then he went in. She knew the moment he did, because his face changed, the way it did, went quiet and far off. She did not know what had happened on the cellar stair in the night. Ward had said there's blood on him, it's his own, and nothing else, and she had not asked Ward and would not ask him, and she sat at the far end of the back room with her book open and watched his hand on the board and wrote nothing.

At a little before the noon bell the first printer began to move.

It went along its rails the way it always went, the carriage and the head and the small dry tapping of the head on the sheet, and the notice came up under it line by line in plain black ink, and then the map, heavy line by heavy line, the square and the streets and the Staple with its ram, and the gallows by the pump, and the sun. Then the second printer began. Wenna took the first sheet off the carriage and held it up to the window and looked at it and did not say anything, and laid it on the drying rack, and fed the next sheet.

"How many?" said Dace.

"All of them," said Emeric. He had his eyes open. He looked very tired. "As many as there's paper for by dark."

The stay was in her case.

She had carried it in her case since the spring. It was the form she had said the ninth clause wanted, on the day at the long table when he had given her the clause and she had read it at the same speed as the eight before it. A stay that isn't in a form is a mood. She had written the form that night, a single sheet, ruled, with the clause at its head in her own hand and a space below for the act stayed and the reason and the date and her name, and she had written it to hold. She had had a clerk copy it and put the copy in the Kaldo strongroom with the Standing Order and kept the original in her case, and she had never filled one in, and she had not until this morning taken it out.

She took it out now. She sat at the table in Pennock's inner room in the middle of the afternoon with the door open onto the counting house and the sound of the square coming in through it, a great deal of sound now, and laid the form on the table in front of her beside her book, and read it.

The Chancellor may stay any act of the holder by a stay in writing. A stay so written stands. The holder may not set it aside.

It would hold. She knew it would. She had written it so that it would, and he had written the clause so that it would, and he had put her there, at the one place in the barony where a single sheet of paper could stop him, on purpose, so that there would be somebody who could. She could fill it in now. The execution of the five lords of the Staple, ordered by the holder this morning, without writ. And a reason. She could think of several. That the Crown had a court. That Keller had a desk. That a baron's own authority had never in her book sent anybody to a rope and it would be the first, and a first was a thing that could not be taken back. That a man who had sat on a cellar stair in the night with blood running out of his nose was not, perhaps, a man who should be deciding anything on the following morning. That it was in the ninth clause precisely for this.

She thought about the whitewashed room, which she was not going to describe.

She thought about the book on the table in the inner room, the brown calf ledger, in a small upright hand she had first seen on a printed page in the spring, a fortnight apart, twice. Held against rent. Losses. She had read those pages. She had read them aloud at the long table with the figures as figures, and Imani had put a name to held against rent, and nobody had known what intake meant, and Ferra had written in her book that nobody did. That had been in the summer. The first line in the brown book was six years old. She had held the barony's books for more years than that, and Wayford's for two, and she had read every figure in every one of them, and the whole time, across the square from the Wayford counting house where she had sat and audited Pennock twice, there had been a whitewashed room.

She did not write that down either. She had written the one line about it that she was going to write, in the spring, after Sethe's boxes. Read the barony's books. Did not read the baron's letters. It had been true and it was still true and writing it again would not make it truer.

She uncapped her pen. She held it over the form, over the space for the act stayed.

She held it there a while.

Then she capped it, and put the form back in the case, and shut the case, and opened her book at the day, and wrote in the same hand as everything else in it.

The stay under the ninth clause was available against the order of this morning concerning the five of the Staple. It was not used. And the hour. And her initial.

The letter to Keller went at noon, with the rider, north.

Emeric wrote it himself and Hobb copied it fair, and it was short, and it was accurate as far as it went. It said that the five lords of the Staple at Wayford had been taken under arms by the barony, and that evidence had been found in the Staple of the unlawful holding and deaths of the barony's people, and that the barony had exercised its high justice, and that the sentence would be carried out at noon on the following day, and that a full account would follow. It asked for nothing. It was dated that morning.

Ferra read Hobb's fair copy before it was sealed. She did not change anything. She wrote in her own book beside the date To the Crown's justice, Keller, by the rider, and under it, because it was a figure and she wrote figures, five days to his desk.

She did not say it aloud. She looked across the table and he was looking at the same line in her book, upside down, and she knew that he had done the same sum, on the same morning, for the same reason, and had written the letter anyway, and had not sent for Keller, and would not.

Neither of them said anything.

Imani found them in the Staple's hall in the late afternoon, and she did not come to them. She sent a knight to say that she wanted the prince, and he went, and Ferra went with him, because it was daylight and the hall was full of people and she wanted to hear it.

Imani was on her knees by the wall beside a pallet, with her sleeves still rolled and her hands clean and red from the lime water, and she did not get up.

"Are they to be put in front of the crowd?" she said.

"If they'll speak."

"If they'll speak." Imani looked at him. It was the look she gave a man in the ward who had told her something about his own body that she knew to be wrong. "Some of them can't hold a cup. One of them doesn't know what year it is. You want to stand them up in front of two thousand people and have them say what was done to them, so the two thousand will believe it and you can hang five lords with the town behind you."

"Yes," said Emeric.

He did not dress it. Ferra watched Imani hear that he did not, and watched it land.

"Then each of them chooses," Imani said. "Not you. Not me. Each of them. And I ask them, not you, and not a knight, and not with you standing there. And any one of them who says no, or doesn't answer, or can't, stays here with me, and nobody asks them twice."

"Yes."

"And they sit. On the day. They don't stand if they can't. And I'm beside them."

"Yes."

Imani looked at him a moment longer. Then she nodded and turned back to the pallet, and he went away down the hall, and Ferra stayed where she was by the pillar, because nobody had told her to go.

Imani asked them.

She went along the wall from pallet to pallet, all eleven, on her knees, and asked each of them the same thing in the same low voice, and waited for the answer, however long it took, and it took a long time with some of them. Ferra could not hear what she said. She could see the faces. Some of them said yes quickly, as if they had been waiting all their time in that room to be asked. Some of them looked at Imani for a long time and then said yes. Two said no. One did not answer at all and was only looking at the arches, and Imani touched his hand and let him be.

The last was a man near the door, whose name Ferra found out later from Imani's register was Wat, who had come from a holding of Hollin's four years before, and who looked, to Ferra, older than anybody she had ever seen. When Imani asked him he did not say anything. He shook his head. Imani nodded and began to get up off her knees to go to the next.

He put out his hand and took her sleeve.

He did not say anything. He held her sleeve, and when she looked at him he let go of it and pointed, with one finger, toward the doors, toward the square. Then he put the finger against his own chest.

"You don't want to speak," Imani said.

He shook his head.

"But you want to be there."

He looked at her. Then he nodded, once, very hard, so that it hurt him, and Ferra saw it hurt him, and he did it again.

Imani wrote something in her register. Then she put her hand over his on the blanket, and held it there for a while, and went to the next.

Marrow was in the counting house.

He had been moved there from the Staple in the early morning in a closed cart, with Rylan inside it with him and two knights on the box, and put in the small stone room behind the relay room that Pennock used for the barony's silver, which had a door with a lock and no window. Rylan was on a stool outside the door. He had been on it since the morning, and Ferra had passed him four times in the course of the day on her way through to the relay and each time he had been sitting the same way, with his hands on his knees and his helm on the floor beside him, looking at the door.

Adlow was in the other small room, beside it, where Pennock kept the old rolls. He had asked for paper. Nobody had given him any.

The fourth time Ferra passed, in the evening, she stopped.

"Have you eaten?" she said.

Rylan looked up at her. "Somebody brought something."

"Did you eat it?"

He thought about it. "No."

She went and got bread and cheese from the counting house kitchen and brought it back and gave it to him, and stood there until he had eaten some of it, and then went on to the relay room without saying anything else, and did not write it down.

By the middle of the afternoon they were coming in off the roads.

Ferra saw it from Pennock's window. The notice had been up since before the noon bell, pasted on the counting house door and the church door and the pump and the Staple's own pillars and every post on the junction road, and carried out of the town by Pennock's runners and the column's riders and a boy of the inn's on a mule, to the Tollet road and the Milford road and the mill road and the villages along the stream, and nailed up at every crossroad and every alehouse door, a sheet of Kaldo's paper with five names on it and a gallows and the sun at its height. And they came.

They came on foot along the south road out of Lisle's wood, whole families, with bundles, as if for a fair. They came in carts from the Milford road with the children in the back. They came singly, men in their working clothes who had put down whatever they were doing in whatever field when the sheet went up on the post at the end of it and walked. By the evening the square was full at its edges and the streets that came into it were filling, and the inn had nothing left to sell, and people were sitting on the church steps and the counting house steps and the edge of the pump's trough, and looking at the Staple, and at the gallows the column's carpenters had begun to build on the flags beside the pump, and talking in low voices, and not going home.

A great many of them were reading the notice. Most of them were being read to. Ferra watched a man at the pump read it aloud to a knot of women, slowly, with his finger under the line, and heard him come to the last paragraph and stop, and read it again, and she saw one of the women put her hand over her mouth.

She told him in the square, at dusk, standing by the half-built gallows with Ward three paces off and the town all round them, because it was the only place and hour in the whole of that day when the two of them were not in a room.

"I nearly filed it," she said.

He did not ask what. He looked at her.

"This afternoon. It was on the table. The form. I had the pen over it." She looked at the gallows. "I didn't."

"I know," he said. "I read your book."

It was all either of them said about it. He went back to the counting house. She stayed a while by the gallows, and then she went back too, and sat at Pennock's table in the inner room with the book open at the day and her case beside it on the table, and the lamp lit, and the square outside the window full of voices in the dark.

She opened the case. The form was on top, where she had put it back. She took it out and laid it on the table beside the book, and uncapped her pen, and held it over the form, over the space for the act stayed, for a long moment, in the lamplight, the way she had held it in the afternoon.

Then she put the pen back into its case.
