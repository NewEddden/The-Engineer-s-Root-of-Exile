Chapter 83 - Wheels

The list had been in the bench book since November.

Tess gave it to him on the last Monday in March, when he came up to the ward in the evening, without being asked and without saying anything first. She opened the book to a page near the front, turned it round on the board across the arms of the chair so that it faced him, and sat back and watched him read it with her hands folded, the way Ferra watched Vantic read a ruling.

At the top, in her forge hand, underlined: What it doesn't do.

He had told her to write it down. He had said it on his way out of the door, the week before he went north, the way you say a thing to a child to stop it asking, and he had not thought about it once since. She had. The page was full. It went over onto the next one.

The seat was too high. Two fingers too high, she had written first, and then crossed out two fingers and written two inches over the top, in a different ink, which meant she had gone back to it since the block. When it was too high her hands came to the rims at the wrong place and she pushed with her wrists instead of her shoulders, and her wrists hurt by night.

The wheels stood straight up. They should lean in at the top. She had worked this out, she wrote, by barking her knuckles on every doorpost in the ward, and then noticing that the doorposts were always at the top of the wheel and never at the bottom, and then sitting with her eyes shut and imagining the wheels tilted, and she had drawn it from the back, two lines like a narrow V standing on its point, with the ground at the top of the V. A wheel that leaned in, she wrote, would keep her knuckles off the doorposts and stand more steadily if she leaned, and she thought it would go straighter. She had put a question mark after the last part.

It would go straighter. On his other world it was called camber, and the people who built chairs for racing set it to the degree. He had not thought of it. He had drawn the first chair from a picture in his head of a hospital chair in a corridor, and nobody in a hospital corridor ever needed to go anywhere fast.

The little front wheels caught. They had been made bigger, because Bram had been right about the ward floor, and they still caught, because they turned on an iron pin in an iron fork and the pin bound when the fork was dirty, which was always.

It ground. Every turn of the big wheels, iron on iron at the hub, a sound like a quern. Under that she had written: Everybody hears me coming. I would like to decide when they hear me coming.

The foot bar was a hand too far out. She had measured her own legs with the block and Bram's cord, lying flat, and written the figure.

At the bottom of the second page, on its own line, underlined twice: No handles at the back. Nobody pushes.

He read it all twice. When he looked up she was still watching him.

"Which of these can't you do?" she said.

"None of them," he said. "Now."



The lathe's first job that was not for itself was hubs.

Bram turned them. Emeric could have, and Bram did not ask him to, and Emeric did not offer. He stood at the door of the lean-to and watched a man who had made things round by eye for thirty years put a bar of brass between the centers and bring the tool up to it on the rest, and feel the cut come, and back off, and come in again. The first shaving came off in a curl as bright as a girl's ribbon. Bram stopped the belt and picked it up off the bed and looked at it, and then he looked at the bar, which was round, and he had not made it round. The machine had.

He did not say anything about that. He started the belt again.

They made her two hubs with brass bushes pressed into them and two axles turned true to the gauge, so that the wheel ran on brass and oil instead of iron on iron, and when Bram spun the first one on its axle in the vise it went round and round and round with no sound at all but the air. He let it run down. Then he spun it again, for no reason that he gave.

The front wheels he set on turned pins in brass, with a cap over the top to keep the dirt out, which was Bram's idea and not Emeric's, and which Emeric would not have thought of in a year.

The spokes they took off Tess's old wheels and laced again into the new hubs, set so that the rims leaned in at the top the way her drawing said. That was the part Emeric had been watching for. In October the wheels had come off the bench like a drunk man, and Vesk had put his hands on them and they had come round. This time Bram put the laced wheel on the lathe between the centers, set a pointer on the rest a hair off the rim, and turned it by hand, slowly, and where the rim came close to the pointer he slackened a spoke and where it ran away he tightened one, a quarter turn at a time, round and round, for most of an afternoon.

Vesk came to the door of the lean-to in the middle of it. He stood and watched Bram for a while with his hands in his armpits, and watched the pointer, and the gap between the pointer and the rim, which was the same all the way round now and getting smaller. Then he went back to the bellows in bay three, where Orrin was talking, and did not come back.

When it was done Bram spun it on the lathe and stood back and sighted along it, as he had sighted along Vesk's wheel in October, with one eye shut.

"Well," he said.

"It's true."

"It's as true as his was." He did not say Vesk's name. "And I did it."

The first chair had been good because magic had made it good, Emeric thought, and there had been one of it, and if it broke there would be a Vesk or there would not. This one was good because a machine could make it again. He did not say that either. He did not need to. Bram was looking at the lathe.

"We could make another," Bram said.

"We could make ten."

Bram looked at him sideways. There were old men in the lower ward who had not been out of their doors since their hips went, and a carter's boy on the west side with one leg, and the woman with the walking frame Abloh had made her out of a towel rail, and Bram knew every one of them better than Emeric did, because Bram had shod their horses and mended their pots. Emeric watched him count them.

"Ten," said Bram, and took the wheel off the lathe.



Imani signed her out on the second Saturday in April, for the day, against her own judgment, and said so, in front of Tess, at the door.

"Down to the powerhouse," Imani said. "The day. In the chair. Back before the light goes."

"Yes."

"It's downhill there." Imani looked at Emeric. She had a way of looking at a man as though he were a wound she had been asked to give an opinion on. "Who pushes her back up?"

He had been waiting for it since November.

"I do."

"The first time," said Imani. "You. Not a boy. Not four of the garrison and an argument in the rain. You." She looked at the back of the chair, where there was nothing to push with. "On what?"

Tess shut her eyes.

It took Bram twenty minutes and two pieces of ash out of the lean-to's offcuts, shaped and pegged into the back rails at the height of a man's hands. Tess watched him fit them with her mouth shut tight. When he had finished she reached round behind her and took hold of one, to see where it was, and let go of it as if it were hot.

"For the hill," she said. "Only for the hill."

"Only for the hill," said Bram, and went to wash his hands.



Going down was easy, which was the trouble with it.

She rolled herself out of the ward and down the cart street with her hands on the new rims, quick and quiet, so quiet that a woman shaking a cloth out of a window above the street did not look round until she had gone by, and Tess saw her not look round, and Emeric saw Tess see it. She went down two streets on her own. At the top of the steep lane into the lower ward she let him take the handles, because the lane was steeper than her arms, and he held the chair back down it with his heels in the cobbles while she sat with her hands in her lap and Imani walked alongside on the other side of the gutter, with her arms folded, not hurrying. At the water gate the chair went down off the sill with a bump that he felt in his teeth. Below the gate the path ran down the outside of the wall to the mill yard, stony and steep and wet, and he took that step by step, leaning back against the weight, and it occurred to him about halfway down that he had walked this path a thousand times and never once noticed that it was steep.

The powerhouse was loud with the river.

Tess sat in the door of it without going in, while the belt went round twenty times and more. Then she went in. She went to the dynamo first, and sat beside it with her hand flat on the housing, not touching anything that turned, the way you put your hand on a horse, and then she went to the board and read every gauge on it, and then she went to the lean-to and sat in front of the lathe, which she had never seen except in a drawing, and looked at it.

"It's smaller," she said.

"Than what?"

"Than I thought." She put her hand on the rail of the bed, flat, the way she had on the dynamo. "I thought it would be bigger. Something that makes everything else."

She spent the day. Bram let her hold the lever on the dynamo for a count of a hundred this time, and stood over her with his hands half out the whole while as if she might fall in, and she did not. Imani went back up to the ward at noon and came down again at four, and stood in the mill yard with her arms folded and watched the light.



Going up was not easy.

The path from the mill yard to the water gate was the first of it, and the worst. It had looked steep from the top that morning. From the bottom, with the chair in front of him and Tess in the chair, it looked like a wall someone had leaned back a little way so that he could not quite say it was a wall. He put his weight into the handles and went at it. The front wheels found every stone. Twice in the first twenty yards a stone stopped them dead and the chair tried to go back down past him, and he held it, and found the stone with his boot, and lifted the front over it, and went on.

Imani walked beside the path on the grass, with her arms folded.

She did not say anything. She did not help. She did not offer to help, and she did not tell him how, and when he stopped halfway up to breathe she stopped too, at exactly the distance she had been walking, and looked at the river, and waited, and when he went on she went on.

The sill of the water gate was a step. A hand high, stone, worn into a dip in the middle by a few hundred years of feet. He pushed the front wheels into it, and the chair stopped, and he pushed harder, and it stopped harder.

"Tip it," said Tess.

He stopped.

"Push down on the handles," she said, not looking round. "Tip me back on the big wheels. The little ones go up over. Then push." Then, because he did not move at once: "I've watched people do it all winter. I know how it's done. I just can't do it."

He tipped her back. The little wheels came up off the stone, and he rolled the big ones into the sill and over it with a lurch, and set her down on the far side, and Tess said "Good," in exactly the voice Ward used, and he would have laughed if he had had the breath.

The lower ward was cobbles, and then it was not cobbles, it was mud where the cobbles had gone, and then it was cobbles again. The lane had a gutter down its middle that carried the thaw and everything the houses on either side had put into it, and the gutter was a hand deep and a foot wide, and to cross from one side of the lane to the other he had to go at it slantwise, one wheel at a time, or the chair went into it nose first. A cart came down the lane toward them, a coal cart with a man walking at the horse's head, and there was no room for both, and the man looked at Emeric, and at the chair, and did not know what to do, and backed his horse twenty yards up the lane into a gateway with a great deal of noise, so that the whole lane came to its doors to see why.

Imani walked beside the chair through all of it with her arms folded.

The children came out after the cart, and followed them. Not close. At the distance children keep when they know they are not supposed to be watching. A woman at a door with a baby on her hip looked at the prince of the realm pushing the girl from the powerhouse up the hill with his shirt stuck to his back, and then at Imani walking beside them doing nothing, and then she looked at Imani a good deal longer than she had looked at him, and went in.

Below the north well the lane turned and steepened, and the turn was cut into the hill so that the outside of it was higher than the inside, and the chair wanted to run sideways down the slope into the wall of the corner house. He had to hold it straight and push it up at the same time, with one arm doing more than the other, and his shoulder began to burn, and then his back.

He understood, somewhere on the turn below the north well, why Imani was doing it this way.

She was not punishing him. Or not only. Every one of the things he was finding out, the sill and the gutter and the cart and the camber of the turn, Tess found out every time she went anywhere, and could do nothing about any of them but ask. Imani had pushed this chair, or had her women push it, on every errand that had taken Tess out of the ward since October. She knew every stone of it. She could have told him all of it in a sentence at the door that morning. She had decided he would learn it in his back.

At the top of the cart street, at the door of the ward, he stopped. His arms were shaking. He leaned on the handles and breathed.

Imani unfolded her arms. She looked at him for a while, in the last of the light, with his hair stuck to his forehead and his hands still on the ash pegs Bram had fitted that morning. She did not say anything at all. She went in, and held the door, and Tess rolled herself in past her, on her own, without the handles, quick and quiet, as if she had been out for a walk.

He stood in the street until his arms stopped shaking. Then he went in after them.

Tess was by the window already, with the bench book open on the board, writing. She looked up when he came to the end of the bed.

"Three," she said.

"Three what?"

"Places it can't go without somebody." She turned the book round. The sill of the water gate. The gutter in the lane above Cooper's Row. The turn below the north well. Each one on its own line, with a small drawing beside it of what was wrong. "The path from the mill doesn't count. Nobody should have to go up that."

He looked at the three lines for a while, and at the drawings, which were good.

"No," he said. "Nobody should."



Imani signed her out for good at the end of the next week.

She did it without ceremony, in the register at the door, in the same hand she had written Tess in with in May, and she wrote nothing under it but the date. Tess went home to the lower ward, which was downhill of nearly everything, and from the next morning she was at the powerhouse every day, from the time the mill yard opened until the light went, and she came up the path from the river at the end of every day behind one of the yard boys, who had been told by Bram that the job paid a copper a week, and who held the handles and tipped her at the sill exactly as she told him to.

He found her there on the Tuesday, in the corner by the dynamo where the noise of the river came in through the belt slot, with the bench book shut on the board and another book open on her knees. His book. The one he had written for her. She had got to the fourth chapter, the one about what happens in a wire when a magnet goes past it, and she had her carving point behind her ear and a stack of loose sheets on the floor beside the chair with drawings on them, and she did not look up when he came in.

The field hummed in the housing beside her. The belt slapped. Out in the mill yard a boy was singing something with no tune.

He left her to it, and went to see about the masons.
