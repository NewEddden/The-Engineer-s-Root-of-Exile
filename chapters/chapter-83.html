Chapter 83 - The Bladed Thing

Nobody at the works knew what it was, and by the end of the winter everybody at the works had decided.

It began as boards. Emeric came down to the pattern shop one morning in the new year with a bundle of thin pine sheets under his arm, each the size of a large book, each with a curve cut along one edge, and a number burned into the corner with a hot wire. There were eleven of them. He laid them out on the joiner's bench in order, one to eleven, and asked Harl, a joiner off the long street whom Bram had taken into the pattern shop when the orders outgrew the apprentices, to make a thing whose shape passed through all eleven curves at once.

Harl looked at the boards for a long time.

"It twists," he said.

"It twists."

"All the way along. Flat here," he put a finger on the first board, "and near on its edge here." He put a finger on the eleventh. "Like a spoon somebody wrung out."

"Three of them," Emeric said. "Round a hub. The same."

He had drawn the curve in the delve, over a week of nights, which was not a thing anybody at the works would ever know and was, by now, not a thing he had to think about. After the nib it had felt almost restful. A blade of the right twist and the right width, standing in the plain light where nothing moved unless he moved it, and then eleven slices taken across it at eleven places from hub to tip, each slice a curve. Each curve he had carried up and drawn onto a pine board, full size, by the plumb-line trick, the true line held in his eye while his hand followed it. Then he had cut the boards himself with a coping saw, badly, and trued them with a file, less badly. It was the most careful carpentry he had done in either of his lives, and it had taken four evenings, and Harl had watched him do the last one through the pattern-shop window and said nothing about it at the time.

It took Harl three weeks. He built it up out of glued layers of pine, like a loaf of sliced bread stacked back together, each layer turned a little from the last, and then carved away everything that was not the blade, checking against the eleven boards as he went. The boards slid onto the blade at their numbered places and fitted, or did not fit, and where they did not he took off a shaving and tried again.

By the end of the first week the crew at the works had seen it.

By the end of the second they had theories.

Tobe said it was a fan. A fan for the furnace, to blow the air in harder than the bellows, and Bram said that was not a stupid idea and would not tell him whether it was right, which Tobe took as confirmation. It was not stupid. It was quite close. It went on the wall of the pattern shop in chalk, under a heading somebody else had written, which was WHAT IS IT, and it had three more under it by the end of the week.

A thresher, from one of the carters, on the grounds that it looked like it would cut something.

A kind of windmill, from Wick, for lifting water out of the wet workings at Grayrock without a man on a pump, which was a good deal more thought than Wick usually let anybody see.

A toy for the prince's own amusement, from somebody who did not sign it and who was, Emeric suspected, Neta.

He read the wall every morning on his way through and did not add to it, and did not wipe any of it off, and nobody asked him directly, because it had become understood at the works, without anybody deciding it, that a thing the prince built without explaining was a thing you guessed at until he explained it. It was a game. He liked that it was a game. He had not expected to.

Bram did not play.

Bram came into the pattern shop every afternoon on his way back from the furnaces, and stood at the end of Harl's bench, and looked at the pine blade growing, and went away. On the ninth afternoon he stayed.

"What happens at the thin end," Bram said.

He did not say what is it. He had not asked that about anything since the bearing, and Emeric understood that he was not going to start.

"It goes faster," Emeric said. "The tip's further from the middle, so it goes round a bigger circle in the same time. The root hardly moves. The tip's going like a thrown stone." He put his hand flat and turned it, slowly, from flat to edge-on, the way the boards did. "That's why it twists. Every part of it has to meet the water at the same bite. The root's going slow, so it's turned across the water to take a big bite. The tip's going fast, so it hardly needs to be turned at all. If you made it flat the root would do nothing and the tip would tear."

"Meet the water."

"It goes in water." There was no longer any point not saying that, since it would go in water in front of everybody in a season. "It turns, and each blade is a little wing, and it pushes the water behind it, and the water pushes back. It's an oar that never comes out of the water."

Bram looked at the pine blade.

"Why's it want to be so smooth? Harl's been at it with glass paper for two days."

"Because a rough face drags. The water's supposed to run along the face in a sheet and leave it clean at the back edge. Put a ridge on it and the sheet breaks up behind the ridge and churns, and the churning's all your work going into making the water spin instead of making it go back." He ran his thumb along the pine. "A good one pushes water. A bad one chews it."

"And too fast?"

He had not expected that one. He looked at Bram, and Bram looked back at him, patiently, and Emeric understood that the old man had not asked because he did not know. He had asked because he had seen a millwheel run too fast in a flood and wanted to know if this was the same.

"Too fast, and the water can't keep up with the back of the blade," Emeric said. "It pulls away from it. It opens. Little holes in the water, full of nothing, right on the metal. And then they shut again, all at once, hard, thousands of them, and every one hits the metal like a hammer the size of a pin. It'll eat iron. It'll pit the back of the blade like a pocked face in a season." He heard himself. "So you don't run it too fast."

Bram did not say anything for a while.

He had got perhaps half of it. Emeric could see him hold the half he had and turn it over, the tip going like a thrown stone, the water pushing back, the holes in the water that hit like hammers, and put each piece of it somewhere he could find it again. And he could see Bram noticing the other thing, which was that there had been no gap in any of it. Not one pause. Every question had had an answer standing ready behind it, complete, like a tool already on its hook, and a man did not get answers like that by thinking. He got them by having known for a long time.

Bram did not ask. He had decided in the spring not to ask, and Emeric watched him decide it again, a little more heavily, and put it away with the rest.

"Iron," Bram said. "Three blades. Off one pattern."

"Off one pattern."

"It'll come out rough."

"I know."

Bram nodded and went home.

It came out rough.

They moulded it in the big flask in the second month of the winter, in green sand rammed round Harl's pine pattern, and lifted the pattern out, and it left a hollow the shape of the blade. Harl had made one blade and a hub, and Bram had made the mould three times over, turning the pattern round the hub at a third of a turn each time, so that the three hollows in the sand were three copies of one pine blade. Then the pour. Then a day of cooling under the sand, which nobody could hurry. Then the shake-out.

What came out of the sand was black and scaled and ugly, with a thick seam of iron standing up along every edge where the two halves of the mould had met, and a stump the size of a fist on the hub where the iron had been poured in, and a crust of burned sand on every face. It looked less like a wing than like something dug up. Tobe looked at it and said that it was definitely a fan, and Wick said it was definitely not, and they took it to the chisel bench.

A day of chiselling took off the seams and the stump. Then the grinding.

There was a grindstone at the mill, a great wheel of yellow sandstone as tall as a man, turned off the mill wheel through a shaft and a pair of wooden gears, and it had been sharpening the barony's scythes and axes since before anybody could remember. Nothing new was needed. Bram and two men held the casting against the turning stone on a rest, blade by blade, with water running over it, and the stone ate the scale and the sand and the worst of the lumps, and the iron came up grey under it, and then bright. It took four days and a good deal of the stone.

Then Bram looked at the three blades, bright and ground, side by side, and held his calipers across one and then the next.

"They're not the same," he said.

"No."

"They're each good. Each one's a fair blade." He put the calipers on the third. "But this one's thicker at the tip than that one, and that one's a hair more twisted than this one, and none of them's the pine."

That was the part that mattered, and it was the part Emeric had been waiting for.

"Three blades, each of them beautiful, and none of them like the others," he said. "Put that on a shaft and spin it, and each blade pulls a little differently and weighs a little differently, and the whole thing wants to wander. It'll try to tear the shaft out of its bearings. Every turn. Hundreds of turns a minute." He picked up a thin plate of iron from the bench, one he had brought down that morning. "So nobody makes them beautiful. They make them the same."

It was a profile. He had had it cut from Harl's first board and filed to the pine master, a flat plate of iron with the curve of one blade section in its edge, and there were eleven of them, one for each of the boards, numbered, and a heavier plate that held them all at their places on a spindle through the hub. You set the casting on the spindle, and swung the plates in at their stations, and where the iron stood proud of a plate's edge, it was wrong, and you took it off. Then you turned the hub a third of a turn and did the next blade against the same plates.

"The file doesn't know what the blade should be," Emeric said. "The plates do. The man only has to be careful."

Bram took the first plate out of his hand and looked at it for a long time. He had heard that sentence at midwinter about a lathe.

"The plates are the thing," he said.

"The plates are the thing. Anybody can polish."

They draw-filed it, which took another fortnight: long flat files held crosswise and pushed along the blade's length, a stroke at a time, stopping every few strokes to swing the plates in and look for light under their edges. Neta did most of it. She had the steadiest hands on the floor, and after a spring on the lathe she was not afraid of work that took weeks and never looked any different from one day to the next. When the plates sat down on all three blades at all eleven stations with no light under any of them, she stopped.

Then sand and oil, on a block faced with leather, round and round the faces until they shone like a knife, which took two days and which Tobe, who did it, said was the only part of the whole business that looked like anything.

It was a propeller. Nobody in the works had the word, and he did not give it to them. It sat on the finishing-shop bench, three bright twisted blades round a hub, a little under a yard across, and people came and looked at it and went away and added to the wall.

It had an answer on the wall by then that he had not expected.

It was in Tobe's hand, under the fan and the thresher and the toy, and it said: SET IN THE RACE. WATER TURNS IT. TURNS THE WORKS. NO WHEEL.

He stood in front of that for some time.

It was wrong. It was the propeller backwards, a thing driven by the water instead of driving it. But it was not stupid, and the more he looked at it the less stupid it got. A thing like this set in a pipe, with the water of the race going through it, would turn, and turn hard, and turn a shaft, and it would sit under the water where a great wooden wheel sat in the air and rotted and froze every winter. He had seen those. He had not thought about them in years. He had never once, in ten years of standing next to the mill wheel, thought to wonder whether the wheel was the best way to get the water's work out of it, because it was the wheel and that was what there was.

Tobe had.

He took out the notebook and wrote it in the second column, in his own hand, with Tobe's name beside it. Then he went and found Tobe at the chisel bench and told him it was the best idea on the wall and not the answer, and Tobe asked what the answer was, and he said he would find out when everybody else did, and Tobe went back to his chisel looking as if he had been given something and could not work out what.

They tried it on the engine's shaft in the last weeks of the winter.

The engine had turned its shaft all winter, doing nothing. Now Bram's men fitted a second shaft to it on a belt, running through the brick wall into the shed next door, and carried on two of Jorrin's bearings, the rings and the hardened balls from the summer, in iron housings bolted to oak blocks. The propeller went on the end of it, in the air, in the empty shed. Rudd raised steam. The belt took up. The shaft began to turn, and the three blades turned with it, slowly, and then faster, until they were a grey disc with a sound coming off it like a long breath drawn through the teeth.

At the speed the engine gave, the shaft began to shake.

It was small at first. A hum, and then a buzz in the oak blocks, and then the whole shaft visibly moving in its housings, a tremor you could see in the light off the blades, and the oak blocks walking on the floor, and a noise from the far housing that rose to a grinding shriek. Rudd shut the steam off before anybody asked him. The blades ran down. The shriek went on for a moment after everything else had stopped.

When Bram opened the far housing, the outer ring of the bearing was cracked straight across, and two of the balls had come out of their groove and gone flat on one side, and there was grey grit in the grease that had been, an hour before, iron.

It was the first time one of those bearings had been asked to do anything. They had turned in Bram's hand in the summer as though nothing could ever stop them. This one had lasted less than a quarter of an hour.

"Out of balance," Emeric said. He had known it was a possibility and had not, if he was honest, thought it would be so much. "One blade's heavier than the others. At speed it's like a stone on a string. It swings the shaft round every turn and the bearing takes it."

"The plates said they were the same," said Neta, who had filed them.

"The plates said their shape was the same. Their shape is the same." He crouched by the propeller. "The iron inside them isn't. There'll be a pocket somewhere, where the metal set thicker, or a bubble where it set thinner. You can't file the inside of a casting."

"So how do you find it?"

He showed them, and it was the simplest thing he did all year. Two iron straight-edges, set up level and parallel on the bench, and a true bar through the hub resting across them, so that the propeller hung between them free to roll. It rolled. It turned a little, and stopped, with one blade pointing straight down, every time. They turned it and let go and it came back to the same blade, pointing at the floor, like a compass needle finding north.

"That one," Emeric said.

Neta took a file to the back of the heavy blade, a little, near the root, where it would not change the face, and they hung it again, and it rolled, and stopped somewhere else. She took a little more. It took the rest of the day. At the end of it they could set the propeller on the edges at any angle they liked and it stayed where they put it, and did not roll at all.

They fitted a new bearing, Jorrin's second, and ran it up again, and it hummed and did not shake, and the oak blocks stood still on the floor, and it ran all afternoon.

Bram stood beside Emeric at the end of it and watched it spin.

"You knew it'd do that," he said. "The shaking."

"I knew it might."

"You didn't say."

"You'd have wanted to fix it before we'd seen it," Emeric said. "And then we wouldn't have known how bad it was, and we wouldn't have known the bearing was the weak part." He watched the grey disc turn. "Now we know what it takes to break one of Jorrin's bearings. That's worth a bearing."

Bram considered that, and appeared to find it reasonable, and did not appear to find it comfortable.

They took it off the test shaft that evening and hung it on a hook on the wall of the shed next to the engine, where anybody going past the door could see it: three bright twisted blades round a hub, balanced to a hair, doing nothing. In the pattern shop, under Tobe's line, somebody had drawn a single careful question mark, in a hand he did not know, and he left that on the wall with the rest.
