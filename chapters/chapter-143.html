Chapter 143 - The Staple

Osric had talked to them twice.

The first time was at noon on the day the column came up the road, when the whole town was in the square to watch the knights ride in under the colours, two by two, with the carts behind and the dust going up off them into the heat, and put a ring round the Staple without a word and dismounted and sat down in it. A man came out of the Staple's arches an hour after that with a white napkin on a stick, which Declan said later was the funniest thing he had seen in the south, and walked across the open flags to where Osric stood, and said that the lords of the Staple were ready to receive whatever officer of the barony had been sent to discuss the matter of the tenth.

"There's no matter of the tenth," Osric said. "There's a paper. You've read it."

The man said that the lords had read it, and that they understood a clerk of the barony's might have written it in some haste, and that they were ready to hear what the clerk wanted.

"The paper says what he wants," Osric said. "Come to the counting house, or stay where you are and be fetched. You stayed. We've come." He looked past the man at the arches, at the bar across the big doors behind them, at the faces in the upper windows. "Tell them that."

The man went back. The second time was the same man, the next morning, with a different napkin and the same speech, and Osric gave him the same answer in the same words, and after that nobody came out.

The column sat in the square all that day in the sun. It ate and slept in turns, and saw to its horses at the town's own troughs, and paid for its bread at the baker's on the corner in Kaldo Copper, and did not speak to anybody who did not speak to it first. The town stood round the edges of the square and looked at it, and looked at the Staple, and by the afternoon had started to bring stools. The Staple's lamp stayed lit in the upper window all night. So did the column's fires.

At dusk on the second day Ward and Rylan went out of the south side of the square in their capes with the hoods up, the way two men go out to see to something, and did not come back, and nobody in the town looked twice.

It was an hour after the second bell of the night when Osric put the first smoke pot over the wall.

There was no moon. The square was lit by the column's fires and the lamps at the inn door and the one lamp in the Staple's upper window, and the Staple's yard gate was on the north side of the building, a pair of old oak leaves in an arch in a wall twice a man's height, and behind the wall were house men. Osric knew how many, near enough. He had watched them go up and down on the yard's inside all day, a head at a time above the coping, and he knew which of them walked like soldiers and which walked like grooms.

Tobe's pots went over the wall one after another, six of them, lit, in a long lob, and came down in the yard behind it and began to smoke. It was a thick yellow-grey smoke and it smelled of a stable on fire, and within a count of twenty it was coming back over the wall in a slow rolling bank, and on the far side of the wall men were coughing and shouting at each other to find the pump.

Osric stood in front of the gate with his helm off, where the men inside could hear him, and raised his voice.

"This is the barony of Wayford. You are in a house the barony has called to account. Your masters were summoned and did not come. We've come for them." He let that go through the smoke. "Not for you. Any man who opens that gate and comes out with his hands open walks to the square and sits by the fire and goes home in the morning to his mother. Any man who doesn't, we'll come in and see about. You've a count of a hundred. I'm counting."

He counted aloud. Declan, beside him, drew his sword. Down the line of knights behind them a dozen blades came out, not fast, one after another, so that the men behind the wall could hear each one.

At forty the wicket in the gate opened and a boy came out with his hands up and his eyes streaming, and walked straight past Osric toward the fires without looking at anybody, and sat down by one of them and put his face in his hands.

At sixty, three more. At eighty, four, and one of them was an older man in Hollin's colours who stopped in front of Osric and said, in a voice with the smoke in it, "There's lads in there won't come. Not because they want to fight you. Because they're more feared of the lords than of you."

"Then they've got twenty," said Osric, and went on counting.

At a hundred he put his helm on.

The gate was barred from the inside and the knights did not try the bar. Two of them went up the wall on a ladder from the carts, one on each side of the arch, and dropped into the smoke, and a moment later the bar went back and the leaves swung and Osric and Declan went through into the yard with the rest behind them.

There were eleven of them who had not come out. Some of them were in the smoke by the pump and did not know which way was which. Some of them were on the steps up to the Staple's back door with spears, and those were the ones who fought, and they fought the way house men fight who have been drilled on a Saturday and never once had a man in plate come at them out of a cloud, which was badly, and briefly. Declan put the first one down with the flat of his blade across the side of the head and the second with the edge, because the second had got his spear into the joint under a knight's arm and was trying to push it home. Osric took the steps. He offered it once more, at the top, in the smoke, to a big man in Placit's colours with a boar spear who looked at him through the smoke for the length of a breath and then came on anyway, and Osric killed him, and stood on the step over him for a moment, and went on.

It took less time than the count had.

When it was done there were four dead in the yard and three hurt, and one of the knights sitting on the pump's trough with his arm held tight against his side and Declan kneeling in front of him with a pad of linen, swearing at him steadily and without heat. The rest of Hollin's and Placit's and Dray's and Vennor's men who had not come out were on the ground with their hands on their heads. The smoke was thinning. The Staple's back door stood at the top of the steps, shut.

Osric looked up at it, and past it, at the roof.

He did not see anything. He had not expected to.

They came down on the Staple from the south-west, out of the dark over Lisle's wood, with their stones dark and their capes out and nothing to see anywhere, and the sound of the gate coming up to them thin and far off on the still air: a voice counting, and then shouting, and then the smoke.

Emeric had the Staple under him in his sense before he had it under his eyes.

He had been spreading it since the thorn, not far, a hand at a time, the way a man puts his hand out in a dark room, and at the top of the glide over the town he let it go further than he ever had in the open, down through the Staple's slates and rafters into the building itself, and the building came up to him the way the deck of the boat had come up in the hold, a still grey layer with shapes standing in it. Men. Men at the back, in the smoke, moving. Men in the hall, a lot of them, standing close together at the far end. Men in a room off the hall, not moving, the way men do not move who have been told to wait. One man alone at the front, upstairs, by the window, sitting. And under all of it, under the weighing floor, something he did not have time to look at, a stillness further down, and he left it, because they were coming in to land.

They came in on the back of the building, on the side away from the square where the stone was sound and dressed and Rylan had gone up on the placing night, and Emeric felt the wall with his off hand before he let it take him, because that was the rule now and the rule was why nobody had fallen off the Western Gate. It held. Pad at the palm, pads at both boots, the three soft pops, and he was on the wall and flat to it with the cape furled and Ward beside him and Rylan above, and the gun across his back in its sling.

Ward had looked at his hip at the thorn, where the rack of candles should have been, and was not. Then he had looked at the horn flask on the belt. He had not said anything.

The gallery window was where Saban's man had said it would be, a long low casement under the eaves on the back wall that lit the gallery over the weighing hall, and Rylan had it open with the edge of a gauntlet in the time it took to breathe, and they went in through it one after another and down onto the boards of the gallery in the dark.

The hall opened under them.

It was the whole ground floor of the Staple, a long high room under the arches, with the beam in the middle of it on its great iron frame and the weights stacked in their racks along the walls, and it was lit by torches in brackets on the pillars, and it was full. The lords were at the far end. Emeric saw them from the gallery rail in the torchlight: five of them, and round them their men, fourteen or fifteen, drawn up in front of them in a rough line between the lords and the doors, with swords out, facing the back of the hall where the noise from the yard was coming in under the door. And behind the lords, along the far wall, the servants who had been waiting on them all day, with jugs and cups, in aprons, pressed back against the wall by the pillars, and not knowing where to look.

Off the side of the hall, below the gallery, a door stood half open onto a room with a fire in it and men in it. The soldiers' room. Seven men, the sense said. Waiting to be told.

Ward had a flash stone in his hand. He looked at Emeric. Emeric nodded.

Ward pulled the pin and counted three under his breath and dropped the stone through the half-open door below them, and turned his face away.

The Western Gate had never been fired in anger, and this was the Western Gate in a man's hand, and the room below the gallery went white.

It went white from the doorway out across the hall floor like a sheet thrown over it, and with the white came the sound, which was not a sound so much as a blow, a flat enormous clap that came up through the gallery's boards into Emeric's boots and went on ringing in the stone after it had stopped. In the soldiers' room men were on the floor. They were not hurt. They were lying where they had been knocked with their hands over their eyes and their mouths open, and the sense said they were all still breathing, and none of them could see or hear anything at all.

Ward went over the gallery rail and dropped into the hall in Heavy, and his boots hit the flags like a dropped anvil.

"Barony of Wayford," he said.

It was the voice he used on the yard at Kaldo. It went down the length of the hall without being raised and every man in it heard it.

"Your lords were summoned. You weren't. Put the sword on the floor, and go and sit against the wall, and you go home in the morning. Pick it up again and you don't. That's all. I'm not saying it twice."

For a moment nobody in the hall moved at all.

Then a man in the line near the door, a young one in Vennor's grey, looked at Ward standing in the middle of the floor in black plate with a lion on each shoulder, and looked at the soldiers' room, where his friends were lying on the floor with their mouths open, and bent down and laid his sword on the flags and walked to the wall and sat down against it with his knees up.

Three more followed him. Then a knot of them near the beam, together, as though somebody had given an order, and the swords went down on the stone one after another with a sound like a dropped rack of pans.

It left the lords with five men between them and Ward.

Placit shouted something. Emeric did not hear what. He was coming over the gallery rail himself, and Rylan with him, and they landed on either side of Ward, and at the far end of the hall the five lords did what he had spent two nights on the stool in the pattern store knowing that frightened men with their backs to a wall would do.

They took the servants.

It was quick and it was ugly. Hollin took a girl with a jug by the arm and pulled her in front of him so hard that the jug went on the floor. Placit took an old man in a black coat by the collar, a steward, grey-haired, who stumbled and nearly fell and was held up by the collar. Vennor did not take anybody. Vennor stood against the wall with his hands empty and his face the colour of the plaster behind him. Dray was not there. Dray had been at the end of the line by the side door when the gallery window opened, and the sense had lost him in the white, and did not have him now.

Strade stood where she was and did not touch anyone.

The five men in front of the lords closed up, and the lords behind them with their servants held in front of their chests, and the whole knot of them was pressed back against the far wall under the torches, and Placit shouted again, and this time Emeric heard it. It was something about a knife.

Emeric took the gravity stone out of his belt.

He had its weight in his hand before he had thought about it. Six ounces in a brass cage. He had set it at four in the pattern store in the evening with the figure in his head and the old man with the bad heart, and there was the old man, with Placit's hand in his collar, and his face grey and his mouth working, and there was nothing to be done about the old man now that was any better than this.

"I'll apologise to you for this later," he said, to the servants, to the old man, to the girl with her jug on the floor at her feet.

He pulled the pin, and counted, and threw.

It went in a long low arc across the torchlight and landed on the flags at the lords' feet and rolled, and he had time to see Placit look down at it, a brass thing the size of an apple, and look up with his mouth open.

Then everything within ten yards of it went down.

It did not fall. It was put down, all at once, as if the air over that end of the hall had become a lid and the lid had come down. The lords went down and their men went down and the servants went down with them, knees and hips and shoulders onto the flags, in one movement, with a single sound that was a great many sounds, bone and plate and cloth and breath, all at the same moment. The torches in their brackets on the near pillars leaned their flames toward the floor. The swords went down with the men and lay beside them on the stone, and a man near the front got his hand on the hilt of his and Emeric watched him try to lift it, and it did not lift.

Nobody screamed. There was not the air in anybody's chest for it. There was a low sound all along the floor, of people breathing under four times their own weight, a sound he knew he would hear again.

He counted. Ward was already walking down the hall toward them, not fast, with Rylan beside him, stopping at the ten yards' mark as though it were a line painted on the floor.

At the count of forty, from the side door, a long way from the stone, a voice.

It was Dray.

He was outside the reach. He had been outside it from the start, at the end of the line by the side door, and he had gone through the side door in the white and not come back, and Emeric had lost him and had not had time to find him, and now the sense found him the way it found anything that moved against the grain of the rest, a shape going fast across the south end of the yard, where the bale sheds stood between it and the smoke and the knights in the smoke, toward the other gate in the back wall. The stable gate. Somebody was standing there with two horses. Dray's groom. The one he had brought instead of a fourth man.

Beyond the stable gate was the back lane, and beyond the back lane the stream ground, and beyond that the dark, and Lisle's wood, and the whole of the south.

There were pickets on the back lane. Two. On foot.

He was at the side door before he had decided anything. It gave onto a short passage and the passage onto the yard, and the south end of the yard was lit by torches on the stable wall, where the smoke had not reached, and Dray was halfway across it, running badly, a heavy young man in good boots running on cobbles, with his arms going and his cloak coming off behind him. He was sixty paces off and going away. The groom at the gate had the horses turned for the lane.

There was no time to load. The gun had been loaded since the thorn. He had loaded it on the hill, with the fourth charge of seven, the slow way, and put it on his back.

He went up.

It was not far. The yard's south wall ran from the corner of the building to the stable range, twice a man's height, old stone with a coping, and he went up the face of the Staple in Lighten beside the passage door, out along the top of the yard wall at a run on his pads, and then off the top of it and down onto its inner face, the yard side, and felt the stone with his off hand as he came onto it. It held. It was good stone, dressed, and dry. He let the pads take him: both boots, flat to the wall, and his off hand flat to the wall beside his hip, the three soft pops, and then he was standing on the inside face of the yard wall a man's height above the yard with his body straight out from the stone over the cobbles, like a bracket, in Lighten, with the stones carrying most of his weight and the pads carrying the rest.

It was the way he had drawn it in the book, and practised it on the keep's east face in the dark with nothing in his hands, and it was the first time he had done it with the gun.

He brought the gun round off his back. He laid the fore-end across his off forearm, the forearm along the stone, so that the wall was his rest, the way a man rests a fowling piece along a fence rail. He put the butt to his shoulder and his cheek to the stock, and the yard lay under him, lit, from the wall to the stable gate, with a man running across it.

He found Dray through the notch and the blade.

The sense gave him the rest. It had Dray the whole time, a dense moving shape in the grey stillness of the yard's air, and it told him without being asked how fast, and in what line, and where he would be in the time it took a ball to cross the yard, and Emeric put the blade of the sight where Dray's right thigh would be and not where it was, a little ahead, low on the leg, where it would put him down and not kill him. He had the light. He had the wall. He had the stones holding him out over the yard as steady as a shelf. He let his breath half out and held it, and felt everything go right, all of it, the way it had gone right on the third shot at dawn, and pressed.

The flint came down. The pan flashed.

The gun went off, and the wall took it. The shove came back through the stock into his shoulder and down through his body into his legs and into the pads at his boots and into the stone, and the stone did not move, and he did not move, and the yard filled with the noise and the smoke.

The ball went low.

He saw it go low. He could not have seen the ball; he saw Dray. The blade had been on the thigh, high on the thigh, and Dray went down as though a rope had been pulled across his legs at the knee, sideways, onto the cobbles, and lay there screaming, and his hands went to the side of his thigh a good way below where the blade had been.

It had gone low. A hand low. The same hand as the third shot at dawn, and for the same reason, and there was nothing he could have done about it, and he had known there was nothing when he pressed.

At the stable gate the groom let go of the horses and ran.

Ward came out of the side door below him.

He did not go across the yard. He went up, a short hard burn off the cobbles that lit his calves for the length of a breath, and came down again in the middle of the yard beside Dray in a long step, and bent and took hold of him under the arms, and went up again, straight up, with Dray hanging from his hands, over the torchlight and the wall and into the dark, and came down on the roof of the passage by the side door and set him down on the leads.

Dray was screaming. He had been screaming since the ball, and now he was screaming words.

"They fly! They fly! Demons, God help me, they fly!"

Emeric came down off the wall.

In the yard, beside the pump, in the thinning smoke, the house men who had been lying with their hands on their heads were lying very still, and not one of them was looking up.

Rylan went up the stair while the count was still running on the floor of the hall.

Emeric knew it because he saw him go, a black shape on the stair at the side of the hall, three at a time, when he came back in through the side door with the gun still smoking in his hands and his shoulder ringing. He knew where Rylan was going. There was a room at the top on the square side with a lamp in the window and a strongroom door in the end wall, and he had felt, coming over the roof, a man alone in that end of the building, by the window, sitting, and another man he had taken at the time for a part of the wall because it was so still, behind the strongroom door, doing something slow with his hands.

He followed. At the top of the stair the warden's room was lit by the lamp in the window and by nothing else, and the man who sat by the lamp was on the floor beside his stool with his hands on the back of his head, because Rylan had told him to be. Rylan was at the strongroom door. It was shut and it was iron-bound and it had a lock the size of a man's hand, and Rylan did not try the lock.

He put the breach stone flat against the door beside the lock, and pulled the pin, and stepped aside against the wall.

The door did not blow in. It was struck. The whole of the stone's charge went into it at a single point in a single blow, like the fist of something very large, and the lock and the wood round the lock went into the strongroom in pieces and the door swung in after them on one hinge and hit the wall inside and stayed there.

The strongroom was full of smoke. Not Tobe's smoke. Paper smoke, and leather, and something that stank. There was a small grate in the end wall with a fire in it and ash spilling out of it onto the floor in a grey drift half a foot deep, and shelves round the walls with gaps in them, and on the floor in front of the grate on his knees, with a ledger open across his thighs and a quire torn out of it in his hand, a man in a buttoned coat.

He looked up. He had a plain face. It was a face that people forgot while they were looking at it, and it was not doing anything now either.

Rylan went across the room to him.

Emeric watched from the doorway and did not move, and did not speak, because it was not his to do.

Rylan took the man by the front of his coat with one hand and lifted him off his knees and put him on the floor, face down, on the boards, not hard, not gently, the way a man puts down a sack he has been told to put down. He put one knee in the middle of the man's back. The ledger slid off onto the ash. Rylan's other hand was free. It was in a black gauntlet with a pad in the heel of it, and it could have closed round the back of the man's neck, and did not. It went flat on the boards beside the man's head and stayed there, and Emeric watched it stay, and watched it very slightly shake, and watched it stop shaking. Then Rylan took it off the boards and reached into the grate.

He took the burning ledgers out of the fire with his gauntlets. He did it the way a man takes bread out of an oven, quickly, one after another, and threw them on the floor behind him and beat them out with his hand, and reached in again, and when the grate was empty of anything that had writing on it he knelt there on the man's back in the ash and the smoke with his hands on his thighs and his head down.

The man on the floor had not made a sound.

Emeric came in and crouched down by his head.

He did not touch him. He did not need to. He put the sense over the man the way he had put it over the building, and it found him at once, a dense still shape on the boards with the grain in it that every living man has and no two the same, and Emeric took the other thing out of the place where he kept such things, where it had been since the hold, since the planks of that boat with the cold coming up through them. A slow beat. Two strings a hair apart, rising and falling against each other on a rhythm neither of them had alone.

He laid it against the man on the floor and listened.

The man had a grain. It was an ordinary grain, as ordinary as his face. There was no beat in it. There was nothing in it at all that rose and fell against anything. It was not like the thing on the deck of the boat in the dark. It was not like it the way a stone is not like a bell.

It was not him.

Emeric stayed crouched where he was for a while.

He had put three things together in the hold, a name a guard had given away and a voice off a ledge and a beat in the dark, and he had called them one man, because three entries that agree about one man are the beginning of a column. They had not agreed. He had written them down side by side because they had come to him on the same night in the same boat, and he had never once, in all the time since, asked whether the guard's master and the voice on the deck were the same man, because he had wanted them to be. The guard had said Marrow wants us down here. He had not said Marrow was on the deck. He had said Marrow wanted.

So this was Marrow. The man who wanted. The fixer, the book-man, the man paid forty and not a Copper more for the second matter, who had hired the boat and the men and paid them and told them where to wait. And on the deck of that boat, not moving, without hurry, putting its weight on no word, there had been somebody else. Somebody Marrow had answered to, or worked beside, or been sent by. Somebody with a beat in him that Emeric would know again, and did not know now, and had not found tonight.

Marrow knew who.

"Your name's Marrow," Emeric said.

The man on the floor turned his head a little against the boards so that one eye could see him. He did not say anything. He did not need to. He looked at Emeric for a long moment with that one eye and then looked away, at the ash, and Emeric understood that he knew, too, that it was not him. That he had been waiting in this room for somebody to come and find out, and had been told to wait, and had known, burning the books one quire at a time and reading each quire before it went in, that whoever came through the door would come for the wrong man.

"Alive," Emeric said, to Rylan.

Rylan did not lift his head. "I know."

Emeric stayed where he was, crouched in the ash by the man's head.

"Who was on the deck?" he said.

Marrow looked at the ash.

It was not a refusal. A refusal is a thing a man does. This was a man who had decided a long time ago, in some room Emeric had never seen, what he would do on a floor like this one, and was now doing it, and would go on doing it for as long as anybody cared to ask. Emeric had sat across tables from that decision before. He had never once seen it come apart by being asked.

He put the gun down beside him on the boards.

He had said at the long-room table, years ago, with six stones in front of him, that he had leaned on a door once and it had given the width of a hair, and that with enough weight behind it for long enough he believed it would give all the way. I've never tested that and I won't. He had said it to six people, about six people, and he had meant it. He noticed now that he had never once thought of it as a promise about anybody else.

He took his mother's stone out of his coat and closed his left hand on it.

Then he put his right hand on the back of Marrow's neck, under the collar, on the bare skin, in the place where Rylan's gauntlet could have closed and had not.

Rylan's head came up.

Emeric did not look at him. He went looking for the fear, the way he had gone looking for it in a bird in a box, and in Imani's ward, and on the edge of a boy's bed with a blanket between his hand and the boy. It had always been the loudest thing there. In Marrow it was not. It was small and a long way down and packed round with something very like calm, the way a man packs straw round the one glass thing in a crate, and Emeric had to go past a great deal of straw to find it.

He had only ever learned to take the edge off. In the hold of a boat, with a bag on his head, he had noticed that he would have tried it the other way if he had had a hand free.

He had a hand free. He turned it the other way.

Marrow did not make a sound. His hand on the boards opened, and the fingers spread and pressed down into the ash until the knuckles went white, and that was all. Under Emeric's palm the skin of his neck went cold and wet.

Emeric went down into the delve, and pulled.

He expected it to fail. Ferra had held on, the first times, because she was frightened, and he had not been able to shift her, and he had built everything he believed about pulling on the back of that. A frightened man holds on. This one did not. Whatever Marrow had packed round the fear was holding every other part of him shut, and the fear, turned up as loud as Emeric could make it, was the one thing in him that was holding nothing. The pull went in through it the way water comes in through the one split board in a hull.

It was the office.

It was always the office, soft at the edges and sharp where it mattered, with the window and the long table and his own figures hanging at the corners of sight, and it was quiet after the smoke. Marrow was standing in the middle of the floor.

He was standing because nobody lies on his face in a room like this. His coat was buttoned to the throat. He looked at the window, and at the table, and at the figures in the air, and then at Emeric, and he did not ask where he was. Bram had asked. Ferra had asked. Marrow put the question away somewhere behind his face with everything else, and waited.

Along the wall behind him, where there had been nothing, there was a shelf.

Emeric had told six people at a table that anything a man had looked at with his own eyes sat on a shelf in this room while he was in it, the way he had seen it, down to the smudges. He had never seen a stranger's. It was longer than any of theirs had been, and it was in order. Of course it was in order. It was a book-man's shelf. It ran along the wall in ledgers bound alike, one to a season, the way a careful clerk keeps a counting house, with the dates on the spines.

Emeric knew the date he wanted. It was in Sethe's small account book in Ferra's box: three days after forty Gold went to M. for the second matter. He walked along the shelf until he came to that spring, and put his hand on it.

It did not come.

It did not come the way a book comes off a shelf. It sat under his hand like a thing cast in the same piece as the wall. He pulled, and the wall pulled back, and he felt it in the front of his head, the pressure he remembered from the third time he had leaned on Ferra: a door that was not going to open.

Marrow had not moved. He stood where he had been standing, with his hands at his sides, and there was a line of sweat along his hairline that had not been there a moment before.

Emeric leaned.

It gave the width of a hair. He knew that width. He had stopped at it once, with Ferra, and put a headache into both of them, and gone no further, and written it down as the edge of the thing. He did not stop at it now. He put his weight behind it, all of it, the way a man puts his shoulder to a door he has decided is coming down, and the pressure in his head went from a pressure to a noise, and the ledger came off the shelf into his hands.

It opened by itself, and things came out of it that he had not asked for. A column of weights in a hand he did not know. A quay in rain. A woman's hands folding a letter. A street with snow on the roofs and a stall of salt fish, somewhere a long way north. He understood, going through them, what they were. They were being thrown at him. A man in a burning house throws the furniture down the stair at whoever is coming up it, and does not much care what it is, so long as it is in the way. Emeric went through them. Every page he turned past cost him, in the front of his head, and he could see across the room that it was costing Marrow more.

Then it was the night.

Black water, and a lantern on it, and the stern of a boat going out from under the lantern into the dark, seen from somewhere low and a little way off. Somebody was standing at the edge of the light with his back to whoever was looking. A shoulder. The line of a coat. Nothing else. The page was the way Marrow had seen it, down to the smudges, and what Marrow had been looking at was the man's back.

Emeric put his thumb to the corner of the page to turn it.

Across the room, Marrow shut his eyes.

The ledger went white in Emeric's hands. Not white the way paper is white. White the way a lamp chimney goes in the instant before the flame dies. Then it was not in his hands, and the shelf was not on the wall, and the floor where Marrow had been standing was only floor. Nothing had pushed him out. Nothing had thrown Emeric out either. Marrow was simply not there, the way a flame is not there, and the office was quiet and soft at the edges with the figures hanging in the corners of the air, and Emeric was standing in the middle of it with his hands out in front of him holding nothing.

He came up.

It had been, outside, about the length of a breath. The fire was still settling in the grate. Rylan's head was still up. And under Emeric's hand the man on the floor had gone slack, all at once and all over, the way a man goes when he is struck behind the ear, and the fingers in the ash had uncurled.

Rylan came off him as if the man had caught fire. He was up on his knees with both hands lifted clear of the body and held out from it, open, the black gauntlets spread, and he was looking at his own knee, and then at the middle of the man's back where his knee had been.

"I didn't," he said. "I had my weight off him. I had it off."

"It wasn't you."

Emeric put the sense over the man. It was hard to do. His head was ringing like a struck pan and the sense came out of it thin and ragged, but it found the grain, the ordinary grain, still there, still moving the way a living thing never stops moving. Slower than before. Even. He held it there until he was sure.

"He's breathing," he said. "It wasn't you, Rylan."

Rylan looked at him. Then he looked at Emeric's hand, which was still on the back of the man's neck, and Emeric took it away.

"Nessa told me," Rylan said. "After. That you put a hand on the boy, so he'd sleep."

"It wasn't that."

Rylan waited.

"I asked him who was on the deck," said Emeric.

"Did he tell you?"

"No."

Rylan looked at him a while longer, in the smoke, with the ash on his gauntlets. He did not ask anything else. He turned the man onto his side, carefully, and put the head on the man's own arm so the face was out of the ash, the way you put a drunk down in a doorway so he will not choke in the night. Then he sat back on his heels beside him and did not look at Emeric again.

Emeric stood up. The room tipped once and came back.

He had it in pieces, and he laid the pieces out, because that was the only thing he knew how to do with a head like this one. It could be done. A man who did not want to come could be pulled, if he was afraid enough and the fear was loud enough to go in through. What was on his shelf could be taken off it. And a man who would not give the one thing would give everything else first, and then, when there was nothing left in the way but that one thing, he would let go of all of it at once rather than hand it over, and go out like a lamp.

He did not know yet what going out like that cost. He did not know whether the man on the floor would wake as the man who had gone down, or as some part of him, or at all. He found, when he looked for it, that he minded that less than he ought to have. He noticed it, in the place where he kept that kind of note, and let it be.

He had not got what he came for. He had the back of a coat at the edge of a lantern.

He picked up the gun.

It was a long time before the hall was quiet.

The count ran out at sixty and the weight came off, and the lords and their men and their servants lay where they were for some time after it had come off, because they did not know it had. Then the knights came in from the yard and took the swords off the floor and stood the men up and walked them to the wall with the others, and the servants got up by themselves, slowly, one at a time. The old man in the black coat did not get up by himself. Two of the maids got him up between them and sat him on a bench by the wall, and he sat there with his hand flat on his chest and his mouth open, breathing, and Emeric went and stood in front of him and looked at him until he was sure.

He was breathing. His lips were a bad colour. They got better.

"Sir," said the old man, when he could, and did not say anything else, and Emeric did not know whether it was a question.

"I said I'd apologise," Emeric said. "I'm sorry."

The old man looked at him as if he had said something in another tongue.

The lords were stood up last. Placit could stand. So could Hollin, and Vennor, and Strade, who got up off the floor by herself before any knight could reach her and stood with her grey skirts torn at the knee and her hands folded in front of her, looking at nothing. Dray was carried in from the passage roof by two knights, with a knight's field dressing on his thigh tight enough to make him scream again when they lifted him, and put down on a bench, and went on talking about demons until Declan told him quietly that if he said the word again Declan would put something in his mouth.

Adlow was in the warden's room, it turned out, behind the strongroom, at a small table in a corner with a lamp and a pen and a ledger of the Staple's that was not one of the burned ones, and he had not moved when Rylan came through, and did not move now when a knight came for him. He had been writing when the gate went up. The pen was still in his hand. The ink on the nib had dried. The knight took the pen off him and put it on the table, and Adlow went down the stair with the rest.

Placit said, in the hall, to nobody, that this was an outrage, and that the Staple was a private house under a Crown charter three kings old, and that its books and its rooms and everything in it were its own and not the barony's.

"The charter reserves a tenth of every duty through that beam to the lord of Wayford," Emeric said. "You've read it. You paid the tenth to Sethe for eleven years and put it nowhere in any book I can find, and since the judgment you've paid it to nobody. Lord Hollin handed me some of it at his own table this summer in a purse and called it the usual." He did not look at Hollin. "The lord of Wayford has a claim on every book that says where his tenth went. I'm the lord of Wayford. The books are mine to read. What's left of them."

Placit opened his mouth, and looked at the stair, where the smell of the strongroom was still coming down, and shut it.

He meant to send them north.

He had meant it since the spring. He had meant it since the first page came out of the printer in the Ambry back room with a lord's handwriting on it, and he had meant it on the stool in the pattern store and on the thorn and on the wall tonight with the gun in his hands, and he meant it now. The five would go north to the Crown in the morning in a closed cart, under the column, with the books, and the Crown's justice would try them, and Keller would want all of it and would get all of it, or all that could be put in front of a court, and the five would be the Crown's and not his.

It was clean. It was the only thing about any of it that was.

Not tonight. Moving five nobles through a dark town with their men scattered in it and half the town standing at the edges of the square with stools was a thing he would not do, because anything could come out of the dark at a cart in a narrow street and he would not be able to see it coming. They would stay in the Staple tonight. The strongroom had a door that shut, still, on one hinge, and a knight could stand in it.

They went up the stair into the strongroom one after another with a knight on either side of each, and sat down on the floor in the ash along the wall under the empty shelves, in a row, because there was nowhere else to sit. Placit. Hollin. Vennor. Strade. Dray, carried, and laid at the end of the row with his leg out straight and his face wet. Marrow was carried the other way, into the warden's room, with Rylan, and the door between shut.

Ward found Emeric at the top of the stair. He had his helm under his arm and his hood back, and there was ash on his cape and a smear of somebody's blood on the heel of his gauntlet that was not his, and he stood beside Emeric on the landing and looked through the broken door at the five on the floor.

"You shouldn't have taken that shot," he said.

It was flat. It was not an accusation. It was the thing Ward said when a thing was true and he had decided it needed saying once and not again.

Emeric thought about the pickets on the back lane, two, on foot. About the groom with the horses. About how far a man on a good horse gets in the dark in the time it takes two men on foot to decide he is the man they want. About the ball going low, a hand low, the same hand, for the same reason, and how the hand could as easily have been a hand high, or a hand to the right, into the big vessel on the inside of a young man's thigh that a field dressing does not stop.

He did not argue.

Ward nodded, as though that were the answer he had expected, and went down the stair to see to the knight with the spear wound.

Emeric stood in the doorway a while longer. The knight at the door moved aside for him without being asked, and moved back when he did not go in. The five sat in a row on the strongroom floor in the grey ash with the lamp from the warden's room coming in through the broken door across their legs. Placit had his eyes shut. Hollin was looking at his hands. Vennor was looking at the floor between his boots. Strade was looking at Emeric, steadily, the way she had looked at him across her own table, as though she were waiting to see how long he would keep it up.

He would write to the Crown in the morning. He would write it himself, and Hobb would copy it, and it would go north with the cart.

He went down the stair to find paper.
