Chapter 143 - Bands

The box had been in the drawer of his office table since September, under a sheaf of Hale's drafts, where nobody but Ferra would ever look and Ferra would never open it.

He took it out on the last night of February and set it on the table under the lamp and opened it. Two stones on the old faded velvet, grey and dull and the size of a pigeon's egg, and beside them the pressed shapes where the others had lain for twenty years. He had not touched them since the lodging at court. He picked up the first and held it in his palm and opened his sense to it, thin, the way he held it open all day now without thinking.

The same as before. A dull cool weight, and at the very edge of feeling, the shape where a note had been. A room somebody had just walked out of.

He did not know whether he was allowed to cut it. He had thought about that in the autumn more than he had thought about anything except the two women he had been failing at the same time. It was hers. It was the nearest thing he had to her that was not inside his own head. And it was a Hollow Crystal, a control stone, the rarest thing in Kaldo, sitting in a box doing nothing, when every band he meant to make needed one at its heart. In the end it was Ward who had settled it, without meaning to, on the road home from court, when Emeric had asked him what she would have said. Ward had thought about it for a mile. Then he had said that the queen he had known had cut up her own good cloak once to bandage a groom, and complained about the cloak for a week.

He took the first stone down the narrow stair.

He made it the root bead of his own band, and he made it small: a grey bead the size of the end of his little finger, faceted, with a hole through it for the cord of fine steel wire the band would be strung on. He did not hurry. He kept his hand on the control stone in the lattice for the whole of the run and his attention in the chamber with the stone, and he felt the old shape in it, the room somebody had walked out of, go down with it as it shrank, and stay. It did not fade. It got smaller and stayed exactly the same, the way a voice does when the person speaking walks away down a corridor.

When it came out of the feed cup it sat in the palm of his hand, grey, very plain, and he closed his hand on it before he put it in the little dish Bram had made for beads.

The second stone had a chip in it.

He found it when he went to put it in the chamber, in the lamplight at the feed cup, turning it to see which way it wanted to be cut. A flake gone from one side, small, old, the edges worn smooth. Somebody had dropped it, once, on a hard floor, years ago. He put it in the lattice anyway and let the control stone pulse it, and read what came back, and it was what he had been afraid of. The chip went in further than it looked. Cut down to a bead, the flaw would run straight through the middle of it, and a bead with a flaw through its middle would hold a charge for a day and then let it go all at once, in a dead zone the size of a room, on somebody's wrist.

He took it out of the lattice. He put it back in the box on its velvet, next to the empty shape where its brother had been, and shut the lid, and put the box back in the drawer under Hale's drafts. Whatever it was for, it was not for this.



The chair failed for the seventh time on the first of March, in the works, in Bram's corner, which was the worst place in Kaldo for it to fail.

It was the air. The ring of air crystals round the base of the chair had been pushing it forward and back across the works floor for a month without trouble, but always forward and back, the way a cart goes. That night he wanted sideways. He took the chair up into Lift, a finger's width off the flags, where it floated like a cork on still water, and pushed the stick hard to the right.

The chair went left.

It went left because the ducts on the left side of the base fired, all of them, as they had been told, to push the chair right; and four ducts firing together at the height of a man's shin make a very great deal of wind in the direction they are pointing. They were pointing at Bram's bench. Bram's bench had on it the whole of Bram's week: the curls of shaving off the new lathe screws, which he had been saving to show Tess; a dish of brass filings; three drawings pinned at the corners with nails; and a slate of chalk sums for the cell casings that he had been working at since supper and had very nearly got right.

All of it went. In one long gust it went off the bench and across the floor and into the open front of Bram's forge, which was banked low for the night, and the hearth took it with a soft roar and a sheet of bright yellow flame that came out of the front of the forge as far as the anvil and went back in again. The shavings burned. The drawings burned. The slate cracked across in the heat with a sound like a stick broken over a knee and the chalk sums on it went with it, every figure, in a puff of white.

The chair, meanwhile, had gone left, slowly, all the way across the works, and had come to rest against the far wall with Emeric in it, pressed against the wall by the right-hand ducts, which were still firing because he had not yet worked out which way he was pushing the stick.

Bram had been at the door when it happened. He came down the length of the works with a lamp, without hurrying, and looked into the front of his forge for a long while. Then he looked at the bench, which was bare. Then at the chair against the far wall.

He did not say anything. He went to the manual, which was on its own stand by the chair's bay, open at the air lattice, and took the pencil off its string and wrote in the margin, in large slow capitals, pressing so hard the pencil went through the page in one place.

NOT NEAR MY BENCH.

The fix was the ducts. They went lower, nearly to the floor, and angled down, so their wind went along the ground and lifted nothing above an ankle; and each one got a guard of fine brass mesh over its louvres, so nothing could be sucked in or thrown out. The manual got a page on it. Bram's line stayed in the margin above the page, and Emeric left it there, because it was the best summary of the air lattice anybody was ever going to write.

The flask cells went into the chair's hearts the next morning. Four of them in each heart, in iron casings with glass liners because Emeric had decided, on the stair, that a chair that could do Rise could go up, and anything that could go up flew. They seated in their slots with a small, very satisfying click, neck first, the square face of each one against its plate. Vesk, who had charged the test stones every morning since November, stood and watched them go in. Then he looked at the empty rack where his test stones had sat every morning since November, and at Emeric.

"You can still charge the spares," said Emeric.

Vesk nodded, slowly, and went off to charge the spares. The chair was the forge's first customer. It was strange to think of it that way, and Emeric did not say it out loud, because Bram would have asked what the customer was paying.



His own stone went into his band over the next two days, and they were two of the worst days he had spent in Kaldo.

The reason was a hold on a boat. He did not tell anybody that, but Ward knew, because Ward had been on the bank that night. More than a year ago Emeric had lain in the bilge of Garrin's boat with his hands tied, in the dark, and the only thing that had saved him was the stone in his coat pocket, the grey one, the Hollow Crystal he had carried since he was a boy; and every hour he had lain there he had known that any man who went through his pockets would find it and take it, and never know what he had in his hand. He had thought about it on every night ride since. A stone in a pocket could be lifted off a man. A stone on his wrist, under an iron separator and a clasp that answered to nobody but him, could not.

So it had to be cut. And to cut it he had to empty it first, because the whole of the delve was in it. The room. Everything he had put in the room for five years: Marcus's drawings and every book he had read in the other life, laid out on its shelves; the six's chairs round the long table; the maps; the slow time of the place, where a night outside could be a week inside; the index of the forge, half begun. All of it lived in that stone. If he cut the stone with the room still in it he did not know what would come out, and he was not going to find out.

He parked it in the root bead.

He did it on the night of the first, in his office, with the panel shut and the bead in a dish on the table and the old stone in his hand. He went into the room, and stood in it, and moved it. It was not like carrying furniture. It was more like walking from one house into another in the dark, holding a lamp, with everything you owned following you by the hand. It took most of the night. When he came out, the old stone in his hand was empty. Not grey. A hole in the shape of a stone, the way the drained one had been before it became the first cell.

And the room was in his mother's stone.

He went in again, once, before he went to bed, to see. It was all there. The shelves, the drawings, the long table and the six's chairs, every book, the slow time running at its own pace. Nothing was out of place. But the light was different. He could not have said how. A little warmer. As though somebody had left a lamp burning in a room he did not use, a long time ago, and it had never quite gone out. He stood in the middle of it for a while, not doing anything, and then he came out, and did not go in again until it was time to move it back.

The empty stone went into the forge the next night and came out a bead. Not as small as the root; a larger bead, longer, with a groove round its middle for the iron separator to sit in. He strung it on the band beside the root bead with the separator between them, and closed the clasp, and the clasp knew him. It was a little sprung thing of steel that Bram had made to his drawing, with a stone in it the size of a grain of rice that he had cut to answer his mana and nobody else's. When he put his thumb on it, it opened. When anybody else did, it stayed shut.

The room went back into his own stone on the morning of the third.

He was glad, and he had not expected to be sorry, and he was a little sorry. The root bead stayed on the band beside it, grey and plain. He left the room's light where it was until the last. Then he moved it.

For those two days, between the emptying and the moving back, he had been without the room, and without the stone that he had carried in his pocket since he could remember. He had not known how much he leaned on it until it was gone. His sense, the thin open feel of everything round him that he kept up all day now without thinking, the way a man keeps his balance, had gone lopsided. He kept reaching for the left side of the yard and finding it not quite there. He walked into a door frame on the morning of the second. Ward watched him do it and did not say anything until the third time, when he said, "Sit down," and Emeric sat down on the mounting block in the yard and stayed there until the world agreed with itself again.

When the room was home and the stone on his wrist was his again he went up on the wall at noon and stood at the parapet and opened his sense as wide as it would go, all the way round, the keep and the yard and the lower ward and the river, and it was all there. The bead on his wrist sat against his pulse, warm. He found he had his other hand round it, and took it away.



The bands for the six and the four knights came out of the forge the same three nights, alongside his own, and he gave them out at the table on the afternoon of the third.

They were not much to look at in the hand, and very good to look at on a wrist. Each was a row of beads on a cord of fine steel wire that could not be cut with anything Kaldo owned except the forge. On the inside, against the skin, a row of small blue faceted beads: battery beads, each holding a charge. On the outside, between them, little barrels of gold, gravity stone cut and polished and capped, that would go heavy if they were told to. An iron separator in the middle. And at the clasp, the grey bead.

For the six, the grey bead was a Hollow Crystal, cut from Kaldo's own few, each one etched inside so that it knew its wearer and nobody else and could carry a small room of its own. For the four knights, Ward and Osric and Declan and Rylan, the grey bead was an ordinary stone, cut from inside to do three things: hold the battery's count, show it, and call out through the bond if the band was ever taken.

Showing it was the part Bram asked about. Emeric had set into the face of each grey bead, under a skin of clear glass, a little picture that came up when you pressed the clasp: a small rectangle, upright, with a nub on its top end, and inside it a bar of light that filled the rectangle from the bottom as far as the band was charged, and beside it a number.

Bram pressed his clasp and looked at the picture for some time.

"Why's it look like that?"

"Because that's what full looks like."

"To who?"

Emeric opened his mouth and found there was no honest answer to that question in this world, and shut it again. Bram looked at him, and at the little lit rectangle with the nub on top, and put his band on, and said nothing else about it ever. Emeric suspected that he had added it to a private list.

He told them what the bands were for. A bank for the delve. Each of the six had been into the room with him; three of them now had been in alone. A solo delve cost the one who did it, and the band would carry that cost instead, from its battery beads, so nobody had to come out of the room grey and shaking the way Armand had come out of it the night the first gravity stone came up the shaft. For the knights, it was a battery and a gauge and an alarm, and they would get the rest later. That was the stated purpose. He meant it. It was true.

"And?" said Armand.

Emeric looked at him.

"You've made a battery with a clasp on it," said Armand, "and you've made a good many of them, and you've put ten of them on the people at this table and the men who guard it. You don't need ten batteries for the delve. You need six, and they'd be in a drawer." He folded his hands on his board. "And?"

So Emeric told them the rest. A body that lived beside a charged stone, day and night, for years, took a little of it in. Not much. Not enough to hurt. The way a man who works in a salt pan ends up with salt in his skin. And as it took a little in, the reservoir it held its own mana in grew a little wider, slowly, without tearing. Mott, on the still crew, had sat beside the recharged stones every working day for two years, and had been read twice since the autumn, and both times read inside a knight's range, with skin as clean as a child's. No tracery. No scar. Nothing.

"Mott is one man," said Armand.

"Yes."

"One man is a story. You can't build a rite on a story."

"It's not a rite. It's a band." Emeric looked down the table. "And one man with clean skin inside a knight's range is one more than the rite has ever made."

Armand looked at him for a while, and then at the band on his own thin wrist, with its blue beads against the skin. He did not take it off.

"Then you'll measure every one of us," he said. "Every quarter. In my book, not yours."

"Yes."

"Well," said Armand, and closed his eyes, and that was his consent.

It was Imani who said the thing nobody else had thought of, and she said it before it could matter, which was the only time a thing like that was worth saying.

"It won't come off," she said.

They looked at her.

"The clasp answers to the wearer. Only the wearer." She had hers on already. She turned her wrist over on the table so the grey bead was uppermost. "If the wearer's awake. If somebody's brought to me off the hill with a crushed arm and the band's above the break and the arm's swelling, and he's out cold, I can't get it off him. And the clasp won't let me cut it, because the wire won't cut. So I'll be standing over a man with a tourniquet I didn't put on him, waiting for him to wake up and open it."

Emeric looked at her. He had not thought of it. He had thought of thieves, of a band being taken off a bound man in a hold, of the beads going heavy on a thief's wrist and pinning him to the ground. He had not thought of a ward, and an unconscious man, and a swelling arm.

"Then you need a way in," he said.

"I need a way in," said Imani. "That's mine. Not anybody's. Not yours." She put her wrist back on the table. "Make me a key. One. For the ward."

He made her one. It took him a week, and it was the only thing the forge made that month that he did not put in the run list, because Ferra wrote it in for him.



Ilsa Renner found the error the same evening.

She had come down the river from Grayrock on the first, for the quarter's sitting, with the mine's books in a satchel and her mother's leave to stay a week. She had grown again since the winter, and she sat at the side table in the counting room with Grayrock's book open in front of her and Ferra's final schedule beside it, checking the one against the other, because the mine's sales through the House were the coin that would pay the debt and the two had to agree.

They agreed. She checked them three times and they agreed to the copper. Then, because she had been taught to, she checked the schedule itself.

She was a long while about it. Emeric was in the room, at the window, with a letter from Aster about the poles, and he saw her stop, and go back up a column with her finger, and stop again. She did not say anything. She got up, with the schedule in her hand, and went and stood at Ferra's desk until Ferra looked up.

"The interest," said Ilsa.

Ferra looked at her.

"It's to the quarter day." Ilsa put the schedule down on the desk and turned it so Ferra could read it, and put her finger on a line. "The principal's right. And the interest is right, to the first of January. But you're paying on the fifth of March." She took her finger away. "There's sixty-three days after the quarter that aren't in it. It's not much. But it's not paid. So when the last of the principal goes in, the House will still be owed sixty-three days, and the covenant says the debt stands until it's paid in full." She stopped, and then added, because she was young and very honest, "So it wouldn't be paid. Not all of it. And the factor would have to write that it wasn't."

Ferra looked at the line.

Then she said, "Yes," in a perfectly ordinary voice, and "Thank you, Ilsa," in the same voice, and wrote a small mark in the margin beside the line. Ilsa went back to her table. Ferra went on with the page she had been on before.

Emeric had stood in a great many rooms with Ferra by now. He knew what her face did when a column came out. He had never seen what it did when one of hers had been wrong, in front of a girl who had been keeping books for less than a year, in her own counting room, on the schedule she had been building since the loan toward one day. He saw it now. It did nothing at all. Her pen went on across the page at exactly its usual pace, and the back of her neck under the red hair had gone white.

He did not go to her. She would not have thanked him for it.

She went home at the usual hour. He found out the rest in the morning.



She went in alone that night.

She had never done it. She had been in the room with him a dozen times since the winter of the group delve, and with the six, and once with Imani; never alone. It frightened her, he knew. Ferra did not like a room she could not leave by a door. But she had the band now, on her left wrist, with its blue beads against her skin and its grey bead at the clasp, and a small room of her own in the grey bead, waiting. She went into it at midnight at the table in her own room by the river, with the schedule in front of her and the lamp turned low, and she did not come out until the bells for dawn.

In the room she redid it. All of it. Not the line Ilsa had found; the whole schedule, from the first page, every year of it, every payment and every quarter and every day of interest between them, in the slow time of the room where a night could be a week. Where nobody could watch her do it. Where nobody could see her check a thing she had already checked, and check it again, and find that it was right, and check it a third time anyway. When she came out at dawn the schedule on her table was a new one, in her hand, with the sixty-three days in it and two other things she had found herself, smaller, that Ilsa had not, and her band's gauge read a little under half.

She was the fourth of the six to go in alone. After Bram, and Armand, and Imani.

And she told Imani first.

That was the second term: no secret one of them had that the other did not. Ferra walked down to the hospital ground at seven, in the cold, with the new schedule under her arm and grey under her eyes, and found Imani in the ward kitchen with a cup of something hot, and told her. Imani heard it all. Then she gave Ferra the cup, and stood with her while she drank it, and then she walked up the hill herself, at half past eight, to the keep, and found Emeric at the yard pump with his sleeves rolled up and his face wet, and told him.

"She went in alone," said Imani. "Last night. The whole of the debt, from the beginning. She's done." She watched him straighten up from the pump with water running off his chin. "She asked me to tell you second."

He stood there and dripped.

"Second," he said.

"Second." Imani's mouth did something at one corner. "She was quite firm about it. I think she'd been looking forward to it."

He found that he was smiling, and that he could not stop, and that his face was wet and cold and he did not care. Imani watched him, with her arms folded over her shawl. Then she reached up, unexpectedly, and with the end of her shawl wiped the water off his chin, briskly, the way she would have done it for a child in the ward, and went back down the hill.



The poles started up the capital road that week.

Aster's letter said so: the first twenty of them set in the ground at the Wayford end, south of the crossing house, with Wayford's own men digging the holes and a carter hired by the day for the poles, and the wire to follow when the copper came. He was working south toward Kaldo, a mile a week if the weather held, and Kaldo's gang was working north toward him from the South Gate. Somewhere in the middle, in the summer, the two would meet. He asked whether there was any preference as to which side of the road. Ferra said the east side, because the ditch was better drained, and wrote it in her reply in a hand that was perfectly steady, and initialled it, and dated it, and put it in the post.
