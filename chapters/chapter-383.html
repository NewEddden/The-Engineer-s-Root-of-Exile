Chapter 383 - The Net

They went eighty miles out to do it, because Tess said a zone head anywhere nearer than that was a zone head over her towers, and nobody on escort one wanted to be the crew that put out the Answer Towers and the Shell and every lamp stone in the lower ward to prove a rocket.

So on the fourteenth of February, a Saturday, at first light, escort one and escort three lifted off the coal quay together and went east over the sea at two thousand feet with the sun coming up in their faces, and the K Endeavor came behind them with the Challenger slung under her keel in her cradles like a seed in a pod, and the Hod above all three of them in the Band with Orla Garran's radiosondes going up off her deck on their little balloons every hour, reporting the wind.

Emeric was on escort one's bridge with Bram and Ruan. Ward was on escort three. Tess was on the K Endeavor, in the hold, with the flywheels, because the flywheels were hers and she'd said that if anybody was going to watch them for the first time with fifty tonnes of submarine hanging off the bottom of a hull she was going to be the one, and she'd sooner sit in a hold with a stick than on a bridge with a view.

"She doesn't like the view," said Bram.

"She likes the view. She doesn't like being looked at while she's looking at it."

"Same thing, with Tess," said Bram, and went back to his notes.

The flywheels had gone into the K Endeavor's hold in the first week of February, four of them, in two pairs, low and to either side of the keel where the Challenger's cradle bolted through.

They weren't much to look at. Four steel spheres the size of a cart wheel, dull grey, each bolted down into a frame that let it tilt on two pins, and inside each one, in a vacuum Tess had pumped out with the Works' best pump over three days, a wheel of Bram's bucket steel spinning on its axle at four thousand turns a minute. An electric motor at each to keep it up to speed. A brake. A gauge. The hum of them, when they were running, was so low it was more a feeling in the deck plates than a sound; the crew said it made their teeth itch, and Tess said that was their teeth's problem.

A spinning wheel held its axis. That was all it did. Push it and it pushed back, sideways, at right angles to the push, and the faster it spun the harder it held. Bolt four of them into a hull and the hull had something to lean against when the wind took it. It didn't make the gravity stones push sideways; Tess had been very firm about that in October when one of Dagny's lads had suggested it. A free stone's up was the world's up, always, however you turned it. The flywheels only steadied. But fifty tonnes of submarine slung under a keel made a pendulum, and a pendulum in a gust was a hull trying to roll itself over, and the flywheels were what stood between the K Endeavor's crew and finding out how far it would go.

At a quarter to eight, forty miles out, a squall came across them from the north, the first of the day, short and hard, a wall of grey rain with a white edge to the sea under it.

Emeric watched the K Endeavor go into it from escort one's bridge. She leaned. Everybody leaned in a squall; you could see the gust hit her by the way the rain went sideways off her flank. And then she didn't lean any further. She hung there at the angle the wind had put her, with the Challenger's black shape under her not swinging, quite still in its cradles, like a weight on a plumb line held in a steady hand; and she held that angle until the squall went past her, and then came slowly upright again, as if somebody were straightening a picture.

On the speaker from her hold, a moment later, Tess's voice, quite flat. "Port pair went to eleven degrees on the pins. Starboard nine. Brake temperature fine. Nobody's been sick." A pause. "Two people have been sick."

"That's the squall," said Bram. "Not the wheels."

"I know it's the squall," said Tess. "I'm telling you anyway. For the book." Another pause. "It held."

"It held," said Emeric.

"Don't sound so surprised," said Tess, and the speaker clicked off.

The target was Bram's, and Bram had built it to be hit, and was nevertheless fond of it.

It was a sledge. A long light frame of ash and steel tube the size of a small boat, with a gravity stone at each corner, charged, and a bucket of old fire stones in the middle that Armand had charged up to their brims the night before so that it would shine on any sense stone in the sky like a lamp. Bram had painted it red. On the front of it, in white, somebody in the yard had painted a face with teeth, and Bram had pretended to be annoyed and hadn't painted over it.

"It's a dragon," said Ruan, looking at it on escort three's deck through the glass.

"It's a sledge," said Bram.

"It's a dragon. It's got teeth."

"It's got a mana sign like a dragon's, as near as Armand can make one, and it flies on stones like a dragon would if a dragon flew on stones, which Armand says it does and I'll take his word for." Bram didn't look up from his notes. "And it's going to come down in the sea today in a net and I'm going to want it back."

The Dragon Net was in escort one's weapons bay, in a long rack under the deck: a rocket the length of a man and a half, in three parts, painted grey, with fins at the tail and a blunt nose. Every part of it was made to fail safe. If its stones died it was a dumb rocket on powder that went where it was pointed. If its powder died it was a lump of steel with fins. It wouldn't go off on its own, it wouldn't go off on the deck, it wouldn't go off if you dropped it, and Ruan had dropped one in the autumn, by accident, from a height of four feet, onto the yard's flags, and nothing had happened except that Bram had said a word Emeric hadn't heard him use since the war.

The first part was the booster. A gravity stone in a steel throat, the oldest trick Kaldo had, a free stone pushing up; it would throw the whole thing up into the sky faster than any powder could, with no flame and no smoke, and fall away empty when it was done.

The second part was the zone head.

That was the one nobody liked. A stone charged past its brim on purpose, so far past that when the seeker in its nose told it to it would spend itself all at once, to dust, and in spending itself blow a dead zone out around it in a ball a hundred yards across. It would stay dead until the mana seeped back in from its edges, and out over open water, with nothing dense round it to feed it back, that could take most of a day. Inside that ball, no stone worked. Not the target's. Not anybody's. Whatever was flying on stones inside it stopped flying.

"Dragons too," Armand had said, in the long room, in the autumn. "That's how it was done before. The twins knew. It's in the old songs, if you know how to read them. You don't fight a dragon in its own air. You take its air away."

The third part was the catch. It came apart from the zone head before the head fired and fell behind it, down and away, out past where the dead ball would be, and waited in live air under the edge of it on a little parachute. And when the target fell out of the dead zone into live air again, as it would, falling, it fired its darts. Six of them. Four gravity darts with barbed heads that would bite and hold and push up, as free stones did, so that a falling thing suddenly had four hands under it slowing it down. One drain dart, a mage's cuff the size of a bucket on a barb, that would take whatever the thing had left in it and hold it empty. And the last one, the stasis dart, a pin stone on a hook, that once the thing was slowed enough would hold it where it was.

"And then the net," said Ruan.

"And then the net," said Bram. "Which is a net."

They fired it at nine.

Escort three let the sledge go off her after deck on its stones and it went up and away from her, wobbling a little at first and then settling, east, at a walking pace, two thousand feet over the grey sea, red, shining on every sense stone aboard both hulls like a coal in a dark room. A mile. Two. Escort one turned to put her weapons bay on it.

"Seeker has it," said Ruan, at the bay's board. He had a set of headphones on and was listening to the little sense stone in the rocket's nose, which made a sound, he said, like a cat purring when it had found something it liked. "Purring."

"Clear on the emergency plate," said Emeric.

The speaker on the bridge said Clear in Ward's voice from escort three, and Clear in Tess's from the K Endeavor's hold, and Clear in Orla Garran's from the Hod, faint, from the Band.

"Fire," said Emeric.

Ruan pulled the lever.

The bay's doors were already open. The rocket went out of them sideways, on its rails, and then the booster's stone woke and it went up. Not with a roar. There wasn't any roar. There was a sound like a sheet being snapped in the wind, and the long grey shape was simply going up, very fast, straight up off escort one's flank, and then curving over as the seeker took it, east, toward the red speck in the morning sky. The booster fell off it, tumbling. The rocket went on. It was small, and then it was a dot, and then it was a dot close to the red speck, and Emeric had the glass on both of them.

The zone head fired.

There was nothing to see. That was the strange thing about it, and the thing he'd tried to tell them all in the long room and not managed: a dead zone didn't look like anything. There was no flash. There was a soft dull thud, like a door closed a long way off, that came to the bridge a few seconds later over the water; and in the glass, round the red speck, nothing changed at all, except that the speck stopped moving forward.

And fell.

It fell like a stone. Its stones were dead. The thing Bram had built to fly on four gravity stones and shine like a lamp had nothing in it now but ash and steel and a bucket of fire stones that had gone as dark as the coal they looked like, and it went down through the morning air, turning slowly end over end, red, toward the sea two thousand feet below.

"Come on," said Bram, very quietly, beside him. "Come on, come on."

The catch was under it. Emeric found it in the glass, a small white parachute with something hanging from it, drifting in the live air a quarter mile below the edge of where the dead ball must be. The sledge went past it falling. And as it went past, as it fell out of the dead air into live air, the catch's own seeker purred, and the darts went.

He didn't see them. They were too fast. He saw the sledge jerk in the air as if somebody had grabbed it. It stopped turning over. It went on falling, but slower; and then slower still; and then, for one long astonishing moment, it hung in the air a thousand feet over the sea with four barbed darts in its frame pushing up and a fat grey cuff on its belly drinking whatever was left in it, and didn't fall at all. And then the stasis dart's hook found the frame and bit, and the sledge stopped where it was, in the air, as if it had been nailed to the sky.

The bridge was very quiet.

"Net," said Ruan, his voice gone odd.

Escort three was already under it. She came across the water at a hundred feet with her after deck open and her own crew at the rails, and the net went up off her on four gravity stones of its own, a great square of steel-wire mesh the size of a barn floor, and wrapped itself round the hanging sledge from below the way a hand closes round a fly. Ward's voice on the speaker said, Have it. And the stasis dart let go on its timer, and the sledge came down into the net with a soft bump that they all heard across the water, and lay there, red, with its painted teeth grinning up at the sky.

Bram took his hands off the rail. They'd left marks in the paint.

"I'd like it back," he said. "Somebody tell Ward. I'd like it back in one piece."

It came back in one piece, more or less. One of the gravity darts had gone through a strut and the cuff dart had bent a corner of the frame, and Bram walked round it on escort three's deck at noon with his hands behind his back and a face like a man inspecting a horse that had won a race and come up a little lame.

The zone head's dust was in a tin on the chart table. Ruan had sieved it out of the rocket's empty second stage after they'd fished it out of the sea: a grey powder, very fine, like ash from a pipe, that had been a stone that morning.

"One stone," said Ward, looking at the tin. "For one dragon."

"One stone for one shot," said Emeric. "If it misses, it's a stone in the sea."

"And if it hits, it's a dead zone a hundred yards across round whatever it hits." Ward looked out at the water. "Over a city, that's every lamp in a street. Every heart a healer's holding."

"So it's never fired over a city."

"It's never fired near Kaldo," said Ward. "I know. You've said." He put the tin down. "I'm saying it for the next one who carries it, who won't have heard you."

"Write it on the rack," said Emeric.

Ruan had already written it. In white paint, on the rack's steel, in his big careful letters: NOT WITHIN FIFTY MILES OF ANY TOWN. NOT EVER. R.

The close-in mount was the afternoon, and it was the one Emeric had been dreading, because it was the one that could kill their own people.

It sat on escort one's starboard quarter on a ring of steel the size of a millstone: a short-barrelled gun on the small quick-firer's line, four barrels round a spindle that an electric motor spun, so that it fired faster than a man could count. A seat behind it with a sight. A steel box beside it the size of a wardrobe, with brass gears showing through a glass panel in its side, that Tess called the director and the crew called the clock. And on top of the box, in a glass cap like a tower's, a small sense stone, faint, that could feel anything with mana in it moving fast through the air nearby and tell the clock where it was.

The clock was the clever part. It was Tess's, all gears and cams and a little valve in a cage at the back that she'd added in December, and it did one thing: you told it where a thing was now and how fast it was moving, by the sight and the rangefinder, or by the stone, and it worked out where the thing would be in two seconds, and pointed the gun there. Not at the thing. Where the thing was going. Then the gun fired a stream of shells into the place, and the thing flew into them.

"That's all it is," Tess had said, in the shed, in January. "A shot at where it'll be. Any farmer with a fowling piece does it without thinking. This does it faster."

Escort three was the enemy for the afternoon. She stood off a mile to windward with Dagny on her after deck and a crate of Bram's darts, small ones, steel bolts the length of a forearm with a gravity stone in the tail and a charged fire stone in the head so they'd shine; and she began to throw them. Not at escort one. Past her, close, in the last few hundred yards, the way a mage's bolt or a thrown spear or a Church hull's shot would come, fast and low and from where you weren't looking.

The first one the gun's seat missed. Ruan, in the seat, saw it late; the sight was a good sight and the bolt was a small fast thing coming out of the sun, and he got the gun round onto it just as it went past the stern.

The second one the stone saw first. It purred, the little sense stone in its cap, and the clock heard it and swung the gun before Ruan's hands had moved, and the four barrels spun and the gun made a noise like a sheet of tin being torn in half, very loud, for a second and a half; and a hundred yards off the quarter the bolt came apart in a puff of grey and fell into the sea in pieces.

Ruan sat in the seat with his hands off the grips, looking at where it had been.

"It went before I did," he said.

"It's meant to," said Emeric.

"I know it's meant to." Ruan put his hands back on the grips. "I don't like it."

They took eleven of fourteen. Three got through, two because Dagny threw them in a pair a yard apart and the clock could only point one way at a time, and one because she threw it from straight above where the gun couldn't depress. At the end of it Ruan was grey with the noise and his ears were ringing and he'd stopped saying he didn't like it.

And then they did the canister, which was the part Emeric had wanted most.

It was the same gun, a different round. Instead of a shell, a fat short case that opened at a hundred yards and threw out a cloud of dead shard flakes, the grey ones that littered the Isle's west beaches and that the soldiers had carried home for charms. Each one had been etched, on the Isle, by Vesk's people, in the autumn. And each one, on its own, would stop one arrow. That was all a dead flake could do: a strain shield the size of a fingernail, good for one blow and then gone. But a thousand of them, in a cloud, in front of a hull, was a thousand blows stopped.

Dagny threw six bolts at once into the cloud. Five of them stopped dead in the air, and hung there for a moment, and dropped into the sea with a sound like hail. The sixth went through where the cloud was thin and struck escort one's rail and stuck in it, quivering.

"Better than the shell," said Ruan.

"It's cheaper than the shell," said Bram. "It's grit off a beach."

"It's grit off a beach that stops arrows. Mages. A man in a Church hull with a spear." Ruan got out of the seat and went to the rail and pulled the bolt out of it. "You could fill a hold with it."

"You could."

And then Emeric said the thing he'd been waiting all afternoon to say, before anybody else said it, which was the rule.

"It can't tell our knights from theirs," he said. "Neither can the stone. The stone feels mana moving fast. A knight in a Band suit coming in to land on this deck in a hurry is mana moving fast." He looked at Ruan. "And the clock'll put the gun on him before you can stop it."

Ruan's face changed.

"Only a control stone can choose," said Emeric. "This hasn't got one and it won't have one. So it doesn't choose. You do, before it starts." He turned to the bridge's speaker and keyed the company plate, so escort three and the K Endeavor and the Hod would hear. "When the mounts are hot, the knights come and go on a marked lane. Port side, aft, painted. Nobody comes in on the starboard quarter, ever, when this gun's awake. And before any knight comes in off the lane for any reason, somebody calls check fire on the emergency plate, and the gun's master switch goes off, and Ruan puts his hands in his lap where everybody can see them."

"Check fire," said Ward's voice, from escort three. "On the emergency plate. Written."

"And there's the key." Emeric touched it on the mount's ring, a brass key in a lock on a chain round Ruan's neck. "And the cut-out. And the cams." He showed them: steel stops on the mount's ring that the gun's own barrels hit before they could swing round onto any part of escort one's own deck or rigging. "It can't shoot its own ship. It can shoot its own people. That's the difference, and it's not a small one."

Ruan took the key off over his head and looked at it, and put it back on.

"One pair on escort one," said Bram. "One on three. The K Amara after, when there's time. Never on the Discovery. Never on Zale's boat."

"Never on anything that hasn't got a gun crew who've done this afternoon," said Emeric.

"And at home," said Ward. "By the towers. On the sea wall."

"At home, by the towers. Ambrose's people on it. And the same lane for every knight over the harbour, and the same words." He looked out at the sea, at the sledge on escort three's deck with its teeth, at the grey tin on the chart table with a stone's ashes in it. "It's a good gun. It'll keep a hull alive. It'll not keep it alive by itself, and if anybody ever starts thinking it will, somebody'll get killed."

"Somebody always gets killed," said Ruan.

"I'd sooner it was later," said Emeric.

Ward brought it up again on the Sunday morning, on South Sentinel, which was where Emeric had meant him to bring it up.

They were on the north headland by eight, above the bay, where the wind came straight in off the sea and the grass lay down flat under it. Saint Nobody rode at her mooring a little under a mile out, white, with the bishop's mitre on her bow gone grey after two winters and most of Abloh's gold flaked off it. Arthur and Bren and Mira had come over on the early boat with Ward. Armand had come because Emeric had asked him to and hadn't said why, and was standing a little apart with his collar up, looking at the crate.

The crate was Bram's. He'd brought it down from the long shop on a handcart, and it was the length of a coffin and about as cheerful, and he'd sat on it on the boat all the way across.

"Yesterday," said Ward. "Your rocket." He didn't look at the crate. He looked at the sea. "A hundred yards of dead air round whatever it hits. Fine. Out here, fine. But a dragon isn't going to come at us eighty miles off where it's convenient. It's going to come at us on top of us, and if I fire your net at something a cable off my own hull, my own hull's in the ball and goes down with it."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"So what do I do when it's on top of us?"

Emeric nodded at the crate. Bram got off it and took the lid off.

It was a rifle, if you were generous with the word. It was longer than Bram was tall. The barrel was as thick as a man's wrist and ran nearly the whole length of it, with a squat steel box on the end of it slotted along both sides like a stove door. There was a pair of folding legs under the front, a pad at the back thick as a saddle's, and on top a telescope the length of a forearm in a brass collar that had Ivo's mark on it. A box magazine stood up out of its side. Nothing about it was pretty. Bram had painted it the same grey as the rockets and hadn't let anyone paint a face on it.

"How long have you had that?" said Ward.

"Since the Isle." Emeric didn't dress it up. "Since the wall with the wings on it. I drew it the week Bram broke a scriber on the scale."

"It's been on my floor since October," said Bram. "Six of them. He said it was for the Church's big hulls."

"It is," said Emeric. "As well."

Ward looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the rifle. "You might have said."

"I'm saying."

Bram took a round out of a tin and held it up so they could all see it. The brass case alone ran from the heel of his hand to the end of his fingers, and the bullet in its neck was as thick as his thumb, black at the tip.

"That's not a bullet," said Bren. "That's a shell."

"It's an anti-materiel rifle," said Emeric. "It's for things, not people. Hulls. Engines. Anything with a skin on it a rifle won't go through." He took the round from Bram and turned it in the light. "The core's tungsten carbide. Same metal as the bulb wire, cooked hard with carbon, in a soft steel jacket so it takes the rifling. It's the hardest thing we can make that we can make enough of."

"Ferra's had the price off me," said Bram. "Every one of those is a fortnight of a joiner's wages. She wants to know who's going to pick the brass up off the sea."

Armand had come closer while they talked. He put his hand out over the open crate, flat, the way he held it over a stone to read it, and kept it there.

"I can't feel it," he said.

"There's nothing to feel," said Emeric. "No stone in it anywhere. Not in the gun. Not in the round." He gave the round back to Bram. "Anything with mana in it can be felt. You told me so about the relays. The net's seeker purrs, and the zone head's a stone stuffed past its brim, and something that lives in the Band will feel that coming at it like a lamp coming down a dark road. It won't feel this. It'll hear it, after, if it's still listening." He looked at Ward. "And a dead zone doesn't touch it. Powder doesn't care."

"The K Amara's got her three forties," said Ward.

"On a mount you crank round with six men on it, laid on something the size of a hull going in a straight line." Emeric nodded at the rifle. "Something that turns like a swallow is inside the swing before the layer's found it. And they're shells. They burst on the skin. This is one man lying on a deck. He swings the legs, and he's on it."

"Show me," said Ward.

They did the scale first, because Emeric wanted to and Armand didn't.

It was the plate from under the carved wings in the Isle hall, the dark curved thing the size of two hands, ridged like the wings above it, that had chipped the point off Bram's scriber and kept no mark. Bram had bolted it over an inch of Kaldo plate on an iron frame and stood the frame on the shingle a hundred yards down the beach.

"It's the only one there is," said Armand. "In the kingdom. Possibly in the world."

"That's why," said Emeric. "I'd sooner find out here what it does than find out from the one it came off."

Arthur took the long shop's ordinary rifle first, the self-loader every knight on the K Amara carried, and lay down in the shingle with it, and put one round into the middle of the scale.

It came back.

Nobody saw it go. They heard it: the crack of the shot, and a flat hard clap from the frame, and then a thin whine going away over their heads and up the headland behind them, and a puff of turf high up the slope where something small had gone into the grass. Mira turned round and looked at the hole in the hillside.

"It threw it back," said Bren.

"It threw it back," said Emeric. He was looking at the scale, which hadn't moved and hadn't marked. "That's what it does. It's not hard, or not only. Its grain pushes. Hit it and it hits you."

"That's a dead one," said Armand quietly. "Off a beast that's been gone a very long time. A live one's skin would push harder, I'd think. I don't know. Nobody does."

"Then you don't hit her where she's thick."

Arthur looked up. "Where, then?"

"Throat. Under the jaw. The wing, where it's skin and not scale. The eye, if you're that good."

"I'm that good," said Arthur.

"He isn't," said Bren.

Bram had pinned a steel plate into the rock at the top of the shingle, a yard square, and Arthur lay down on it with the big rifle on its legs and his boots on the steel. Emeric heard the heel switches click, both of them, and the magnets catch. Arthur put his cheek on the stock and looked through Ivo's glass a long time.

"Ears," said Emeric, and they all put their hands over them.

It was not like the other rifle. The sound was a blow in the chest, a door slammed in a stone room, and the slotted box on the muzzle threw the blast out sideways and lifted two sheets of grit off the shingle on either side, and the whole headland seemed to jump. Arthur's body went back an inch against his boots and stopped there.

Down the beach, the frame had fallen over.

They walked down to it. The scale was still bolted to the plate. There was a hole through both of them, clean, the size of a thumb going in and the size of a fist where it came out the back of the Kaldo plate, with the steel turned out round it in a ragged flower. Behind the frame the round had gone on into the shingle and dug a trench in it the length of a man's arm.

Armand knelt and looked at the hole for a long time without touching it.

"That's a dead one," he said again, but differently.

"I know," said Emeric.

Bren wanted a go. Everybody had known Bren would want a go. He didn't want the plate or the boots; he wanted to do it kneeling, the way you'd take a deer, because he said if a knight couldn't hold a gun up a knight had no business with it; and he got it up onto his shoulder easily enough, because he was as strong as any of them, and took a long breath, and fired.

He went backwards a yard and a half across the shingle on his backside with the rifle across his lap, and lay there looking at the sky.

"Oh, no," said Bren.

Mira laughed so hard she had to sit down.

"Your arms can hold it," said Emeric, when he'd stopped laughing himself, which took a while. He gave Bren a hand up. "Your weight can't. It doesn't matter how strong you are. Strong doesn't make you heavy. The round goes one way and the gun goes the other, and the gun takes you with it." He knocked a knuckle on the steel plate. "That's what the boots are for. On a deck they'll hold you to the deck. Off a deck, you lie down on something and let the legs take it."

"Where did that one go?" said Ward.

Nobody knew. Out to sea, probably. Bram wrote it down.

Then the long one. Arthur lay down on the plate again with the boots on and the glass on Saint Nobody, thirteen hundred yards out on the swell, and Ward stood behind him with the big glass from the boat, and Emeric watched the wind on the water.

The first round went short and left; Ward saw the splash a boat's length off her bow. Arthur didn't say anything. He moved something on the side of Ivo's sight, two clicks, and lay still again for long enough that Mira started to fidget.

The second went through the mitre.

"Through," said Ward, with the glass up. "Both sides, I think. There's daylight out the far side of her." He lowered the glass. "Fletcher'll want a word."

"Ferra'll want a line," said Bram. "One hull, sainted. Holed."

Ward took his time on the walk back up the headland, and when he got to the top he stood beside the crate with his arms folded and asked the things Ward asked.

Six rifles: two to a hull, the K Amara and the two escorts. Six hundred rounds, which Ferra had counted twice, in iron boxes in each hull's weapons bay. Arthur and Bren on the K Amara's pair, if Ward agreed; Ruan's two best on each escort, once they'd done a morning like this one. The plate and the boots, always. Nobody kneels. And the mundane recipe on Bram's floor, the barrel and the core and the case, written down, so the yard could make them again without anybody from the convoy.

"Better than one stone for one dragon," said Ward.

"It's the other way round," said Emeric. "Ten rounds for one dragon, and you'll miss with nine." He looked out at the sea, where Saint Nobody was riding a little lower by the bow than she had been. "And it's for if something comes to us. Not for going to it. We don't know what's out there. We don't know what they think of us. I'm not having the first thing we say to anybody be one of these."

"Write that on the crate," said Ward.

"I'll write it," said Bren; and that afternoon, in the long shop, he did, in chalk, on the crate's lid, in very large letters: IF THE DRAGGON COMES TO US. NOT US TO IT. Bram saw the spelling and didn't rub it out.

He gave Sorrel the sheet that afternoon, in the Motion Shop, when the shop was shut and the only people in it were Sorrel and Wren and himself.

It was one sheet. The size of a drafting board, rolled, in a tube. He'd drawn it at night over a fortnight, in the hours after the books, in three pencils; and he'd broken his own rule to do it, nearly, and he knew it. Everything he'd ever given Sorrel since the first glider had been a question. A wing section with nothing on it but why? A drawing of a seed falling. A box kite. He'd never once drawn her an answer.

But she was sailing with him in April, on the K Discovery, as the scout, and the plane was going to be built while she was away, by people who'd never asked any of those questions. And a stall had already broken one of her gliders under her, and she'd walked out of the kindling and sat on the grass for an hour not saying anything.

So he'd drawn the shape.

She unrolled it on the long bench under the window with Wren holding one end, and the three of them looked at it.

A plane. Not her glider, not a glider; a plane with an engine in the nose and a propeller and two wings one over the other, braced with wires, and a tail with a fin and the two flat surfaces either side, and a seat behind the engine. Seen from the side, from the top, from the front. Drawn in her proportions, the long tapered wings she'd been working toward since the first glider, the narrow body, the high tail. Every line clean. Not one dimension on it anywhere.

"There's no sizes," said Wren.

"No."

"How do we build it with no sizes?"

"You don't," said Sorrel. She hadn't looked up. "I do the sizes." She put her finger on the wing, on the line where the top wing met the body. "That's mine. The area. The twist." She moved it along. "That's mine. How big the tail." She looked up at him. "You've left all of it."

"I've left everything you've already started," he said. "And everything that's interesting."

She looked at him a while longer. Then she looked back at the sheet.

The three pencils were three colours, and he'd put a key in the corner. Black was proven: things that had flown, in his head, on Earth, a hundred times, that she didn't need to find out. Blue was experimental: things he thought were right and wasn't sure. Red was must be tested: things that would kill her if she got them wrong and that nobody could work out at a bench, only in the air.

The structure was black. Where the loads went. The spars, the struts, the wires, the points in the body where the wings bolted on and where the engine sat and where the seat went; he'd drawn those exactly and not held anything back, because a wrong guess about where a wing's load went was a wing coming off in the air, and he wasn't leaving her that to find out. The engine was a box with a note in it: Wren and Bram. Light. Every pound in the nose is a pound the wing carries, which was Bram's, from the airship days. The controls were cables and pulleys, drawn out, the stick and the pedals and the wires to the surfaces, and a little cluster of dials in front of the seat, and beside it, in his hand, No stones, because that had been her condition from the beginning and he'd never once argued with it.

The materials were a list. Ash, spruce, Milford linen doped stiff, Polytechnic alloy for the fittings, with two or three things written beside each one that would do instead. Her choice.

And down the right-hand margin, in red, a column.

Stall. Spin. The wing root cracking after many flights, not one. The engine's twist on takeoff pulling you left. Flutter: a surface shaking itself apart at speed. Ice in the carburettor on a damp day, when it isn't even cold.

Sorrel read it twice.

"That's a list of ways to die," she said.

"Yes."

"You've never given me one of those before."

"You've never been building something I won't be here for." He kept his voice level. "I'll be on the same ship as you. You'll have the sheet, and the numbers, and you'll work them aboard. You'll fly models off the deck. You'll have hull four's workshop on its days." He looked at Wren. "And Wren'll have a table of offsets. Every curve on that sheet, as numbers, in a column, so they can go down the relay in Ferra's cipher, because a drawing can't. And the Motion Shop builds to the numbers. And when it's built, it sits on the bench field on its wheels and it runs its engine and it taxis up and down. And that's all it does."

"Until I'm home," said Sorrel.

"Until you're home. You fly your own. You always have." He nodded at the sheet. "It'll be waiting."

Wren was looking at the red column.

"I'm not flying it," she said. "Before she's back. Just so you know. Even if it's finished. Even if it's perfect." She looked up at him. "I'll want to."

"I know you will."

"I'll want to really badly."

"I know."

"I'm just saying it so it's said," said Wren, and he thought that she'd learned that from Tess, and that Tess had learned it from him, and that this was how it went, probably, and that it was all right.

Sorrel rolled the sheet up again, slowly, and put it back in its tube, and held the tube against her chest with both arms the way she'd held the first glider's wing, years ago, before she'd known what any of it was for.

"It's beautiful," she said.

"It's yours."

"It's not mine yet," said Sorrel. "It's yours with my shape on it." She went to the door with the tube. At the door she stopped. "When I've done the sums it'll be mine."

"Yes," he said. "That's the idea."
