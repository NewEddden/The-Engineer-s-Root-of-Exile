Chapter 317 - Corbin's Price

The number took her a week.

Ferra's took two days. Ferra lay on the scanner's table in the long room off the ward on the morning after the chair, with her hands folded on her stomach and her eyes on the ceiling and her mouth shut, because Imani had told her to keep it shut, and held still for the three views, top and side and end, a quarter hour each, while the frame went over her and Bettany turned the plates; and Imani read the plates that night and again the next, and wrote a number at the top of a clean card in Ottoline's ruling, and under it the date, and under that Armand's number for Ferra off last winter's card, for comparison. Then she sat looking at the two numbers and told nobody.

Her own took a week, because she could not hold still on the table and read the plates at the same time, and would not let anybody else read them; and because the frame crawled.

It had always crawled on the dense. That was the scanner's way, since the first one in the infirmary's basement: it went over a commoner like a hand over a page, and over a knight like a hand through mud, because a knight's body was full of his own mana and the frame's mana had to push its way in through it. On Ward it took an hour a view. On Armand, since the spring, Imani had given up. On herself, the year before, it had taken a quarter hour, as it took on anybody who was not a knight or a mage; and now it took most of an hour, and she lay on the table in the long room while Bettany turned the plates and felt the frame go over her slowly, slowly, as if it were wading.

That was a number by itself. She wrote it on the card before she had read a single plate.

Then she read the plates. She read them over six nights, alone, in the long room, with the lamp; and at the end of the week she came up the stair to their rooms at midnight with two cards in her hand and sat down on the end of the bed, where Ferra was asleep and Emeric was not, and held them out to him in the dark.

He lit the lamp. He looked at them.

The numbers at the top were capacity. Armand's word was the number: what a body could hold, at most, the way a stone had a charge it could hold and no more. Armand had read every soul in the castle every winter for years, with his whole sense, standing at the head of the long room table while they came past him in a line, and given each one a number to the year, and Ottoline had put the numbers on cards; and Imani's cards for herself and for Ferra had last winter's numbers on them in Armand's hand.

Both of this year's were higher. Not by much. By more than they should have been. Commoners' numbers did not move. That was the first thing anybody in the tower learned and the last thing Armand had ever doubted: a man born without the gift held what he was born holding, from the cradle to the grave, give or take a fever.

And there was a second number on each card, under the first, that Emeric had never seen on a card before.

"What's that?"

"The room," said Imani.

She said it as if she had been saying it for years; and he understood from the way she said it that she had been saying it to herself all week, in the long room, at night, over the plates, trying it out, until it was hers.

"The number's how much you hold," she said. "Most. Full. Armand could always tell you that to the year, and I can tell it off the plates now if I've a week. But that's not what you've got. That's what you could have." She put her finger on the second line. "This is what's empty. Today. This morning, at ten past nine, when I was on the table. How much room there is in me that isn't full." She took the card back and looked at it. "It moves. The number doesn't, much. The room does. It's different after you've slept. After you've eaten. After you've been on a ward for twenty hours. It was different on the Tuesday from the Monday, on Ferra." She was quiet. "Armand never once read anybody's room. He couldn't. He could read what was there. Not what wasn't."

"Can you?"

"Slowly." She put the cards down on the blanket between them. "Off the plates. With the frame. In a week." She looked at him. "But I can. And nobody else can. Not Armand. Not you, I think."

"Not me."

"No." She sat for a while. "Then it's mine."

"It's yours."

She did not say anything about the two numbers at the top being higher than they had been, or why. She did not ask him. She had not asked him in the dark in his lap a week ago either, with Ferra's lamp gone out under Ferra's hand; and he saw now, by the lamp, that she was not going to ask him until she had worked it out herself, and that she had nearly. She put the cards in the pocket of her dress, and blew out the lamp, and lay down beside Ferra in the dark without undressing, on top of the blanket, and was asleep in a breath, the way she slept after a week on a ward.



Tess ran the slag on the thirtieth.

She ran it at the Motion Shop's bench, where she kept a second rig for when she was in Kaldo, because she ran everything. That was the whole of the reason, she told Ferra afterward, when Ferra asked why on earth she had put a pot of skimmed scum through the threshold rig; it had been on the shelf by the furnace room door where the things went that Tess said to keep, and it had fire dust in it, a pound and a half of good dust gone to waste, and she wanted to know whether it was waste.

The threshold rig was hers. A row of little test moulds on the Dust House bench, each one a slab of plain render with a different share of dust ground into it, from a twentieth by bulk at one end up to a third at the other; and a keystone set in each, and a thermometer in each, Merrin's, so that she could see at what share of dust a slab began to be warm. It had always been a third. Under a third the dust in a slab was too thin to touch itself all through, and the instruction from the keystone could not carry; it went a little way into the slab and died, like a word said into a gale. At a third, the slab came on all at once, warm from end to end. Everybody in Kaldo who poured anything knew it. A third.

Tess ground the slag fine, in the mill, and mixed it into her moulds at every share, and set the keystones, and lit them.

The slab at a tenth came on.

She did not believe it. She tore it out and poured it again, and it came on again; and then she sat on her stool at the bench with the thermometers in a row in front of her, and the one at a tenth sitting where the one at a third had always sat, warm; and she looked at it for an hour without moving, and then sent Wren across the works yard for Bram.

"It's the melt," she told him, when he came. "That's all I can think. It's been through the melt." She held up a pinch of the ground slag in her fingers. "When you stirred it in, it didn't just float. It went through the steel and through the dross, white-hot, and it smeared. Every grain. All over every other grain, thin, like butter on bread. So it doesn't need to be a third any more. It reaches. A tenth of it does what a third of the raw stuff does, because it's spread so thin it touches itself everywhere." She put the pinch down. "Your bar cracked, Bram. Your slag didn't."

Bram looked at the thermometer and rubbed his jaw.

"Slag cement," he said at last.

"What?"

"We grind the furnace slag into the cement already. Have done since the year of the walls. It's half of every bag of cement that goes out of the works." Bram rubbed his face. "So this goes in the same. In the same bags. Ground the same. And it's a third the dust." He looked at her. "A third the dust for every wall. Every pour. Every plate."

"Two thirds less," said Tess. "Yes."

Ferra re-priced the whole dust line that night. All of it: every keyed render and every warm wall and every form liner for the winter pours and every pound of armour backing in Kaldo plate, everything that took dust, which by the end of the year was most things; at a tenth instead of a third. It took her the evening and half the night, at her table, with the grey book and the stone book and the works' year book open together, and Emeric asleep in the chair beside her, because he had tried to help and been told he was slower than doing it herself. In the morning the dust mill's output, which had been a quarter of what the works wanted, was three quarters of it.

And at the top of the new page, in her upright figures, she had written: Bram's mistake.

Bram saw it at breakfast. He asked her to change it.

"No," said Ferra.

"It's not a mistake. It's slag. It's a... it's a finding."

"It's your mistake," said Ferra, buttering her bread. "You poured fire dust into a melt because you were sure it would work, and it didn't, and you skimmed it off, and you'd have thrown it on the heap if Tess hadn't been sitting by the door. That's a mistake. It's the best mistake anybody's made in this town in a year. It's going in the book as one." She bit the bread. "I'm not changing it, Bram."

Bram looked at her across the table for some time, with the face of a man who has been paid a compliment and cannot find a way to refuse it.

"Dust's not endless," said Ferra, more quietly, after a while, to her plate. "Every grain of it was a stone. Every stone that goes into a wall's a stone that doesn't go in a ship or a lamp. If the walls take a third as much, the stones go three times as far." She turned a page. "That's a great deal of a mistake."



Then the plating, which was Tess's, and which went wrong in three ways, all of them good.

"If it won't go in," Emeric had said to her, at the table, in October, the only thing he had said about it at all, "put it on."

So she put it on. The works had had nickel since the year of the hospital's instruments and chromium since the year after, and the plating room off the forge shop had a row of tanks in it where Bram's people hung parts in a bath with a current through it and a bar of nickel at the other end, and the nickel came off the bar through the water and grew on the part, a skin, bright, thin, even. Tess stirred dust into the bath. The metal would grow on the part, she reasoned, and wherever a grain of dust was touching the part when the metal grew, the metal would grow round it and swallow it, and lock it in the skin. A skin with dust in it. Enough dust, close enough, and the skin would take an instruction.

Her first test was a spoon. She used lightning dust, because it was on the bench.

The spoon came out of the tank the next morning covered in fur.

It was a fine grey metal fur, a quarter of an inch long, standing straight up off every part of it: the bowl and the handle and the back, soft-looking and stiff, like the coat of a very small and very angry animal. Lightning dust carried current. The nickel had not grown round the grains. It had grown on them, each one, because each one was another place for the current to go; and then on the nickel that had grown on them, and on that, outward, in little branching trees, all night.

Wren found it. She came into the plating room at seven, saw it on the rack, and stopped dead.

"It's got a beard," she said.

Tess, coming in behind her on her stick, saw it, and said nothing for a while.

"It's Bram," said Wren.

And so it was Bram's spoon. It was Bram's spoon by noon, all over the works, and by evening in every kitchen in the castle; and nobody ever called it anything else, though Bram, shown it, said it looked nothing like him, and then looked at it again and touched his beard, and went away.

Lightning dust came off the plating list. Tess wrote it in her book in red, though it had not hurt anybody, because she said Bram's feelings counted.

The second test was gravity dust, on a flat steel plate, and it did not grow a beard; it sank. Gravity dust was heavy. It went to the bottom of the bath and lay there, and the plate hanging in the bath came out with its bottom edge thick with a crust of dusty nickel and its top edge bare and bright. Tess looked at it for a minute and then sent Wren to the works for a pump and a length of pipe with holes drilled in it, and laid the pipe along the tank's floor, and pumped air up through it, a steady curtain of bubbles from the bottom of the bath to the top, all night, so that the dust could not settle and went round and round in the water like snow in a shaken jar. The next plate came out even.

The third was shard dust, and it worked, and it was the best of the three, because of Bram.

Shard dust, laid in a skin on a steel face and keyed, did a little of what the shard stone did: it slowed what came at it, over the last inch, so that a thing that hit it arrived softer than it set out. Tess had plated a test square with it, and a knife thrown at the square had hit it and dropped, blunt-end first, as if it had been tossed underhand. Bram did not believe the knife. He said a thrown knife was nothing; a knife was a toy. He said that if Tess wanted to show him a thing that slowed what hit it, she could put it on something that got hit for a living.

So she plated an anvil.

It was a spare, from the back of the forge shop, a small one; Bram had chosen it himself, scornfully, and watched her hang its face in the bath overnight with the bubbles going and the shard dust going round, and set the keystone in its side in the morning, and key it. Then he took his own hammer off its hook, the small one he used for the finishing work, and stood over the anvil, and lifted the hammer, and brought it down on the plated face the way he had brought a hammer down on an anvil fifty thousand times since he was a boy.

The hammer stopped dead.

It did not ring. It did not bounce. It came down onto the face and, over the last inch, slowed, as if it had gone into thick mud, and arrived on the steel with a dull small tap like a man knocking on a door he does not want answered; and stopped, and lay there on the face, and did not come up again.

Bram stood with the hammer in his hand, its head lying on the anvil, and looked at it.

An anvil's whole work was to give the hammer back. That was what an anvil was. A smith hit it and it threw the hammer up again, and the smith rode the throw into the next blow, and so a smith could work all day; and this anvil had taken the hammer and kept it, and given nothing back, and lay there under it like a sack of meal.

"It's dead," said Bram.

His voice was a voice Emeric had never heard him use. He sounded personally insulted, as if the anvil had said something about his mother.

"It's not dead," said Tess. "It works. That's what it's for. That's what it does."

"It's an anvil."

"It's a very good anvil for not being hit."

Bram lifted the hammer and brought it down again, harder, and it stopped again, over the last inch, with the same small dull tap; and he stood there with his whole weight on the hammer's handle and looked at the anvil with such a depth of betrayal in his face that Emeric, in the door, laughed out loud before he could stop himself.

Bram turned round and looked at him.

"Sorry," said Emeric. He was not able to stop. "Sorry, Bram. It's... your face..."

"It's a dead anvil," said Bram, with dignity.

"It's the right thing," said Emeric, when he could. "It's just on the wrong thing." He wiped his eyes. "Put it on the canister sleeves. The liquid mana. Every flask the fleet carries, in its double case. If a ball hits the case, or a splinter, it slows over the last inch, and the inner case has a chance." He looked at the anvil. "That's what it's for. Catching. Not hitting."

Tess was writing. "Catch plating," she said. "Shard, on the sleeves. Water dust, on pump parts, to shed. Fire, on leading edges." She paused, and looked at the plate on the rack with the even skin of gravity dust on it, and at the tank with its bubbles. "And water dust on a hull. A ship's. Below the line." She wrote it, and underlined it. "Next."

Bram, at the anvil, picked up his hammer and turned it over in his hands, and looked at the dead face, and then at the sheet of thin copper on the bench beside it that his finishing man had been planishing all week on the old anvil, with the hammer bouncing and the sheet crawling and buckling under every blow.

He did not say anything. He picked the anvil up, with a grunt, and carried it over to the finishing bench himself, and set it down where the old one had been.

Nobody said anything to him about it. He would not have admitted it was useful if he had been asked under oath; and nobody ever did ask him, and the dead anvil stayed on the finishing bench, under the copper, for the rest of his working life.



The Well's crew jumped on the thirtieth, over the Mere, from the Hod.

It was the rule. Imani's rule, and Bram's, and Orla Garran's, and nobody's that anybody could remember first making: that every soul who would ever live on the Well would have jumped out of a ship, over water, with a chute and a bottle and a mask, before they ever went up to live on it. The Well would hang four miles up. If everything on it ever failed at once, every stone and every engine and every bank, the drill was that the station would dump its tank high, and fall slow on its inducers, and open its chutes, and come down on the sea or the Mere and float; but if that failed too, a person had their own chute and their own bottle, and the first two miles down from the Band were too thin to breathe and too cold to live, and a person had better know what that was like before the day it happened.

So the Hod went up over the Mere on the thirtieth, at noon, in a clear cold sky, with twenty of the crew aboard in their Band suits and the two Kalden knights in theirs, to two miles; and Imani went up with them, because she would.

She stood at the chute locker by the Hod's after hatch with the crew in a line in front of her and Bettany beside her with the list, and before she looked at a single one of them she counted the chutes.

She counted them out loud. Twenty-two packs on twenty-two hooks, and the spares on the shelf above, and the bottles and masks in their rack beside, one each, and the reserves, one on every pack's front. She counted every one, and touched every one, and only when she had counted them all and Bettany had said the number back to her did she turn round and count the people.

"Why the chutes first?" said Tamsin Reeve, the knight, who was nearest.

"Because you'll always tell me you're all here," said Imani. "And you'll always be right. Nobody ever forgets to count the people." She took the first pack off its hook and held it out. "Count the chutes before you count the people. Then there's one for each of them."

Orla Garran took the pack, and turned it over, and looked at the packing tag on its back with Bram's floor's number on it and the date and the packer's mark; and nodded; and put it on.

They went out of the after hatch one at a time, clipped to a static line along the Hod's keel that pulled each chute open behind them as they fell away from her, because it was their first; and Emeric, at the hatch with Imani, watched them go. Orla first, without a word, without looking down, as if stepping off a quay. Then the works' seven and the Hod's four, who had been in the Band so often that the air under them meant nothing; and the nurse, Agnes Tull, a quiet woman who closed her eyes at the hatch and kept them closed; and the cook, who touched his brow for the Eye and went. Tamsin Reeve went last but one, laughing. Lucan Ashby went last of all, very white, because he flew better than he fought and knew it, and had never in his life been in the air without his suit's hearts lit under him, and found that he did not like it.

The chutes opened below the Hod one after another, white, in a long string down the sky over the Mere, like a line of washing.

All but one.

Emeric saw it from the hatch. The ninth chute, one of the Hod's Band hands, came off the static line and out of its pack and did not open. It went up behind the man in a long white twist, streaming, the silk tangled round itself in a rope, and the man under it fell, fast, much faster than the others, past the open chutes below him one by one, with the twisted silk cracking over his head.

Emeric had his hand out before he knew it. He had the man. He could feel him, falling, a commoner's light note dropping through the cold air, and he could take him, as he had taken a ton off a fouled chain; and Imani's hand came down on his wrist at the hatch, hard, and held it.

"Wait," she said.

He waited. It was the hardest thing he had done that year.

The man's hand went to the front of his harness. Emeric saw it go, very small, two thousand feet below, a dark speck against the grey water of the Mere with a white rope streaming over it; to the front, to the red handle Bram had put on every pack's front over the reserve, where the drill said it was and where the drill had made every one of them put a hand on it fifty times on the ground with their eyes shut. The hand found it. It pulled.

The reserve went out of its pack on the man's chest, white, small, round, and filled with a crack Emeric heard from the Hod, and the man's fall stopped, all at once, with a jerk, under a white dome half the size of the others, swinging; and the tangled main came down on top of it and slid off it, and hung below him from his harness like a long white tail.

He came down into the Mere a long way from the others, faster than they did, and hard. The boats were there. The boats were always there.

Imani took her hand off Emeric's wrist. She did not look at him. She was looking down at the white dome on the Mere's grey water, and the boat pulling toward it, and her face was perfectly calm.

"That's why there's one," she said.

The man was in her infirmary that evening with a wrenched shoulder from the reserve's jerk and nothing else, sitting up in bed, very pleased with himself and very frightened, and telling everybody who came near him that he had pulled the handle with his eyes shut, the way the drill said. Bram came to see him at seven and stood at the foot of the bed and asked him which packer's mark had been on the tag of the chute that fouled. Then Bram went down to the works, where the chutes were packed, and nobody saw him for the rest of the night; and in the morning every packer on the floor had a new rule, written in Bram's hand on a board over the packing tables, that every chute was packed by one and checked by another and signed by both, and that the second one had better not be a friend of the first.



On the last night of October it was Hollow Night.

It was the third. The first had been a story night, two years ago, Emeric's, a thing he had made for the children out of a book: turnip lanterns, sweet or story at every door, children dressed as anything they could think of that nobody had seen. It had been weird the first year. It was not weird now. Grayrock had it, off the rail. Milford had it. The chancery in the capital, Saban said, had asked him once what Kaldo's mask night was, as if it were a thing to be worried about.

Emeric walked down through the lower ward at nine with Ferra on one arm and Imani on the other, because they had said he would, and because he wanted to.

The lanterns were out on every step. Turnips, carved, with a candle or a little light stone in each, grinning in rows up every stair in the ward; and the children were out in the lanes in their costumes in the cold, in knots, going from door to door with their bags, shrieking, with their mothers behind them in shawls. There were dragons, three children under a sheet with a stick-and-paper head. There were tall ones with long ears, and short broad ones with hammers, and a slime that was a boy in a dyed oilcloth sack who could not see where he was going. There was a mermaid, carried, because her tail was too tight to walk in.

And there were the demons.

Half the boys in the lane were in black. Black wool, black sacks, black cloth tied over their faces with eyeholes in it, and no crest on any of them, and nothing on their chests; and they went from door to door in a pack, silent, with their bags, and when a door opened they stood in a row on the step and said nothing at all, which was the point, and which made the women at the doors laugh and give them two sweets each instead of one.

"Demons of the Isle," said Imani. "Listen to them not saying anything."

"Finn started it," said Ferra. "The first year. Rylan's never once been able to tell him not to."

Emeric watched them go up the lane: a pack of small boys in black, silent, crestless, coming out of the dark at doors with nothing on their faces, the way the freed men off Rowena's ships had said the knights had come out of the sea. They had made a game of it. They did not know that the men they were dressed as were going north in a month in the same black wool, and that some of the knights who had been those men on the Isle in the year of the camp had been loading the K Endeavor's hold all afternoon with chutes and bottles and racks of shells for a war, and were down at the yard now, by the light of the yard's lamps, still loading.

At the bottom of the lane, through a gap between the houses, he could see the yard. The K Amara's black flank in the lamplight in the long shed's open door, and the carts going in and out of the shed with their loads under canvas, and the cranes swinging. Somebody on her deck was singing. The sound came up the lane small and far off in the cold, between the children's shrieking, a work song, the one the yard sang when it was loading and wanted to get done.

A boy in black came out of the dark at his elbow, and stood, and said nothing, and held out his bag.

Emeric looked down at him. He had a sack on his head with eyeholes cut in it, and black wool on his arms, and no crest; and he stood there, perfectly silent, with the bag held out, and waited.

Imani gave him a sweet. Ferra gave him a sweet. Emeric, who had nothing, put his hand in his coat and found, after a moment, the slip Ferra had given him at breakfast on the twentieth with the K Amara's lift budget on it, eleven thousand four hundred and six, that he had carried in his coat since; and folded it in four, and put it in the bag.

The demon looked into the bag at the slip. Then up at him, through the eyeholes, for a long moment.

"That's not a sweet," it said.

"It's a number," said Emeric. "It's worth more."

The demon considered this. Then it went off up the lane after the others, silent again, with its bag, and the slip in it; and Ferra put her face in Emeric's shoulder and shook; and Imani said, "Read it again," very solemnly, to nobody, and that set Ferra off worse.



Saban came up the hill on the sixth of November with three slips, and a face that had priced all three before he came through the gate.

"Kesrath's man is in the capital," he said, in the long room, before he had sat down. "Has been since the first week of October. A lord of their council, not a clerk; a cousin of their king's. He crossed the strait at night in a fishing boat, under Varek's nose, the week after our September run, and took horse in the march, and nobody in the capital paid him any mind, because envoys come and go." He sat. "I'd say his king sent him to ask who was blowing holes in the sea under his tower. He's seen the Lieutenant twice. The first time, three weeks ago, in council, with everybody looking, and nothing came of it. The second time, four days ago, in the Lieutenant's own rooms, with nobody else there but the two secretaries. An hour and a half." He turned his hat. "The day before that, a rider came in to Kesrath's man's lodging from the coast, on a horse that had been ridden to death, with a letter."

"The bell," said Ward.

"The bell."

"You know what was said," said Ferra.

"I know what was agreed. My man in the chancery copies things fair." Saban took the first slip out of his hat. "Kesrath recognizes His Highness the Lieutenant of the Realm as the lawful governor of Randillia while the king cannot govern. In writing, to go under their king's seal. The first crown outside this kingdom to say so." He laid it down. "And His Highness the Lieutenant of the Realm, as governor, recognizes the right of the crown of Kesrath to Verandel's Claim."

The long room was quiet.

"The whole of it?" said Ward.

"The whole of it. The Claim's end of the Isle, as far as Randillia's charter runs. Every right that's ours by the charter, given to them by a man who's got no right to give it, for a sheet of paper saying he has." Saban turned his hat on his knee. "It's not signed. Not yet. It's agreed. The envoy took it home to his king to be sealed. My man thinks a month."

Emeric looked at the far wall.

The Claim was nothing to Corbin. He had never been to the Isle. It was a strip of dune and marsh at the west end of a contested island five hundred miles from the capital, with a charter that said it was the crown's, and a pitch lake that the Kesrathi end shared, and a fishing village and some cliffs, and nothing else that anybody in the capital had ever thought worth a lord's afternoon. And now it had oil under it; and somebody in the capital had heard that the oil was Kaldo's; and Corbin had looked at a map and seen that he could give it away for a thing he wanted more.

"There's a second slip," said Saban.

The second slip was the capital's gossip, and Saban did not read it; he told it. It was in every tap and every vintners' hall and on the steps of every parish church in the lower town by the end of the week: that Kaldo had been stealing the crown's oil out of the crown's own island for years, by sorcery, under the sea, and selling it, and that the duke's ships flew on it, and that Kesrath had caught them at it and hauled up the proof onto a beach. Nobody in the capital had seen the bell. Nobody in the capital knew what it was or what oil was or where the Isle was, very nearly. They knew the duke was a thief now as well as a traitor.

"It's true," said Emeric.

"It's true," Saban agreed. "That's what makes it good gossip. The best gossip's always true at the bottom. You just have to change what's on top." He turned the hat. "It's not proved. Nobody in the capital can prove it. Kesrath can't prove whose the bell is. But the Lieutenant doesn't need it proved. He needs it said. And he's having it said."

Ward had not moved since the first slip. He was looking at the table, at the place in front of him where there was nothing, the way Emeric had seen him look at a field before a fight.

"Kesrath won't wait for the seal," he said.

They looked at him.

"They've forty sail in their roads. They've had them there since March, paid for by somebody. Varek's across from them on the strait with half the host, and he can't leave, and they know he can't." Ward put one finger on the table. "Now they've got a paper that says the Claim's theirs, from the only man in the capital who can say so. And they've got a beach with our bell on it that says there's oil under the Claim that somebody's been taking. And they know whose ships come and go at night." He laid a second finger beside the first. "So they'll land. They'll land on the Claim the night they know our ships are somewhere else. Not before; they've seen what we've got, or heard it. They'll wait till we're over the capital, with every hull we've got hanging over the cathedral, and then they'll put their forty sail across the strait onto the Claim and take the wells and the beach and the cliffs, and sit on them, with a sheet of paper in their pocket that says they're only taking what's theirs." He took his fingers off the table. "And we'll have to choose."

"Choose what?"

"Which war," said Ward.

Nobody spoke.

"Then we have two," said Emeric.

Ward looked at him.

"We don't choose," said Emeric. "We have two. The capital, with the fleet. And the Isle, with whatever we can spare from it. Not to take anything. Not one foot of Kesrath's end; it's theirs, it's always been theirs, they've a charter and a tower and three hundred years. Just to hold the Claim. The wells and the beach. Till it's over." He looked at Ferra. "What can we spare?"

"I'll tell you tomorrow," said Ferra, who was already writing.

"And there's the third slip," said Saban.

He did not take it out of his hat. He held the hat on his knee and looked at it.

"The carter," he said. "My man found him on his way back south, at the inn, with a new felloe and a load of empties. He asked him what you wanted to know." Saban looked at Emeric. "How many carts a year to the yard at Ostrel. Since he started."

"And?"

"He couldn't say for the whole road. He's one man. But he knows his own, and he knows how long the queue at Pryor's Gate is, because he's stood in it every trip for nine years, and a carter can tell you to the wagon how long a queue is." Saban took out the slip at last. "The first five years, he says, the oak was the same every trip. Eight or ten long carts ahead of him at the gate, the north forest men, with their baulks. The sixth year it was twelve. The seventh, sixteen. Last year twenty." He put the slip down. "This year, since the spring, he's stood behind thirty-one. Every trip. Thirty-one carts of long oak at the gate ahead of him, going up, and the gate men waving them through without opening a tarpaulin. And the bronze is doubled. He hauls twice what he hauled in the spring." Saban looked at Emeric across the table. "He said it was a great deal of roof."

Ferra had stopped writing.

She looked at her book, at the page where she had written roof in August and put her little hooked mark beside it. Then she drew a line through roof, one line, very straight, as she drew a line through any figure she had found was wrong; and wrote nothing beside it. She did not need to. Everybody at the table had seen the white hull in Emeric's drawing.

"How many?" said Ward quietly. "Ships. Out of that much oak."

"I don't know," said Emeric. "More than one. More than we have." He looked at the slip. "Double this year. They're hurrying."

"For what?"

"For us," said Emeric.



They drew the plan for the cathedral the next night, at the long table, with the capital lit on the sand table in the map room across the yard and the drawings carried back and forth between the two by Tess's boy until the boy said his legs would not do the stair again.

Emeric wrote the rule at the head of the sheet before anything else. He wrote it large, in ink, so that it could not be rubbed out, in a hand that he made himself keep steady; and the table watched him write it.

If anyone is inside, nothing falls.

Then the five steps under it, one at a time, because a thing that had to be done with ten ships and a city of a hundred and twenty thousand people in it had to be broken down until each piece of it was something a man could do with his hands.

One. The leaflets. The printing works' big press, the forme that had stood in its chase since August with a border and a rule and no words: forty thousand sheets, quarter-folio, dropped from hull two over every ward of the capital on the day before. What Kaldo was making war on, and on whom it was not. The hour. The distance to keep. A map. Plain words, as plain as Ferra could make them, which was very plain; and nothing else on the front, because a sheet with two things on it is a sheet with one thing nobody reads.

Two. The back of the leaflet. That was the place for the letter, they all agreed; it was only nobody had yet said whose.

Three. The proof. On the morning, early, one rod, on something empty that every soul in the capital could see and hear: the crown's drill yard on the east side of the lower city, four hundred yards of beaten earth where men drilled in lines, and nobody on it at that hour. If it came down at dawn, nobody in the city would think the leaflets were a bluff.

Four. The voice. At mid-morning the K Discovery, low, unarmed, over the square in front of the porch, with the brass horns on her that had called the hours of a launch at the yard since the first lift; calling the hour, and the distance, and the name of every gate that was open. And the cathedral's own bells rung at the end of it, by a shore team of Saban's people inside the close, as the last warning, so that every soul in the city heard the cathedral itself say it.

Five. The count. Emeric, and every mage aboard, sweeping the building from above with their whole sense before anything fell, until it read empty; and a shore team under Rylan going in through the porch to walk it, room by room, cellar to bell loft, for anybody who could not walk out or would not.

"And if they won't?" said Imani.

"Then we wait," said Emeric.

"How long?"

"Until they come out." He did not look at Ward. "However long."

Ward let it go. He had said it on the wall walk in August. Emeric felt his note at the end of the table, steady and hard, and knew that he was being held to it.

"Not by the river," said Ward instead.

They looked at him.

"Saban's man sold him the river. He's got two chains and a boom under the old bridge and a guard boat at each end, and he thinks that's where we'll come from." Ward put his finger on the sand table's drawing, on the old bridge. "So we come from the sky, which he knows, and never once go near the water, which he doesn't. And when the boom's still there on the morning, with men sitting on it in boats waiting for us, that'll tell Saban his man's still believed." He took his finger off. "That's all. I'd not have us forget it in the hurry and send a boat."

"Written," said Ferra.

The king was on the bench by the wall, where he always sat. He had said nothing at all for two hours. He had listened to the five steps one by one with his hands on his knees, and Emeric had felt his note through every one of them, steady, a knight's under a king's, and had not been able to tell from it what he thought. He had been crowned under that roof. He had stood at its altar rail with each of his children.

It was not the king who spoke.

Doran had come up from the parish at nine, because Ferra had asked him to the table about the death book and the winter's burials, which were his; he had sat at the table's foot through the death book, and stayed, because nobody had told him to go, through the five steps. He was an old man now. He had been old when Emeric came south, a thin stooped priest in a patched black coat with a voice too big for him, who had kept the parish's death book through the plague and buried every one of them himself and never once asked the Church for a copper; and he had sat through two hours of a war on his own Church's greatest house with his hands folded on the table and his face as grave and quiet as if he were at a deathbed.

"The back of the sheet," said Doran. "Whose letter?"

Nobody answered him.

"You haven't said, because you don't know who'd sign it. You can't sign it yourself, Your Grace; a letter from you on the back of a letter from you is the same letter twice. You can't ask the king; he can't sign anything." He looked down the table. "You need a churchman. A man the faithful would believe. Who'd tell them that it's no sin to leave a church when a church is going to fall, and no sin to stand in a street and watch it, and no sin, God help us, to be glad it's gone if it was the house that did what that house has done." His big voice did not rise. "A man who still believes. Because if it's a man who doesn't, they'll know, and they'll stay inside to spite him."

"Father," said Emeric.

"Let me finish." Doran unfolded his hands and laid them flat on the table, old hands, spotted, with the knuckles swollen. "I've kept my faith. You know I have. You've never once asked me not to. Through the plague, and the cellars at Wayford, and the heresy case they built out of my own death book, God forgive them; through all of it, I kept it. I've not kept the Church. I gave that up a long time ago, in my own vestry, with the death book open in front of me, the night I counted what the close had done and not done." He looked at Emeric. "But I've kept my faith. And my faith says the Light was never in the stone. It was never in that house. It was never in any house, and the men who told the people it was, so they'd be afraid to leave, were lying, and knew it."

The long room was entirely silent.

"So I'll write it," said Doran. "On the back. In my own name, and my parish, and my years. That the Light was never in the stone, and it's no sin to go." He folded his hands again. "Nobody asked me. I'd like that written too, Ferra. That nobody asked me."

Ferra wrote it.

On the bench by the wall the king had bowed his head; and Emeric, who had his sense on him, felt the knight's brightness in his father's note go down and down, very slowly, as if a man had sat down at last after standing a long time, and stay there.
