Chapter 269 - Tullio's Book

The first of the old clients left the House on the second of June, by letter, and the letter was very polite.

It came to Vantic at her plain desk at the end of the bank's long room, by the morning post from the capital, in a lord's blue wax; and she read it at her desk in the time it took the clerk to count a carter's deposit, and folded it, and put it in her book. The lord had banked with House Ducatis for thirty years and his father before him. He wished the House well. He had heard that its head now kept her seat in a southern barony, in a town of which he had heard a great deal, much of it, he was sure, untrue; and he felt, on reflection, that his affairs would sit more comfortably with a house whose head kept her seat nearer the capital, and the crown, and the Church. He had instructed his steward accordingly. He remained, with every respect, her servant.

"How much?" said Emeric, when she showed it him that evening in Ferra's room. She had not meant to show it him. Ferra had asked her, at the door, what the blue wax was, and Vantic did not lie.

"Enough to notice," said Vantic. "Not enough to matter."

The second came on the fifth. The third, on the ninth, did not trouble to be polite; it was from a wool merchant in the river towns who had borrowed from the House for twenty years and never once been late, and it said that he did not care to have his paper in a house that took its orders from a heretic duke, and that he would be obliged if his balance were sent to him in coin, by the end of the month, to a house in the capital he named.

Vantic read that one at her desk too. She did not fold it. She read it twice, and put it on the desk in front of her, flat, and looked at it for a while with her hands on either side of it.

"The name of the house," said Ferra, who had come into the bank at eleven with the quarter's sealed returns from Wayford and found her like that.

"Yes," said Vantic.

Ferra read it upside down from the other side of the rail. It was the name of the house in the capital that kept the crown book: the House's own oldest office, in the street behind the palace, where Vantic's eldest brother had sat at the same desk for twenty years.

"He's asked them to send the coin to Tullio," said Ferra.

"He's asked them to send the coin to the House," said Vantic, "at the House's capital office, which is Tullio's book." She folded the letter at last. "The coin goes from my vault to my brother's. It doesn't leave the House. On paper nothing's happened." She put it in her book with the others. "On paper."



Tullio left on the fourteenth.

Vantic did not say so. Saban did, on the evening of the fifteenth, at the long table in the delve, where he said most things that he did not want to say twice; and he said it the way he said everything that came up from the capital on his wires, in the flat tone of a man reading a price off a board.

Tullio Ducatis had taken the crown book out of the House.

Not stolen it. He had been entitled. The crown book was the House's lending to the crown and the high nobles, and Tullio had kept it twenty years; and the custom of the House, older than any of them, was that a book went with the hand that kept it if the hand chose to go. He had chosen. He had sent his resignation to the head of the House at her seat in Kaldo on the fourteenth by the ordinary post, and on the same day he had taken a lease on a house of his own in the street behind the palace, three doors down from the House's capital office, and the crown's own treasurer had walked down those three doors that same afternoon and sat in Tullio's new front room for two hours. And the Lieutenant's secretary had walked down them on the fifteenth.

"Corbin's," said Ward.

"Corbin's," said Saban. "The crown's debt is in that book. The palace roof. The Isle garrisons. The pensions. Half the old lords' mortgages." He turned a page. "And Lord Grier's. All of it, and the bribe with it, the one that was written off. It went through that book. It's in Tullio's hand."

Nobody said anything.

"So the crown goes on dealing with the House," said Saban, "at Tullio's door. As if nothing had happened. And at the House's door, here, it'll deal with Vantic as little as it's able. It won't break anything with her. It'll just go to Tullio first." He shut the book. "The crown's going to have a banker. It's going to be him."

Emeric looked down the table. Vantic did not sit at the long table; she was not a delver, and did not know it existed. But he could see her as clearly as if she had been sitting in Saban's place, at her plain desk in the bank, with three letters in her book.

"She knows," he said.

"She's known since the fourteenth," said Saban. "I'd lay money she knew before he did."



Cassian came on the eighteenth, on the morning boat from the coast, and meant to stay a week.

He was not what Emeric had expected. He had expected something like Vantic: small and grey and still. Cassian Ducatis was a tall loose-jointed man of forty or so in a very good brown coat that he wore as if he had slept in it, which he had, on the boat, with his long legs over the rail; and he came up the steps of the bank at nine with a satchel over his shoulder and his hat in his hand, and stood in the middle of the long room under the tall windows and turned all the way round, slowly, like a man in a church, and then went to the counter and asked the clerk to open him an account, with a single Silver, in his own name.

The clerk did. Cassian took the note and held it up to the window and looked at the falcon for a long time.

Then he went to the rail at the end of the room. Vantic had not looked up.

"You're the only one who ever beat me at the coast game," he said. "Did you know I still play it? Every year. I've never won since you left."

"You didn't win when I was there."

"No," said Cassian. "That was rather the point." He put his elbows on the rail and looked at the bird in his note again. "Tullio's gone to the Lieutenant."

"I know."

"He was always going to. You knew that when you took the seat." Cassian turned the note over. "I didn't come to talk about Tullio."

Vantic put her pen down.

"I came," said Cassian, "to see what it is you think you're doing here. And to see whether I think so too." He folded the note and put it in his coat. "I'll want a week."

He took three.

He spent them, as far as anyone in Kaldo could tell, going everywhere. He went down to the port and stood on the quay and talked to the lightermen for an afternoon about tonnage. He rode the rail to Grayrock and back, twice, with a notebook on his knee, and on the second time got off at every halt and walked the platform and got on the next train. He went to the brickfield and asked to see the kiln the bank had lent for, and was shown it, and asked what it cost to fire it, and wrote down the answer. He sat in the records hall for a whole day reading the census and the market returns, in a corner, without asking anybody for anything, and Ottoline brought him cards when he looked as if he wanted them and he thanked her each time as if she had done him a great service. He bought a ticket on the public lift hull to Milford and back, a Silver, and came back in it white and quiet and went to bed. He ate at the inn by the bridge every night, alone, at the table by the window, and the landlord said he tipped like a prince and asked questions like a tax man.

He did not come to the castle. He was not asked, and he did not ask. On the last evening of the third week he came to the bank at closing, when the clerks had gone, and sat down across from his sister at her plain desk, and talked to her for two hours with the door shut; and when he came out he went to the inn and packed his satchel and took the morning boat.

Vantic said nothing about it for a day. Then she came into Ferra's room on the Saturday afternoon, which she did not do, and sat down without being asked, which she did not do either.

"Cassian will run the coast houses for me," she said. "And the trade paper. Under the seat. Answerable to me."

Ferra put her pen down.

"He said Tullio took the book that was worth the most," said Vantic, "and he'd take the book that'll be worth the most in ten years. He said he'd been all over Kaldo for three weeks looking for the thing that would fall over, and couldn't find it, and he'd never in his life not found it." She was looking at the window. "He said he'd sooner lose money for a year with me than make it for ten with Tullio."

"Is he right?"

"About losing money for a year?" Vantic considered. "Yes."

"About the rest."

"I don't know." Vantic stood up. "I've never been able to tell when he's lying. He knows that. It's why he plays the coast game." At the door she stopped. "He asked me to tell the duke that he rode in the hull to Milford, and that it was the worst hour of his life, and that he'd like to buy one."



Doran asked Emeric about the cups on the Wednesday after Cassian left, on the church steps, after evensong.

The cups were the white metal. Three hundred and twelve pounds of it had come out of the pot room by the end of March, and over five hundred more by June, and not a pound of it had been sold; it lay in the works' store in grey bars, stacked like bread, and Bram kept a man on the store's door at night, not because anybody in Kaldo would know what to do with it, but because Bram did not like leaving anything that heavy and that new where it could walk. Emeric had meant to sell it to the House. He had meant to sell it to the House at the price of what it was: a metal as light as wood and as bright as silver that would not rust, that could be beaten into a cup or drawn into a wire or cast into a part for a machine, and that was, at this moment, in the whole world, as far as he knew, made in one room in Kaldo by the pound.

Vantic had not let him.

"Who's it for?" she had asked him, in May, across her desk in the bank, with one of the bars in front of her.

"Anyone. Everyone. In ten years it'll be on every table in the kingdom."

"In ten years. And this year?"

He had not had an answer.

"This year," said Vantic, "it's for the people who've just stayed with me." She had turned the bar over with one finger. It was very light. "I've lost three houses' worth of clients since the bank opened. I'll lose more by the autumn. The ones who stay are staying because they've decided to, against their friends, against the Lieutenant, against what's said from the pulpit. I want to give them something nobody else has." She had looked up. "Cups. Buttons. A set of spoons for a lord's table that his guests will pick up and put down again because they're too light to be real. At silver's price. The House buys it from the works at a price Ferra and I will settle, and the House sells it to its own, and nobody else in the kingdom can buy it at all."

"And when the price falls?"

"When the price falls," said Vantic, "they'll have had it first. That's what they'll be paying for."

So the House had sold the white metal to the people who had stayed with it: a set of cups to a lady in Wayford who had kept her account when her husband's brother took his away; buttons for a merchant's coat in Grayrock; and, at the end of May, a whole canteen of cutlery, two dozen of everything, to the steward of a duke on the north road, for the duke's table, at a price that had made Ferra put her pen down and look at the wall for a while.

Doran had seen the canteen. He had been at the duke's table, in June, for a christening.

"I picked up a spoon," he said now, on the church steps, in the long light, with his hands in his sleeves. "I very nearly threw it over my shoulder. I thought somebody had made a joke of tin."

"It's not tin."

"No. I know what it is. I know where it comes from." Doran looked down the steps at the square. "I wanted to ask you something, and I've been a week deciding how." He was quiet a moment. "Are you selling them something you know will be cheap?"

Emeric looked at him.

"It's a fair question," said Doran. "I've a duke's steward in my parish book who paid more for a set of spoons than his under-gardener earns in nine years. I'd like to know whether, in nine years, the under-gardener will be able to buy the same spoon at the market for a Copper. Because if he will, and you know he will, then the steward's paid a great deal for nothing but the being first. And I'd want to know, as his priest, whether he was told."

It was the kind of question Doran had been asking him for ten years. It was why he had not, in ten years, once regretted Doran.

"It's rare today," said Emeric. "That's true today. It's the rarest metal in the world today, there's not half a ton of it in the whole world and it's all in a shed by the river." He looked at the square. "It won't be in nine years. Not if the pot room does what I want. In nine years it'll be on the market. Maybe not for a Copper. But cheap."

"And the steward?"

"Vantic told him." Emeric had not known that until Ferra told him, in May; he had been ashamed, then, that he had not asked. "She tells everyone. She sells it with a letter in the box. In her hand. It says the House expects the price to fall within the decade, and that she's selling it now, dear, to the House's own, because it's new and it's beautiful and they stayed." He looked at Doran. "The steward's letter's in the duke's book. I've seen the copy."

Doran was quiet for a long time.

"Then she's more honest than most of my bishops," he said at last, "and I'll not say so to anybody, and nor will you." He took his hands out of his sleeves. "Thank you."

He went in. Emeric stood on the steps a while longer in the evening light.

Ferra had written the year down. He knew she had, because he had seen her do it, in May, in the corner of her book, in a column she kept on the back pages of every book she had ever kept and never showed anybody; a column of things she thought would happen, and when. She had looked at the first bar of the white metal on Vantic's desk, and at the pot room's figures, and at the coal returns from Grayrock, and at the plans for the falls dam, and she had written a year in that column, small, and turned the page before he could read it.

He had not asked. He would know when it happened, because she would come and stand in his door with the book open at that page and not say anything.

The money from the white metal, every Silver of it, went back into the pot room: a second row of pots, and the rectifier for them, and the bus bars, and the men. Ferra had written that line before the first cup was sold.



The bailiff came for Hob on the twentieth of June, at a quarter to eleven in the morning, at the bottom of ward 3, and Emeric was not there.

He was at the coal ground, two miles downstream, standing in a field of thistles with Bram and a surveyor's chain, and he heard about it at a quarter past twelve from a boy on a bicycle who had come down the river road so fast he fell off at the end of it. By then it was over, the first part. By then Ferra had done it.

Tess saw it begin. She told it afterward, to Emeric and Bram, in the shed at the head of the rails, in order, the way Imani had taught her to tell a thing that might matter: first what she saw, then what she heard, then what she thought, and never the three mixed.

What she saw was three men on horses coming down the lane into the printer's ground from the Wayford road, at a walk, in good coats, with a dog. The one in front was older, grey-whiskered, with a leather case on his saddle and a staff of office across it, white, with a brass knob on the end. The two behind were young and big and wore no coats at all, only shirts and leather jerkins, and had sticks.

The printer was running. They were on the second terrace by then, four more houses on the next strip of ground, and the gantry was on its fourth course with the nozzle going round and the blades pressing; and the crew were where they always were, on the pump cart and the mixer and the winch. Hob was at the winch.

The three men rode in along the cart track and stopped at the end of the rails. The older one looked at the gantry going over the ground with its blades down for a while, and Tess said that what she thought, from his face, was that he had never seen anything like it and did not want to be seen not having seen it. Then he looked at the crew, one man at a time; and when he came to Hob, he stopped looking.

"Hob Tanner," he said. Not loud.

Hob let go of the winch.

What Tess heard then was the bailiff read a paper. He read it from his saddle, without getting down, in a reading voice, the voice of a man who had read a great many papers to a great many people who did not want to hear them: that Hob Tanner, cottager, of the manor of Brackwater, in the barony up the Wayford line, had on the Lady Day of last year put his mark to an indenture of service with his lord, Sir Aldous Crane of Brackwater, for a term of three years; that he had served one; that this spring past he had left the manor with his wife and children by night on the railway without leave of his lord or notice to his lord's steward, owing two years' labour under his mark; and that the bailiff of Brackwater was come under his lord's warrant to bring him home.

The crew had stopped working. The gantry had stopped. Tess had stopped it; she had pulled the lever back without thinking, and the nozzle hung over the half-made wall with a bead of grey going slowly stiff at its mouth.

Hob said nothing. He was a broad slow man with a face like a field, and he stood at the winch with his hands hanging and looked at the bailiff and did not say anything at all. At the far end of the rails, by the first terrace, a woman had come out of the door of the end house and stopped on its step. She had a baby on her hip.

The bailiff rolled up his paper.

"Come on, then," he said. Not unkindly. "There's a horse for you. Your wife can follow with the children on the cart, I've a man arranging it at the station. His lordship'll not hold the year against you if you come quiet. He's short of hands. He'd sooner have you back at the plough than in the stocks."

Hob looked at the woman on the step.

Osric had come out of the shed.

He had been at the tracer table with Tess; he came out without a coat and walked down the side of the rails to where the bailiff's horse stood, and stood there, between the horse and the winch, not touching anything, with his hands at his sides. He was not armed. Tess said she did not think he had meant to come out. She thought he had found himself out there and decided to stay.

"Sir Osric," said the bailiff, who knew him, it turned out; everybody up the Wayford line knew the knights of Kaldo by now, by sight. "I've a warrant."

"I don't doubt it," said Osric.

"Then stand aside."

"I'll stand here," said Osric, "till somebody comes who can tell me whether to." He did not look round. "Tess. Send for the Chancellor."

Tess had already sent the water cart's boy up the lane, running. She did not say so. She said, "Yes," and sat in her chair in the shed door with her hand on the lever, and watched.

The bailiff looked down at Osric for a long moment, and then at the two young men behind him with their sticks, and then at the gantry standing over the ground, and the crew standing round it, nine men, not moving, with their sleeves rolled and their hands empty and a great many of them, now Tess came to count, bigger than the young men with the sticks.

He did not tell the young men to do anything. Tess said that was when she first thought he might be a decent man.

They waited.

Ferra came down the lane at five past eleven, walking fast, without her coat, with her book under her arm and a second book under the first one: the register for ward 3, the one with the census lines in it, bound in the brown cloth of the records hall. She did not run. Tess had never seen Ferra run. She walked down the lane and along the cart track and up to the end of the rails as if she had been coming that way anyway, and stopped beside Osric at the bailiff's stirrup, and looked up at him.

"I'm the Chancellor of Kaldo," she said. "You've a paper. May I see it?"

The bailiff gave it to her.

She read it standing there in the sun, the whole of it, with the second book under her arm. It took her a little while. She read the mark at the foot of it, and the witnesses, and the seal, and turned it over and read the back, which was blank; and then she gave it back to him.

"That's a true indenture," she said.

The bailiff looked surprised.

"It's properly drawn and properly witnessed and that's Sir Aldous's seal. I've seen it on his letters to the crown." She opened the brown book. "And this is the census of Kaldo. The ward 3 sheet. March." She found the page without looking for it and turned the book round on her arm so that he could see it from the saddle. "Hob Tanner. Printer's crew, the winch. Married, Bet. Three children, Nell, Davey and the baby, who isn't named on my sheet yet because they hadn't named her in March. Dwelling, ward 3, first terrace, the end house." She turned the book back. "He's on Kaldo's register. He has been since the twenty-ninth of March, when he came to the records hall with his wife and stood at the counter and gave his name. He answers to Kaldo's court."

"He answers to his lord," said the bailiff. "He put his mark to it."

"He did. And there'll be a court to say what that's worth in Kaldo, and it'll sit in the records hall, and it'll sit in the open, and anybody who wants to can come and hear it." Ferra closed the book. "But nobody whose name is on that sheet goes out of this barony without a hearing. Not to a lord. Not to the crown. Not to anybody."

The bailiff looked at her.

"By whose law?" he said.

There was a short silence.

"By Kaldo's," said Ferra.

"I've never heard of it."

"No," said Ferra. "You've not lived here."

The bailiff sat on his horse a long time. Then he looked at Hob, at the winch, with his hands hanging; and at the woman with the baby on the step of the end house, a printed house, with a stair in it; and at the nine men round the gantry; and at Osric, standing at his stirrup, not touching anything.

"I'll want it in writing," he said. "The hearing. The day. Who sits. I'll want it sent to his lordship, and I'll want his lordship's lawyer to be let speak at it."

"You'll have it by the evening post," said Ferra. "In my hand. And his lawyer can speak as long as he likes." She looked at the bailiff's two young men. "And if he wants to bring those two with him, they can sit at the back with everybody else. The hall has benches."

The bailiff turned his horse.

He went back up the lane at a walk with his young men and his dog behind him, and stopped at the top of it, and turned in his saddle and looked back at the gantry; and then he went on.

Hob had not moved from the winch. When the bailiff had gone over the top of the lane, he sat down where he was, on the clay, quite suddenly, as if his legs had been taken out from under him; and the woman with the baby came down off the step of the end house and walked all the way along the rails to him, and stood over him, and put her free hand on his head.

Ferra watched that for a moment. Then she turned round, and saw Tess in the door of the shed, with her hand still on the lever.

"Start it up," she said. "That bead's going hard."



The hearing was on the first of July, in the records hall, with the doors open.

Emeric did not sit. He had thought about it for ten days, and talked about it with Ferra for three of them, and in the end he did not sit; because he was the duke, and Sir Aldous Crane was a knight-lord of a neighbouring barony whose bailiff had been turned away at Kaldo's gate, and if the duke sat on it, then whatever was decided would be the duke deciding it, against a neighbour, and the neighbour would write to the crown that the duke had done it. Ferra did not sit either. She had given the answer at the gate, and you did not sit in judgment on your own answer.

So the court of Kaldo was what the court of Kaldo had been since the records hall opened, when a court was needed and nobody else would do: three of the town's own, chosen by lot from the register by Ottoline's tabulator that morning in front of witnesses. A cooper from the lower town. A widow who kept the bakery by the north well. A clerk from the station, who had been on the register four years and had come from Wayford himself. They sat behind the long counter in the records hall where Ottoline's clerks usually sat, on three chairs, with the census and the indenture on the counter between them, and they looked, all three, as if they would rather have been anywhere else in the world.

The hall was full. People had come in from the market and the works and the printer's ground; the crew were there, all nine, along the back wall, and Gale the mason with them, for reasons of his own. The benches were full and the aisles were full and people stood in the doors and on the steps outside. Hob sat on a chair in front of the counter with his wife beside him and the baby on her lap.

Sir Aldous's lawyer was a thin man in black with a voice that did not need to be raised in a hall to fill it, and he spoke for an hour.

He spoke well. Emeric, at the back by the door, where he had decided to stand because standing anywhere else would have been sitting, listened to all of it, and could not have said it better. The indenture was true; the Chancellor had said so herself. Hob had made his mark. He had taken his lord's seed and his lord's cottage and his lord's protection for a year, and then he had gone, by night, owing two. Sir Aldous's fields were short of hands; they had been short of hands since Kaldo's railway came up the Wayford line, because Kaldo paid more for a man on a winch than Brackwater could pay for him at a plough, and every spring more of Brackwater's men went down the line on the morning train and did not come back. Sir Aldous did not grudge Kaldo its wages. He grudged it his men. A lord who could not hold a man to his mark had no men, only weather; and a kingdom whose lords could not hold their men to their marks was a kingdom where the fields went unploughed. If Kaldo's court kept Hob Tanner, then every indentured man up the Wayford line would know by the end of the summer that the morning train to Kaldo was a road out of every promise he had ever made, and he would take it.

"And then," said the lawyer, "what will a man's mark be worth? In Brackwater? Or here?"

He sat down.

The hall was quiet. Along the back wall Gale was nodding slowly, as if he agreed with every word, and did not like it.

Then the station clerk from Wayford, at the end of the counter, leaned forward and said, in a voice so small that half the hall did not hear it, "May I ask the gentleman a question?"

The cooper looked at the widow. The widow looked at the cooper.

"I suppose you may," said the widow.

"The indenture," said the clerk. He had it in front of him, and he put his finger on it. "Three years. One served. Two owing." He looked at the lawyer. "Two years of what?"

The lawyer looked at him.

"Of his labour."

"Yes, sir. But it's a sum, isn't it? In the end?" The clerk's finger moved down the paper. "It says here, at the bottom, in the custom of the manor, that if a man's labour isn't given it's to be paid at the rate of the manor, so many Copper a day, for the days owing. I know that line, sir. It's in every indenture up the Wayford line. My father's had it. It's for when a man's sick, or dies." He looked up. "So it's a sum. His lordship's owed two years at the manor's rate. That's a sum of money."

"His lordship is owed his man."

"His lordship's owed what the paper says, sir," said the clerk, and went pink to the ears, "and the paper says that if the man doesn't give his labour, his lordship's owed the money for it." He took his finger off the paper. "I only asked because Kaldo pays more."

It took them twenty minutes, in the end, in the back room, with the door shut.

When they came out, the cooper read it, because the widow would not and the clerk could not stop going pink. It was short. The indenture was true. The debt was true, and it stood: Hob Tanner owed Sir Aldous Crane of Brackwater two years' labour, at the rate of the manor, as the paper said. He would pay it. He would pay it in coin, from his wages, through the bank, a part every week, over a year; Kaldo's wages made that possible, the cooper said, looking at the paper, where Brackwater's would not have. The bank would send it to Sir Aldous's steward every quarter day. And when it was paid, it was paid, and the paper was done.

But Sir Aldous could not have the man.

The man's name was on Kaldo's register. He stayed.

The hall did not cheer. Emeric had half expected it to; he had felt it in the room, the thing that comes before a cheer, like the pressure before rain. It did not come. People sat on the benches and looked at each other, and at Hob, and at the lawyer, and said nothing at all; because most of the people on those benches had come down the Wayford line, or the Grayrock line, or the Milford road, on a train or a cart or on foot, in the last six years, and a great many of them had come owing something to somebody, and they had just heard a court tell them what it was worth, and what it was not.

Hob's wife was crying, without any noise, into the top of the baby's head.

The lawyer stood up. He gathered his papers. He bowed to the counter, correctly, the bow of a man who has lost a case he expected to lose and been paid for it either way. Then he said, to the room, not to the counter, in his voice that did not need to be raised:

"His lordship will write to the crown."

"He's very welcome," said the widow, before anybody could stop her, "and so's anybody."



Ferra was on the wall walk that night at eleven, by the river tower, with her book shut on the parapet; and Emeric came up the stair and stood beside her, and for a while they did not say anything.

Down in the lower town a window was lit at the end of the first printed terrace, late, where nobody in ward 3 was usually up at eleven.

"He'll write to the crown," said Emeric.

"He will. It'll go on the pile." Ferra did not look round. "Corbin's clerk will read it. It'll go in somebody's book. A duke's court stole a man from his lord. That's what it'll say." She was quiet. "It's even true. If you read it standing in Brackwater."

"Is that why you're up here?"

"No."

He waited.

"The bailiff asked me by whose law," said Ferra. "At the gate. And I said Kaldo's." She put her hand flat on her book. "And then I came up here tonight to find it. I went through everything. The covenant. The charter of the barony. The crown's grant. Your father's letters patent. My own rules for the records hall, from the day it opened, that I wrote out myself in a fair hand and pinned on the counter." She took her hand off the book. "It's not anywhere."

"No," said Emeric.

"You knew."

"I knew it was what you did. I didn't know it wasn't written. I never asked." He leaned on the parapet beside her. "Where's it from?"

"Me." Ferra looked down at the lit window. "Since the records hall opened. Since the first man came to the counter and stood there and gave his name and asked whether it meant anybody could come and take him. I said no. I didn't think about it. It seemed like the kind of thing the register was for. If your name's in my book, you're mine, and nobody takes what's mine out of my book without asking me." She almost smiled. "I've said it to a hundred people at that counter. More. I've never once been asked to show where it was written. Until today."

"Then where's it written?"

"Nowhere," said Ferra. "It's mine. It's a thing I do." She turned her head and looked at him in the dark. "And when I'm gone it's nobody's."

He did not say anything for a while.

Below them, at the end of the terrace, the lit window went dark.

"Then we'll write it down," he said.

"The Assembly." Ferra looked back at the town. "Its first sitting's in July. They'll want to pass something. They'll want to pass something nobody can say was the duke's." She opened her book on the parapet, in the dark, where she could not possibly have seen to write; and wrote anyway, by feel, two lines, small. "I'll give them that."

"What does it say?"

She shut the book.

"I'll tell you when they've voted," said Ferra. "So it's theirs."



He scared Imani on the Thursday.

He had not meant to. Afterward he would swear he had not meant to, and she would not believe him, and Ferra would not believe him either, and Ward, who heard about it at breakfast, would put his cup down and look at him for a long time and say only, "On the ceiling," as if a man of thirty-three who was a duke of the realm and the master of four baronies should not, under any circumstances, be on a ceiling.

It was eleven at night. The ward was quiet, two lamps lit at the far end over the beds where the night's patients slept, Bettany at the desk by the door with her sheet. Imani had gone into the store room at the back to count the lint, as she did every Thursday at eleven, because on Fridays the carter came from the mill and she wanted to know before he came how much to send him back for.

Emeric had come down to the ward because he could not sleep, and because he could not sleep he had been walking on things.

He had been doing it for a month. Since the night at his window. He did it at night, alone, because the violet showed at his soles in the dark and because a man walking on the wall of his own keep was not a thing he wanted the town telling itself about in the morning; up the east face of the keep and along the parapet and down the far side, for the practice, and to see how little he could use. He had walked on the ceiling of the forge cellar, with the door locked. He had walked up the side of the station's water tower, once, on a Sunday, very early, and down again, and Bram had seen the boot marks on the iron on the Monday and stared at them for a long time and said nothing to anybody, which with Bram was worse.

He had come into the ward by the yard door. He had seen the store room door ajar and the lamp lit inside it, and he had thought, quite simply, that he would surprise her. That was all. He would come in on the ceiling and she would look up and he would be there.

He went up the corridor wall and onto the corridor ceiling, slowly, a step at a time, feeling for the plant, with his coat hanging down off him toward the floor like a curtain and his hair the same, and his stomach doing what it always did for the first few steps, telling him firmly and in detail that this was not down. Along the ceiling to the store room door. Over the lintel. In.

She was at the high shelf, on the little step, with her back to the door and her lamp on the shelf beside her and a jar in each hand, counting.

He walked across the ceiling to a place above her shoulder, very quietly, with his weight light and his soles planted on the plaster, and stood there, upside down, with his coat hanging past her head toward the floor and his face a hand's breadth above hers.

She turned round.

What he had meant, he said afterward, was that she would look up. What she did was turn round on the step with a jar of lint in each hand to reach for the lamp, and find a man's face directly in front of hers, the wrong way up, with his hair hanging down past it and his coat hanging down past that, a hand's breadth away, smiling.

She hit him with the jar.

Not hard. She did not have time to do it hard. Her right arm came up on its own, the way a healer's arm comes up when a patient in a fever comes up off the bed at her, and the jar went past his ear and hit the plaster of the ceiling beside his boot with a crack and broke, and a great white cloud of lint came out of it and filled the room.

She did not scream. Imani did not scream. She said, very loudly, a word he had never heard her say, which was a word he had last heard from a lighterman on the quay at the port when a crate went into the river; and then she stood on the step in the cloud of lint with the other jar raised over her head in her left hand, and looked at him.

He was laughing too hard to say anything. He was laughing so hard that the plant was going, he could feel it slipping, his soles sliding on the plaster; and he leaned down, the wrong way up, through the lint, and kissed her.

It was the first kiss he had ever given anybody upside down that he had meant. The other, years ago, had been an accident, and it had been the other way round. This one was not an accident. Her mouth was the wrong way up under his, and tasted of the tea she had been drinking, and she was so angry that for a moment she did not kiss him back at all; and then, for a moment, she did.

Then she pushed him in the chest with the hand that did not have the jar in it.

"Get down," she said, against his mouth, "or I'll count you."

He got down. He let go of the plant and turned in the air as he fell, the way he had learned to, and landed on his feet on the store room floor in the settling cloud of lint, and stood there in front of her, the right way up, with white fluff in his hair and on his shoulders and all down the front of his coat, still laughing.

Imani got down off the step. She put the second jar on the shelf, carefully, where it belonged. She picked a piece of lint off his eyebrow.

"Bettany's coming," she said.

Bettany was coming. They could both hear her, up the ward, with the lamp, at a run.

"What do I tell her?"

"You tell her," said Imani, "that the duke came into my store room at eleven at night by the ceiling and I hit him with the lint, and that I'll want a new jar from the works in the morning, and that she's to put it on his account." She picked another piece out of his hair. "And then you're going to help me count what's left, because I'll not do it twice."

Bettany came round the door with the lamp held high, and stopped.

She looked at the duke, in the middle of the store room floor, covered in lint. She looked at the Secretary of Health, standing in front of him with her hand in his hair. She looked up at the ceiling, where there was a dent in the plaster beside two faint marks, side by side, a foot apart, that might, in a good light, have been the prints of a pair of boots.

She looked at those for some time.

"I'll fetch a broom," said Bettany, and went.

Imani took her hand out of his hair. She looked up at the boot prints on the ceiling too, for a while; and then at him; and he saw it go across her face, all of it, the fright and the fury and the laugh she had not let out yet and then, last of all, something else, quieter, that he did not have a name for and did not need one.

"Don't you ever," she said, "do that again."

"No."

"I mean it."

"I know," he said.

She looked at him a moment longer.

"Not in here," said Imani. "There's glass in here." She turned back to the shelf and took down the next jar. "Twelve," she said. "Start counting."
