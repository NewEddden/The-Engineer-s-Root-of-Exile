Chapter 336 - The Survey

The ground at Wayford had frozen a hand deep in the night, and by seven Dagny's team had broken it from the posting house to the market cross with picks, and stood in the trench in the half dark breathing like a row of kettles.

Kaldo's six were in the works' canvas gone pale at the knees, steel in their boot toes and rubber boots over them for the wet at the bottom, which this morning was ice. Of Wayford's seven, three were in canvas because they had asked for it and Aster had paid, and four in the leather and old army boots they had always worn, who would not.

The pipe lay along the bank on its blocks in twelve-foot lengths, galvanized, grey with frost, from Tess's shop by Tuesday's mail train; and beside each length a ring in a pail.

The rings were the trouble in February. They were the Isle's rubber, a fat black ring in a groove inside each socket that took the next length's plain end and held the water, and let the joint give a little in the ground without leaking, which lead would not. In the cold they went stiff as a boot heel, and a stiff ring rolled in its groove when the spigot went past instead of lying down under it, and a rolled ring held for a week and then did not.

So the rings went in hot water first. It was written on the board at the end of the trench in her own hand: RINGS FROM THE PAIL, NOT THE BOX. The brazier by the posting house steps kept a copper on, and Hob Varney's whole job until ten was to keep the hot pails coming down to the trench.

"They'll not freeze in a minute," said Will Coxon, from the bank.

He was Wayford's best mason by his own account and most other people's, a short wide man of fifty with a face like a walnut, who had been told in October by the steward that he would work beside Kaldo's team or not at all, and had come, and had not stopped saying so.

"They don't freeze," said Dagny. "They stiffen."

"Forty years I've laid drains in this town, and I never warmed a thing I was going to bury."

"You never laid one that held water at a hundred feet of head."

It was true, and it was the wrong thing to say, and she heard it go along the trench. Coxon looked at her for a while as if she were a wall somebody had put up with the joints in a line, and went back to his own length and said something to his lads that made two of them laugh.

He thought Kaldo's lads were arrogant. He had said so to Aster in the posting house taproom, where everybody could hear. Dagny had thought about it since, at night, in the room over the saddler's. She had come up the line in September at twenty-three, a woman, with a rule for everything, and told men who had laid stone here before she was born that their drains were wrong. Their drains were wrong. That was the trouble with being right: it did not make you any easier to have in the trench.

He was half right. She knew which half.



They laid six lengths by noon: the length lowered on two ropes, the plain end wiped, the ring out of the pail and into the groove with a thumb run round it twice, the soap, the bar and the block, a man leaning on the bar until the paint line on the spigot came flush to the socket's face. Her six did not talk. Wayford's seven talked the whole time, mostly about Kaldo's six, and after an hour the two had begun, a little, to meet in the middle.

At half past twelve Master Simms brought the school down to look at the trench before its dinner, as he did every day, in two lines. There were sixty of them now. There had been eleven when he came up the line from Sela's room four years ago, three and a half years late, as Sela had written at the foot of his letter herself. He was a thin dark serious man, one of Sela's first pupils, and two Wayford girls off his own first bench taught under him now, and walked at the back of the lines to stop the boys going down the bank.

"What's the ring for?" said Simms to the front line.

Nobody knew, and he looked at Dagny, and she got up with her bread in one hand and showed them with a ring and the cut end of a spare socket: how the plain end went in and the ring lay down under it; and how a cold ring went in rolled up on itself like a rope, and left a gap at the bottom where you could not see it, that would let the water out under the street for years before a cart found the hole one morning.

The children looked at the rolled ring as if it had done something to them personally.

"That's why the water's hot," said a boy.

"That's why," said Dagny.

Coxon was eating on the bank with his back to the posting house wall, and did not look up.



The wrong joint was the ninth length, and it was hers.

It was after two, and Hob was up at the copper because the last pail had gone tepid; and she reached for the ring and took it, without thinking, off the top of the box by her knee, where the cold ones went back. Her thumb went round the groove twice. The soap, the bar, Jonah Wickes leaning on it, the paint line flush. "Home," she said, and reached for the next ring.

"Hold on," said Jonah.

He was a carter's son from the bridge ward, nineteen, who had come down to Kaldo on Aster's list and spent a season on her rows in Grayrock learning the pipe; the slowest she had ever taught, and the only one who had never had to be told a thing twice. He was on his knees by the joint with his face down near the frozen sand, and he had his glove off.

"Paint line's flush," said Dagny.

"It is." He put his bare fingers under the socket where nobody could see and ran them round the joint slowly from six o'clock to six o'clock, the way she had taught him on the rows, the way she had not done herself since about her first year. He stopped at seven. "There's a lip."

She got down beside him and felt it. A hair of black rubber standing proud of the socket's face at the bottom of the joint, the width of a fingernail, where the ring had rolled under the spigot and been pushed out of its groove and folded. It would have held the test. It would have given way under the posting house's front step some night in the summer, under a hundred feet of head, and taken the step with it.

"Cold ring," she said. "Out of the box. Mine."

The trench had heard her tell sixty children about cold rings at half past twelve; so she stood up and said it again to the bank, so there would be nothing anybody could say behind her that she had not said first. "That's my joint. Jonah's caught it. Pull it."

They pulled it, the bar the other way and two men on the rope, and the ring came out in a black twist with its back broken. Dagny dropped it in the spoils box and wrote on the board, small, under her rule: one spoiled, 4 Feb, D. Hob came back with the hot pail at a run, having heard. Jonah laid the joint again himself, out of the pail, and ran his bare fingers round it afterward, and found nothing, and put his glove back on.

When she looked up, Coxon was at the trench edge above her with his hands in his coat pockets.

"Your lad," he said.

"He's Wayford's."

"I know whose he is. I knew his grandfather." Coxon looked down the trench at Jonah, who was not looking at either of them. "He did that like he was feeling for a crack in a lintel. Who taught him that?"

"I did."

Coxon nodded slowly, as if a sum had come out to a figure he did not much care for and could not fault.

"Then you'll want to start doing it yourself again," he said, and went back to his own length; and it was the first thing he had said to her since September that she could not have answered, and she did not try.



Aster walked the trench at four in his mended brown coat, reading it as if it might later be produced in court. Then he held out his hand for her day's figures and read them standing in the trench in the last of the light, every line, to the copper: lengths laid, eleven; rings, twelve, one spoiled; sand, four loads; hours by name; the wage at the steward's rate; the pipe drawn against the mail train's bill.

"The spoiled ring," said Aster. "You've charged it to the works."

"It was my joint."

"So I read." He considered it with his whole face, the way another man considers a horse. "It was laid in Wayford's ground out of a box Wayford bought. I'll take it on the steward's book. It's a half copper's worth of rubber, and I should like it to be ours."

"Why?"

"Because the main is," said Aster, and initialled the corner of her board in pencil, and gave it back.



The tap went in at six, by lamplight, in the posting house kitchen, because the cook had heard at four that the house connection was made and said she would not sleep with it in the wall and not running.

The kitchen was long and black-beamed and hot, with a stone sink under the window where for two hundred years every drop for every pot had come in through the yard door in a bucket. The new pipe came through the wall above the sink in a sleeve of Coxon's mortar, because Coxon had said nobody from Kaldo was cutting a hole in that wall, and stopped in a brass tap with a cross handle that Tess's shop had sent up wrapped in a sock.

The kitchen was full, Coxon at the back by the yard door with his arms folded, come to see that his wall held, and Aster at the end of the table with his book.

The cook was a big red woman named Radley who had run that kitchen thirty years and been afraid of nothing anybody had seen. She stood at the sink with her hand on the brass cross and did not turn it.

"Which way?" she said.

Somebody laughed. Dagny showed her with a finger in the air, and she turned it. It coughed and spat air, and she stepped back; and then the water came, cold and hard and white with its own speed, into the stone sink, and splashed her apron, and kept coming.

Nobody said anything for a bit. The water ran.

"Well, that's the girls out of a job," said Mother Tennick from the cross, who went everywhere; and the girls, who had carried buckets in that yard since they were eight, looked at each other and did not, it was plain, mind at all. The cook held her hand under the water until it hurt, and said to nobody, "It's colder than the pump." Then she filled a kettle, which took no time at all, and looked at it as if somebody had cheated her of something.

Coxon went and looked at his mortar round the sleeve, closely, with a lamp, and seemed satisfied, and went home without saying anything to anybody.

Aster wrote in his book. Dagny had learned to read Aster upside down by October. The date and the hour, and: the first water in a house in Wayford. And under it, in the same hand, a line he did not seem to know she could read. It is colder than the pump.



Saban came up the K Amara's after ladder on the evening of the fifth with snow on his hat and did not take it off, which meant he was going out again.

"The green door's opening," he said.

Emeric was at the chart table with Ward and his father, his stitched forearm on the table where Imani had told him to keep it, "so you notice when you start using it."

"Shutters off the first floor," said Saban. "Fires in every room. Oats bought for a hundred and fifty horse. A rider in at the north gate this morning in Aldane's colours with his horse blown." He laid a slip on the chart. "And my man on the north road has the muster at Highcairn broken up on the third and coming south, with Sallow's horse and Vane's. Nine hundred, a third of them horse. Brask the day after tomorrow and under the walls the day after that." He looked at Emeric. "You asked for a day. That's your day."

"Why now? There's nothing left to march at."

"His sister," said Harrold, from the bench under the chart rack, where he still sat in the evenings though he had his rooms in the palace again. "His sister and her son, kept under Kaldo's watch, to be carried south when the fleet goes. Aldane's never been a clever man. He's always been a loyal one, to his own. He'll have thought of her on a Kaldo hull, and got on his horse."

"The fleet can meet him at Brask," said Ward.

"The fleet can sit over him at Brask," said Emeric. "And then he's a duke with nine hundred men under Kaldo's hulls who can't go back without having been sat on, and can't go on." He rubbed his eyes with his good hand. "There's no way out of it for him that a man like that will take."

"Then he'll take the one that's left," said Saban, "and charge."

"I'll write to him," said Harrold, after a while. "He'll read it as a letter from the man who has his sister. But I'll write it."



The prince sent for Ward a little before ten.

He sent one of Osric's two on his passage, a young woman of the yard named Avis who had not been asked for anything since January; and she came down to the palace gate and said His Highness asked if the captain would step up, and that he had said please.

The prince's rooms were at the west end over the long gallery, too warm, with a fire made up by somebody who did not know how, smoking. He was at the writing table in his shirt. His jaw had gone from black to yellow on the left and was still the shape of a man's knuckles under the hinge, and he sat a little crooked, the way a man sits with a rib that has been told about. He was sealing a letter.

One sheet, in his own upright fast hand; and the wax was his own, pressed with the ring off his finger, not the household's seal and not the Lieutenant's. He set it on the table edge nearest Ward.

"My uncle's on the north road," he said. "My mother had it from his rider this evening. I expect you had it this morning."

Ward said nothing.

"Yes. I expect you did." The prince looked at the letter. "That's to reach him before Brask. By whatever rider Kaldo has that's fastest. I'd send my own, but I seem to have stood them down."

"What's in it?"

The prince looked up. It was not the look from the chancery stair that went down a step at a man. It was a tired man's look, from a chair.

"That there's nothing here to protect," he said. "Which is true. That the king is the king and the court is his, and I've no claim against it. That I'll go south with my mother when I'm carried, and would sooner go as a man kept than a man fought over. And that if he brings the north's sons to these walls for me, I'll not go out to him." He took his hand off the table. "And some things about my mother, which are his and hers."

"He'll not thank you."

"No." The prince almost smiled, on the side of his face that would. "He'll never speak to me again. He's the last man in the realm who would have, so it's a small saving." He sat back, rib first. "He gave me my first sword and it was too heavy, and he told everyone it wasn't. He'll read that in a tent outside Brask and think Kaldo has its hand on my neck and wrote it for me." He looked at the window. "Let him, if it gets him home."

Ward picked the letter up. "Who's to know?"

The prince thought about it. "I'd not have thought anybody. It isn't a thing a man does in front of people." He did not look round. "Good night, Captain."

Ward took it through the snow to Saban's attic in the drapers' street house and told him what was in it in about a dozen words, because that was how many it took. Saban looked at the seal for a long time.

"Does His Grace know?"

"No."

"Are you going to tell him?"

Ward thought about the smoking room with nobody in it.

"No," he said; and Saban put on his hat and went down to wake his best rider himself.



The host turned at Brask on the seventh, at about three in the afternoon, in the water meadows under the city's west wall: a rider in at the gallop, a long while of nothing with the duke's banner standing by the ford in the cold, then the trumpets and the horse wheeling slowly in fours and going back up the north road with the foot behind it. Aldane rode at its head, Saban's man said, and did not look back at the city, or anywhere.

"Why?" said Emeric.

"Couldn't say," said Saban pleasantly, and went on to the next slip; and Emeric, who had known Saban thirteen years and never once heard him decline to guess, looked at him, and then at Ward, who was studying the river charts as if they had been printed that morning. His father's letter had gone out at noon on the seventh, in its sixth draft, and could not have reached Brask by three.

He let it go. There were a good many things that winter he had decided to let go, and he had found, a little to his surprise, that he could.



He gave Keller the survey on the eighth.

It had lain all through the war in the bottom of the K Amara's chart table drawer, a hand's breadth under the letter he had not sent. He took it out without moving the letter and carried it down to the chancery's north range himself.

Keller's windows were still too high to see out of. He read the two papers the way he had five years before, the commission with its cracked red seal and the survey with the long grey shape below the drowned shaft, one finger moving down the margin and stopping over the seal without touching the wax.

"I told you to keep it in a dry place," he said. "And that a thing in a man's coat could be produced when the court was one that would hear it." He looked up at the high window. "There's no cardinal on the spiritual bench. The chancery buys its own candles. The king hears his own court from the chair." He folded his hands on the papers. "This court will hear it. I'll send His Grace of Aldane his summons tonight. It'll find him on the north road, I imagine, going home." He dipped his pen. "I'm told he changed his mind at Brask. I'm told nobody knows why."

"Nobody does," said Emeric.

"Then I'll not ask," said Keller, and wrote.



The king's court sat in the council hall at nine on the ninth, from the chair.

The benches were full. Somebody had hung a cloth in the night over the Eye on the empty cardinal's chair, and nobody had taken it off. Aldane's seat on the first bench was empty. At the barrier stood the steward of the house with the green door, a tall stooped man in Aldane's gold and black, with a letter: that His Grace of Aldane was of a house as old as the king's, and would answer to the king in his own hall if the king wished to come, and would not be summoned to the crown's table like a farmer in a dispute over a hedge. His voice got smaller toward the end.

"Thank you," said Harrold. "Sit down, man. You've done what you were sent for. Master Keller."

Keller did not make a long matter of it. The clerk of the council read the commission aloud, every line: the three surveyors of the crown's own school by name, the hill in Grayrock, the commissioner's private interest at the commissioner's cost, to report to him alone; the date; the seal. Keller laid the survey beside it and told the court, out of the crown's own mining returns, what the crown had taken as its fifth from that shape every quarter since Kaldo declared it. Emeric felt the benches hear the figure. It went along them the way a draught goes along a row of candles.

"The largest body of stone in the south of the realm," said Keller. "Measured on His Grace's commission under His Grace's seal, and kept from the crown by him, and by the late baron of Grayrock at his word, until it came into hands that declared it within the season, as the law requires." He put his hand flat on the commission. "The crown's fifth is the crown's from the hour a find is known. Not from the hour it's declared. To keep a known find is to keep what is the crown's. The old law has one word for a subject who puts his hand on what is the crown's own, whether it is a stone in a hill or a letter in the king's mail. Wayford was forfeit on that word. The council knows the price."

He sat down.

Nobody stood up for the house of Aldane. Harrold waited for it a long time, with his boots flat on the bare boards, and the benches looked at their boots and the empty seat.

Then a lean grey lord stood up on the second bench, with a black band on his sleeve that had been there long enough to go brown at the edges.

"Majesty," he said. "I'm His Grace's cousin. My son rode in his host on the Wayford road and came home to me in a box." He did not look at the empty seat. "I've not come to speak for the house of Aldane. I'll stand with the crown."

He sat down. Emeric, at the foot of the dais in the Holder of Kaldo's place, did not look at him, because he thought the man would not have wanted it. Harlow, nineteen, over his horse's head at a hundred paces.

His father gave it from the chair in the old form. The duchy of Aldane, Highcairn and the uplands and the three abbey towns, every right and rent and court in it, forfeit to the crown from this day, and the town house in the upper town with everything in it, cellars to leads. The holdings held of the duke to be held of the crown, and no man of them to answer for his lord. And Aldane to keep his life and his name, by the king's grace, and the use of the great hall at Highcairn for as long as he lived: the hall only, and nothing in it, and not an acre round it.

"He wished me to come to him in his own hall," said Harrold to the steward. "Tell him I've left it him."



The crown's escheator went into the house with the green door the next morning with two clerks and a list, and Ward and four of Osric's people behind him because Harrold had asked it, and Emeric in the lane outside because he had not been asked.

At a quarter past twelve a clerk came up the area steps very fast and white, and Ward went down. He came up with an iron box under his arm, plain, about the size of a man's two fists, with a hasp and a small lock; and gave it to the escheator, because it was the crown's, and the escheator wrote it on his list.

"And a stone," said Ward in the lane. "In canvas, on trestles, behind the wine. Long as a man. The canvas has the palace's mark on it."

The old altar stone out of the crypt chapel: Sabina's decoy, sent out of the palace's north gate at three in the morning with lamps lit, for the cardinal's man on the corner to watch. It had lain behind a duke's wine for seven months, and the cardinal had spent five months trying to get into a house he would never have been let into, for the wrong thing. His father had it carried back to the crypt that afternoon on the same dray, by daylight, where an old chaplain found it at evening on its footings and sat down on the step beside it for an hour.

The iron box came to the chart table at six, in his father's coat pocket.

"The escheator has it as an iron box, locked, contents not known," said Harrold, and set it on the river charts. "I'm told you know them."

"I think so."

"Then it's yours. I've not opened it. I don't want to know what Aldane kept in iron in his cellar." He did not reach for it again. "Is it a thing you need?"

Emeric put his good hand on the lid. It was cold, and there was nothing in it he could feel, which was what iron was for.

"It's a thing somebody needs. An old man in Kaldo. It was taken off his wrist in a lane while he went for a pie."

"Then take it to him," said Harrold, "and don't tell me what it is."

Emeric did not open it either. It went into the chart table drawer beside the letter, and the key back into his shirt.



His father had gone to Corbin on the evening of the court.

He asked Emeric to walk up with him, in the voice he used for asking whether the river was up. Avis was at the passage door. She looked at Emeric, and he shook his head, and she went down to the end of the passage and stood with her back to them.

The rule was that the door stood open. His father went in, and Emeric stayed in the dark of the passage a yard from the frame, where he could see a slice of the room and hear all of it, and feel both notes the way he would have felt two fires through a wall.

Corbin stood up too fast when he saw who it was, and the rib caught him, and he held the chair back with his face doing nothing.

"Sit down," said Harrold.

Corbin sat. His father stood by the smoking fire with his hands behind his back and looked at it.

"That wants a better draught."

"Nobody here knows how to make one."

"I don't either. I've had it done for me all my life." He turned round. "I'm not good at this. I've not come with it written down, and if I had I'd lose my place." He was quiet. "Your brother showed you the exile."

"Yes."

"It was mine. To get him out from under the knives. You didn't win it. I let you think you had, because a man who thinks he's won stops looking." He said it the way he would have read a line off one of Ferra's slips. "I'm sorry for that. Not for the plan. For letting you have it."

Corbin said nothing.

"For thirty years there was a man at my elbow who watched every face I made, and the court watching him watch me. The one person I loved where anybody could see it, they killed on a road. So I stopped letting anybody see anything." He looked at the window, at the dark where the spire had been. "You were standing nearest. You looked at a man hiding everything and saw nothing there. Anybody would have."

"You went up the stairs after the carriage," said Corbin, quite level. "To his door. Every night. I saw you."

"Yes."

"You never came to mine."

"No," said his father. "I never did."

He did not explain it. Emeric, in the passage, waited for him to, and he did not.

"I'll say this once, and badly," said Harrold. "I left you fourteen months in that chair at fourteen years old while I hunted the men who paid for the carriage, and I never once came in to see how you sat in it. I failed Zale, who went over the sea because I'd made no room for him here, and I've never once written to him first. I failed Waylon, who was three, and was kept in this palace like a coat in a cupboard while I looked the other way. I failed your mother, who was the most correct queen this realm has had and was never once thanked for it, by me or anybody. I failed Rowena, who deserved better than a man who married her because the council said so and then stopped looking at her. And Amara I failed on a road, which you know."

The fire smoked.

"There was a bind," said Harrold. "It was real, and you couldn't see it, and that was my doing too." He looked at his hands. "There was probably a way to be a father inside all that. I didn't look for it."

Corbin sat with his hands flat on the table a long time. Emeric felt his note do a thing it had not done in the yard or the council chair: it went small and stayed small, the way a man's goes when he has carried something a very long way and set it down and does not yet know what to do with his arms.

"The night you named me Lieutenant," said Corbin. "You left before the cups. A letter from the south. I'd remembered it twenty years as you being bored." He was looking at the table. "In the yard I found more of it. At the edge, where I'd never gone. You stopped in the doorway and looked back. At me. For a breath." He looked up. "Was that true? Did you?"

Harrold stood by the fire and thought about it. Emeric watched him through the slice of the door: an old man trying, honestly, to see a doorway twenty years gone, and not finding it.

"I don't remember," he said at last. He looked at his son. "I probably did."

Corbin nodded, once.

After a while his father asked whether he was eating, and said he would send down to the K Endeavor for some of Mistress Imani's soup; and Corbin said he had heard about the soup, and his father said everybody had heard about the soup. Then good night, and good night, sir; and his father came out into the passage and stopped beside Emeric in the dark without looking at him, and they went down the west stair with the lamp.

At the foot of it his father said, "Was that any good?"

"I think so."

"I lost my place twice."

"Nobody noticed."

"He noticed," said Harrold. "He's my son." And went on across the gallery with the lamp, a little faster than before.



Vantic came to the drapers' street house on the tenth at eleven, by the side door, in grey, with a single sheet in a card folder, and sat at the long table in the back room with her gloved hands folded on it and said nothing at all until he had read it.

It was the House's book for the Polytechnic alloy, which Kaldo's works called aluminum, three years on one sheet: cups, buttons, cutlery, a duke's dinner service, a set of altar plate, a tray; who had bought, and at what, the price held at silver weight for weight all the way down because the House had held it there. More than half the weight, when he added it, had gone to two kinds of house: the Empire's great merchant houses, through the House's correspondents at the ports, and the Church's, through Ostrel. The altar plate was Ostrel's. So was the tray. At the foot, under a ruled line, what all of it had brought in silver; and it was within a few hundred of what the falls dam's turbines had cost Kaldo in Ferra's book.

"The enemy bought the turbines," he said.

"The enemy bought very fine cutlery," said Vantic. "We bought the turbines. Read the dates."

The sales were thickest last summer and autumn, the same months as the imperial letters of credit in her two ledgers: the Empire's left hand paying to burn Kaldo's ships and its right buying Kaldo's spoons.

"When the river dam runs," said Vantic, "the pot room runs all night on power that costs nothing, and the metal costs what the bauxite costs. What is it worth then?"

"What tin's worth. In five years a woman in the lower town cooks in it."

"Then every house on that page has paid silver for tin." She laid one gloved finger on the sheet. "Some are Ostrel's, and I'll not lose sleep. Some are a widow in Fairhythe who bought six cups for her daughter's wedding because the House said they were rare, and they were, the day she bought them. I'll not have one of them say the House sold her a thing it knew would fall." She took the finger away. "So when it falls the House says so before anybody else can. Anyone who bought from the House at the old price brings it in with the House's bill, and takes the difference. Not in silver. In credit, in the card bank, when it opens. An account in their own name, with the difference in it, from the first day."

He sat back. It was a beautiful thing and a little frightening, the way most of Vantic's were: paying back in money that did not exist yet, in a bank that did not exist yet, to people who would walk out of it with a card and a story about the honest House.

"The Church's houses too?"

"Everybody on the page. If Ostrel wants to carry its altar plate into a Kaldo bank and open an account in Kaldo's book, Ostrel may." She almost smiled. "I'd like to see the face of the clerk who carries it."

"How many accounts?"

"From this page, about four hundred. From the stories they tell, thousands." She closed the folder. "Nobody who trusted us loses. That's the advertisement."

"Ferra will want to see the page."

"And she'll find something I've got wrong." Vantic stood and drew her gloves straight. "That's why I brought it to you first. You'll say yes. She'll make it work."



The K Discovery took the escheator north on the eleventh, with Ward and six of Osric's people, and Emeric, because Saban had told him at breakfast who else was living at Highcairn.

They came down over it in the middle of the afternoon: grey stone on a cairn-topped hill over the north road, in a country of dry-stone walls running over the bare tops like stitching. The crown's officers from Brask had been at work a day. Loaded carts were going down the hill road, and the household beside them on foot with their bundles, a falconer among them with a hooded bird on his fist, none of them looking back.

The great hall was a long high room with a timber roof black with three hundred years of smoke, and there was nothing in it. Paler squares on the walls where things had hung. Long dents in the rushes where the tables had stood. The hearth cold, the dais bare of its chair, the banners and their poles gone.

Aldane stood in the middle of it.

A big man going heavy, in black and gold, his white beard cut square; standing quite still with his hands at his sides, the way a man stands who has been told to wait and has not been told for what. He had kept his sword, which was his name's. He did not seem to know he had it on. He looked at Emeric for a long time and said nothing, and Emeric said nothing back.

There was one other man in the hall, by the cold hearth: a little stooped, in a good black coat gone shiny at the cuffs, a sack at his feet, a face Emeric had never seen and knew anyway, from a ledger in a cellar under Wayford with a magistrate's seal at the foot of every page. Wayford's magistrate, who had dined with Placit and signed whatever was put in front of him, and gone out of Wayford by Lisle's woods road the night the house shook, with his plate in a sack. He was standing in the hall because it was the last room in the north anybody had let him into.

"Him," said Emeric to Ward, low. "For the crown's court. Wayford wants him."

Two of Osric's people went across the rushes to the hearth; and the magistrate saw them coming and picked up his sack out of habit, and then seemed to understand, and put it down again.

Aldane did not watch. He turned from all of them and walked up his hall over the rushes, slowly, a big old man with a sword he had forgotten, to the place where his chair had stood, and stood there on the step with his back to the room, looking at the bare wall where his house's banner had hung for as long as there had been a king.

Ward watched him go. He stood at the hall door with his thumbs in his belt and watched the duke walk the length of the room, and stand, and not turn round; and he did not say anything, then or on the hull or on the way home, and Emeric did not ask him to.



The pilot shaft furnace had been running since the ninth of January, and Bram had been waiting for it to go wrong since the tenth.

It was a small thing as Kaldo's things went: a brick-lined steel shaft three storeys high in its own shed on the river side of the steelworks yard, taking a ton of Grayrock ore a day in pellets the size of a child's marble at the top; and coming up through the pellets from a ring at the foot, red-hot, the hydrogen off the cells. It took the oxygen out of the ore the way the blast furnace's coke did, and left it as sponge iron, grey and very nearly pure, to be drawn out cold at the foot for Tess's arc furnace. Out of the top, where the blast furnace sent up its black, came only steam.

The cells were in their own shed forty yards up the yard, and the gas came down in a steel main on trestles, through the stove that heated it red, and in at the shaft's foot through a valve.

The valve had been Dov's quarrel since November.

"It's a steam valve," Dov Kettle had said at the drawing board in the Works Office, with his finger on it. He said everything slowly, and Bram had learned in four years that the slower Dov said a thing the surer he was. "Tested to twice. It'll hold steam till the stars go out."

"Then it'll hold this."

"Steam's a fat thing. This is thin. It goes through what holds steam like water through a basket." He had tapped the gland, the packed collar round the spindle. "There. Every valve weeps a little at the gland. Steam weeps, you see a white feather, you tighten the nut. This'll weep and you'll see nothing."

"Then what?"

"Then nothing," Dov had said. "Till something."

He had wanted a valve with no gland at all, the spindle sealed in a bellows; Bram had said it would take the shop two months and a trial was a trial; and Ferra, on her interim say until the green plan came before the six, had given it a ton a day for a quarter, with the steam valve and Dov's rules both. Dov had written his objection in the works book in his slow square hand and then fitted the steam valve himself, better than anybody else in Kaldo could have, because that was what Dov did with objections.

His rules were the rest of it. The shut valve for the whole line outside the shed wall at the cells' end, where a man could reach it without going near the shaft. The ridge louvre open, winter or no. Soap on every joint every shift. No spark within a hundred yards of the gas.

And not Ambrose. Ambrose Lark had wanted the shaft shed's empty east end in December for his spark sets, which spoke in a fat blue crack across a gap fifty times a second; Bram had said yes, and Dov had said no in front of Ferra, and Ferra had written it down on Dov's side. Ambrose had gone to the old wire shed at the far end of the powerhouse yard and complained about the walk ever since.



It went wrong on the twelfth of February, a clear cold afternoon with the sun full on the yard, at about a quarter to three.

Bram was coming down the yard with the morning's figures, and looked straight at the foot of the shaft from thirty yards in bright sun, and saw nothing at all.

Dov saw it. He was walking the main with his soap pot, and stopped at the foot of the shaft; and Bram saw him stop and did not understand, because there was nothing there. Then he did see it. Not a flame. A wobble in the air over the valve, as if the air had been poured too thick, the way it goes over a road in August; the shed's far wall bending and swimming through it; and the grey paint on the valve body going brown at the gland, and lifting in a blister, and the blister going black.

One of the sponge lads was by the valve with a shovel, the youngest on the floor, fifteen. He had felt it on his face and was bending down, puzzled, to look.

"Back," said Dov.

He did not shout. He took the boy by the collar and moved him six feet as if he were moving a sack, and walked out of the shed door and up the yard toward the cells, not running, past Bram without looking at him; and at about the twentieth yard he began to run.

The boy put his hand up to his face and felt his eyebrows not there.

The lagging on the red pipe above the gland began to smoke. A broom leaned against the shaft's foot ring went black down its handle and then all at once burst into an ordinary yellow flame that everybody could see, and somebody shouted, and everybody in the shed went out of both doors the way Dov had drilled them, without stopping for anything.

Up the yard Dov reached the shut valve outside the cells' wall and turned it with both hands.

It did not stop at once. The main was full, and the gas went on coming out of the gland for a few breaths, while Bram stood in the shed door and watched a thing he could not see burn a hole in the air. Then the air over the valve was only air, and the far wall stood up straight, and there was nothing but the broom, which a lad put out with a bucket, and the lagging smoking, and a black blister on the valve the size of a man's palm.

Nobody was hurt but the boy's eyebrows. Afterward, nobody wanted to say aloud what the leak might have been doing under the shed roof for an hour before it lit itself off the hot pipe, or what a spark gap going fifty times a second at the east end would have done with it.



Bram had the whole works' feed taken off at the switch house by three. The line had to be purged and walked joint by joint before a breath of gas went into it again, and Dov said that would take till morning; and the cells were on one feeder with the pot room and the rolling mill, because Bram had put them there in the autumn and thought it tidy. So all of it came off together. The fire stones in their iron pockets under every pot would keep the baths molten through a cut for six hours or so, and not for a night.

"The coal station," said Ferra, from the switch house door.

She had come down from the keep at a run, which nobody in Kaldo had ever seen her do, with her book under her arm, and was not out of breath, or not so that anybody could tell.

The coal station had stood cold on the river bank below the thistle field since the spring of year thirteen, when the falls dam took the town: its boilers drained and its stacks capped and its turbine hall swept every week by a crew of eleven who would not take the new jobs. Seven had taken the dam and the cells at better pay, and four had not, and seven from the coke ovens had come the other way to join them, because a station that could be lit wanted somebody who knew how to light it. They called themselves the reserve. Everybody else called them the cold crew, and they did not care.

Orrel Marrick, the station's head fireman for its one year of fires and its head of nothing for most of a year since, came up the bank at a quarter past three in his shirt with his cap in his hands.

"How long from cold?" said Bram.

"Five hours. Four and a half, for you." He was not a man who smiled. He was very nearly smiling. "We do it every month, Master Bram. Dry, on the tally. We've never once had the fire."

"You've got it now."

Marrick put his cap on.

They lit at twenty to four. Bram stood on the bank at seven and watched the first smoke in eleven months come out of the uncapped stacks into the dark, fat and black and stinking of Grayrock coal; and a little after eight the turbine came up to its speed with a long rising whine, and the reserve took the pot room's bus on its own with the cells cut out, and held it. The fire stones under the pots had had an hour and a half left in them.

Marrick's crew stood in the boiler house door afterward in a row, black to the elbows, and did not need to say anything. They held the bus all night. At six, when the dam's feed went back on, Marrick drew his fires and wrote in the station's tally, which nobody but his crew had read since the spring, the date and the hours and the load; and Ferra, who had come down to see it, asked if she might read it, and initialled the corner.



She had written the leak rule the evening before, at the Works Office table at half past four, before anybody had washed.

Dov sat at the end of the table in his shirt with a mug of tea he was not drinking, the back of his right hand red where the shut valve's wheel had stood in the sun all afternoon, and had not noticed. The sponge lad sat by the stove with no eyebrows and a nurse either side of him, having refused to go up to the hospital.

Ferra opened the works book to a clean page after Dov's objection of November and wrote the date.

"Say it," she said to Dov.

"You want it now."

"I want it while it's true. In a week it'll be a story, and everybody will remember it smaller. Say it so I can write it."

So Dov said it, slowly, to the mug, and she wrote it in his words and did not tidy them. Gas goes up and goes through: none of it piped into a room with a shut roof. No glands on the gas; the bellows valve or nothing. The shut valve outside, upwind, where a man can walk to it. Soap on every joint every shift and after every touch. Nothing that sparks within a hundred yards, and nobody to ask for an exception, because the answer is no. And the last, which he thought about a long time: You'll not see it. Look for the air going wrong.

She read it back. He nodded. She wrote his name at the foot of it first and hers under it, and turned the book round to Bram.

"It's his," said Bram.

"It's his," said Ferra. "You're signing that you didn't listen."

Bram signed it, and sat looking at the page with the pen in his black hand.

He had seen the other thing at the shaft's foot by accident, looking at the valve: the grate that held the pellets up, its bars sagged a finger's width in a month of red heat. Steel went soft red-hot and stretched under a load like honey. And Emeric had said a thing on the set in January, tired, between two other things: that what Bram had found at the furnace in October was true and only half of it. Specks cracked steel when they were coarse. Ground fine enough and spread through it even, they pinned it. Made it stronger red-hot, not weaker. Then he had gone on to the next thing, and Bram had thought about it every night since.

"The charging room's bucket," said Bram.

Ferra looked at him.

"The sweepings. Off the readers' benches, the cracked ones, the dust off the cutting wheels, all in together. Grey. It reads dead. There's a bin of it in the stores." He put the pen down. "I want it."

"What for?"

"To grind. Very fine." He looked at his hands. "I'll tell you when I know."

Ferra got up and went to the shelf at the back where the old stores ledgers stood, the keep's first years, and ran her finger along the spines and took one down without hesitating, and turned the leaves until she found it. She had always been able to do that. Tess said it was the only magic in the family.

A line near the foot of a page in the first summer, in her own hand, younger and rounder. Charging room sweepings, mixed, grey, read dead: of no value. Written off. And the date.

"In ink," she said.

"Is that bad?"

"It's permanent." She did not strike it through; she never struck through anything. She wrote under it, small, the day's date and: Restored to stores at the Secretary of Works' request. Value: to be found. And initialled it, and turned the book for Bram to initial too, and he did, in soot.

Dov had finally drunk his tea. He read the ledger upside down.

"What's he want dust for?"

"Don't know yet," said Ferra. "Write your objection in the book."

Bram walked home a little after nine, when the station had the bus, by the ring road and the gate. Hollis had sent down from Milford that week that the crawler tractor the vehicle works had built him, on its tracks, had gone out on biodiesel pressed from Milford's own soy onto a bottom field where a wheel would have sunk to the hub, and ploughed it, and smelled like a fish supper. And under the arch of the South Gate by the tram stop, one of Maren's girls had opened a coffee house on Monday, six tables and beans come over the sea in Zale's holds, and half the lower ward had said it was like drinking a burned fence and gone back the next day. It was open when he passed. It smelled like a burned fence, and very good. He did not go in.



The set in the K Amara's iron room came clear a little after eleven that night, and it was Ferra, and she sounded the way he felt.

He had been told on the ladder, back from Highcairn, that there had been a fire at the works in Kaldo, nobody dead, and had sat down on the ladder's step with his stitched arm across his knees until the clerk had told him the rest.

She gave it him in figures: the valve, Dov's walk and then Dov's run, the boy's eyebrows, the coal station lit from cold, the fire stones' hour and a half, the rule in Dov's words with her father's name under it. The bucket last, dryly, and he heard her not quite laugh.

"In ink," he said.

"In ink. My first summer. I've corrected it." The pen. "Your Aldane."

"Alive. In his hall, with nothing in it." He leaned his head back against the iron. "Wayford's magistrate was standing in it with him. With a sack."

"I'll want his name for the book." She did not ask anything else. She would, at the table, when he was home.

The set hissed between them a while, and it was not the bad kind.

"I'd have liked to be there," he said.

"You'd have been in the way. You'd have run to the valve. Dov walked." Then, lower, as if she had turned from the line room's window to say it: "I'd have liked you there."

He held the horn.

"I've a thing to say to you," he said. "It's not a thing for the set."

He could hear her breathing at the other end of seven hundred miles, and the pen not moving.

"Then don't say it on the set," said Ferra, very nearly steady. "Say it on the quay. I'll be counting. Say it when I get to you." A pause. "I'll want to see your face."

"I'll want to see yours."

"You'll see it," said Ferra, as if she were reading it off the manifest. "Go to bed, Emeric. You sound like a man who's sat on a ladder."



The war was over.

Nobody had said so; the bells were under the cut stone on the summit with everything else. It was over the way a fever is over: one morning a man notices he has been well for some days. The Isle sat behind Rylan's staked line in the soot, unsigned and quiet. The capital's towers stood empty with their chests cold. The man in white and gold was in Ostrel, home. The king sat his chair at nine with his boots on the bare boards, and the chancery bought its own candles.

In the west wing over the long gallery a man with a yellow jaw sat by a fire nobody knew how to mend, with his mother three doors down the passage, and waited to be carried south and kept.

And a mile over the city, in the cold, Kaldo's hulls hung at their stations with their crews aboard and their stores in and their lists made, and the K Endeavor's hospital hold lit all night at the far end where the cradle was lashed under its canvas, and an old man's iron box in a drawer; waiting for the weather, and for the word, to go home.
