Chapter 5 - The Bound Man

In the morning Emeric boiled water.

He had Rylan, groggy and grey and furious with himself, build the fire back up. He had Declan, who still couldn't walk quite straight, find the smallest pot in the carriage stores. He put a strip of clean linen in it, and a needle from the sewing kit, and a length of thread, and boiled them all together while the others watched.

"Arm," he said to Ward.

Ward gave it to him. The knife had gone in through the bracer's leather and out the far side of the muscle, a clean in-and-out a finger long. It had stopped bleeding in the night. Emeric washed it with water that had boiled and cooled. He washed his own hands the same way, twice.

"Too small to see," he said, mostly to himself, threading the needle. "Living things. On your hands, in the water, in the cloth. They get into a wound and the wound goes bad. Boiling kills them, so you..."

He stopped. Declan was nodding with great seriousness, the way men nod at a lord who has said something mad. Osric was eyeing him as though the poison had got to him after all. Ward held his arm still.

Emeric closed the wound in eight stitches.

He had never stitched anything in his life. He knew how it was done as surely as he knew his own name, every motion of it, the angle of the needle, the bite of the stitch, how tight was too tight. His hands followed what he knew, slowly, a little clumsily, and the stitches came out even. When he tied off the last one Ward looked down at his arm, then at Emeric, and didn't ask.

The arm never went bad. Over the next seven days it closed clean at the edges, and nobody connected that to the boiling. Emeric noticed nobody connecting it. Being right, apparently, was not the hard part.



After that he asked Ward the other thing.

"Teach me again," he said. "The sword."

Ward was buckling the bracer back on over the bandage. He didn't look up. He finished the buckle, tested it, and went to his kit, and from the bottom of it drew out a long bundle in oiled cloth and unrolled it on the grass. Two practice blades lay inside, wood weighted with lead along the spine, the grips worn smooth. They were old. They'd been oiled recently.

Emeric looked at them, and then at Ward.

Ward handed him one and kept the other, and put his wounded arm behind his back.

"Feet," Ward said.

He put Emeric on the ground four times before breakfast. One-handed, with a hole through his forearm, barely moving his feet. Each time Emeric got up a little slower. The fourth time he lay on his back in the wet grass and looked at the sky, and Ward stood over him and said, "Better," in a voice that made it clear it wasn't, and offered his good hand.

Rylan, watching from the fire with his head in his hands, laughed for the first time that morning.



They questioned Petram on the road.

Ward wanted to do it the other way. He said so on the first day, flatly, with his hand on the bound man's collar: a night with a knife and a fire and they'd have everything. Emeric said no.

It was the first time in fifteen years he had overruled Ward on anything, and he did it on method, not mercy. "A man you've cut tells you what stops the cutting," he said. "And if we ever stand him in front of a magistrate, a confession he gave with a blade in him is worth nothing. I want him whole, and I want a witness to everything he says."

Ward let go of the collar. He didn't agree. He stepped back.

So they did it slowly, over days, with Petram riding bound in the carriage across from Emeric and his boots taken off, and Rylan driving outside, saying very little now.

The money came first. Half up front, in a purse handed over in a stable yard in the capital, the rest on completion. Petram named the sum, and Emeric worked it through: more than fifteen years of a guard's wages, less than twenty. Over a hundred Gold Rand. Twice what his father had given him to live on for the rest of his life.

Then the road. Then, last of all, the target. They'd bought Petram before they'd told him who he was going to kill. Whoever had done it knew how to buy men, and had done it before.

"It wasn't only the coin," Petram said, the first day, and looked at Emeric with an expression Emeric couldn't place. He decided it was resentment and moved on.

Garrin had been first. Garrin had brought him in. And Garrin, Petram said, had been in place for the better part of a year.

That stopped him, because the roster for his escort had been drawn up eleven days before they left. Garrin had been planted for a year on a posting that hadn't existed until the roster was drawn. Either someone had got into the roster office after the fact and changed the names, or someone high enough had written the roster the way they wanted it from the start. He went round it three times looking for another way it could have happened.

There was no third door.

He wanted to write it down. He had the notebook on his knee and the stylus in his pocket, and his father's rule was in his head, and he left the page blank.

"What did he look like?" he asked. "Garrin. When he came to you."

Petram thought about it. "Ordinary," he said. "He looked like the rest of us."

The next day: why Petram. Because he had family in Kaldo. That was the whole of it. He hadn't been there in nine years and couldn't have named the baron, and when Emeric asked about the town, the roads, the lie of the land, he knew nothing that would have helped an ambush. They'd wanted a man whose family could be reached.

Emeric looked out of the carriage window at the back of Rylan's head.

Rylan had told this man his wife's name. His children's names and ages. His street.

He said nothing to anyone.



The last thing came out by accident, on the seventh evening, in the middle of an argument about water.

The attack, Petram said, had been planned for the ninth night, in the forest past Wayford, where the road went bad for most of a day. He'd moved it four days early because Ward had tightened the watch after the second night and he'd lost his nerve.

Emeric kept his face still.

The forest past Wayford. The exact stretch his father had whispered to him in the office. Rest the horses before that stretch. Not after.

It could be nothing. A king knew his own roads. Any man planning an ambush on the south road would pick that forest, because there was nowhere else on it as good. Or it was something. He held both readings up against each other and couldn't find a hair's difference in their weight. It was the same load he couldn't bring himself to run, and he set it down where it was.

They had passed through Wayford the day before, a town of inns and stables strung along a crossroads. On the eighth and ninth days they rode the forest. Emeric watched the trees the whole way. Nothing came out of them. The road was as bad as promised and the horses were rested, and they came out the far side with nothing worse than a cracked wheel spoke.

On the eighth day Petram stopped talking altogether.

He'd been giving them a little each day, then less, and then on the eighth morning he gave them nothing at all. He sat in the carriage with his bound hands in his lap and looked out of the window, and when Emeric asked him a question he didn't seem to hear it.

Emeric considered it. Late fear, now that the forest was behind them and there was nothing ahead but a cell. Or simply the end of what he knew. Both fit. He decided it didn't matter which and called it settled, and nobody, Ward included, had any reason to ask him whether he'd got it right.



At night the carriage was his.

He sat inside it behind closed shutters with a candle on the floor and Armand's notebook open on the seat, and practiced.

By day, while the wheels jolted under him, he read. The notebook was dense and dry and in places brilliant, and Armand's handwriting got smaller when he was excited. Between chapters Emeric took the stylus and a writing slate out of the trunk and drew on the slate with the stylus dry, no stone and no ink, only the motion. Straight lines. Curves. The simple repeated strokes he half-remembered from childhood without remembering learning them. His hand wandered. It went on wandering for days.

At night he worked on the rest.

He put a copper coin on his palm and reached for it, and lifted it.

It was harder than the candle had been. The pull came from somewhere low in him that felt like holding a door shut against wind, and a little ring of light opened in the air above his hand with a mark at its center and closed, and the coin rose a finger's width off his skin and hung there. He counted. At six it dropped. He tried again. At eight. By the third night he could hold it at hand height for a count of ten, then twelve, and the headache afterward was a little less each time.

Then the candle, bending the flame toward his finger and holding it there. That one came easily, the first thing in days that had.

On the first night, when he opened the carriage door to empty the wash basin, Ward was sitting with his back against the rear wheel, awake, looking at the strip of ground under the door. Where a faint light had been showing, in short pulses, for the last hour.

Ward didn't say anything. Neither did Emeric.

The second night, when Emeric climbed in, Ward's cloak was hanging over the gap at the bottom of the door, pinned at both corners.



On the twelfth day the road ran beside a long wood, dark and close-set, and Rylan said over his shoulder that it was the Whispering Woods and they were nearly there.

Halfway along it, Emeric felt it again. Looked at. The same pressure between the shoulders he'd felt in the gallery above the kitchens. It came from the trees on the left, steady, unhurried.

He put his head out of the window and looked. Trunks, shadow, a jay going off somewhere deeper in. Ward rode on the far side, looking ahead. Nothing moved in the wood that shouldn't.

He sat back. It stayed with him until the trees thinned, and then it was gone, as it had gone at the end of the gallery.

They crested the last rise an hour after noon.

He smelled Kaldo before he saw it. A sweetish, heavy smell that came up the road to meet them from two miles out, and that he knew the meaning of instantly and wished he didn't.

Then the land fell away in front of them and there it was.

A circular stone wall with towers spaced along it, hugging a town crammed so tight inside that the roofs were one roof from here, thatch and old timber in a hundred shades of brown. A moat ran round the outside of the wall, with one bridge across it to a gate on the south side. At the center of everything the ground rose to a walled inner ward, and on it stood the keep, square towers and smaller round ones with blue conical roofs, and around its foot a ring of water like a moat inside the town.

East of the wall ran a river, wide and brown and moving. A stone bridge crossed it to a straggle of wharves and huts on the far bank, and of all the mooring posts along those wharves, perhaps one in four had a boat at it.

Fields lay all around, patched and green, a great many of them empty. And from far to the north a long line of stone arches came striding across the land toward the town, an aqueduct running in from a lake that shone at the edge of sight. One arch near the middle was down. The channel ended in the air on one side and started again on the other.

Far off to the south, past everything, a flat grey line that was the sea.

Emeric sat with his elbows on his knees and read it the way he'd read a set of drawings.

Defensively, it was superb. Water on one side, a moat all round, one gate.

Economically, it was dead. A river full of free trade, and three moorings in four empty.

As a place to live, it was a bucket. Three thousand people packed inside a wall, drinking from wells dug into the same ground they buried their dead in. The smell told him that much from two miles out.

And the river. A big, steady river running past the town day and night, turning a few mill wheels and doing nothing else at all. All that power going by, unused.

There was nobody waiting for them at the moat bridge. No herald, no steward, nobody to greet a prince. Either the baron hadn't been told they were coming, or he had.



The man on the gate was about nineteen, with a bad shave and no idea what to do with a prince. He looked at the carriage, at Ward, at Emeric, and then at Emeric's eyes, and went red to the ears, and opened the gate, and that was all the ceremony there was.

The keep had been built for a barony twice the size. Emeric could see it the moment they rode into the inner ward. The drawbridge chain had been replaced with rope. A stretch of wall above the stables was dark with damp and sagging outward. Half the torch brackets along the walls were empty. In one corridor there were pale squares on the stone where paintings had hung, and the hooks were still there.

A steward named Hale met them, a tired, careful man doing the work of three, and took them up.

The baron was behind a desk in a room that had been someone's grand study once. He didn't stand up.

Emeric had decided on the road how to play it. The drunk had kept him alive for fourteen years. He saw no reason to drop it before he knew who he was dealing with.

"Baron Kaldo, I presume," he said, with the drunk's loose cheerfulness.

The man let him be wrong for a beat too long. He was past sixty, heavy-shouldered, grey, with the face of someone who'd been told bad news for thirty years and had stopped flinching at it.

"Kaldo is the land," he said. "Not the name. The name is Wexler."

Kal had been a knight, Wexler said, who took land instead of a captaincy, had no heir, and left it to the man he trusted most, his steward. The Wexlers had held it four generations since. Through a war. Through a plague. A week ago a rider had come down the south road with one line from the crown, and Wexler gave it to Emeric word for word in a voice with nothing in it at all.

"Keep it running for him."

Nobody had asked Wexler.

They talked a while, and Wexler asked his questions, and they were good, polite, ordinary questions: who would the prince's secretary be, and when were his household staff arriving, and would there be letters to send. And Emeric, being a drunk, answered them truthfully and carelessly, and didn't notice until much later that by the end of a quarter hour Wexler knew he had no secretary, no steward, no letters, no informants, and nobody coming.

He couldn't remove Wexler. That was plain within minutes. The steward was Wexler's. The gate was Wexler's. Every arrangement in the barony ran through this man and his thirty years. Emeric had a paper from the crown, three guards who could stand, one who'd been stabbed, and a prisoner.

So he made the one move he could.

"My prisoner stays near me," he said. "The room nearest mine. My own men on the door, in turns. None of yours."

Wexler understood what that meant: the prince didn't trust the house with a prisoner. He took a moment over it.

"As you like, Your Highness."

They gave Emeric rooms on the east corridor, and Petram the third door along, two doors from his own, with Declan taking the first watch.

In the corridor, Ward fell in at his shoulder as he had in the capital.

"How did I do?" Emeric said.

"Spectacular, Your Highness."

It was the same word as in the capital. This time Ward meant it.

Emeric didn't go to see Petram that night. He had good reasons. The man was exhausted, and so was Emeric, and nothing useful would be said tonight, and it would look like weakness to Wexler's people. They were all sound reasons. He noticed, lying in a strange bed, that every one of them had arrived after he'd already decided not to go.

"That's true," Ward said from the doorway, when he gave him the reasons anyway. "Which is why it works."

Two floors below, in the old grand study, Wexler sat at his desk long after the candles should have been put out, doing his own arithmetic.
