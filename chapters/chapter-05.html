Chapter 5 - Root Causes

The room was cold again by the time he sat down. It had been cold every night since he arrived. Whatever the steward's kitchen had sent up sat at his elbow going cold faster than he was eating it, and he didn't notice.

He had a quill, ink, a blank page, and a week of unsorted input finally ready to become something with a shape. The ink was terrible and the paper was worse. He used them anyway.

He worked top to bottom the way he'd have worked a punch list on a job site. Root causes first, symptoms sorted underneath them instead of promoted into problems of their own.

The water went at the top, because it mattered most and because it was the one he already had a plan for. Whatever was still killing people here was coming out of the ground where the water sat, and it didn't take a villain to explain it. A burial ground upriver of the wells. Latrines dug too close to the bank by people who had never had a reason to map those two facts against each other. Active, ongoing, killing somebody this month. Everything else on the page could wait longer than this could.

Directly under it, and this was the entry he spent the longest on, he wrote down that the water did not explain the year that emptied the town.

Bad water kills steadily. It takes ones and twos across years, which described everything since perfectly and described the die-off not at all. Something had come through this place fast enough to take a thousand people inside twelve months and then stopped taking them that way, and it had left before he arrived, and nobody here had a separate word for it because dead was dead.

He underlined that twice. Two shapes, one name. If he fixed the second and let the town hand him credit for the first, he would have bought himself a reputation he hadn't earned and a blind spot he'd never see again.

Below that, not a problem so much as a permanent condition he'd be building around. The town had lost more than half its people, first to war and then to sickness. Two separate failures stacked on each other. Whatever he did from here he'd be doing it with fewer hands than the job needed, and no amount of clever engineering put that number back up.

Wexler went on the page too, and he was careful with the wording even writing it only for himself. Disengaged, at minimum. The man hadn't ridden out to greet him. He'd corrected him out of spite rather than courtesy. He'd shown no flicker of interest in his own barony's condition through the one real conversation they'd had.

All of that was consistent with plain apathy. It was also consistent with something worse, and he had no evidence for worse, so he wrote down apathy and made himself leave it there. Acting on a theory he hadn't earned was how competent people ended up as the next cautionary tale.

The fallow fields went down as an open question, not a conclusion. Could be the labor shortage explaining itself. Could be something else. The idle mill went beside it for the same reason, noticed and not understood, and he was careful not to hand the two of them a shared cause just because they happened to be broken in the same town.

Last was the castle. Rope where a chain should be, wall damage hidden instead of fixed, a steward doing the work of three men. He almost left it off. It wasn't a cause of anything. It was what every item above it looked like from the outside, and fixing those would fix this without him lifting a finger toward it. The ledgers would tell him faster, and he doubted he'd get those out of the baron without a fuss.

He read back over what he had. Seven items, and exactly one of them had a next step he could take tomorrow.

That was fine. That was how it always started.

He'd sent the maids past the barony's edge for water that morning, up beyond the burial ground and the latrine trenches to a source running clean of everything the settlement dumped downhill. The extra walk annoyed them. Hauling water that far when there was a river at the bottom of the slope struck them as backward in a way that had nothing to do with the distance. He was still a prince, though, and telling them it would make sense later got them moving.

He wasn't going to walk into a stranger's house and ask them to boil water he hadn't been drinking himself. Everything that came up to his rooms went over a fire first, same as he'd be asking of them, and if it turned out he was wrong about any of it he would find out before anyone else did.

The boiling was a second measure rather than a lack of faith in the source. Clean water was clean water, but he didn't know what else the ground up there carried, and he'd rather burn extra wood than gamble.

The parish house smelled like tallow and old paper. Father Denn met them at the door and was polite in the specific way people are polite to nobility they don't trust yet, which was to say he was extremely polite and did not move out of the doorway.

"The book stays here," he said. "That's not me being difficult."

"What is it, then?"

"It's the only place in this town that knows who everybody was." He said it without any weight on it. "Half of them have got nobody left to say their names. If that book leaves this house and doesn't come back, they didn't happen."

Emeric hadn't expected that and had to spend a second putting his prepared answer away.

"Then send someone with it," he said. "Or come yourself. I'll work wherever you want me to work and you can watch me do all of it. I'll copy what I need and leave the original in your hands. What I can't do is read four years of it standing in your doorway."

Bram said something low and local from behind him that Emeric didn't catch. Whatever it was, it worked better than the offer had. They carried four books out between them. Rylan took the heaviest without being asked, and Father Denn walked the whole way to the smithy with them and sat in the corner for six hours, and Emeric let him.

He cleared a table and started stacking pages by year, then by month, then by something the others couldn't see yet. Names, ages, cause where a cause had been written down, and beside every entry a location. Which well. Which street. Which row of the burial ground.

"You're not reading it," Bram said, watching him move faster than reading allowed. "You're sorting it."

"Same thing, further out." Emeric didn't look up. "One death near the north well doesn't mean anything. Six in the same month, all on the same side of it, means something."

Rylan leaned over the table and stayed quiet a while. Then he put a finger on a line near the bottom of a page from three years back.

"That's my aunt's row." Flat, no weight on it, but his hand stayed on the page a second longer than it needed to. "Same well."

Emeric marked it and kept going. He said nothing about the pause. The pattern would say it for him soon enough, and it would say it to a hundred people who'd lost someone the same way.

By the time the candle burned low he had the beginning of a map on cheap paper. Dots for deaths, clustered thick around the north well, thinning toward the east side of town where the water ran higher and cleaner.

Not proof. A shape.

"That's it?" Bram said, looking at the scatter of ink. "That's your evidence?"

"That's the start of it." Emeric sat back. "Now I go find the households near the thick part of that map and ask them to try something different for a season. Not everyone. Enough."

He took the map to Imani before he took it anywhere else, because she was the only person in Kaldo who could tell him what wasn't on it. She looked at it a long while without touching it.

"Your dots are thin on Weir Row." She tapped the paper, three houses east of the north well. "Four there the summer before last. None of them are in the priest's book. None of them had anybody left to pay the burying."

"Names?"

She gave him eleven, across five years, from memory, in the order they'd died. Emeric wrote them all down and marked them and watched his own map get worse, which meant it was getting truer.

"It's still the ground," she said.

"It's the water sitting in the ground."

"That's the same sentence with a word moved."

"That's the whole difference." He set the quill down. "You can't move the ground. You can boil the water."

Imani didn't answer for a while.

"Five whole years I've been telling this town it's the ground." She pushed the map back across to him. "If you're wrong, you've cost them a winter of firewood. If you're right, I've cost them five years." She got the door for him. "So be sure. Then come and get me, and I'll say it standing next to you."

They went house to house with it, and doors opened that wouldn't have opened for a prince, because Imani was the one knocking.

He kept it plain and skipped the lecture entirely. The water near the burial ground wasn't safe to drink and hadn't been for a long time. The sickness that kept coming back wasn't a curse on the town, it was that ugly little problem going unnoticed for decades. He showed them where the clean water was, said very little else, and left it there.

He couldn't force anyone to change how they lived. That was how riots started and it wasn't a road he had any interest in walking.

It took two weeks to know anything, and what he learned wasn't clean.

The Hallards, three doors down from the north well, switched without complaint and boiled every cup before it touched a mouth, and nobody in that house so much as coughed wrong the whole fortnight.

The widow Corwin tried it four days, decided the extra walk to the clean well wasn't worth the ache in her knees, and went back to the water she'd always used. Her youngest ran a fever by the end of the week, mild and survivable, but a fever all the same.

Old Pell refused outright, told Rylan to his face he'd drunk from that well sixty years and wasn't about to let a boy prince tell him different, and nothing happened to him at all, which did more damage on that street than any failure could have.

The Talbrooks split it down the middle, boiled for the children and didn't bother for themselves, and it was the parents who got sick.

No clean line anywhere in it. Nothing a determined skeptic couldn't put a hole through. Laid next to the dots on the map, though, there was enough there for anyone looking carefully. Boil the water and the odds moved. They didn't move for everyone, and some who ignored every word of it walked away fine, and the odds still moved.

Emeric logged each result the way he'd have logged test data that refused to behave. Not proof the method had failed. Proof it was real.

Imani brought him most of it herself, house by house, in the order she'd walked them. She read the Talbrooks off her list last, the parents sick and the children not, and then she stood in Bram's back room saying nothing for a length of time Emeric had the sense not to interrupt.

"Boiled for the little ones and not themselves," she said eventually. "I've watched that house five years. I'd have told you the parents were the strong ones."

"You'd have been right. That's what makes it evidence."

"I know what makes it evidence." She folded the list and put it away. "I'm deciding what to do about being wrong for those five years, and I'd rather not do it in front of you."

She was at the north well the next morning telling people to boil it, which was the only answer she ever gave him.

The holdouts didn't come around all at once. They came around one bad week at a time, one neighbor's fever weighed against another neighbor's dry summer, until boiling water before it touched a mouth was just what people did on that end of town. Old Pell was still drinking straight from the well the last anyone checked, daring the rot to try him again. Nobody was betting on him changing his mind. He was the exception people talked about now, though, and not the rule.

A month on from the first map, Bram closed the smithy early and brought him home instead.

From the outside the house looked like every other house on the row. The upkeep showed the moment he was through the door. A young woman put a cup of something herbal in his hand and was gone back into the house before he could thank her.

Emeric sat with it going cold on his knee and thought about the list.

"I plan on solving this town's problems while I'm here, Bram." The look on his face did most of the work before the words caught up. "I'm going to need a lot of help. I can't do it alone, and if we're getting this place out of the state it's in, I need the town on board and not watching from a distance."

"Easier said." Bram sipped without looking up. "Hope's costly here. Nobody's willing to pay for it twice."

"The water I can handle. I think I already have. The fields are going to be worse." Emeric turned the cup a quarter turn. "There's magic for growing things. I've seen it done. I've got the capacity to try it on one field, once, badly, and I'd be no use to anyone for two days afterward and the field would still need doing again next season."

"So don't."

"So don't," Emeric agreed. "Anything I have to stand in the middle of isn't a solution, it's a leash. The day I'm not here, it stops, and this town is exactly where it was with one extra reason to never trust a noble again."

Bram grunted at that, which from him was closer to agreement than most men's yes.

"It makes no sense that the capital hasn't sent anyone down here already." Emeric put the cup on the floor. "Imani sent word three times the first year. Through the castle, because that's the road it goes down. Nobody came. Nobody wrote back to tell her why."

He let that sit a moment and then moved past it, because he had nothing to do with it yet and saying more would have been theater.

"Fixing the fields properly isn't the hard part. I know ways to get more out of a harvest than anyone here's used to seeing. Faster growth, bigger yield, ways to collect and store it that don't rot half of it before winter. All of that needs metal. And it needs a smith."

Bram's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "Are you trying to hire me, boy?"

"Yes. You and your workers. Exclusive, long-term. The whole barony's smithing, if you'll take it."

"Tell me more of these ideas of yours and I'll think about it."

"All right. So. This is how farming actually works."

He spent the rest of the evening on it, and the discipline was in what he left out. Everything he couldn't prove to a man with no way to check it went unmentioned. No invisible machinery running underneath every green thing Bram had ever grown. Nothing that required believing him first.

What he kept was what he could show. Rotate what you plant so the soil doesn't get bled dry of the same thing every season. Feed it what it's missing instead of guessing. Water on a schedule instead of whenever somebody remembers. None of it needed magic to work. Magic was only going to make it faster and bigger and less dependent on however many hands the war and the sickness had left standing.

Bram listened without interrupting, which Emeric had already learned was as close to enthusiasm as the man came. When it was done he set his cup down and studied him the way he had at the forge, like a piece of metal he still hadn't found the flaw in.

"You talk like you've already built this in your head."

"I have. I just don't have the hands or the metal to build it yet. That's what I'm asking you for."

Bram was quiet a long moment. Somewhere in the back of the house there was the occasional clatter of a pot, close enough to be listening and far enough to pretend she wasn't.

"I'll think on it," Bram said again, which Emeric was starting to understand was not a no.

"You haven't introduced me to your family."

"Ferra. My daughter." He didn't call her in. "Wife went in the plague. Two of us since."

"I'm sorry."

"Everyone is." Bram said it without any edge, and finished his cup, and that was the end of the subject.

Ward was still shadowing him on the walk back to the castle. At this point it was ritual.

Tonight had a surprise waiting. Wexler was standing in the entry hall.

"Hello, Emeric." Flat, with everything underneath it packed away.

There it was again. Anyone below a prince on the royal ladder used the title before the name. Emeric didn't care about the pleasantry itself, being half a man from a world that had never had one, but the baron knew the custom and was actively choosing not to follow it, and had chosen an empty hall to do it in where there was no audience to perform for and no one to call it out.

He couldn't call it out either. Why make bad blood in a place where most people would happily cast him as the villain of the story?

"Hello, Wexler. What can I help you with?"

"I hear you've been making trips around my barony." Barely bothering to hide the snideness. "Is everything all right?"

Emeric saw no reason to lie. The baron knew where he'd been and when. No ruler lets a man with any measure of power roam his land untethered, and while Emeric doubted anyone had followed them, since Ward would have handled that quietly, tracing a man's steps after the fact cost nothing but the price of asking. Information was never hard to buy.

He gestured for the baron to follow him into a side room rather than answer in the hall, and noted the half-beat before Wexler moved.

He laid out the surveys and what he was trying to learn about why the town kept losing people. Wexler's face did nothing at all through it. He listened.

"Surveys," he repeated, turning the word over for a hidden edge. "You've spent a month in my barony doing surveys."

"Water sources, burial sites, dates of first symptom onset, cross-referenced against household proximity to the river." Emeric said it the way he'd have said it to Bram, or to himself. Not performing an answer, just giving one. "The wells nearest the latrine outflow correlate with the earliest and worst cases. That's not proof yet, it's a pattern. I'd want another season before I called it anything else."

Something moved behind Wexler's eyes, small, gone before it registered.

"And what would you do with another season, Your Highness?"

"Put the latrines and the burial ground downstream of the wells, or move the wells upstream of both. Whichever's cheaper to dig. Boil the water in the meantime, and tell people why instead of just telling them to." Emeric shrugged, because to him it was obvious. "It won't take without buy-in from somebody people already trust, though. I can hand a stranger a theory and get nothing. Bram can hand them the same theory and get compliance by dinner."

He hadn't meant it as a display of anything. It was true the way water finding low ground is true.

Wexler stayed quiet a moment too long.

"Bram," he said finally, "doesn't take to strangers."

"He didn't take to me either, at first." Emeric didn't dress it up. "I gave him something worth taking to."

The lazy performance-of-the-minimum the baron had worn since the first day wasn't there anymore. What replaced it wasn't respect, and Emeric couldn't have said what it was, only that the man in front of him had gone very still.

"Get some rest, Your Highness." Wexler stood, which he hadn't bothered to do before. "You've had a long day."

Emeric went up the stairs turning the last part over and got nowhere with it, which was becoming a pattern of its own. He could read a town's water table off a burial ledger in an afternoon. He had been in a room with this man three times now and still couldn't tell you what he wanted.
