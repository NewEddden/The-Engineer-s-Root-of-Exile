Chapter 5 - The Rock That Behaved Like a Spring

The carriage in the courtyard was sizable and entirely plain.

No crest. No lacquer. Nothing on it that read from any distance at all. It was the vehicle a noble house uses when it wants somebody to leave somewhere quietly, and the plainness was not an economy, it was the specification.

His eye caught the undercarriage before he reached the door.

Four black stones, flecked through with points of light, fitted into the axle housings and carved in a dense repeating lattice that ran without a break across every visible face.

He crouched.

Nobody cuts a pattern that consistent for decoration. Decoration wanders. Decoration gets tired at the corners and a carver improvises where nobody will look. This did not wander anywhere, which meant the pattern was not on the work. The pattern was the work.

A stable hand read the crouching as concern and rocked the frame to reassure him.

The whole carriage went down and came back in a slow cushioned wobble, settling through three diminishing movements, and no cart axle in the history of carts had any business producing that.

Gravity stones, carved to resist and release force at a fixed threshold. No spring. No linkage, no moving parts, nothing to lubricate, nothing to fatigue. Somebody had made a rock behave like a spring and had done it without putting a spring anywhere in the build.

He had watched that exact motion before, on a different world, under a different vehicle, crouched in a driveway.

He stood up with his hands cold.

The plain carriage was a lie in both directions. Unremarkable outside, obscenely expensive underneath, which was his father's method applied to a vehicle. It was also, he thought, precisely what had been done to him.

What bothered him more than either was that he had ridden in carriages exactly like this one his entire life and had never once wondered.

He had been a boy who took things apart. He had put that boy away as a costume choice at eight years old and had never gone back for him, and the first pull of the old curiosity in a decade had arrived on the underside of an axle in a courtyard at dawn.

He could not name the feeling. He turned it over most of the morning and got nowhere with it.

Six guards rode with him.

Ward at the lead. Rylan, Declan, Osric, all three known to him by face and habit from fifteen years of the castle training yard, which was not the same as knowing them but was not nothing.

Then Petram. Then a lean quiet man called Garrin who had said perhaps ten words since the gates.

Two men he did not know, on a journey his father had engineered.

He did the arithmetic and got a comfortable answer inside of a minute. His father's people, quietly added, because of course he would, because that is what the man had been doing for fifteen years and there was no version of this road where he had not done it again.

He was pleased with himself for working it out and he stopped thinking about it for two days.

The city went by in rings.

Wall, rooftops, wall, rooftops, each band narrowing as it climbed until the pattern ran out of room and the castle took over. The river looped the outer works and the bridges were the only ways through, and every bridge had a tower looking down its length. Each ring sat higher than the one below it, so that the whole city was a stack of fallback lines with clean sightlines from the crown.

He counted the rings without deciding to.

Somewhere past the second wall it came to him that the city did not smell.

The Emeric of this world had never known it to. The Marcus of the other one had read enough to know exactly what a city of this size was supposed to do to a nose, and the books Armand had kept pressing on him back when he still opened books had all said the same thing about it, and the reason was mages. Not battle mages. Utility. Water, waste, farming, a dozen systems every kingdom needs and this one had bothered to staff properly.

Two knowledge sets, one observation, and neither of them could have produced it alone.

The mage tower came back as a memory with no picture attached to it.

Armand had taken him up there once as a boy. He had stood at the crown of it and watched the Circle's floating gallery hang clear of the tower's spine with nothing under it but air. Ten men at any hour, each gripping a crystal the size of a melon, eyes half closed. And behind each of the ten, another man, waiting to step into the gap the instant the first one's grip went.

He had asked an acolyte why nobody had simply built the thing to hold itself up.

The answer had stuck word for word for fourteen years. The gallery held the Circle's council, and it floated because the Circle answered to no staircase.

A boy who had not yet lost interest in how things worked had decided on the spot that it was the finest thing he had ever seen.

The verdict had not travelled. Only the record of having reached it.

Twenty men a day. Twenty before them, and twenty before those, generations of men passing an unsolved engineering problem hand to hand because somebody a long time ago had decided that the cost was the point rather than the flaw.

Above the rooftops the sky had traffic.

Small ships, hulls no longer than a market barge, hanging over the inner rings by the same trick. He watched one settle toward a landing tier in a series of small corrections, each one of them a man's entire attention, and he had the shape of it before he could talk himself out of the read.

The stones did not hold. They were held.

Somebody stood in every one of those hulls pouring himself into the lift a breath at a time, because the moment the pouring stopped the stone would remember it was a rock and behave like one. A carriage spring does its job whether or not anyone believes in it. These did theirs exactly as long as a living man kept deciding they would and not one second past the point his mana ran dry, and every acolyte in this city knew what running a stone dry does to the man doing the running.

That was why you only saw them here. Not the rarity of the stones, though the stones were rare.

Because only the capital could afford to spend people at this rate, and only the capital had a story polished enough to make the spending read as an honor instead of a fare paid in years off a man's life.

He had signed that contract himself once, in a waiting room that smelled like new carpet, and they had called it a trial and watched his vitals right up until the day it killed him.

He looked away from the ships.

"Goodbye, sweet Randillia City," he said, and heard the sadness in it, and was not sure who it belonged to.

The roads degraded on a schedule.

Smooth inside the walls, and that was before you accounted for what was bolted under the axles. Cobble past the gates, not terrible. After that they stopped pretending to be roads at all and the stones started earning their keep every hundred yards.

He noticed that every wonder he had admired this morning stopped at the same distance from the same wall. None of it could be packed. None of it could be sold, or taught, or carried out through a gate.

A gravity stone could be carried. He noticed the exception and turned it over and found that it was not an exception at all, and that the difference between the two was the whole of the thing.

The stone under the axle did its work at a fixed threshold that some carver had cut into it once, a long time ago, and had then walked away from and probably died. It went on doing it in the dark, for anybody, forever, whether or not a single living person understood how.

The stone in the hull did its work exactly as long as a man stood over it and fed it, and stopped the moment he did not.

One of those is a machine. The other is a job.

This kingdom had built every one of its wonders out of the second kind. That was why they all ended at the same wall. A job cannot be shipped, and the man doing it cannot be in two places, and a hundred years of the finest minds available had gone into making the arrangement beautiful rather than into making it unnecessary.

He had the observation. He did not have the doctrine. It simply sat badly in him for an hour and then went quiet.

Kaldo was twelve days out.

On day two he rode up front beside Ward for the pleasure of it, and because sitting in a box with his own head had stopped being restful.

"Rank the gifts."

"Your Highness."

"Best to worst. Go."

Ward considered the road.

"The coat was good."

"And?"

"That is the ranking."

"You've given me a list of one item and called it a ranking," Emeric said. "That's not a ranking. That's a survivor."

Ward said nothing at all, which he had learned over fifteen years to read as the man's version of a laugh.

"There has to be a second item."

"There does not."

"Corbin gave me seven crystals. Seven. That is a volume of gift. Volume counts for something."

"They do not do anything."

"Neither does a ranking with one item in it, and here we both are, discussing it."

Rylan laughed from the box behind them and then arranged his face when Ward looked round. Osric said something about the wooden soldiers, low, to Declan, that Emeric heard perfectly well and pretended not to and enjoyed enormously.

"New order," Emeric said. "Lie to me. Fifteen years, and I want one untrue thing said about me. A compliment. Anything. As your prince."

Ward thought about it for long enough that a lesser man would have taken offense.

"You are not the worst prince I have guarded."

"How many princes have you guarded?"

"One."

The laugh came out of him sideways and surprised them both. It was the first real one in two days and it hurt somewhere under the ribs the way laughing does when you have been holding something very carefully in that exact spot.

When it had gone he said the other thing, because it had been sitting there since the courtyard.

"I'm sorry about the posting. Whole career, and they send you south with the family embarrassment."

"I requested it."

Emeric turned his head.

"Did you."

"It was granted the same hour."

He decoded it instantly and wished he had not.

A transfer request goes to a captain. The captain takes it to a quartermaster. The quartermaster loses it under a pile for a week and finds it again when somebody asks twice. That is the whole machine and it has never once run faster than that for anybody.

Same hour meant that somebody with authority had been waiting for the request.

Or had arranged for it to be made.

What he could not tell was which, and therefore whether the man riding beside him had volunteered or had been aimed.

He did not ask. He worked out, in about four seconds, that if Ward had been aimed then Ward did not know it, and that asking would take something from him and return nothing to anybody.

"You're an idiot," he said, "and I'm glad."

Ward accepted that the way he accepted weather.

They rode on, and Emeric sat with a fact about the man's life that the man did not have, and noticed that it was the first time he had ever done that to him, and noticed, further, how little effort it had taken.
