Chapter 5 - Root Causes

The room was cold again. It had been cold every night since he arrived. Whatever the kitchen had sent up sat at his elbow going cold faster than he was eating it, and he didn't notice.

He had a quill, ink, a blank page, and three days of unsorted input finally ready to become something with a shape. The ink was bad and the paper was worse. He used them anyway, and worked top to bottom the way he'd have worked a punch list on a job site. Root causes first, symptoms filed underneath them instead of promoted into problems of their own.

The water went at the top. It mattered most and it was the only one he already had a plan for. Waterborne, almost certainly bacterial, and it took no villain at all to explain it. A burial ground uphill of the wells. Latrines dug close to the bank by people who had never had a reason to lay those two facts side by side. Active. Ongoing. Killing somebody this month. Everything else on the page could wait longer than this could.

Under it, not a problem so much as a permanent condition he would be building around. The town had lost more than half its people, first to war and then to plague, which was two separate failures stacked rather than one large one. Whatever he did from here he would be doing with fewer hands than the job needed, and no amount of clever arrangement put that number back up.

Wexler went on the page, and he was careful with the wording even writing it only for himself. Disengaged, at minimum. Hadn't ridden out. Corrected him out of spite rather than courtesy. Showed no flicker of interest in his own barony's condition across the one real conversation they'd had.

All of that was consistent with apathy. It was also consistent with something worse, and he had no evidence for worse, so he wrote down apathy and made himself leave it. Acting on a theory he hadn't earned was the standard route by which competent men became cautionary tales.

The fallow fields went down as an open question rather than a conclusion. Could be the labor shortage explaining itself. Could be something else. The idle mill went beside it for the same reason, noticed and not understood, and he was careful not to hand those two a shared cause simply because they happened to be broken in the same town.

Last was the castle. Rope where a chain should be, wall damage hidden instead of repaired, one steward doing the work of three. He almost left it off. It wasn't a cause of anything. It was what every item above it looked like from outside, and fixing those would fix this without him lifting a finger toward it. The ledgers would tell him faster, and he doubted he'd get those out of the baron without a fight.

Six problems. Exactly one with a next step he could take in the morning. That was fine. That was how it always started.

He sent the maids past the barony's edge for the water, up beyond the burial ground and the latrine trenches to a source running clean of everything the settlement put downhill. The extra walk annoyed them. Hauling water that far with a river at the bottom of the slope struck them as backward in a way that had nothing to do with distance, and he was still a prince, so telling them it would make sense later got them moving.

He was not going to walk into a stranger's house and ask them to boil water he hadn't been boiling himself. Everything that came up to his rooms went over a fire first, the same as he'd be asking of them, and if he turned out to be wrong about any of it he would find out before anybody else did.

The boiling on top of the clean source wasn't a lack of faith in the source. He didn't know what else the ground up there carried, and he would rather burn extra wood than gamble. He wasn't trying to prove the source was bad. He was trying to leave nothing to chance in the one household he could actually control.

Bram was already at the cart when they came for him, arms folded, wearing the expression of a man who would rather be at his forge. Rylan fell in on foot with one hand loose near his belt out of habit rather than expectation, asked nothing, and took the shorter route past the mill without being told.

The parish house smelled of tallow and old paper. Father Doran was somewhere past sixty, careful with his hands, and polite in the specific way people are polite to nobility they don't trust yet.

Emeric learned the name before he asked for anything, and paid attention to the man attached to it, because a priest in a barony this far out still answered up a chain that ended at a cardinal who had blessed Emeric out of a room six weeks ago and meant every word of it as a diagnosis. Whatever Doran wrote in a letter, somebody eventually read.

Doran was reluctant to hand over anything at all until Bram said something low and local that Emeric didn't catch. Whatever it was, it worked. They carried four books out between them. Rylan took the heaviest without being asked.

Back at the smithy Emeric cleared a table and started stacking pages by year, then by month, then by something the other two couldn't see yet. Names, ages, cause where a cause had been written down, and beside every entry a location. Which well. Which street. Which row of the burial ground.

"You're not reading it," Bram said, watching him move faster than reading allowed. "You're sorting it."

"Same thing, further out." Emeric didn't look up. "One death near the north well means nothing. Six in one month, all on the same side of it, means something."

Rylan leaned over the table and stayed quiet a while. Then he put a finger on a line near the bottom of a page from three years back.

"That's my aunt's row." Flat, no weight on it, though his hand stayed on the page a second longer than it needed to. "Same well."

Emeric marked it and kept going and said nothing about the pause. The pattern would say it for him soon enough, and it would say it to a hundred people who had lost somebody the same way.

By the time the candle burned low he had the beginning of a map on cheap paper. Dots for deaths, clustered thick around the north well, thinning toward the east side of town where the water ran higher and cleaner.

Not proof. A shape.

"That's it?" Bram said, looking at the scatter of ink. "That's your evidence?"

"That's the start of it." Emeric sat back. "Now I go find the households near the thick part of that map and ask them to try something different for a season. Not everyone. Enough."

He took the map to Imani before he took it anywhere else, because she was the only person in Kaldo who could tell him what wasn't on it. She looked at it a long while without touching it.

"Your dots are thin on Weir Row." She tapped the paper, three houses east of the north well. "Four there the summer before last. None of them are in the priest's book. None of them had anybody left to pay the burying."

"Names?"

She gave him eleven, across five years, from memory, in the order they had died. Emeric wrote all of them down and marked them and watched his own map get worse, which meant it was getting truer.

"It's still the ground," she said.

"It's the water sitting in the ground."

"That's the same sentence with a word moved."

"That's the whole difference." He set the quill down. "You can't move the ground. You can boil the water."

Imani didn't answer for a while.

"Five whole years I've been telling this town it's the ground." She pushed the map back across to him. "If you're wrong, you've cost them a winter of firewood. If you're right, I've cost them five years." She got the door for him. "So be sure. Then come and get me, and I'll say it standing next to you."

They went house to house with it, and doors opened that would never have opened for a prince, because Imani was the one knocking. He kept it plain and gave no lecture. The water near the burial ground wasn't safe and hadn't been for a long time. The sickness that kept coming back was not a curse on the town, it was an ugly little problem that had gone unnoticed for decades. He showed them where the clean water was, said very little else, and left it there.

He couldn't force anyone to change how they lived. That was how riots started, and it wasn't a road he had any interest in walking.

It took a fortnight to know anything, and what he learned wasn't clean.

The Hallards, three doors down from the north well, switched without complaint and boiled every cup before it touched a mouth, and nobody in that house so much as coughed wrong the whole two weeks. The widow Corwin tried it four days, decided the walk to the clean well wasn't worth the ache in her knees, and went back to the water she had always used. Her youngest ran a fever by the end of the week, mild and survivable, but a fever all the same. Old Pell refused outright, told Rylan to his face he had drunk from that well sixty years and wasn't about to let a boy prince tell him different, and nothing whatsoever happened to him, which did more damage to the case on that street than any failure could have. The Talbrooks split it down the middle, boiled for the children and not for themselves, and it was the parents who got sick.

No clean line anywhere in it. Nothing a determined skeptic couldn't put a hole through. But laid next to the dots on the ledger map there was enough for anyone looking carefully. Boil the water and the odds moved. They didn't move for everyone, and some who ignored every word walked away fine, and the odds still moved.

Emeric logged each result the way he would have logged test data that refused to behave. Not proof the method had failed. Proof it was real.

Imani brought him most of it herself, house by house, in the order she had walked them. She read the Talbrooks off her list last, the parents sick and the children not, and then stood in Bram's back room saying nothing for a length of time Emeric had the sense not to interrupt.

"Boiled for the little ones and not themselves," she said eventually. "I've watched that house five years. I'd have told you the parents were the strong ones."

"You'd have been right. That's what makes it evidence."

"I know what makes it evidence." She folded the list and put it away. "I'm deciding what to do about having been wrong for five years, and I'd rather not do it in front of you."

She was at the north well the next morning telling people to boil it, which was the only answer she ever gave him.

The holdouts didn't come round all at once. They came round one bad week at a time, one neighbor's fever weighed against another neighbor's dry summer, until boiling water before it touched a mouth was simply what people did on that end of town. Old Pell was still drinking straight from the well the last anyone checked, daring the rot to try him again. Nobody was betting on him changing his mind. He was the exception people talked about now, though, and not the rule.

It took the better part of six weeks from the night of the list to the morning Emeric stopped hearing arguments about it, and in that time he had gone into the room two doors down from his own exactly once.

Petram had lost weight and gained a stillness, and had answered every question put to him with the flat courtesy of a man who has decided what he is going to say and has had five weeks to rehearse it. He had been approached in the capital. He had never seen a face. The money came through a man who came through another man. He said all of it without hesitation, and Emeric came out of the room and stood in the corridor working out how much of it was true and arrived at most of it, which was the worst available answer, because the useful part of a story like that is never the part that gets rehearsed.

He went back twice more that month and got nothing new, and kept going anyway, which Ward noticed and did not comment on, and which Emeric decided to count as approval.

By the time Bram invited him back for a drink, the argument about the water had mostly made itself.

The smithy was closed for the evening so Bram brought him home instead. From outside the house looked like every other house on the row. The upkeep showed the moment he was through the door. Living with a blacksmith clearly had its advantages.

Bram's daughter poured him something herbal without a word and went back into the house before he could thank her.

"I plan on solving this town's problems while I'm here, Bram." The look on his face did most of the work before the words caught up. "I'm going to need a lot of help. If we're getting the town out of the state it's in, we need the town on board and not watching from a distance."

"Easier said." Bram sipped without looking up. "Hope's costly here. Nobody's willing to pay for it twice."

"The water I can handle. I think I already have. The fields are going to be a bigger problem." Emeric turned the cup a quarter turn on his knee. "I can grow that field myself. There's enough in me to do it. But what happens the day I'm not here to do it again? Growth magic needs a steady hand behind it, and if that hand's mine, it's mine instead of everything else this barony needs it for."

He set the cup down.

"There's something else I want to ask you about, and you can tell me it's not my business. Imani says she sent word to the capital three times the first year of the plague. Through the castle, because that's the road it goes down. Nobody came. Nobody wrote back to say why."

Bram was quiet for a moment.

"You're asking whether Wexler burned them."

"I'm asking what you know."

"I know his rider went out. Twice that first year I saw the man take the east road with a satchel, and I know what a courier's satchel looks like because I put the buckle on it." Bram turned his cup. "Third time he went himself. Gone eleven days. Came back and didn't say a word about it to anybody, and the cordon stayed up, and no one came."

Emeric sat with that longer than he meant to, because it took the shape of his own suspicion and turned it inside out.

He had been carrying a theory around for six weeks in which a lazy, contemptuous baron sat on a dying town's letters. What he had instead was a man who had sent them, and then gone himself, and then come back and stopped talking. That was not apathy. Apathy doesn't ride eleven days.

Which left the other end of the road, and the other end of the road was a building he had recently been thrown out of.

Nobody wrote back to say why. Somebody in the capital had received word that a barony was dying and had made a decision about it, and the decision had been silence, and silence of that kind requires a person, a desk, and a reason.

He wrote nothing down about it that night. He had learned in the last six weeks what happened to conclusions he reached early.

"Fixing the fields properly isn't the hard part," he said instead. "I know ways to get more out of a harvest than anyone here's used to seeing. Faster growth, bigger yield, ways to collect and store it that don't rot half of it before winter. All of that needs metal. And it needs a smith."

Bram's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "Are you trying to hire me, boy?"

"You and your workers. Exclusive, long term. The whole barony's smithing, if you'll take it."

"Tell me more of these ideas and I'll think about it."

"All right. This is how farming actually works."

He spent the rest of the evening explaining how a plant grows, root to leaf, in terms he hoped would land without sounding like something out of a heretic's mouth. He skipped everything he couldn't prove to a man with no way to check it. Nitrogen. Photosynthesis. The invisible machinery running underneath every green thing Bram had ever grown or sold metal to somebody else for growing.

What he kept was what he could show. Rotate what you plant so the soil doesn't get bled of the same thing every season. Feed it what it's missing instead of guessing. Water it on a schedule instead of whenever somebody remembers. None of it needed magic. The magic was only going to make it faster and bigger and less dependent on however many hands the war and the plague had left standing.

Bram listened without interrupting, which Emeric had already learned was as close to enthusiasm as the man came.

Somewhere in the middle of the part about feeding the soil what it was missing, the clatter of a pot in the back of the house stopped, and did not start again for some time.

"You talk like you've already built this in your head," Bram said, when it was done.

"I have. I don't have the hands or the metal to build it. That's what I'm asking you for."

"I'll think on it," Bram said, which from him Emeric was starting to understand was not a no.

"You haven't introduced me to your family."

"It's me and my daughter. Ferra. Wife went in the plague, two years back. Been the two of us since."

Emeric had a condolence half assembled and heard, before it got out, exactly what it would sound like coming from the man who had spent the evening explaining that the plague had been a solvable problem the whole time.

"I'm sorry," he said. "That's all. I'm not going to make it a lesson."

Bram looked at him for a second, and then hauled the mood back the way he hauled everything back.

"I know my daughter's fine looking. You can't have her."

"I'd have to get through her first and you second, and I've seen your arms."

In the back of the house, one pot met another pot with rather more force than the task required.

Emeric decided he had heard nothing at all, and by the look on Bram's face that was the correct decision.

He walked back up to the castle in the dark with Ward somewhere behind him, which by now was less a security measure than a ritual neither of them mentioned. Wexler was standing in the entry hall.

"Hello, Emeric."

Flat, with everything that might have been under it packed away. Anyone below a prince used the title before the name. Emeric didn't care about the pleasantry itself, being half a man from a world that had never had one, but the baron knew the custom and was choosing not to follow it, and choosing was the whole content of the sentence.

He also couldn't call it out. Bad blood in a town where most people would happily cast him as the villain was not a purchase he could afford.

"Hello, Wexler. What can I help you with?"

"I hear you've been making trips around my barony." Snide, barely bothering to hide it. "Is everything all right?"

Emeric saw no reason to lie. The baron knew where he'd been and when. No ruler lets a man with any measure of power roam his land untethered, and while Emeric doubted anyone had followed them, since Ward would have dealt with that quietly, a man's steps can be traced afterward by anyone willing to ask. People gave up information for pleasure, payment, or pain, and Kaldo was cheap.

He gestured for the baron to follow him into a side room rather than answer in the hall.

The move landed. Nothing showed on Wexler's face, but something happened behind it, and Emeric had a fair idea what it was, because moving a conversation from one room to another in your own house is a privilege, and he had just taken it without asking.

He laid out what he had been doing. Wexler listened without expression.

"Six weeks," Wexler said. "You have been in my barony six weeks and you have spent them counting corpses."

"Water sources, burial sites, dates of first symptoms, cross-referenced against household distance from the river." Emeric said it the way he'd have said it to Bram, or to himself, without performing it. "The wells nearest the latrine outflow correlate with the earliest and worst cases. That isn't proof. It's a pattern. I've spent a fortnight testing it on households who agreed to be tested, and the odds moved."

Something crossed behind Wexler's eyes, small, and gone before it registered.

"And what will you do with it?"

"Put the latrines and the burial ground downstream of the wells, or move the wells above both. Whichever's cheaper to dig. Boil in the meantime, and tell people why instead of only telling them to." He shrugged, because to him it was obvious. "It doesn't take without somebody they already trust. I can hand a stranger a theory and get nothing. Imani hands them the same theory and it's done by dinner."

Wexler stayed quiet a moment too long.

"Bram doesn't take to strangers."

"He didn't take to me either." Emeric didn't dress it. "I gave him something worth taking to."

The disdain was gone. That lazy performance of the minimum the man had worn since the first afternoon wasn't there, and what replaced it wasn't respect. It was the stillness of a man recalculating a threat he had priced too cheap.

Six weeks was not the impressive part and both of them knew it. Six weeks was the number of weeks Wexler had spent watching, deciding this would burn itself out the way everything did here. What had brought him down to the entry hall was a woman at the north well telling her neighbors to boil their water, and a blacksmith who had gone with the prince to the parish house in front of the whole street.

"Get some rest, Your Highness." Wexler stood, which he hadn't bothered to do before. "You've had a long day."

It wasn't a dismissal. It was a man buying time to think.

Emeric went up the stairs past the room two doors from his own, where Petram was awake in the dark rehearsing a story that was mostly true, and did not stop at the door, because he had a better question to ask now and he wanted to have it properly sharpened first.

Who in the capital reads the letters from a dying town, and decides not to answer them?
