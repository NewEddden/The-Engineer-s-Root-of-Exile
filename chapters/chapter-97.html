Chapter 97 - Steam at the Mill

The engine house went up against the mill's south wall in the last three weeks of October, in stone, by the same masons who had keyed the aqueduct's arch in June, and it looked like a chapel somebody had forgotten to finish.

It was tall and narrow, because the beam had to rock inside it. It had one great arched window on the river side for the light, and a chimney of tithe-house brick going up beside it higher than anything in Kaldo but the keep, and a door at the end wide enough for a cart. The masons did not like it. They said it was the wrong shape for a house and the wrong shape for a church and the wrong shape for a barn, and asked what it was for, and when Emeric told them they went away and argued about it and came back the next morning and built it anyway, very well.

The boiler came down from bay three on rollers, behind four of Hollis's oxen, with Hollis walking at their heads complaining about his oxen the whole length of the lower ward. It took a day. The whole of the lower ward came out to watch the long iron drum go by on its rollers, twenty feet of it, with the smallest yard boy sitting on top of it waving, and Hollis said that if it went into the canal he was not getting it out, and it did not go into the canal.

Then the rest, a piece at a time.

The cylinder, standing upright on its end on a bed of stone at one side of the engine house, bolted down through its flange. The beam over it, a single great oak from the baron's woods, squared and strapped with iron, rocking on an iron pin on top of a wall that ran across the middle of the house, so that one end of it was over the cylinder and the other end over the flywheel pit. The piston rod coming up out of the cylinder to the near end of the beam. The connecting rod coming down from the far end of the beam to a crank on the flywheel's shaft. The flywheel, cast in eight pieces in the cupola and bolted together in the pit, taller than two men. And beside the cylinder, standing in a stone cistern that the mill race ran through, the condenser, where the spent steam would go to be cooled back into water, and the air pump, a smaller pump worked off the beam, whose whole work was to draw that water out of the condenser again, and the air with it, so that the hole the steam left behind stayed empty.

Bram fitted the governor last.

It was the two iron balls on their arms that he had called a toy in September, spinning on a spindle above the steam pipe, driven by a belt off the flywheel's shaft. He had made it in a week and polished the balls until they shone, which he did to nothing else, and he fitted it on the last afternoon with Tess watching from her chair and nobody else in the house, and when it was on he stood back and looked at it.

"Said I'd make it last," he said.

Tess wrote it down.



They raised steam for the first time on the first Tuesday of November, on a morning so cold that the race had a skin of ice at its edges.

Haddon had laid the fire the night before and slept beside it on a sack. Coal, from Grayrock, a bed of it the whole length of the fire tube, banked and waiting. At first light he lit it, and stood at the firebox door with the door half open and his face orange, and said its colour out loud, as he did everything, to nobody.

A cold boiler with twenty feet of cold water in it took a long time to make steam. Three hours. Four, on a morning like this. Emeric had known that, and had planned for it.

Mott came down from the still at six with a basket.

It was an ordinary basket, the kind the still crew carried the barony's fire stones in from Armand's room to the still and back, and in it were a dozen of them, charged the day before, red at the heart and warm through the wicker. Mott carried it as he carried everything, in his bare grey hands, without hurry. He knelt at the firebox door beside Haddon and laid the stones in among the coal along the fire tube one at a time, as far in as his arm would reach, and then went back to the still without a word.

The stones did what they did. They gave up their heat all at once, the way a charged stone did when it was let go, and the coal round them caught from them and went to white, and the fire tube went from cold to hot in a quarter of an hour, and the water round it began to tick, and then to talk, and then to roar softly to itself inside the drum.

Steam was up in an hour.

Haddon opened the try-cocks. The top one gave a hiss of white steam. The middle one gave steam and spitting water together. The bottom one gave water, hot and clear. He shut them and looked at Emeric with his eyebrows up, and Emeric nodded, and Haddon grinned, the first time anybody had seen him do it.

"Coal does the work," he said. "The stones only wake her up."

That was exactly the order Emeric had wanted, and he did not say so. The coal was the engine. Coal burned in a dead zone and came down the river by the barge and did not need a mage to fill it. The stones were a way of not standing in the cold for four hours, on mornings when there was a mage in Kaldo to charge them. If there were no stones the engine would still run. It would only start later.



The whistle was Emeric's own idea, and he regretted it at once.

It was a brass cup on a stem on top of the drum, with a lever, and a slot cut round the rim of the cup so that steam let up the stem came out through the slot and across a sharp brass lip and screamed. A steam whistle. Haddon was to blow it if the water went low or the fire went wrong or anything happened that he did not understand, and somebody would come. It was the first thing on the engine that Haddon was allowed to touch without asking.

So he blew it, at a little after seven, to see.

The noise went out of the engine house and down the race and across the lower ward and over the wall and up the hill and into every house in Kaldo at once, a long high tearing scream, like a pig the size of the keep being killed slowly, and went on for as long as Haddon held the lever, which was some time, because Haddon had gone white and could not seem to let go of it.

When it stopped, the dogs went on.

The lower ward came out. All of it, in a body, in its shirts and its aprons and with its breakfasts in its hands, down the lanes to the race, to see what had died. The school came out, Sela behind it. The garrison came out of the guardroom with its crossbows. And down the hill from the church, in his old cassock, with his skirts held up in both fists, at a speed nobody in Kaldo had ever seen him use, came Doran, and stopped at the engine house door, red in the face and breathing hard, and looked at the whistle on top of the drum with the last of the steam drifting off its lip.

"I thought," Doran said, and stopped, and did not say what he had thought. He looked at the whistle a while longer. Then he looked at Emeric, and seemed about to say something about it, and decided against it, and went back up the hill, a good deal more slowly.

Hollis came last, because he had been in his byre, and stood in the door with a bucket.

"That'll sour the milk," he said.

"It might," said Ambrose, who had come running from the tithe houses with his coat on inside out. He looked at the whistle with great interest. "It might very well. A shock like that, through the cow. It's not unlike lightning, when you think of it."

Hollis looked at Ambrose. It was the first time in their acquaintance that Ambrose had agreed with him about anything, and he plainly did not know what to do with it.

"It's not like lightning," he said, at last, as if betrayed.

Bram settled it. He came out of the engine house with his hands behind his back and looked at the whole of the lower ward standing in the frost in its shirts, and said, in his loud flat voice, that from tomorrow the whistle would go once, at first light, short, and anybody who worked at the mill or the works or the powerhouse who was not at his bench by the end of it was late, and went back in. Nobody argued. It seemed, on the whole, a better use for it than what it had just been used for.



The test was at noon, and nobody needed it.

That was why Emeric had chosen the day. The October rain had brought the river up. The wheel was turning the mill's shaft and the dynamo off the race as well as it ever had, and all eight lamps on the wall had been lit every night for a month, and if the engine did nothing at all that day, nobody in Kaldo would have been an hour worse off. It was the right day to find out what it would do.

Bram knocked the wheel out of gear. There was an iron clutch on the main shaft where the wheel's drive came in, a pair of toothed collars that a lever slid apart, and he leaned on the lever and the collars came apart with a clank, and the wheel went on turning outside in the race, uselessly, with nothing on it, and the shaft inside slowed, and stopped.

Then he knocked the engine into gear on the other side, and Haddon opened the steam.

The piston went up. Emeric could not see it go; it was inside the cylinder. He saw the end of the beam over the cylinder rise, slow, heavy, the whole great oak of it tilting on its pin like a man getting up out of a chair, and the far end go down, and the connecting rod go down with it, and the crank go round, and the flywheel turn. A quarter turn. A half. And then the valve gear Bram had made changed over with a clack and let the steam in on the other side of the piston and out of the first side into the condenser, where it vanished, and the beam came down again, harder, because it had the steam pushing on one side and the empty hole pulling on the other, and the flywheel went on round.

And round. And the beam rose and fell, and rose and fell, slow and heavy and steady, with a long sighing breath out of the condenser at every stroke, and the flywheel came up to speed and the shaft turned, and up on its spindle the governor's two shining balls began to spin and to fly outward on their arms as the engine went faster, and as they flew out they drew down a little rod that closed the steam a little, and the engine slowed a little, and the balls came in a little, and opened the steam a little, and they found a place between the two and stayed there, spinning, and the engine ran at the speed Emeric had drawn in September and did not go faster or slower.

Bram watched the governor while the flywheel went round a hundred times.

"Toy," he said, at last, very quietly, in a tone Emeric had never heard him use for a toy.



Imani came in the afternoon, because Tess had asked her to.

Tess had not said why. She had sent a message up to the ward by Arthur that said only that the engine was running and that Imani should come and see it, and Imani had come, in her apron, with her sleeves rolled, in the middle of a day she had no time in, which Emeric understood was the reason Tess had asked. Imani had signed her out of the ward in March against her own judgment. She had never yet seen anything Tess had made work.

She stood in the engine house and looked at all of it, the beam and the flywheel and the governor spinning, the way she looked at a body on her table, from one end to the other, missing nothing, and asked Tess three questions about the condenser that Tess answered with great care. Then she went and stood by the drum, close, with one hand on the brick of its bed, and looked up at the safety valve on the top of it. At the lever. At the weight on the end of the lever, and the little wire through it, and the flat grey lead seal on the wire with the House's mark struck into it.

"Vantic's," she said.

"Vantic's."

Imani looked at the lead for a while, and the corner of her mouth went in, as though she had found something in a patient's history that she had not expected and rather approved of.

Haddon had the fire high. He had had it high since noon, because the engine was running and the whole of Kaldo had been in to look at it and he was twenty and the fire was his, and he had been showing it off to everybody who came in by opening the firebox door and letting them see it white. Emeric had told him twice. He had been told by Bram once, which should have been enough.

Emeric was at the try-cocks. He had opened the top one, and the steam had come out of it harder than it ought, and he had shut it, and looked up at the lever on the safety valve over Imani's head, and seen the lever stir.

Not lift. Stir. A hair. A thread of steam came out from under the brass plug where it sat in its seat, thin as a breath, and the lever settled again, and stirred again. He knew what that was. He had set that weight himself, and Vantic had sealed it there, and he knew to a stroke of Bram's pump what the drum was carrying when that lever began to stir. The plug would lift in a few breaths, all at once, and the steam would come out of the top of the drum with nothing but the air above it, a yard high, and come down again as scalding rain on whoever was standing at the side of the drum with her face turned up to read a lead seal.

He crossed the engine house in three steps and took her by the elbow and moved her.

One step. Away from the drum and back, with his hand on her arm just above the elbow, on the rolled cuff of her sleeve. He had decided to do it before he moved, and he did it exactly, and let go.

The valve lifted.

It went off over their heads like the whistle's big brother, a deep shaking roar, and a column of white steam stood up off the top of the drum to the beam and spread there and came down, hissing, in a fine hot rain across the place where Imani had been standing, and spattered on the brick of the bed, and steamed. Haddon, at the firebox, slammed the door and opened the damper and stood back with his hands up as though somebody had drawn a sword on him. The lever came down. The roar stopped. The beam went on rising and falling as if nothing had happened, because nothing had, to the beam.

Imani had not moved since he let go of her. She looked at the wet brick where she had been standing. Then she looked at her arm, where his hand had been, and then at him.

She had let him. He had felt her let him: there had been a quarter of a breath, as his hand closed on her arm, when everything in her had set itself to pull away, and then had not.

"Do you want to know why I let you?" she said.

He did not say anything. She did not seem to need him to.

"You looked at the lever first," Imani said. "Then at me." She unrolled her sleeve down over her arm, where his hand had been, and buttoned the cuff. "If you'd looked at me first, I'd have broken your wrist."

She went to Haddon at the firebox, and took his hands, both of them, and turned them over and looked at the backs, where the fire had been at him all day, and told him that he would put grease on them tonight or she would come down and do it herself in front of everyone, and went back up the hill to the ward.

Haddon stood looking at the lever on top of the drum, and the lead seal on its wire, for a while after she had gone. Emeric watched him look at it. He watched him work out what the lever was for, and what was holding it down, and what the lead meant, and then decide something, and turn away, and shut the damper another notch.



Tess built the indicator in a week.

He had given her one sentence for it, on the first evening, in the engine house, with the beam going up and down over their heads. If you could draw what the steam does inside the cylinder through one whole stroke, he had said, you'd know what the engine's worth.

That was all. She had looked at the cylinder, and then at him, and then at the cylinder, and written the sentence down, and the next morning she had Bram turning a little brass cylinder on the lathe the size of a man's thumb.

What she made screwed onto a cock in the side of the big cylinder. Steam from inside came up into the little one and pushed a little piston up against a spring, harder when the pressure was high, less when it was low. On top of the little piston's rod was a pencil. Beside the pencil was a drum of brass with a card wrapped round it, and a cord from the drum to the beam, so that as the beam went up the drum turned one way under the pencil and as the beam came down it turned back. The pencil went up and down with the steam. The card went to and fro with the stroke. And between them, on the card, in one stroke, the pencil drew a shape.

She showed him the first one on the Monday. It was a loop. A fat lopsided loop, high and square along the top where the steam came in and pushed, sloping down where it was cut off and ran on by itself, dropping hard along the bottom where the condenser pulled, and back up the side. It was the most beautiful thing he had seen in Kaldo, and it was an indicator card, and he had never once in his other life expected to see one drawn by hand by a girl in a wheeled chair who had never been told that it was a thing anybody had done before.

"The space inside it," Tess said. "That's the work, isn't it. One stroke's worth."

"That's the work."

"Then how much is it?"

He told her how it was reckoned on his other world, though not where. A horse. What a strong horse could do in a minute, steadily, all day, at a mill gin, which was to lift a hundredweight about three hundred feet. Or three hundredweight a hundred feet. The same work.

She took the card and the cylinder's size and the strokes a minute, which she had counted with Bram's sand-glass, and went to the end of the bench and did it on her slate. It took her most of the afternoon. Bram, Haddon and Hollis, who had come to complain about the whistle and stayed, stood about and did not help, because none of them could have.

"Fourteen," she said, at last.

"Fourteen what?" said Bram.

"Horses."

Hollis looked at the beam going up and down.

"There's not a horse in Kaldo," he said, "could lift a hundredweight three hundred feet in a minute."

"No," said Tess, writing. "That was rather my point."



The saw went on that week too.

It hung in the mill's lower floor, beside the old grindstones, where a belt off the flywheel's shaft came through a slot in the engine house wall and turned a crank, and the crank drove a frame up and down in a pair of oak guides, and in the frame was the blade, stretched tight, with its teeth down. A sash saw, the old up-and-down kind that mills on his other world had run off water wheels for six hundred years. A log went onto a carriage under it and was pushed against the blade a little at each stroke by a ratchet, and the blade went up and down, and the log went through.

The first plank came off it at a little after noon on the Thursday, oak, from the baron's woods, the length of three men, straight as a rule.

Bram ran his hand along it. Then he stood it against the wall with the others that came after it, and by evening there were twenty, which was more than four men with a pit saw would have cut in a week. They were for the shed on the far bank. There was no shed on the far bank yet. There was a slope of mud and willow on the far bank above the east bridge, where Emeric had promised Fletcher in February a slip under a roof before next winter was out, and these were the first boards of the roof.



The night they ran the lamps on steam alone was the Saturday.

There was no need for it. The river was high, and the wheel could have done it as well as it had done it every night for a month. That was the point, again. Bram knocked the wheel out of gear at dusk and knocked the engine in, and the dynamo turned on steam and nothing else, and down in the dynamo house Tess sat at her cut-outs in her chair with the lamps' circuits laid out before her on the board, each one on its own copper switch, and put them in one at a time.

The South Gate first. Emeric went out to the door of the engine house to watch it come up, white, on the wall a long way off, and went back in. The governor's balls had dropped in a little as the lamp took its load, and opened the steam a little, and the engine had leaned into it and held its speed. The river tower. The balls dropped in a little more. The gate tower. The bridge corner. The lower ward, three of them.

Seven. The beam went up and down a little heavier, with a longer breath out of the condenser at every stroke. Haddon, at the firebox, fed it without being told.

Bram had gone out to the race while the lamps were coming up. He came back in with two buckets of water, one in each hand, full to the brim, and went past the engine, into the mill, through the door into the old back room where the dynamo had first been set up, the room Harl had sat in every night through the winter of the dynamo, on his stool by the door, with a bucket on each side of him in case of fire.

The stool was still there. Nobody had moved it. It stood by the door where it had always stood, with the dust on its seat that nobody had been allowed to wipe off.

Bram set the buckets down, one on each side of it. He did it carefully, so that they did not slop. Then he stood beside it with his hands at his sides and did not say anything.

Tess put in the eighth.

It came up on the far side of the wall, over the narrow walk above the lower ward, the last of them, and from the back room's window Emeric could see it come, white and steady, at the end of the long curve of the wall, eight lights in a row in the dark above the town, burning on coal and water, with the river going past beneath them doing nothing at all.

Bram looked at the stool for a while. At the two buckets.

"You'd have hated it," he said to it. "The smoke."

And he went out to look at the wall.

Ambrose was in the engine house door when Emeric came back through. He had been there since dusk, with his coat on the right way out this time, and had said nothing the whole evening, which nobody who knew him would have believed. He was standing in the door with his back against the jamb, looking out across the race at the eight lamps on the wall, burning steady, all of them, not flickering and not failing and not bursting, the thing he had told every hall and every tower he had ever stood in that a man might do anything with, if he could only make it keep running.

He did not say anything now either. His mouth was a little open.

Tess had come through from the dynamo house in her chair as soon as the eighth was in. She was at the cylinder with a fresh card on the indicator's drum and the pencil on the card, taking the loop again under the load of eight lamps, to see how much fatter it had got. She did not look up.
