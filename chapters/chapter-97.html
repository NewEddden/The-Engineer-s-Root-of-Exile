Chapter 97 - Lift Points

The spare section came up from the lower water in the second week of the thaw, lashed on the steamboat's deck under a tarpaulin, and nobody on the river road could have said what was under it except that it was large and square and plainly heavy.

Fletcher had been told it was going up to Kaldo to be checked against the master gauges, because it was the first piece of iron hull ever finished in four baronies and the gauges were in Kaldo and nowhere else. That was true. Jorrin, who had stoned the face of the printer's second head flat twice since midwinter and seen its lines come back sharp both times, spent the first morning on it with the rods out of the felt-lined box, and found the two station frames true to within a hair, which from a beach with no lathe on it was better than anybody had a right to expect. He wrote it down, and it went into the letter that would go back down the river with the section.

It was a ring of iron the length of a man, plated and riveted and closed at both ends with a bulkhead, like a slice cut out of a loaf. Fletcher had built it before the keel went down, to learn the joins on a piece that would never have men in it, and had got them wrong twice and right the third time. And he had written, in the summer, in a letter Emeric had read three times and never answered: It's heavier at the stations than any join needs to be. I built it as drawn.

He had. The two station frames were twice the weight a join needed, because a join was never what they were for. Every hull that came off Fletcher's beach to that drawing would have its frames at the same fixed stations, and every one of those frames would be strong enough to be lifted by. The stations had been this since the autumn before last, in Fletcher's cottage, when Emeric had talked about cutting out a holed section and riveting in a new one, which was true and useful and had never been the reason.

The stone had come down from Grayrock at the turn of the year.

It was the second raising, smaller than the first, and it had come off two faces. Hask had carried the screen on past the fork in the autumn and cut into the lower branch, forty yards further into the hill and a good deal deeper. The Crown's appointed man, a notary out of Milford who had gone down the shaft once and come up saying once was his share, had taken the fifth under seal at the pithead. Hask marked every piece with the face it came off, because he marked everything, and Ilsa copied every mark Hask made into the tally, because she copied everything.

Emeric chose four pieces of a pound each, near enough, by the king's weight, and chose them for soundness and for size. Two came off the old face and two off the new. He knew which were which. At the bench it had not mattered where a stone came from, so long as it was read.

Nevin rough-cut them and Emeric squared their faces on the hold bench, which took a set of its own, and Armand read them on three days at three hours. The two from the old face read close to each other. The two from the new face read close to each other and a long way from the first pair. He trimmed for it, the way the bench had taught him. And he put like stones on the diagonals, one pair corner to corner and the other pair the same, so that whatever small difference the reading had missed would cancel itself fore against aft and side against side. It was the kind of thing a man did when he was being careful.

Four pounds of stone, at the Crown's price, was eight Platinum Rand. Eighty Gold. It was not far short of what Fletcher was being paid for the whole of the hull on his stocks, and the section it was going to lift had cost eight. Armand wrote it in the ledger without comment, which from Armand was a comment. Four pounds, for a lift.

Bram made the housings.

He made them off the bench drawing, the one with no figures on it and two words at the foot, at the size the new stones asked for. Jorrin turned the collars. Bram cut four holes in the section's frames, two to each frame, low at the turn of the bilge where Fletcher had made the frames heaviest, round and clean and each the size of a man's fist, and seated a housing in each and bolted it through. He did not ask what they were for. He had made the first four to hold a plate the width of a hand, and he had read the foot of the drawing twice, and here they were holding a boat.

Emeric took the plan to Ferra first.

He had written it out fair on three sheets. The place, the hour, the stones and their weight and their cost, the four numbers, the weight of the section, which Fletcher's reckoning put at a little under two tons, bulkheads and all. Who would stand where, and what each of them would do at each count if it went wrong. By the two numbers in Armand's book and a winter on the bench, a pound of stone carrying half a ton would hold for about as long as a man could hold his breath. He had not planned for more. It needed to go up, and hang level with nobody holding it, and come down level when the stones were spent, and those three things would take a breath.

She read it at her table, with the lamp at her elbow, all three sheets, at the speed she read everything.

There was a clause in a document in her strongbox, the ninth of nine, that he had written in his own hand two winters ago and set in place without telling her. The Chancellor may stay any act of the holder by a stay in writing. A stay so written stands. He had never seen her reach for it. He had brought her the plan first, before Bram or Armand had seen it written down, because it was the kind of act a stay was for, and they both knew it.

She laid the third sheet on the other two.

"What happens if it drops?"

"It floats."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she picked up the first sheet again and read the whole plan through a second time from the top, the place and the hour and the stones and the counts, slowly, the way she read a column that had come out too round. When she reached the end she squared the three sheets to each other and to the edge of the table, and held them out to him.

He had expected her to ask about the eighty Gold. She had told him once, across a table of lamps, that the stone was not money but things, and she had evidently meant it.

"Then I'll come and see it float," she said, and wrote the hour in her book.

She wrote it with his pen, the one from the summer before last. The plain one from the tray of forty had gone into her drawer under the spare ink the day it arrived and, as far as he knew, had never come out. He noticed that, and did not know what to do with having noticed it, and took the plan and went down the stair.

They did it at first light, on the bank beside the outside foundry, at the upstream end, where the bulk of the foundry stood between the shelf and the island and the town could see nothing.

It was the same flat hard shelf where Armand had lifted the Wayford hull a year ago, with the same drop at its edge of about a man's height, and below the drop the shallows ran out a long way over gravel. Bram had set four oak stands in a foot of water at the foot of the drop, and in the evening, with sheerlegs rigged on the lip and the foundry sent home early, they had swayed the section off the steamboat and down onto them, so that it stood over the water with its keel plates a hand clear of the surface. Under it hung Armand's two slings, slack, with a carriage stone in its iron cup at each end.

There was mist on the water, thin, and the far water above the branch was grey and flat all the way to the lake. Far out on it there was a dark mark that might have been a log. Ward looked at it once, and then back at Emeric, and did not say anything.

There were five of them. Armand, because if it dropped he could lift it, and nobody else on that bank could lift anything heavier than a sack for longer than a breath. Bram, because the housings were his. Ward, because Ward was always there. Ferra, because she held a stay and had not used it, and had said she would come and see it float. And Emeric.

Vantic was not there. He had thought about it for most of a week, and in the end he had not asked her, and he was not proud of the reasons.

Bram took the aft line, made fast to a ring on the after bulkhead, and Ward the fore. Armand stood at the lip with his hands at his sides. Ferra stood a little back from the edge with her book shut under her arm. Emeric went to the lip above the middle of the section and stood where he could see all four housings, low in the frames, black collars on grey iron, and put his hands out.

He reached for all four at once.

In the yard and on the bench he had set stones one at a time. He could not do that here. A section set one corner at a time would try to climb at that corner with the other three still on their stands, and it had not been built to be picked up by a corner. So it was one working. Four threads at once, each to its own stone, each given its own number, the four numbers he had carried in his head for a week the way other men carried a tune. He felt each stone take its work, and the whole weight of the thing come onto them, two tons of iron, half a ton to a stone. Then he let go of all four threads in the same instant and drew nothing back.

It cost him more than the bench had, by a long way. It cost him about what a long night in the delve with six people in it cost him, all at once, and the bank tilted under his feet and came back.

The section came up off the stands a hand's width, and hung there.

It hung level. The water in the dish Bram had wedged on the forward bulkhead lay on its ring all the way round. Water ran off the tops of the four stands where the iron had sat on them and dripped back into the shallows, and there was grey morning light between the keel plates and the wet oak, a hand of it, all the way along. Nothing touched it. Nobody was feeding it. Armand stood at the lip with his hands up and open, ready, doing nothing at all, because there was nothing for them to do.

Nobody cheered. Nobody said anything. It was a slice of iron hull hanging a hand above four wet stands in the mist at the edge of a river, and it was very small, and it was very quiet. On the morning he was sent south the sky over the capital had had traffic, and every hull in it had a man standing in it, pouring himself away a breath at a time. Nobody was standing in this one. The stones under it were spending themselves as they held, and they would not hold forever the way a carriage stone did its one thing forever. But for about the length of a held breath it was the first thing in the world lifted by a machine.

Bram swore, once, quietly, which was as close as Bram came.

Ward was counting. He had counted in a gallery at Grayrock with his hand on a rope, and he counted now, under his breath, without seeming to know that he did it.

"Walk her," Emeric said.

They walked her. Bram leaned on the aft line and Ward on the fore, and the section came off the stands the way a cloud comes off a hill, slow, level, with no sound but the lines creaking, a pace, two paces, three, out over the open shallows where there was nothing under it but a foot of clear water and gravel.

He felt it go before it went.

He had the medium open and all four stones in it, dense and strange and hot, and at Ward's count of fifteen, a few breaths short of where the arithmetic said, the fore stone on the near side began to thin. It thinned the way a lamp thins at the end of its oil. And a breath after it, the stone at the other end of its diagonal, aft and far, the other one off the old face, began to thin the same way, and the two off the new face did not thin at all.

The near fore corner sank a finger's width. Then a hand's.

The section racked. Two corners went down and two did not, and a slice of hull that had been built to be held along its length was being held by two opposite corners and let go by the other two, and it twisted. There was a sound in the after frame like a board taking weight. Then the rivets went. They let go along the after frame one after another, loud as shots across the flat water, crack and crack and crack, and a heron went up off the far bank with a noise like a sheet being shaken out. The near fore corner went into the shallows.

Armand took the rest of the weight.

His hands had been up the whole time, and he took it through the slings the way he had taken the Wayford hull, and the falling side of the section stopped falling and came down the rest of the way into the water in a long slow settle instead of a drop. It settled onto the river. It floated.

It lay in a foot of water, twisted along its length, two corners low in the water and the other two riding high, because the two stones off the new face were still pushing up on their corners with nobody to tell them to stop. The after frame was split from the turn of the bilge to the second strake. There was a line of empty rivet holes along the split like the holes in a belt.

Armand sat down on the bank.

He sat the way he had sat a year ago, when his legs decided the matter for him, and stayed there with his forearms on his knees, grey, breathing hard. He had held it for a count of less than ten, and it had cost him the day. Catching was harder than lifting. A thing already falling had to be stopped before it could be let down, and he had stopped it all at once. Ferra went up the path at a walk that was very nearly not a walk, to send for Imani.

Ward was still counting. Emeric heard him go past twenty, where the arithmetic said everything should have been spent, and past forty, and on past fifty. At seventy-one the two stones off the new face went, both of them, within a breath of each other, and the high side of the section came down into the water with a slap, and the whole thing rocked twice and lay level at last in a foot of water, floating, with a split frame and a row of empty holes.

Every build still works when its stones die. He had built everything in Kaldo on that for ten years. This one had worked, because it was a boat.

Nobody said anything for a while. Bram took up the slack of his line and made it fast to a stake. Ferra came back down the path, and opened her book, and wrote WHAT WENT WRONG at the top of a clean page, as she had at the head of every trial's book since the first, and began.

Ward stood at the edge of the shelf with the fore line slack in his hands, looking at the section lying in the water. He looked at it for a long time, with the look Emeric had seen on him once before, beside Armand's bench, with a thumb of stone pulling upward in his fist.

"It's a boat that doesn't need the water," Ward said.

He said it flatly, the way he said the number of men on a road. Ward had served for years under the capital's sky, where boats that did not need the water went over the inner rings every day with a man standing in each of them, and he had never said it about any of those. He had said it about this one. It was the first time anybody had said it aloud in Kaldo, and it was Ward who said it.

Emeric did not correct him.

Ferra's pen stopped on the page, and then went on.

He knew what had happened before he knew why, and he knew why before anybody told him.

He had felt it. The two stones off the old face had spent themselves at very nearly the pace the arithmetic gave them, both together. The two off the new face had held on three times as long as anything in Armand's book said they should. Every stone he had ever set on the bench had come off the old face, and at three times their own weight they had all spent at the same steady pace. The new face's stones had read differently on Armand's bench, and he had trimmed for the reading, and it had not once occurred to him that a stone which read differently might also spend differently, because nothing he had ever set had given him cause to wonder. Under a thousand times their weight the two faces had come apart like two horses in a team that walk together perfectly well and do not run together at all.

The bench had never been asked to run. The four numbers pinned on the wall of the pattern store had been perfectly true. They had been true for a bench.

He had set the stones level, and they had lifted level, and they had held two tons of iron in the air over open water with nobody feeding them, and there had been more lift in two of them than he had counted on. The lift had been there all along. What had failed was the balance. He had balanced them at the start and then let them spend at two different rates under a load that no bench would ever put on them, and he had put the two rates on opposite diagonals, so that when they parted company they would twist the section between them. He had done that on purpose. It had been the careful thing to do.

Ferra came to the long room that night with Ilsa's tally.

She had not been asked for it. She laid it on the table in front of him, open at the page for the second raising, and put one finger on the marks against the four pieces he had drawn from the chest, two of them for the old face and two for the new, in Ilsa's small upright hand that had copied them from Hask's. Then she took her finger away and waited.

"It was in the book," she said.

"I know. I put them on the diagonals so it wouldn't matter."

"It mattered."

"It mattered," he said.

She did not ask whether he was all right, which he was grateful for. She did not go for a while, either, and stood at the corner of the table with her book under her arm looking at the tally and at nothing in particular, and he was more grateful for that, and did not say so.

When she had gone he sat with the tally and worked it through to the end.

A hull's stone would have to be cut from one face of the body and nowhere else. Read together on Armand's bench, on the same days, at the same hours. Set together in one working. Spent together. And replaced together, all of them, never a fresh stone beside a tired one, never a stone off one face among stones off another. A matched set. The master foot hung in its case in the yard, cut in iron by Bram and never once redefined, and every gauge in four baronies was measured against it and nothing else. One foot. One set, off one face, never mixed.

And the hull on Fletcher's stocks had been drawn around exactly this. Its frames stood at known stations, heavy enough to be lifted by, and a frame that split the way the spare section's had split this morning could be cut out and replaced in a week. The wooden boat in her padlocked shed could never have taken it. Her frames stood wherever Fletcher had wanted them when he built her for a Wayford trader, she had flexed in the river the first week she had an engine in her, and a wooden hull racked the way the section had racked would have opened along every seam she had.

The lake had seen it.

Ward told him on the walk back up from the shelf, in the tone he used for a weather report. There had been a man out on the far water at first light in a skiff, a long bowshot off, checking his night lines. He had stopped checking them. He had sat quite still in his skiff with the lines in his hands for as long as it had taken, and then he had rowed away towards the lower town rather faster than a man rows with a catch.

By the end of the week Saban had four versions of it from the lower town, and he set them out on the table in the long room one after another, with evident pleasure, the way he set out a comparison of prices. In the first, a boat had flown over the lake at dawn and come down in the reeds. In the second, the prince had walked out across the water to meet it. In the third, the old tower mage had raised a drowned barge off the bottom of the lake for practice and let it fall again. The fourth was the closest to the truth and the least believed: a piece of iron had hung in the air over the shallows by the foundry with nobody touching it, and then fallen in.

"They like the barge best," Saban said. "I'd have said the barge."

On a boat last spring, on the river above the lake, a woman who read other people's positions better than anybody he knew had said to him: You know we can't let anybody hear of this one. He had said I know, and made a joke about his sheds. There had been no leak to find and no clerk to question. He had done it himself, at first light, over open water, in plain sight of a lake, and there was nothing he could do about it now that would not make it worse. The lower town talked to the river, and the river went down to the lower water, and the lower water went to the sea. How far it would go from there, nobody could say.

Vantic came on the first day of the quarter, as she always did, to go through the quarter's service with Ferra and copy what she needed out of every book in the barony into her own.

The four stones were in the strongroom ledger in Armand's hand, and they went into her report in hers, on a line of their own. Trial fittings. Four pounds, spent. Ferra told Emeric that evening in one sentence, without looking up from her column. Vantic had copied the line, and read it over, and turned the page. She had not asked what had been fitted, or to what, or what the trial had been.

In all the years Ferra had known her, Vantic had asked a question about every line in every book that had ever cost more than a Gold Rand. Ferra did not say what she made of it, and Emeric did not ask, and they both went on with what they were doing.

Fletcher's letter came up with the lower-water carter three weeks after the section went back down the river.

It was short, and in his square careful hand, and it said the section had come back to him and he had it on its cradle on the beach. It said there were four holes cut in her frames, two to each frame, low at the turn of the bilge, round and clean, each the size of a man's fist, and that something had been bolted into them and taken out again. It said the after frame was split from the turn of the bilge to the second strake and fourteen rivets were gone out of it, and that she had been twisted along her length, not much, but enough that he could see it with a line. It said he could mend her, and it would take a week, and he would send the bill.

And at the end, on a line by itself: What have you done to her?

He did not ask why. Emeric read the letter three times, the way he had read the last one, and every time he noticed what was not in it. A man who had built the stations heavier than any join needed, as drawn, without asking, had got his section back with four holes cut in the heaviest part of her frames, and he had asked what, and not why. He thought in trim and load. He had started to work it out, and his letter was very careful not to say so.

Emeric took out the notebook that night and opened it at the second column.

He had carried the rule in his head for three weeks. He wrote it down now under the line for the hull, in plain words, because a rule that lived only in his head was only a habit. Matched set. One hull, one face of the body. Read together, set together, spent together, replaced together. Never mixed.

He looked at it for a while. Then, under it, he wrote one more word.

He had never written it anywhere. Not in the notebook, not on a slate in the delve, not in the margin of any drawing, including the ones with the stations on them. He had built toward it since the autumn before last without once putting it down, the way a man does not say a thing aloud in case saying it makes it smaller. Two tons of iron had hung over a river for the length of a held breath with nobody feeding it, and two of the stones under it had held on for the length of a long one, and Ward had looked at it and said the thing without being told.

Flight.

He looked at it for a long time. He did not cross it out.
