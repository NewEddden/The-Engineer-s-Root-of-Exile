Chapter 159 - The Watcher's Rule

The order came up from Grayrock on the steamboat in the second week the prince was gone, and it was the biggest order Bram had ever held in his hand.

Bryda's clerk had written it out fair. Four hundred lengths of rail at thirty feet, of the new shape, for the Grayrock line, to be laid from the fourth mile to the hill before the autumn rains closed the road, and a figure against it that made Bram put the sheet down on the bench and pick it up again. It was not a figure for iron. It was a figure for time. Bryda had worked out, somewhere up in her tally house under the hill, that if the rail came by the end of summer her carts would stop hauling ore four miles to the railhead and her men would stop dying on the road in the winter, and she had written the figure the way you write a figure you have been saving.

He took it to the rail works himself and pinned it to the post by the stand.

"Four hundred," said Rudd, reading it at arm's length.

"Before the rains."

Rudd looked at the stand, and the furnace, and the new engine in its shed behind the brick wall, and the second heap of slabs cooling in the yard from the converter's morning blow.

"We'll do it," he said.

They ran it hard.

Bram knew they were running it hard and he let them, because they were doing it right. The rail works had been rolling for a month and a half now, since the stand was set, and it had rolled well from the first week. The rolls Jorrin had grooved to the prince's shape took the slabs white and passed them through nine times and gave them back as rail, thirty feet of it, with a head and a web and a foot, bright and ringing, and the men who walked it out of the stand on tongs to the cooling bed had stopped marvelling at it by the second week and started swearing at it like any other work. The engine drove the stand. The engine was the new one, the first engine Bram had built to the prince's second drawing, larger than the works engine by half, with the first boiler ever riveted in Kaldo from Kaldo's own plate, and it had run since the spring without a fault.

Rudd drove it. Kester watched it.

That was how it had been since the day it was fired, and it had worked, and Bram had watched it work. Rudd at the throttle and the fire door, with his whole mind on the stand, on the slab going in white and the rolls taking it and the next pass, calling for steam before the stand had finished asking for it. Kester at the gauge, in his corner of the shed by the wall, with the brass face of the gauge in front of him and the water cocks at his hand and the lever of the safety valve over his head with its weight on the end, saying nothing unless there was something to say.

There had been nothing to say for a month and a half.

The order came, and there began to be.

It was a stand problem. The stand wanted heat in the slab and pace in the rolls, and a slab going cold halfway through its passes was a slab that cracked at the foot and went back into the converter as scrap. So the stand wanted steam without a stop. And a boiler that was being asked for steam without a stop, all day, in the heat of early summer, ran high. Kester's gauge ran high. The safety valve lifted, three or four times a day, with its scream, and let the extra go, and every time it lifted the engine dropped for a breath and the rolls slowed and a slab went cold in the middle of its fifth pass.

Rudd began to hate the valve.

Bram was in the pattern store when the rail works blew, and he heard it through two walls and across the yard and knew what it was before the sound had finished.

It was not a bang. People who have never heard a boiler go think it will be a bang. It was a sound like a great canvas tearing, very loud and very long, and under it a roar that went on after the tearing had stopped, and then a hissing that went on after the roar, and then people shouting, and then one man screaming.

He was out of the pattern store and across the yard with his apron still on.

The engine shed was full of white. It came out of the door and the high windows in a cloud and rolled across the yard and up, and it was hot, and it smelled of iron and wet brick and something else. He did not go in. He knew better than to go into a shed full of live steam, and he knew it the way he knew not to put his hand in a ladle, and he stood at the door with two of the stand men and held them back by their shirts while the steam came out past them and thinned.

The brick wall held. The prince's wall. The one Bram had asked him about in the first year, a wall between the engine and everything else thicker than any wall on the island but the keep's. The stand men on the other side of it had got their heads down, and none of them was hurt.

When the white thinned enough to see through he went in.

The engine stood. The boiler stood, on its brick bed, all in one piece. Its shell had not burst. The firebox had. The side of the firebox, the flat plate that faced the fire door, was bulged inward like the side of a kettle somebody has sat on, and split along a seam at the bottom, and through the split the water in the boiler had come out all at once into the fire and turned to steam and come out of the fire door.

Rudd had been standing in front of the fire door.

Rudd died a little before noon the next day, in the long ward, with Imani and three of her people round him and Doran at the door. He did not speak. Bram did not think he could have heard anybody if they had spoken to him.

Two stand men were burned, on the arms and the face, from the edge of the steam as it came round the corner of the boiler. Imani kept them. She said they would keep their sight. One of them would not have the use of his left hand. She said it the way she said a dose.

Kester was not burned at all. He had been in his corner by the gauge, beside the wall, and the steam had come out of the fire door and gone the other way.

He found out how it had happened from Kester, that afternoon, sitting on the bench outside the ward door because the boy would not go home and would not go in.

Kester told it without looking at him.

The gauge had been over the mark since the morning. Not far over. The valve had lifted twice. After the second lift Rudd had put a bar through the valve lever and wedged it down, against the frame, so it could not lift, because they had a slab in the stand and the order was four hundred. Kester had seen him do it. Kester had said, that's to come out, and Rudd had said, after this one.

After that one there was another in the furnace, and it was white, and Rudd said, one more heat.

The gauge was further over.

"I called stop," said Kester. He said it to his hands. "I called it. I said it out. The needle was past the red and still going and the water in the glass was dancing, and I called stop the way it says in the book. Fire out and valve open and clear the shed." He was quiet. "And he said, one more heat. And he'd said that to me every day for three years when I was on his fire. One more heat. And I let him."

"How long?"

"I don't know." Kester looked at the ward door. "The time it takes to pass a slab. Once. He'd got it in. He'd got it in and it went through the first pass and he turned round to the fire door for more steam for the second, and he opened it, and it went."

He was nineteen. He had been the most careful young man in the shop.

"He was right," said Kester. "That's the thing. He was always right. He'd been driving since the first engine and he knew what it'd stand, better than me, better than the book. He knew. And I knew the book was right and he was wrong and I let him, because he was him."

Bram did not answer that.

He did not answer it because he could not think of anything true to say that was not also cruel, and because he was thinking that he had written the line himself. The driver makes it go. The watcher stops it. He had written it on the back of the prince's own rule and signed it and had it read to both of them in that shed. And he had put a nineteen-year-old at the gauge against a man of fifty-four who had driven every engine Kaldo ever had, and he had thought that the writing would be enough, and it had not been.

The prince was six days down the coast, or at Emberfell by now, or on his way back, and there was no way to tell him.

Bram had not understood until that afternoon how used he had become to the prince being there. Not in the shop. The prince was often not in the shop. But the prince was always somewhere Bram could send a boy to, or the keep, or the flat, or Wayford at the far end of the relay, where a message could reach him in a day. There had never, in eleven years, been a thing go wrong in Kaldo that the prince did not hear about before it was cold.

The relay stopped at the marsh. There was nothing after the marsh but the sea.

So it was Armand who came down to the rail works in the afternoon, slowly, on Ferra's arm, and stood in the shed and looked at the firebox with the seam split along its bottom, and asked Bram what was needed, and gave it. He gave it the way a man gives things who has held a barony before: without pretending to understand the engine, and without asking Bram to explain it to him, and without once saying what the prince would have done. He ordered the rail works shut until Bram said it could open. He had Ferra enter it. He sent word up to Grayrock by the steamboat that the rail would be late, in his own name, as Secretary of the Arcane holding the barony under the sealed letter, and he signed it with his hand braced flat on a book.

And Ferra held the book.

She came down after Armand went up, alone, with her book under her arm, and walked round the engine shed and the stand and the cooling bed, and wrote. She wrote the time and the order and the gauge and the valve and the bar through the valve lever, which somebody had pulled out afterward and which was lying on the floor by the boiler, bent. She wrote Rudd's name, and his age, and the stand men's names and their burns. She wrote that the watcher had called stop, and when, and what was said. She wrote it in the same hand she wrote everything in, standing, at the same speed.

Then she looked at her father.

"Go home," she said.

"In a while."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she went.

He did not go home.

He went back to the pattern store after dark, when the men had gone, and lit the lamp over his bench, and sat down at it, and put his hands flat on the wood either side of the band on his wrist.

He needed to see it.

He had seen it. He had stood in the shed in the afternoon and looked at the bulged plate and the split seam and the bent bar on the floor, and he had put his hands on the cold firebox and felt the shape of the bulge. But that was the after. He needed the before. He needed to stand inside the boiler with the fire going and the water in it and the needle over the red, and walk round it, and see where it was going to go and why, the way the prince did. The way the prince had shown him, eleven years ago, at the kitchen table in the first year, when they had gone into the delve together and built the furnace and walked inside it and seen the air, and come back and she had been counting.

He could not do that without the prince.

He had never done it without the prince. None of them had. The prince opened the door and they went through it. Even since the bands, this last half year, even with the stones on their wrists and the little blue beads full, it had always been the prince who opened it, and they had followed him into the room above Kaldo, and sat at the long table there, and done what they did. The bands made it cheaper for him, and they made the six of them able to stay longer. They had not made the six of them able to open it.

Bram sat at the bench with his hand on his band and tried.

He tried the way the prince had told them to try, over and over, all winter. He shut his eyes and tried to see the room. The long table in the air, and the light that was not quite any light, and Kaldo below, and the door. He tried to see the door. He had seen it a hundred times. He knew what it looked like: plain, like any door, and nothing behind it, and then everything behind it.

Nothing came.

He tried for a long time. He sat at the bench with his eyes shut and his hand on the band and saw the long table, and the light, and the door, as hard as he could, and they stayed where they were, which was in his head, like any picture a man makes of a thing he remembers.

He opened his eyes and looked at the lamp.

He was an old man and a smith, and he was sitting in the dark in a pattern store trying to imagine a room, and he could not do it, and he had never been able to do it, and he was never going to. He had never in his life been able to see a thing that was not there. He could not draw. He could not picture a face. When the prince talked about the room above Kaldo, and Ferra and Imani talked about what they saw there, Bram had always seen it only when he was in it, and never out.

He thought about the boiler instead.

He did not mean to. It was only that he was tired and it was what he had been thinking about all day and the room would not come, so the boiler came. He thought about it the way he thought about any piece of work: not as a picture, as a making. He thought about the shell first. He had rolled the plate for it himself, in the mill yard, the second week after the coaster's, and bent it on the rolls into a cylinder and closed the seam with rivets, one at a time, hot, with a boy holding up and him on the hammer. He knew every one of those rivets. He thought about them one by one, going round the seam.

Then the ends. Then the firebox, inside the shell at the back. The firebox plates, and their seams. And between the firebox and the shell, the stays. Short bolts of iron that went through the water space from the firebox wall to the shell wall and held the two together, so that the pressure of the water pushing in on the firebox from all sides could not push its flat sides in. There were a great many of them. He had cut and threaded every one of them himself, because he would not let anybody else do the stays, and screwed them through and riveted over their heads.

He thought about them one by one. Row by row. The top row, over the crown. The side rows. The rows round the fire door, where the plate was cut away for the door and the stays had to go close.

He did not know when it happened.

He was thinking about the third stay in the second row to the left of the fire door, the one he had cut from the end of a bar of old iron because the new steel bar had run short that day and he had not wanted to wait, and he was thinking about the thread on it, which he had cut a little shallow because the old iron was hard, and he was looking at it.

He was looking at it.

It was in front of him, a hand's breadth from his face. It was a stay. It was the stay. Grey iron, threaded, with his own riveted head on the end of it and the marks of his own hammer on the head. And round it the firebox plate, and past the plate the water, and past the water the shell, and he was standing between them, in the water space, in a gap no man could stand in, and the water was round him and did not wet him and the fire was behind the plate and did not burn him.

He was in it.

He did not move for a long time. He was afraid to. He was afraid that if he moved it would go, the way the pictures in his head went when he tried to hold them.

It did not go.

He understood it slowly, standing there in the water space with the stay in front of him, and the understanding came to him the way the working of a new tool came, by holding it and turning it over and feeling where it gave. It was his. This place was his. It was not the room above Kaldo, and it was not the prince's room, and nobody had opened it for him. He had made it. He had made it the only way he had ever made anything, one piece at a time, out of what he knew, and it had opened round what he made because he had made enough of it to stand in.

It had a door. He could feel the door, behind him, without turning. The prince had said that, a long time ago, at the table: that everybody who could do this had a room of their own, and a door to it, and that the room was theirs, and whoever stood in their own room was its master and nobody came in without leave. Bram had nodded at that, the way he nodded at most of what the prince said about the delve, and not understood it, because he had never had a room. He had always been a guest in the prince's.

He understood it now. This was his room, and that was his door, and he was the master of it. Nobody could come in unless he let them. Nothing could be put in it unless he put it there or allowed it. And it was full of a boiler, because a boiler was what he had brought.

He walked round the boiler.

He walked it the way he had wanted to all day. He walked through the water space from the fire door to the back and up over the crown of the firebox and down the other side. He looked at every stay. He looked at every seam. He saw where the metal was thin and where it was thick, and he saw it true, because it was all out of his own hands and his own memory and there was nothing in it he had not made.

Then he put the fire in it.

He did it the way he would have lit a real one, without thinking, and the fire came up behind the firebox plate, and he felt the heat through the plate, and the water round him began to move. He put the pressure in. He put in the needle, over the red, where Kester had said it was. He put in the bar through the valve lever, wedged down, so the valve could not lift.

And he watched.

He watched the firebox plate by the fire door begin to move. It moved very slowly, inward, toward the fire, pushed by all the water behind it that had nowhere to go because the valve could not open. He watched every stay round the fire door take the load and hold it. He watched the third stay in the second row to the left, the one he had cut from old iron with its thread a little shallow, take the load and begin to stretch, and he watched the thread on it give, one turn, and then the next.

He watched it pull through the plate.

And when it went, the plate round it had nothing to hold it, and the stays either side of it took its load as well as their own, and they held, and held, and then the next one went, and the plate bulged in, and the seam at the bottom of it opened like a mouth.

He stood in the water space of the boiler he had built, in the room he had made, and watched the stay he had cut go, and the seam he had riveted open, and he understood both halves of it. The stay would have held at the red. It had held at the red every day for a month and a half. It had not been made to hold at what was past the red with the valve wedged shut, and nothing in that boiler had, and nothing should have been asked to. The bar had killed Rudd. And the stay had been where the bar found out.

Both. Not one or the other. Both.

Ferra had come back.

He did not know when. He found out afterward that she had come back to the pattern store a little before the third bell, because she had gone home and he had not been there, and she had known where he would be. She had come in and found him at his bench under the lamp with his hands flat on the wood and his eyes open and nobody home in them.

She had seen that once before. At her own kitchen table, in the first year, with the prince across from him. She had checked their breathing that time and said their names and been halfway to the door for Imani when they came back arguing about air angles, and she had been furious, and she had set her terms, and one of them was that nobody did this alone in a room, and nobody did it in the middle of the night.

She checked his breathing. She said his name once, not loudly. She got nothing.

She did not go for Imani.

She pulled the other stool up to the end of the bench and sat down on it, and took her book out of her coat, and opened it at a clean page, and wrote the hour at the top of it. Then she sat with her pen in her hand and watched her father's face and counted.

He came back into the pattern store with the lamp in front of him and his daughter at the end of his bench with a book.

For a moment he did not know which of them was real. Then his hands on the bench told him, the grain of the wood, and the ache in his back from sitting, and he breathed out.

"How long?" he said.

Ferra looked at her book.

"Four minutes," she said. "Nearly five."

It had been hours. He had walked that boiler for hours. He knew it had been hours, because he had watched the plate move a hair at a time and watched the thread give turn by turn.

"You were alone," said Ferra. She said it without heat. She was looking at him the way she looked at a figure she had been told was right and had not yet checked. "In a room. At night."

"I was."

"Nobody opened it for you."

"No."

She was quiet for a while. She looked at his wrist, at the band, and back at his face.

"What did you see?" she said.

He told her. He told her about the stay and the thread and the bar, and the plate moving, and the seam. He told it plainly, in order, the way he would have told it to the prince, and she wrote it down, all of it, without interrupting, and when he had finished she looked at what she had written.

"Both," she said.

"Both."

She closed the book on her pen. She did not say anything about the rule she had set at her own kitchen table eleven years ago, the one he had just broken. She did not say anything about going for Imani, or not going. She sat there a moment longer at the end of his bench, with the book on her knee, and then she reached over and put her hand on the back of his, on the bench, briefly, the way she had not done since she was a girl.

Then she took it away, and stood up, and put the stool back where it went.

He wrote the rule before he went to bed.

He did it at the bench, under the lamp, on the back of the same sheet: the prince's rule on the front, the man who watches the fire holds the works, and under it on the back his own line from the spring, the man who watches is not the man who drives. He read them both through. Then he dipped his pen and wrote under them.

Nobody overrides the watcher. Not the driver. Not the master of the works. Not the holder of Kaldo. Not me. A watcher who is overridden and lets it be done has not been overridden; he has stopped being the watcher, and the fire is out until another is found. A driver who asks is dismissed.

He looked at that for a while. Then he wrote one more line.

The stays on every boiler in this barony are to be of the new steel, and cut to full thread, and none of them is to be cut by me alone.

He signed it. Bram, Secretary of Works. He wrote the date under his name, and sanded it, and sat with the sheet under the lamp while the ink went from wet to dry.

In the church, Doran had sat up late too.

He had the parish book open on the lectern in the vestry with a single candle, at the page of the dead, which had filled up faster this year than any year since the plague. The fire in the tannery lane was on it, three lines, in the winter. And under those, in his own careful old hand, he wrote the next line, slowly, because his hand was not what it had been.

Rudd. Of the works. Aged fifty-four. Died of scalding at the rail works by the river, in the service of the barony. Buried in the churchyard by the east wall.

He sanded it, and closed the book, and blew out the candle, and sat in the dark for a while before he went to bed.
