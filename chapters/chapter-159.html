Chapter 159 - The Quarryman's Hand

The cement kilns were short of stone on the first of July because Emeric had moved the Milford line's culverts up a month.

That was the beginning of it, and he knew it was, and nobody at the long table ever once said so to him, which was worse. The culverts were the stone-and-cement pipes that would carry the streams under the new banks, twelve of them between Kaldo's bridge and the bench, and the plan had had them built in August, when the banks were settled. He had looked at the plan in June and seen that if the culverts went in in July the banks could be finished a month sooner, and the line could reach Milford before the harvest instead of after it, and the first grain could come in by rail; and he had said so, and Ferra had priced it, and it had gone through. And so the cement works wanted twice the limestone in July that it had wanted in June, and the quarry north of the quarry road where the limestone came from was a face behind, and Fennick, who had the bench half open and was not going to leave it to anybody, left it to his foreman for two days and came back to Kaldo to fire the quarry face himself.

He fired it on the second, at ten in the morning.



Emeric heard it from the works. Everybody in the lower town heard it: a flat hard crack from the north, over the wall, that made the glass in the works' windows hum. That was nothing. The quarry fired every week, and the town had stopped looking up at it. Then a second crack, close behind the first, the way Fennick's shots always came, one after another down a row like a man drumming his fingers. Then a third.

And then a pause, and a single crack, much too soon, and nothing after it at all.

He did not know what he had heard. He knew afterward. He stood at the works door with a drawing in his hand and listened to the silence after the single crack and knew only that Fennick's rows never stopped in the middle, and then the bell at the north gate began.



The quarryman's name was Abel Dunning, and he was twenty-six, and he had worked for Fennick for nine months, since Fennick first came to the quarry and asked for men who did not mind a loud noise.

He had been lighting the last row. That was the way Fennick did it: the holes drilled and charged and tamped along a row across the face, each with its fuse hanging out of it, and the fuses cut to different lengths, the first shortest and the last longest, so that one man could go along the row lighting them one after another and walk away, and they would go off one after another behind him in their order, each one a breath after the last, and break the rock small. Abel had lit four. He had turned to the fifth. And the fifth had gone, while his hand was still on it.

Imani had him on the table in the cutting room inside an hour.

Emeric did not go in. He stood in the passage outside with Fennick, because Fennick had come down the quarry road behind the cart with Abel on it at a run, with his scorched coat flapping and his two-fingered hand bloody to the wrist from holding the man's arm shut, and had come into the hospital behind the stretcher, and Imani had turned round in the cutting room door and looked at him and said, out, in a voice that put him out; and he had gone as far as the passage and stopped, and stood against the wall, and Emeric had stood beside him.

It was a long time. Linnet went past them twice with water and once with a basin covered with a cloth, and did not look at either of them. Through the door they heard Imani's voice, low and level, giving orders, and the smell of the ether came out under it, sweet and thick, and they heard the saw. Fennick did not move when they heard the saw. He stood against the wall with his hands hanging at his sides and looked at the opposite wall, at a place on the whitewash where there was nothing, and his long jaw did not move at all.

Emeric had never seen him not laughing. He had seen Fennick in the guardroom at midnight with his coat full of spark holes, and on a barge in the middle of the river in a rain of his own green fire, and on the bench in the wet with water running off his hair; laughing, every time, at the noise, at the rock, at Ward, at the wind, at nothing. He looked at Fennick against the hospital wall and did not recognize him.

Imani came out at last, drying her hands. Her apron was not clean.

"He'll live," she said, to Fennick. Not to Emeric. "If it stays clean. It'll stay clean, if he lets it. The hand's gone. I took it here." She put two fingers of her own right hand across the inside of her left wrist, a little above the joint. "There was nothing below that I could have kept that he'd have wanted to keep." She looked at Fennick's own hand, the bloody one, with its two fingers missing. "Let me see that."

"It's not mine," said Fennick. "The blood."

"I know whose it is. Let me see the hand."

He held it out. She looked at it, turned it over, and let it go.

"Go and wash," said Imani. "Then go and sit with him. He'll wake at six and he'll want to see the man who gave him the work." She looked at Fennick a moment longer, very directly. "Not because he blames you. Because he's twenty-six, and you're the nearest thing he's got to somebody who'd know what to say."

Fennick went to wash. Imani watched him go down the passage. Then she turned to Emeric, and her face was not the face she had turned on Fennick.

"I want the table," she said. "Tomorrow. And I want an hour of it."



Fennick found the fault that night, with Tess.

He had the coil of fuse that the fifth length had been cut from on Tess's bench in the powerhouse, under the big lamp, unrolled along the whole length of the bench and over the end of it onto the floor, a grey cord as thick as a pencil: a core of fine black powder in a wrapping of jute, and the jute painted over with tar so that the wet could not get at the powder. He had made it himself in the winter. He made all his fuse himself, every foot of it, because he did not trust anybody else's, and he had kept it in the cold store under the north wall, in its coils, on a shelf, as he had always kept it.

He went along it a foot at a time with Tess's magnifying glass. Tess sat beside him in her chair and went along it the other way from the other end, with her own fingers, because she had found long ago that her fingers saw things in wire that her eyes did not.

She found it first. Two yards from the end where the fifth length had been cut off, at a place where the coil had been wound too tight round its own centre, on the inside of the bend. She stopped her fingers on it and did not say anything, and Fennick came along the bench to her and put the glass over it.

A crack. In the tar. Fine as a hair, across the cord, at the bend; and another a finger further on, and another, a whole row of them, like the cracks in the glaze of an old jug, every one of them right through the tar to the jute. Where the cold had made the tar brittle, and the coil had been wound too tight, and the tar had cracked on the inside of every turn.

"It doesn't burn inside," said Tess quietly. "Not where there's a crack. The fire gets out through the crack to the jute, and the jute's dry, and it runs along the outside, on the jute, in the open air." She put her finger on the next crack, and the next. "Faster than the powder burns inside. Much faster. Along the outside, crack to crack, like a fire running along a hedge." She took her finger off it. "A minute and a half of fuse goes in twenty breaths."

Fennick looked at the cracks under the glass until the lamp's wick wanted trimming.

Then he laughed. It was not his laugh. It was short and dry and it had nothing in it at all.

"Cold store," he said. "I put it in the cold store because it's dry. I've always put it somewhere cold and dry. In the capital the powder house was in a cellar." He laid his three-fingered hand flat on the coil. "And I wound it tight because a tight coil takes less shelf. And it was a cold winter, and I never once unwound a coil to look at the inside of the turn, because it was my fuse, and my fuse doesn't fail." He took his hand away. "My fuse. My coil. My shelf. My man."

Tess did not say anything. She rolled her chair back a little way from the bench and looked at him.

"Every coil I've got," said Fennick. "Every foot. Out. Burned. Tonight." He looked at Tess. "And then I'll make it again. And I'll want you to tell me how to keep it so it doesn't do that. Because you saw it with your fingers and I didn't see it with a glass."

"Warm," said Tess. "Not hot. Dry and warm, like bread. Loose coils, on pegs, not on a shelf." She thought. "And every coil you open, you cut a yard off the end of it first and burn it against the sand glass. Every morning. Before anybody lights anything. If the yard burns short, the coil goes in the river."

Fennick looked at her. Then he went and fetched a pencil and a scrap of paper from the bench and wrote it down, every word, in a hand Emeric had never seen him use, slowly, like a boy at a slate.



He went to Ferra in the morning, before the table. Emeric was in the counting room when he came in. He had not slept; he had sat by Abel's bed in the ward from six until Imani put him out at midnight, and then burned his fuse in the river meadow until three, every coil, a long hissing snake of fire along the grass in the dark that the night watch on the wall had watched from start to finish and reported to Ward.

He stood in front of Ferra's desk with his hat in his three-fingered hand.

"Abel Dunning's wage," he said. "What the quarry pays him. Every week."

Ferra looked up. "The barony pays a man's wage when he's hurt at the barony's work," she said. "It's been the rule since the oven shed. Six months, full. Then we see."

"No," said Fennick. "I pay it. Out of mine. Not six months." He put the hat on the desk. "For as long as he lives. Or I do. Whichever's shorter." He looked at the hat. "And if I'm the one that's shorter, out of whatever I leave."

Ferra looked at him. Then she opened the book at a fresh page.

She did not argue with him. Emeric had expected her to; it was not the barony's way, it would make a muddle of the quarry's book and the works' and Fennick's own, a private man paying a public wage. She did not argue at all. She wrote the line, in her small square hand, and the amount, and the date, and under it three words, and turned the book round for Fennick to sign.

F. Crake. His own.

Fennick signed it. He picked his hat up off the desk and went out.



Imani had her hour.

She did not use it to accuse anybody. Emeric had braced for that too, sitting at the end of the long table with the culverts heavy in his chest, and it did not come. She stood at the head of the table with a sheaf of paper in her hand that she had written in the night, between Abel's waking and his sleeping, at the desk in the corner of the ward, and she read it.

Rules. That was all. Two pages for the quarry and the bench and every other place in four baronies where anybody lit a charge. Every coil of fuse tried each morning, a yard cut off and burned against the sand glass, before any charge was lit, and a coil that burned short thrown in the river. Fuse kept warm and dry and loose, on pegs. Every hole counted when it was charged, and every shot counted when it went, aloud, by a second man who did nothing else, and if the count was short, nobody went back to the face for a full hour by the glass, whatever the foreman said. One man lit. Everybody else back behind the marker before the first fuse was touched, and the marker set by Ward and not moved.

And two more pages for the works. For every belt and gear and shaft that turned: a guard over it, of iron or wood, bolted, so that no hand or sleeve or hair could go into it by accident. Sleeves rolled or tied. Hair tied. A lever on every machine that stopped it, that any man standing at it could reach with either hand. Nobody alone at a machine that could take a hand. Nobody looking at the arc without the glass, which was there already, in her own hand, on every door it was used behind; she read it again anyway.

When she had finished she put the pages on the table.

"I'll want them on every door," she said. "Not in a book. On the door, where a man reads them going in. In big letters, for the ones who read slowly, and Sela's school to read them aloud to the ones who don't." She looked down the table. "And I'll want them to be mine. Not the works'. Not the quarry's. Mine. When they're broken, the man who broke them answers to me, and so does his foreman, and so does whoever pays the foreman." She did not look at Emeric. She did not need to. "Bram's men have lost two fingers and a thumb at the works since the new year, that I've stitched. The levy on the Milford line has had a leg broken under a spoil wagon and a man crushed against a cutting wall who'll walk with a stick. I didn't write any of them down anywhere but my own book, because nobody asked me to. Now somebody's lost a hand, and I'm asking."

Nobody said anything.

"Yes," said Emeric.

"I wasn't asking you," said Imani. "I was asking the table." She looked round it. Ferra. Bram. Ward. Saban. Armand, with his eyes shut, at the end. "It's a seat's work. Somebody's got to own it, the way Ferra owns the book and Ward owns the wall, or it belongs to nobody. Every man who's ever lost a hand at a machine in the history of the world lost it in a place where it belonged to nobody."

Bram was the first. He did not say anything. He put his big scarred hand on the table, palm down, flat, beside the pages, the way he put it on a bench when he had decided a thing, and left it there. Then Ward did. Then Ferra, and Saban, and Armand, without opening his eyes.

Fennick was not at the table. He was not entitled to sit at it. But he was in the doorway, where he had been all the time, with his hat in his hand; and when the last hand went down on the table he came in, without being asked, and took the top page of the quarry rules off the pile, and the pen out of Ferra's stand, and signed it at the foot, first, in the slow boy's hand Emeric had seen in the powerhouse. Then he went out again.



Tess and Bram started on the hand a fortnight later, when Imani said the stump could bear to be measured.

Abel Dunning did not want a hand. He said so, in the ward, at length, to Tess, who had come down on the motor with her data book and a tape and a lump of soft wax, and sat by his bed and waited for him to stop. He did not want a wooden hand like the old soldiers in the capital had, that did nothing and creaked. He did not want pity. He did not want to be the man in the dock quarter that children followed to stare at. He wanted his hand, and he could not have it, and he would rather have nothing than something that reminded him every morning that it was not.

Tess heard all of it. When he had finished she put the data book down on the blanket.

"I'm not making you a hand," she said. "I can't make a hand. Nobody can." She put her own hand flat on the arm of her chair, the way she did when she was about to say a thing she had already thought through. "I'm making you a thing that grips. That's different. My legs don't walk. I can't make them walk. So I made a thing that goes." She looked at the stump, bound in Imani's linen. "It'll be a hook. Two hooks, iron, that close on each other, on a cup that fits your arm, on a strap over your shoulder. You'll open it by moving your shoulder, like this," she shrugged, forward, one side, "and it'll close on its own, on a spring, and hold what's in it till you shrug again." She picked the data book back up. "And it'll be ugly, and the children in the dock quarter will follow you to stare at it for a month, the way they followed my chair. And then they'll stop. And you'll be able to hold a cup and a spade and a crowbar and a girl's hand, if you can find a girl who'll let you." She opened the book. "Hold your arm out. I need to measure."

Abel Dunning looked at her for some while, the girl in the black chair with the data book, who had been cut down at the dynamo house in the war and had not walked since.

He held his arm out.



The man from the north came to the river inn a week after that, on the coast boat, and asked for Fennick by name.

He was well dressed and pleasant and paid for his room in silver. He asked in the taproom, the first evening, whether a Master Crake still worked in Kaldo, the powder man, the one who had been with the royal fireworks makers in the capital; and the taproom told him, because there was no reason not to, that Fennick had his yard out past the quarry road and could be found there most mornings. The next morning he walked out to the yard.

Fennick heard him out. He told Saban afterward that the man had been very civil, and very well informed. He had known about the gravity drive at Grayrock, and the bench on the Milford line, and the quarry. He had known about Abel's hand. He had offered three times what Kaldo paid, and a powder house of Fennick's own building, and a free hand with it, at a place in the north whose name he had not quite said, for a lord whose name he had not said at all; and he had said, gently, with real sympathy, that a man who had had a thing like the quarry happen on his watch might be glad of a fresh start somewhere nobody knew him.

"What did you tell him?" said Saban.

They were in Saban's room off the counting house, with the door shut. Emeric was in the window seat. Fennick stood in the middle of the floor turning his hat.

"That I've a man's wage to pay," said Fennick. "Here. Every week. For as long as one of us lives." He turned the hat once more. "He said the north would pay it for me. Gladly. On top. I said it wasn't the north's to pay." He looked at Saban. "Then he went back to the inn, and I came here. He's tall, grey at the sides, a ring on his little finger with a blue stone, a cut on the back of his right hand that's healing. He drinks wine, not ale. He was asking the boatman this morning when the next coast boat goes north."

Saban had written all of it down while Fennick said it. He looked at what he had written.

"Thursday," he said. "The boat."

"I know," said Fennick. "I asked too."

He put his hat on and went to the door, and stopped there with his hand on the latch, and looked back at Emeric in the window seat.

"The culverts," Fennick said. "I heard. At the table. That you moved them up a month." He was not laughing. "I'd have fired that quarry in August instead of July if you hadn't. And the coil would have been warm by August, and the tar soft." He looked at Emeric steadily. "And it would have been somebody else's hand, some other day, from some other coil, because I'd never once looked at the inside of the turn. It was my coil." He opened the door. "Don't you go carrying it. It's not yours. I'm carrying it, and I've only got the one hand to spare."

He went out. Emeric sat in the window seat and looked at the shut door, and did not get up.
