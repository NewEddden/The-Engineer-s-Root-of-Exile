Chapter 181: The Volume Code

The summons came on the third morning, by Kassim.

Emeric was at the table in the inn's back parlour with Saban and the settlement's engrossed terms when the knock came, and he knew the man in the door before he knew why. Long face, long hands, a knight's coat in the king's green with no device on it, and the particular stillness of a man who had gone into the chamber the same night as Ward and come out of it the same way. They had met perhaps four times in Emeric's life. Every letter Ward had sent north since the exile had gone through this man's hands and been read aloud by him in a room Emeric had never seen.

"His grace will see you," Kassim said. "Now, if you're able."

"I'm able."

Rylan stood up from the door. Kassim looked at him and then at Emeric.

"The corridor," Emeric said. "Not the room."

Kassim nodded, as if that were the answer he had been told to expect, and turned for the stair.

They walked. The inn was in the chancery lanes and the king's house was up the hill behind the hall, and Kassim took them by the back way, through the yard of the saddlers and across the lower court where the washing hung, and nobody looked twice at a knight in green with a young man behind him. At the gate to the privy garden Kassim spoke for the first time since the inn.

"Ward's well?"

"Ward's well. He's holding the wall."

"He would be." Kassim looked straight ahead. "He wrote in the winter that you don't sleep."

Emeric said nothing to that. There was nothing to say to it that Kassim would not already have read aloud to somebody.

The king's day room was on the first floor of the old house, over the garden, and Emeric had been in it once as a child, for a reason he no longer remembered. It was smaller than he had kept it. A hearth with a low fire. A table under the window with papers on it held down by a dagger in its sheath. Two chairs, one of them good. A hanging on the wall to the left of the hearth, green and gold, too long for the wall, so that its hem lay in a fold on the boards.

He looked at the door before he looked at his father, because he had been taught to.

One leaf of oak, perhaps an inch and a half thick, hung on three strap hinges, with a gap under it the width of a finger where the boards had worn. A good door for keeping out a draught. A poor one for anything else. Behind the hanging by the hearth there would be the servants' door, because there always was, and the servants' passage behind that, and the servants' stair. He did not open his sense. He did not need to. He had grown up in this house, and in the passages of this house nobody was ever quite alone and nothing said above a certain loudness was ever quite private.

That had been the lesson. He had been small enough that his father had to stoop to talk to him, and his father had taken him out to the mews on a wet afternoon for no reason Emeric could see, and walked him up and down the long dim shed between the hawks on their blocks, and explained it as if it were a game. Anything I say to you loud enough to carry, I'm saying to the door. Understand? Not to you. To whoever's behind the door. If I shout at you, I'm shouting at them. If I want you to know a thing, I'll tell you so nobody else can hear it, and it'll be short, and you won't answer it out loud. And then his father had shouted at him, there in the mews, about a broken window in the east range that Emeric had not broken, and between the shouts, close, with his hand on the back of Emeric's neck, had told him in a voice like a hawk shifting on its block that he had done well at his letters that week. Emeric had shouted back that it wasn't him, it was Corbin, and his father had said, low, Good. Again.

They had played it perhaps a dozen times. Then the afternoons in the mews had stopped, the way things in that house stopped, without anyone saying so, and he had kept the rule the way a boy keeps a coin somebody gave him, not spending it. He had used pieces of it since. At the Wayford dinners he had complained of his father at the top of his voice with his cup in his hand and thought of the mews while he did it, and it had made the mask sit easier, knowing that somewhere in the world there was a man who would have understood exactly what he was doing and why.

He had never once used the whole of it. Neither had his father, with him. Not since the mews.

Harrold was standing at the window with his back to the room.

"Shut the door," he said, without turning.

Emeric shut it. It closed with a sound like a box lid, and did nothing at all about the gap underneath.

His father turned round, and his face was red, and his voice filled the room to the corners.

"Do you know what they are saying about you in this city?"

It was a voice for a hall. It was the voice he had not used on the dais, saved up. It went off the plaster and the boards and under the door and, Emeric was entirely sure, through the hanging and down the passage behind it, and it was meant to.

"No, my lord," Emeric said. He said it sullenly, loudly, the young man in the tight coat.

"They are saying that my son hanged five people of noble blood in a market square like a sheriff hanging sheep thieves. They are saying he raised an army out of weavers and debtors and the sweepings of the south road and set it against a duke of this realm. They are saying he makes things that fly." Harrold came away from the window. "They are saying a great many things, and I have had to sit in my own hall and listen to all of them read out by a man with a voice like a bell, and I have had to say nothing."

"Keller said I was within the law."

"Keller!" His father's hand came down flat on the table and the dagger jumped on the papers. "Don't quote Keller to me. Keller is paid to know the law. You are supposed to know better than the law. You are supposed to know that there are things a lord may do and things a lord may not be seen to do, and a king's son hanging nobles in a square is the second kind, whatever it says in the rolls."

He came round the table. He was close now, closer than Emeric had stood to him in years, near enough that Emeric could see the grey in the brows and the broken veins over the cheekbones and smell woodsmoke and horse and something like cloves. He took Emeric by the back of the neck, the way he had in the mews, not gently, a father's grip on a boy who has disgraced him, and it would have looked like that to anyone who could see it, and nobody could see it.

"I can't keep you from the next one," Harrold said.

It was not a whisper. A whisper hisses, and hissing carries. It was lower than that, breath shaped into words at the edge of the ear, under the level where a door's gap could pass it. It took the space of one indrawn breath, the breath a man takes between one angry sentence and the next.

Then, loud again, over it, "Did you think of that? Did you think of anything?"

"They were murderers," Emeric said, loudly. His heart was going hard. He kept his voice where the young man would keep it, high and aggrieved. "You read what they did. Everybody read it. Keller had it."

"I read it." His father let go of his neck and walked away, towards the hearth, and stood with his back to the hanging. "I read it and I agreed with it, and I said so in one line, because one line was what a king could say. Do you know what it cost to say one line?" Loud. "Do you know what it costs me to have Aldane in my hall with his file?"

"He started it."

"He did not start it. You started it, the morning you rode into Wayford with a summons in your pocket." Harrold turned. "Aldane only finished what you started, and badly, and now he has lost a field and his cousin's boy and he has come to me with a file, and I have had to give him something, and what I had to give him was you."

He came back across the room, three strides, and this time he did not take Emeric by the neck. He put a hand on his shoulder and leaned on it, heavily, as a man leans on a son when he is tired of shouting.

"The Crown will buy your stone," he said, under it. "Grayrock's. From the spring raising. I'll set the price. You'll not ask me about the price."

And then he straightened, and his voice went up again, before Emeric had finished hearing the words. "Three hundred men. That is what you have left. Three hundred, and a licence you will have to beg for from my chancery like a merchant asking leave to sell wine. You will hate it. You will send me letters about it. I will not answer them."

"That's not fair, my lord."

"No," Harrold said, loudly. "It is not."

Emeric stood in the middle of the room with his father's hand gone from his shoulder and the warmth of it still there, and did the arithmetic without meaning to.

Grayrock stone was sold under the covenant at the covenant's price, which the Crown had set years ago when it sealed the covenant, and which Ferra had written at the top of the gravity page in her small square hand. The Crown bought it at that price and no other. It always had. A king does not overpay for anything. A king who overpays is a king who is being robbed, or a king who is paying for something that is not in the bill.

He understood it all at once, the way he sometimes understood a machine: not the parts one after another, but the whole of it moving.

It was money. It was money that could not be seen to be a gift, going from a father to a son who had been sent away, by the only road a king had that nobody could call a road. The Crown's treasury buying the Crown's strategic stone, at a price the Crown was entitled to set, for reasons the Crown did not have to give. Anyone who looked at it would see a sovereign buying gravity stone from a southern barony, which every sovereign in the world would do if he could, and paying a little over the covenant for it, which might be generosity or might be policy or might be carelessness in the treasury, and was nobody's business. It could not be traced to a father's hand, because a father's hand had never touched it.

It was a way of saying something that could not be said out loud. It was the whole of the mews, in coin.

And he could not thank him for it, because thanking him would be asking about the price.

"You think I don't know what you've built down there," his father was saying, loud, pacing. "You think the Crown's riders are blind. Kilns and walls and a mill that makes rails, and a harbour, and now a city on the flat where there was nothing but sheep. I know what you've built."

He was at the window again. Emeric went to him. It was what the young man would do: go to his father at the window to argue, stand too close, lower his voice at last out of shame. He stood at his father's shoulder and looked out at the privy garden and the wet box hedges, and said, in the breath between two of his father's sentences, the thing he had carried up the road for twelve days.

"Why did you let Corbin have the file?"

His father did not look at him. He did not answer. The breath came and went, and nothing came under it. When his father spoke again, it was loud, and it was about the hangings.

"Five of them. In a square. With their families watching."

"Their families weren't watching," Emeric said, loudly, sulkily, as the young man would. "Their families were at home. Everybody else was watching."

"That is worse."

It was, he thought. His father was right about that too.

He tried once more, a little later, in the space between a sentence about the Circle and a sentence about the Church, and what he asked was whether his father believed the file. His father did not answer that either. He went on about the Church. He did not even pause where the answer would have gone. The question went into the gap under the door with everything else.

Emeric understood that the silence was an answer of a kind. Whatever his father would say to him under his breath, he had already chosen it, and it was short, and it did not include anything Emeric had come with. He had come with the file and Corbin and the three hundred and the licence and who had chosen Fenner and why Grayrock was not in the duke's articles. He had come with all of it arranged in his head in order, like the terms of a treaty. His father had not come to negotiate. His father had come to tell him three things, and had already told him two.

It went on for most of an hour. There was a knock at the door once, a servant with wine, and Harrold roared at him to leave it outside, and the servant left it outside. There was a sound behind the hanging once, very small, like a foot shifting on a stair, and neither of them looked at it.

Near the end Harrold sat down in the good chair. He did it heavily. He was an old man, Emeric saw, older than he had been two years ago, and shouting for an hour had cost him. He sat with his hands on his knees and his head down, breathing, and Emeric stood in front of him the way a son stands in front of a father who has finished with him, waiting to be dismissed.

"You'll go back south," Harrold said, loud, but tired now, the voice coming down. "You'll send your men home. You'll write to my chancery for your licence and wait for it. And you will not come up this road again unless I send for you."

"Yes, my lord."

"And you will not," his father said, "embarrass me like this again."

"No, my lord."

His father looked up at him.

It was the first time in the hour he had looked at him straight, and there was nothing in his face that the door could have seen through the gap, and Emeric thought that there was no one alive now who could read that face except himself and perhaps Kassim.

"Your mother would have built it too," Harrold said.

It came under the breath before go. It was so quiet that for a moment afterwards Emeric was not sure he had heard it, and then he was sure, because his father's eyes were still on him and they were wet, and his father did nothing whatever about it.

"Go," Harrold said, loudly.

He did not ask the other thing.

He had not meant to ask it. He had known on the road north that he would not ask it, as he had known once before, the last time he stood in this house, that he would not. It was not a question for a door with a gap under it. It was not a question for any room in this house. He had carried it since the day his mother died: what his father had done that day, and why, and whether the answer was the one Emeric had lived with or some other answer that would make all the years since into something else. He carried it now to the door and put his hand on the latch and did not turn round.

His father had told him three things. That he could not protect him. That he would pay him, and he was not to ask. That his mother would have built what he had built. None of them was an answer to anything Emeric had come to ask. All three of them were true. Emeric knew they were true the way he knew the fill under the hall had settled on the west side: not by being told, but because that was the shape it had.

He opened the door.

The corridor was bright after the day room, because the corridor faced south, and there was a footman at the stair head with a tray and a jug of wine, standing very straight and looking at nothing. There was a maid further along with an armful of linen who had stopped where she was. Rylan was against the wall opposite the door, where Emeric had left him, with his arms folded and his eyes on the floor. Kassim was nowhere.

Emeric let the young man come back into his face: a son who had been shouted at for an hour by his father behind a door that everyone in the corridor had been able to hear through. He let it come up red. He did not look at the footman, and the footman did not look at him, and that was the way it was always done in this house.

By supper it would be in the kitchens. By tomorrow it would be in the chancery lanes and the saddlers' yard and the upper market. The king had had his exiled son in and torn strips off him for an hour. Five nobles hanged, an army of weavers, a duke humiliated, and the boy had stood there and said yes, my lord and no, my lord like a schoolboy. Serves him right. The king was still the king.

That was what the door was for. It had done its work. Every loud word was already on its way down the servants' stair, and every one of them was true in its way, and none of them was in the shape it had been said in.

The other three he would carry south in his head, where Ferra's book could not enter them and Saban could not ask about them and Ward, who had carried a letter a year for this man and read nothing into it, would not be told. He could not write them down. He could not say them to anyone, ever, in any room. They were his father's, and they had been said at the only volume at which his father could say anything that was his own.

Rylan pushed off the wall.

"Bad?" Rylan said. Low.

"Loud," Emeric said.

They went down the main stair together, past the footman, and out into the wet garden, and neither of them said anything else until they were through the gate.
