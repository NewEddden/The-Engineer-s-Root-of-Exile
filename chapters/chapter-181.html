Chapter 181 - Dry Dock

Paskin dug the dock in a week, which he said was a disgrace, because he could have dug it in four days if he had been allowed to dig it in daylight.

It was on Kaldo's own ground, on the river flat between the shed and the old wool crane, where nobody on the capital road could see and nobody on the river would think anything of a hole beside a shipwright's shed. Linus had pegged it in June: a basin a hundred and forty feet long and forty wide and twenty deep at the head, with its mouth on the river and a slope at its foot where the ship would come in. Paskin and four of the students stood along its edge on the night of the fourth of August, after the moon went down, and leaned on the ground; and it opened, the way the cuttings had opened, quietly, sliding out sideways into long low banks; and the levy came behind them at first light with barrows and carted the banks away before the river woke up.

Six nights. On the seventh the masons came, and began facing the sides with the new cement.

The recipe went in beside it, the same week, the way Ferra's rule said it had to, so that the next dock anybody built in Randillia could be built with nobody leaning on anything. Two rows of oak piles across the dock's river mouth, driven by Fletcher's men with a drop hammer off a tripod, and the space between the rows packed solid with puddled clay from the brickfield, trodden down by boys in bare feet until it would hold water like a pot. A cofferdam. Behind it, on the bank, a steam pump: an old engine off the works' spare floor, on a new bed, with a pump of Bram's on its crank and a pipe over the dam into the river. When the dam was in, and the masons were done, the pump would take the river out of the basin and keep it out, for as long as the coal lasted, and men could walk on the floor of it in their boots where a ship had floated an hour before.

"Earth on the slow way's a month," said Paskin, standing at the edge with his pipe, on the morning of the twelfth, looking down at the masons. "Two, with the dam." He took the pipe out. "And no Visitor to hide it from." He put the pipe back. "I'll say this for the slow way. Nobody comes asking about it."



Ward swam the garrison in it on the thirteenth.

The masons were done, and the dock had been flooded once, from the river, through a sluice in the dam, to see whether the facing held, and it held; and while it was full, and before the pump took it out again, Ward marched the garrison down from the keep yard in their kit, every man, mail shirt and boots and belt and sword, and stood them along the edge of the basin, and told them to swim it.

It was in the Water book. It had been in the Water book since the winter river after the abduction, when half the garrison on the bank had not been able to swim and Emeric had come across on Rylan's back. Since then it had been the mill race every summer morning in shirts, and Osric sinking, and Rylan floating on his back laughing; and the basin was the first test in kit.

They went in in fours. Most of them came out the far end, with their faces red and their mouths open, hauling themselves up the steps the masons had cut. Three had to be pulled out by the boatmen Ward had put along the sides with poles. Declan swam it twice, because his leg was still stiff from the gorse bend and he wanted to know if it would hold, and came out the second time looking pleased and grey.

Osric sank.

He went in off the edge with the third four, with his sword on his hip and his red-flowered cuffs, and went down like a stone, and stayed down. Declan was in after him before the boatmen had their poles round, and came up with him by the collar, and towed him to the steps, and Osric came up the steps on his hands and knees and lay on the edge of the dock with water coming out of his nose and his mouth and the joints of his mail.

"Again," said Ward, from the edge.

Osric lifted his head.

"Not now," said Ward. "Tomorrow. And the day after. Till you come out the far end." He looked down the length of the basin. "In this, you've got a boatman and a pole and Declan. In the river at night with the ship gone down under you, you've got nobody." He turned away. "Four men in five, by the end of the month."

Osric lay on the warm stone and looked at the sky.

"I float in the bath," he said, to nobody.



The K Discovery came into the dock on the morning of the eighteenth, and Emeric hid her with steam.

It had to be done in daylight. She could float into the dock at night under the motor, quietly, at a walking pace, the way she came and went from the shed; but she had to be centred over the blocks on the dock floor as the water went down, and settled on them true, a hand's width at a time, and that wanted men on ropes on both sides and Fletcher on the dock edge with a plumb line and light to see the plumb line by. And she had never been seen out of the shed in daylight in her life. Not her legs, not her foils, not the dark copper of her dome. A boat in a shed was a boat in a shed. A hundred feet of brown oak with three bronze legs hanging down out of her bottom, sitting on blocks in a hole by the river in the sunshine, was something a fisherman would remember.

So he made a cloud.

He had failed at it in November, on the river below the wall, with the four knights coming down behind it in the dark: he had made the steam, which was easy, because it was fire and water and fire was his; and then he had pushed it with air, which was not, and he had pushed it the only way he knew how to push air, which was hard, and blown a hole in it the size of a barn. Sorrel had watched from the wall. In the morning she had told him what he had done wrong in five words, and he had spent the spring finding out what they meant.

Don't push it. Lead it.

He knelt on the river bank at the dock's mouth at nine in the morning, on the downstream side, with his hand flat on the water, and made the steam. It came up off the river in a long white bank, thick, rolling, the length of three barges, between the dock and the open water, and the river hissed under it. That was the easy half. Then he did not push. He let the cloud lie where it was, and felt for the air the way Linnet felt for it, a crowd and not a wind; and the morning breeze was coming off the marsh, slow, from the east, upriver; and he did not fight it. He made a way. A thin, cool channel of air along the river's surface on the far side of the bank, the width of a man, leading the breeze round the cloud instead of through it; and the cloud leaned into the channel, slowly, like a crowd leaning toward an open door, and settled along the river between the dock and anybody on the water, and stayed.

It cost him three times what fire cost, and he felt every bit of it, a hand closing behind his eyes the way it closed when he held two doors. But it held. He made more steam as it thinned. It held all morning.

An eel boat came down the river at ten, on the far side, and its men looked at the great white bank lying along the bank below the shed, steaming in the sun, and one of them called across to ask what it was; and Fletcher, on the dock edge with his plumb line, called back that the new pump's boiler had a leak in it the size of a fist and the engine man was a fool, and the eel men laughed and went on downriver.

Behind the cloud, the K Discovery came out of the shed under the motor and slid into the dock's mouth and up the basin, with men on ropes on both sides walking her up the edges; and the dam's sluice came down behind her, and the pump began to thump, and the water began to go.

It took four hours. Fletcher called her down a hand at a time, with his plumb line against her stern post and his eye on the blocks, and the ropes eased her over, a foot to port, a hair to starboard, and the water went down past her copper and past her foils and past the feet of her legs, and at a little after one in the afternoon she touched the blocks with all her weight, along her keel, and settled, and stood in the dry dock on the floor of a hole in the ground, out of the water, for the first time since she was launched.

Emeric let the cloud go. It drifted off down the river in long rags on the breeze and was gone in a quarter of an hour, and nobody on the water looked twice.

He sat down on the bank where he was, and stayed there for a while.



They walked under her all afternoon.

It was strange to see her from below. He had stood on her deck and sat in her cockpits and lain on his back in the hand's width of air between her hulls, and gone down in the diving dress a dozen times to feel along her foils in the black water with his hands; but he had never stood under her and looked up. Her bottom was a great dark curve of copper sheathing over his head, green at the waterline, weeded; and her three legs came down out of it on their pivots like the legs of some enormous patient animal, and her foils stood out from them, wet and shining, with the weed hanging off the port quarter one like hair.

Fletcher went over every inch of her with a lamp and a scraper and a mallet, and Bram with him, and Tess at the dock's edge in her chair with her book, calling out what she wanted looked at next. The copper was sound. The foils were sound. The port quarter leg's pivot pin was worn, a little, on its underside, a long shallow groove where it had borne the ship's weight on the slip every night for two years; not dangerous, Bram said, but it would be in another two, and the forge's bearing steel would make a better pin than the one he had made.

"I've been diving to feel that pin for a year," said Fletcher, under the leg, looking up at it with his lamp. "In the dark. With my fingers. Every month." He touched the groove with his thumb. "I never felt this."

"You can't feel a groove with gloves on," said Tess.

"I know I can't." Fletcher took his thumb away. "That's why I built a shed with a hole in it."

The tugs were at the other end of the yard, on the old slip, where the K Discovery had been built: two of them, small, sixty feet, with iron hulls of Bram's riveted plate and a funnel each and a compound engine each off the works' patterns. The first had been in the water since July. The second went in on the twentieth, off the old slip, with Maren breaking a bottle of the still's worst spirit across her bow and the whole works cheering, and floated, and did not list, which was all anybody had asked of her. They would tow the salt boats and the ore barges up from the bay, and berth anything that came up the river too big for the quays, and they were in Ferra's book on a line of their own under the port. The rail siding went in the same week, a spur off the Grayrock line along the quay's edge, so that ore could go from the wagons into the barges without a man touching it.

Odo stood at the end of the siding with his notebook and watched the first wagon come down it, and Emeric heard him say, to nobody, that it was the first time since the plague that there had been anything on Kaldo's quays worth a railway.



Imani came down to see the ship in the dock on the evening of the twentieth, after the tug's launch, when everybody else had gone up the hill.

She stood on the dock edge in the dusk with her arms folded and looked down at the K Discovery standing on her blocks on the dry floor, and at the legs, and at the dark curve of copper going away above her, for a long minute without saying anything. Then she said that it was the first time she had seen the whole of anything in Kaldo at once, and that it was bigger than she had thought, and uglier underneath, which was true of most things.

Then she said: "I want to go up."

He looked at her.

"Not in her," said Imani. "With you. In the suit." She did not look away from the ship. "I've stitched every knight who fell off something in those suits. Osric twice. Every one of them comes in and tells me what it's like, up there, and I sit there with my needle and listen to it and I haven't the least idea what they mean." She turned her head. "I'd like to know. Once. I'll not ask again."

"It's not safe."

"Nothing they do is safe. I'm the one who knows that." She unfolded her arms. "You've the suit. And you've the other thing. Your own gravity." She looked at him steadily. "I've known about that longer than about the rest. I'm asking you to use it."

He did not say anything for a breath.

"I've told Ferra I was going to ask you," said Imani. "Before I came down. She said to make you go slowly over the works chimneys." A pause. "That was all she said."



He put the suit on in the shed, by lamplight, a plate at a time, the way Tess had taught them. It was his own, plain, without a lion: the back heart and the chest heart, the harness, the belt with the lever in its notched quadrant, the stick in the left gauntlet's grip, the cape-wing furled on its battens. He left the helm on its rack. He wanted to see.

Imani stood in the shed's river doors in her ordinary clothes, a dark skirt and a jacket and her hair tied up in its cloth, and watched him do it with the attention she gave a dressing.

"Give me your hand," he said, when it was done.

She gave it. Her right hand, warm, dry, a surgeon's hand, steady.

He went up off the slip on the lever, a notch, two, the hearts humming up behind his breastbone and between his shoulders; and as his feet left the stone, he reached for her. Not with the suit. With himself. The thing that was his and nobody else's, the weight in everything, the pull that every body in the world had toward the ground; he found it in her, through her hand, the whole of it, and took it in his own hands, and held it. Gently. She was willing, and a willing body was easy; there was nothing in her pushing back against him, no fear in her note, only a kind of fierce attention. He held her weight off her the way a man holds a cup of water level.

Her feet left the slip.

She did not make a sound. Her hand closed on his, hard. She looked down at her own shoes, a foot above the stone, then two, then the height of the shed doors, and the river going black under them, and the lights of the keep coming up over the shed roof; and then she looked at him, and her face in the last of the dusk was an expression he had never seen on it in seven years.

He took them out over the river.

Slowly, at first, a man's height above the water, then higher. The stick in his left hand and the push from the hearts behind him and his own gravity holding her beside him at the end of his arm like a lantern on a pole, light, steady, turning when he turned. Over the dock, where the K Discovery stood on her blocks in the dark like a beached whale. Over the shed roof. Up along the river past the water stair, where somebody on the quay looked up and saw nothing, because the sky was dark and they were dark against it. Over the bridge. Over the marsh, a long way out, where the water lay in sheets and channels under them catching the last of the light from the west, and the birds got up off the reeds in a long dark cloud below them, crying, and went away.

She said nothing at all, the whole way out over the marsh. He could feel her note beside him, fast and bright.

Then she said, over the wind: "Let go."

He looked at her.

"Not my hand," said Imani. "The other thing. For a breath. I want to know what it's like when it's real." She was looking down, at the marsh, two hundred feet below in the dark. "They fall. All of them. That's what they tell me about. The falling, before the cape throws. I want to know what it is."

"Imani."

"One breath," she said. "You'll catch me. I know you will. That's why I'm asking you and not Osric."

He held her hand. And let go of the rest.

She dropped. All of her weight came back into her at once and she went down past his feet with her hand still in his, her arm straight up over her head, her skirt flying, a man's height, fast, faster, and he felt her note go white, a single bright shout of it without any sound; and he took her weight again, all of it, in the space of a heartbeat, gently, the way you catch a cup that has slipped off a shelf, and she stopped, and hung in the air below him at the end of his arm, swinging a little, weightless, over the dark marsh.

And she laughed.

Not the short dry laugh through her nose. The other one. The one from the ore wagon on the Grayrock line, coming down the hill at a speed nobody in Randillia had ever gone, with her hair coming down and her head back. She hung under him over the marsh by one hand and laughed out loud, helplessly, the whole of her shaking with it, the sound of it going away across the water in the dark, and the birds that had settled got up again, crying, and went.

He brought her up beside him. She was still laughing. Her hair had come down out of its cloth and was blowing across her face, and she did not push it away.

"That," she said at last, breathless. "That's what they mean."

He took her home over the town.

The long way round. Over the lit works across the river, slowly, over the chimneys, as Ferra had said; over the ward, with its one lit window, where Imani looked down and said a name, the girl on night duty, under her breath, as if checking she was there. Over the square and the records hall and the school with its bulbs in the windows. Over the roofs of the lower ward, tile now, most of them, dark and warm and smelling of the day's sun, with a lit window here and there and once a man in a yard washing at a pump who did not look up. Over the wall. And down, slowly, onto the leads of the north tower, the flat lead roof at the top of it, inside its low parapet, where nobody came at night but Ward, and Ward was on the river stretch.

Their feet touched the lead. He gave her weight back to her, a little at a time, so that she would not stagger; and she stood on the roof of the north tower in the dark with the whole of Kaldo below her in every direction, lit, and the river going away silver to the marsh, and her hair down round her shoulders.

She did not let go of his hand.

She turned it over, instead, in both of hers, the way she turned a patient's hand over on the table; and slid her fingers up under the edge of his gauntlet to the inside of his wrist, and found the place, and held it.

She was quiet, counting. Her lips moved.

He knew what she would find. He could feel it himself, going in his wrist under her fingers, in his throat, behind the chest heart's plate.

She stopped counting. She looked up at him, and her face in the light coming up off the town was the face from the marsh, the one he had never seen on her before tonight; and there was something in the corner of her mouth that was very like the thing in Ferra's when a column came out right.

She stood up on her toes and kissed him. Short. Once.

"A hundred and twenty," said Imani.
