Chapter 187 - Nowhere to Hide

Isolde drew her father's house for them on the morning of the twenty-second, at the long-room table, in the chancellor's pen, from memory, and it took her two hours, and she did not get one door wrong.

She drew it the way Ferra had taught her to draw a ledger in February, with a rule and a steady hand: the house first, the long front with its two wings, the yard behind with the kennels and the stable and the brewhouse, the gate, the wall. Then every room in it, floor by floor, with its doors and its windows and its stairs. Then, in a second ink, the people. Who slept where. Her father and her mother in the great chamber over the hall. Bastian in the east wing, at the end, where he could come and go by the back stair without being heard. The two little ones with their nurse in the room over the kitchen, because it was warm. The steward, now, in her own old room, because it was nearest the stair and he liked to hear who went up and down it. The guardroom by the gate, with a cross in it for every man who would be there at the third hour of a winter night, and how many of them would be awake, and where they sat, and what they did with their hands.

"Dice," said Isolde, drawing a small square in the guardroom. "Two of them. Every night. They play for the next night's watch. The one who loses sits up." She looked at the square. "They think nobody knows."

"And the dogs?" said Imani.

"Four. In the kennel. They know me." Isolde put her pen down. "They'll know my shawl, if it's on somebody."

"They'll know my ether better," said Imani. "Dogs first. They always go first." She looked across the table at Emeric, very dry. "I've had practice."

When the drawing was done Emeric took it into the room. He sat in his chair at the long-room table with his eyes shut and the band against his wrist and took Isolde's two hours of pen and rule into the room with him, the way he had taken Linus's map, and laid it out on the floor there, every line, every cross; and when he opened his eyes again Isolde was looking at him across the table with her head on one side.

"You've gone somewhere," she said. "Just now. With your eyes shut."

"I've learned your house."

"In a breath?"

"In two hours," said Emeric. "Yours. I only had to watch."



Tess checked Osric's suit in the shed that evening, before they went, and nobody had asked her to.

Emeric saw it because he was at the bench by the river doors with the ether case open, counting pads. The suits stood on their racks in their row, black and violet in the lamplight, with their hearts charged and their capes furled and the shed's cold coming in off the river round them. Tess came down from the works on the motor at the sixth hour, which was not her hour; she came in through the river doors and stopped her chair at the end of the row, at Osric's rack, the third from the end with the lions on its breast, and looked at it.

Osric was not in the shed. He was in the keep yard with Declan, being told the house by Ward for the third time.

Tess leaned out of the chair and checked the suit. She checked the chest strap, which rode up when he turned, and the harness, and the watchdog's springs, and the cape's battens, and the chute's pack, and the cell latches in both hearts, and the leads to the count stone on the belt, with her quick cold hands and her face close to the plate, in the order she always checked them, without her book. She did not write anything down. When she had finished she sat back in her chair and looked at the suit for a while longer, at the lions on its breast, with her hands in her lap.

Then she turned the chair and went out through the river doors again, and up the slope toward the works, at a walking pace, wheels down.

She had not seen him at the bench. Or she had, and had decided he was not there. Emeric counted the next pad, and the one after, and lost his count, and began again.

He did not say anything to Osric, when Osric came in to put the suit on. Osric put it on, and buckled the chest strap, and turned, and lifted his arm after he turned and not before, and the strap did not ride up; and he looked down at it with a puzzled expression, as if it had done something clever on its own, and said, to nobody, "Huh," and put his helm on.



Two houses in one night, nine miles apart in one valley. That was the shape of it, and Ward had made them say it back to him in the yard until the words had stopped meaning anything and become a thing their mouths did.

The K Discovery went north at dusk on the twenty-second by the same road as before: down the river and through the marsh channels to the sea, dark, and up the coast flat out over the black water, and in over the land for the last hour slow and silent at a mile's height. Eleven aboard this time. Fletcher, Cade and Tobe. Imani in the saloon with the ether case on her knees and her bag at her feet. And seven in plate: Emeric, Ward, Declan and Osric for the Sallow house, in their suits, with their helms on their racks; and for Vane's, Rylan in his, and two knights of the garrison in their own mail and black cloaks, Corran and Brannoc, big quiet men Rylan had picked himself, who would go down the ladder because they had no suits and had not asked for any.

She came in over the valley at the third hour after midnight, when the lamps were all out, and held between the two houses, high, a mile up, over the dark spine of the hill that divided Sallow's land from Vane's.

Emeric lay on the deck and opened his sense, and put it down into the valley like a hand into dark water.

The Sallow house was asleep. He could feel the whole of it, from a mile up, in his sense, against Isolde's drawing in his room: the long front, the two wings, the yard. Every note in it, laid over her crosses, one for one. The great chamber over the hall, two notes, slow, a man's and a woman's. The east wing's end, one, young and restless even asleep. The room over the kitchen, three, two of them very small. The steward in her old room by the stair, awake, which he would be; his wrist would be hurting. The kennel, four, warm and close and dull. And the guardroom by the gate, eight notes, six of them asleep on their benches and two of them awake at a table, bent toward each other.

Dice.

And twelve more scattered about the yard and the stables and the brewhouse loft and the kitchen floor: grooms, a brewer, two cooks, a boy asleep in the hay. Twenty-two men, if you counted the boy, and Emeric did. And the family.

"As she drew it," he said, to Ward, aloud. "All of it. The steward's awake."

"He would be," said Ward. He put his helm on. "Imani."

Imani came up out of the saloon with the ether case. She gave each of them their pads, sealed in oilskin, in a pouch on the belt, and their flask, and she said the count to each of them again, the one she had said in the long room: hold it over the face, not on it, for a sleeper, and count to forty, and for a waker a hand over the mouth and the pad and hold and count to sixty and do not let go at thirty because they always go limp at thirty and come back at forty. She looked at Osric while she said the last part, and Osric nodded very seriously and said, "Sixty," and she nodded back.

"Nobody of the household dies," said Ward.

"Nobody of the household dies," said four voices.

They went over the rail.



On the capes, not the hearts. The hearts were loud. The capes made no sound at all but the cold air in the battens, a long whisper going down the dark.

Emeric went first. He let himself fall, and the watchdog threw at three, and he lay on the wing and slid down the black air toward the house in a long slant, with the others behind him strung out in a line he could feel and not see: Ward close, Declan steady, Osric last, lying on his wing like a man lying on a bed, not kicking, the way he had learned to over the river four times out of five. At the bottom, over the Sallow house's home field, they pulled the rings, and Sorrel's chutes threw, and they came down the last hundred feet under silk into wet grass two hundred yards from the wall, and lay in it, and gathered the silk, and did not get up.

Emeric opened the hair-width.

It was easier than in the air. He was on the ground, with no push to hold and no wind to read, and the three notes he wanted were close round him in the wet grass, Ward's wall and Declan's quiet and Osric's loud bright one that was never quiet even lying still. He found all three and held them, three doors the width of a hair, and the cost came in behind his eyes the way it did; and then he reached into the room and took up Isolde's drawing, and laid it over what they were seeing.

He felt Osric's breath catch.

The dark wall in front of them, and over it, faint, in thin lines like a pattern on a window, the house behind it, drawn in Isolde's hand. The yard. The kennel. The guardroom with its eight crosses. And moving in it, because he laid his sense into it as he held it, small faint marks where every note was: the six asleep on the benches, the two at the table, bent toward each other over the dice.

Dogs first, said Emeric, down the hair-width, to all three at once. Then the guardroom. Ward and Declan, the two at the table. Osric and I, the benches. Nobody runs.

Ward's note did the thing it did when Ward nodded.



The postern in the yard wall was barred inside with an oak bar in two iron brackets, as Isolde had drawn it. He put his hand flat on the outside of the door and found the bar through the wood with his sense, the dense dull note of old oak and the colder note of the iron; and lifted it. Not with a glyph. Not with a light. With the thing in him that he had cut in the room in the summer to do exactly this, and had practised on a bar in the keep's own cellar every night for a week until he could do it with his eyes shut and no sound: the weight in the bar taken off it, gently, and the bar lifted up out of its brackets the height of a finger, and laid down along the inside of the door on the flags, as softly as a man lays down a sleeping child.

The postern opened. It did not creak. Isolde had told them which hinge creaked, and Declan had brought a little oil.

The dogs were asleep in the kennel in a warm heap. They woke when the four black shapes came across the yard, all four at once, the way dogs do; and lifted their heads; and Emeric felt them decide whether to bark, the decision going through the four dull warm notes like wind through grass. Ward was already at the kennel door with Isolde's old blue shawl over his arm. He held it out. The dogs smelled it. One of them, a big grey hound with a white muzzle, got up and came to the bars and put its nose into the shawl and breathed in, long, and its tail moved, once, against the straw.

Imani's pads went in through the bars after the shawl. Four dogs. Forty, each. Ward counted under his breath. The big grey hound lay down with its nose still in the blue wool, and sighed, and slept.

The guardroom.

The two at the table had their backs to the door, both of them, bent over the dice, as Isolde had drawn them; the one losing had his elbows on the table and his head in his hands, and the one winning was saying something under his breath that was probably very rude. Ward and Declan went in through the door like smoke. A hand over each mouth, at the same breath; the pads over the hands; the men's chairs going over backward, slowly, held, so that they did not fall but were lowered; and their heels drumming once on the boards, and their hands coming up, and Ward and Declan holding them, and counting. Thirty. They went limp. Ward did not let go. Forty. They came back, both of them, a heave and a kick, as Imani had said they would, and Ward held his man as he would have held a horse, and Declan held his. Fifty. Sixty. And they went, properly, the long slack going-out of a man under ether that nothing would bring back for an hour.

Not a blow struck.

On the benches round the walls the six sleepers slept on, and Emeric and Osric went from bench to bench with the pads, over the faces, not on them, counting to forty each under their breath; and the snoring changed, man by man, from a sleeper's snoring to the slow deep breath of a man who will not wake if the roof comes in.

Osric counted out loud. Very softly. Emeric heard him, at the second bench, the third. Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty. He did not lose count once.

Then the yard, the stables, the brewhouse loft, the kitchen floor. The grooms in the straw. The brewer. The two cooks on their pallets by the banked fire. The boy in the hay, curled round himself, who did not wake at all, and to whom Osric gave only half a pad, and then stood over him for a while after to watch him breathe, and looked at Emeric, and Emeric said down the hair-width that the boy was fine, he could feel him, he was only asleep, and Osric nodded and moved on.

Twenty-two. Emeric counted them by his sense, every note in the yard and the outbuildings gone from a sleeper's waking-and-turning to the long flat stillness of the ether; and inside the house, the family, and the steward, and the nurse. Untouched. All of them asleep but the steward, and the young restless note at the end of the east wing.

Bastian was awake.



Osric took him on the back stair, as Isolde had drawn it, because Bastian came down it, as Isolde had said he would, with a candle and a sword, having heard something; and Osric, who had been standing in the dark at the stair's foot in his black plate with his lions on his breast, put out his hand in the dark and took the young man's sword arm at the wrist as he came off the last step, and held it.

That was all. He did not take the sword. He did not hit him. He held his wrist, with the sword in its hand pointing at the floor, and Bastian Sallow pulled, once, hard, with all a strong young man's strength, and his arm did not move; and he looked at the black gauntlet round his wrist, and up at the helm with its violet panes and the dark behind them, and the candle in his other hand shook, and he did not shout. Emeric felt him decide not to. His note went white and then very quiet.

"Good lad," said Osric, gently, through the helm. "You'll come down to the hall now."

The steward they took in his room, which had been Isolde's, sitting up in her old bed with his bandaged wrist in his lap and a candle lit, not asleep, as Emeric had known he would not be. He saw the door open and the black shape in it and did not make a sound at all, because, Emeric thought, there was nothing left in him that was surprised by anything that came out of the dark after the road; and he got up when Ward told him to, and came.

The nurse, and the little ones, and the lady of the house, Declan woke with a hand and a lamp, gently, and a low voice, and walked down.

Lord Aurel Sallow, Emeric woke himself.



A hand on the shoulder, and a lamp.

The great chamber over the hall was large and cold and hung with green and silver, the house's colours, faded, and the bed was a big curtained thing that had been old when Sallow's grandfather was born. Emeric drew the curtain back. The man in it was asleep on his back with his mouth open, a heavy grey-haired man of sixty with a broad face that had been handsome and was now mostly heavy, and a hand on the coverlet with three rings on it; and Emeric put his own hand, in its black gauntlet, on the man's shoulder, and held the lamp up beside his own helm, and waited.

Sallow opened his eyes.

He looked at the gauntlet on his shoulder. Then at the lamp. Then at the black helm beside it with its violet panes, a foot from his face, with nothing behind the panes but dark.

He did not shout either. Emeric had not been sure that he would not. A man of sixty, a lord, the queen's own brother, in his own bed, in his own house, with forty men under arms; he might have shouted for any of them. He opened his mouth to do it, Emeric saw, and then he listened, for the space of one breath, to his house. And his house was silent. No dogs. No feet on the stair. No voice in the yard. Not a sound, in the whole of it, the way a house is never silent, ever, not at the third hour of a winter night with twenty-two men in it.

He shut his mouth.

"Up," said Emeric. "Your family's in the hall."



The hall was long and dark and smelled of the evening's fire, and the fire was down to coals in the great hearth at its end, red and low; and there was one lamp, on the high table, that Declan had lit.

They were all there. Sitting where they had been put, on the bench along the hearth, in the red of the coals: the lady of the house, a thin dark woman in a night robe with a shawl round her shoulders, with a child asleep against each side of her, a boy and a girl, eight and six perhaps, who had been carried down and had gone straight back to sleep the way small children do. The nurse at the bench's end, with her hands over her mouth. The steward, standing, with his bound wrist held against his chest, very straight. And Bastian, at the bench's other end, on his feet, with his sword still in his hand and Osric's black gauntlet still round his wrist, and his face in the coal light white and young and furious and frightened, all at once.

In every doorway, a black knight. Ward at the great doors. Declan at the stair. Osric with Bastian. Not one of them moved.

Emeric walked Lord Sallow down his own stair, in his nightshirt, with a hand on his arm, not hard, and brought him into the hall, and stopped him at the high table, by the lamp.

Then he took his helm off.

He did it so that they would all know whose face it was. That was the whole point of it; he had decided it in the long room on the morning of the twentieth with Ferra's four lines in front of him, and Ward had argued against it for an hour and then stopped, because Ward knew it was right. A black knight in the night was a demon, a story, a thing to be frightened of and argued about and never answered. A face was a man. A man could be answered. And a man who could be answered was a man who could be held to what he said.

The hall looked at his face in the lamplight.

Sallow knew it. Of course he knew it. He had sat at his sister's table in the capital, at the king's, with this face at the far end of it, the drunk third son of the dead queen, staring at his cup. Emeric watched him know it. He watched it go through the heavy grey face like a crack through ice: the face, and the name, and everything that had been said at dinners in the capital about the face and the name for years, and then the fact of it, here, in his hall, in black plate, at the third hour of the morning, in a house where nothing made a sound.

"You," said Sallow.

"Me."

Emeric took the folded paper out of his belt. Not Ferra's blank one. The other. The marriage lines, in Hale's fair hand, on the mill's best paper, with the barony's seal at the foot of it pressed so hard into the red wax that the arms stood up off the sheet. He laid it on the high table in front of Sallow, by the lamp, and turned it round so that it faced him, and put his finger on the names.

"Your daughter was married yesterday," he said. "In Kaldo's church. By Father Doran. In daylight, with the doors open, in front of the town. She said yes so loud they heard it in the lane." He took his finger away. "To Sir Jabari Mensah. Who resigned his post to you in writing before he left it. You'll have had the letter. Your sergeant at the tower will have sent it."

Sallow did not look at the paper.

"You've stolen my daughter," he said. His voice was thick. "Out of my own coach. On my own road. Your black things came out of the sky and took her and left my man tied to a wheel with a letter that said nothing." He looked at Emeric with his heavy face working. "Nothing. Do you hear? A sealed letter, with nothing in it."

"There was nothing in it," said Emeric, "because there was nothing to say to you then. This is what there is to say."

And he took his hand away from the table, and did not lift it, and did not point it, and did not look at the wall.

There was a sword above the hearth. Sallow's own; Isolde had drawn it on her plan, in its place, on two iron pegs above the chimney piece, a long old blade in a worn scabbard with green and silver on its hilt, that had been Sallow's father's and his father's, and had not been taken down off its pegs, she said, in her lifetime.

It came down off its pegs.

By itself. Nobody near it. No glyph. No light. Not a sound but the scabbard's iron chape scraping once on the stone of the chimney piece as it came free. It came away from the wall and down through the air of the hall, slowly, level, over the heads of the lady and her sleeping children on the bench, over the coals, the length of the hall at the height of a man's chest, as if somebody very tall and very careful and entirely invisible were carrying it in both hands; and it came to the high table, and came down onto it beside the marriage lines, and lay there, along the table's edge, with its hilt turned toward its owner.

The hall had stopped breathing.

Nobody in that hall had ever seen magic without a glyph. Nobody in Randillia had. Emeric had known that when he cut the thing in the room in the summer, and known it when he practised it on the cellar bar, and known it in the long room with Ward arguing; and he watched them all know it now. The lady on the bench with her hand gone white on her son's shoulder. The nurse with both hands over her mouth. The steward, very straight, with his eyes shut. Bastian, at the bench's end, with his own sword in his own hand and Osric's gauntlet on his wrist, staring at his grandfather's sword lying on the table where nobody had put it, and every line of his young body pulled toward it.

And Sallow. Sallow looked at the sword, and at the face of the man who had not lifted a hand, and at the black knights in his doorways who had not moved; and Emeric saw him understand, slowly, all the way down, what had been done to his house tonight, and that he would never in his life be able to tell anybody how.

"Your daughter Isolde Mensah," said Emeric, "and her husband Sir Jabari, are under Kaldo's protection. From tonight. Kaldo will ride for them. Wherever they are. Whoever comes." He put his hand flat on the table between the marriage lines and the sword. "Any hair on either of their heads is war between your house and mine. Not a quarrel. Not a suit in a court. War."

Sallow said nothing.

"I walked into your house tonight while it slept," said Emeric. "Every man you keep is asleep in your yard and your guardroom and your hay, and not one of them saw a face, and not one of them will wake with a mark on him. Your dogs are asleep. Your gate's shut. Your doors are barred, except the one we came in by, and you'll find that one barred too, in the morning, from the inside." He looked at the man across the table. "If I can reach you here, in your own hall, in your nightshirt, with your wife and your children round you, then there's nowhere on any battlefield in Randillia you could hide from me." He was quiet. "And I won't have to hide there."

The coals settled in the hearth. One of the children on the bench stirred against its mother and said something in its sleep, and was still.

"I'll remember this," said Bastian.

His voice was very young. He said it from the end of the bench, with his arm in Osric's grip and his grandfather's sword on the table in front of him and his face white in the coal light; and he said it to Emeric, straight, looking at him, the way a young man says a thing he has decided he will say whatever it costs him.

Emeric looked at him.

"Good," he said. "Remember all of it." He picked his helm up off the table. "Remember that nobody in your house was hurt tonight. Remember your sister chose, out loud, in front of a town. Remember who came to tell you so to your face, instead of in a letter with nothing in it." He put the helm under his arm. "Remember all of it. Not only the part you'll want to."

He turned to go. At the great doors, where Ward stood, he stopped, and looked back once down the long dark hall: the lady on the bench with her sleeping children; the steward with his eyes shut; the young man with his arm held; and the old man at the high table in his nightshirt, by the lamp, standing over a sheet of paper and a sword, with both his hands flat on the table on either side of them, the way a man stands at a table when he has been told a figure he cannot argue with.

"Osric," said Emeric. "Let him go."

Osric let go of Bastian's wrist. Bastian did not move. He stood at the end of the bench with the sword in his hand and did not lift it, and did not lift it, and they went out.



The bar went back into its brackets inside the postern behind them, lifted from outside, the way it had come out, and laid down gently. In the morning there would be twenty-two men waking in the yard and the guardroom and the hay with headaches and nothing to say; and a lord at his high table who had not gone back to bed; and a sword on the table that nobody in the house would ever be able to bring themselves to put back on its pegs. And every door in the house barred from the inside.

They went up the ladder from the home field, one after another, with the silk of the chutes stuffed into their belts, and the K Discovery took them in at a mile's height in the dark over the hill; and Emeric came over her rail last and lay down on her deck on his back and let the hair-width go, all three doors at once, and the cost came in behind his eyes like a closing hand and stayed.

Imani was on the deck beside him. He had not heard her come up. She took his wrist, inside the gauntlet's edge, without asking, and counted.

"You've been holding three," she said.

"For an hour."

"An hour and three quarters," said Imani. "I watched the glass." She did not let go of his wrist. "You'll have the headache till noon tomorrow. You'll eat what I give you and you'll not argue." She was quiet, counting. "Did anybody die?"

"Nobody."

"Good," said Imani. She let go of his wrist, and put her hand flat on his chest plate instead, over the left side, the way she had in the gate passage once, and in his office the morning she kissed him, and left it there. "Then lie still, and let me be pleased about it."



Rylan's team came up the ladder half an hour later, over Vane's woods, nine miles up the valley. Corran and Brannoc on the treads, and Rylan on his hearts behind them, quietly, the violet of his panes the only light.

He told it on the deck, in the dark, with his helm off and his black hair wet with the cold. He told it the way he told anything, which was briefly, and with a kind of grim enjoyment that he did not try to hide.

Vane's house kept fewer men than Sallow's, and kept them worse. Corran and Brannoc had put the four at the gate under before any of them had looked up. There had been one dog, old and deaf, that had not woken at all. And Lord Haskett Vane, fifty-eight and kind to his dogs, had woken at the third hour of the morning in his own bed to find a black knight with lions on his breast sitting on the end of it, with a knife across his knees.

"Not holding it up," said Rylan. "Across my knees. Just there. So he could see it."

"What did you say?" said Ward.

"One sentence. He'd only have remembered one." Rylan looked at the dark valley below the rail. "I told him the lady wasn't coming for the season. And that the eight men he'd hired to bring her were left alive in a ditch on his own road four nights ago, by people who could have left them otherwise. And so was he, tonight." He put his helm down on the deck. "Then I got up off his bed and went out by his door."

"Did he say anything?"

"He asked if I'd shut it behind me," said Rylan. "I said I would. I did." He sat down on the deck with his back to the rail. "Kind to his dogs. She was right about that." He shut his eyes. "I'd not have wanted her there either."

Nobody on the ground saw the ship go. She went north first, the wrong way, over the hills, and turned west over the dark country to the sea, and down the coast flat out over the water with the dawn coming up behind the land to her left; and she was in the marsh channels at sunrise, and in the shed by the full light of the morning, and Fletcher had the river doors shut before the first eel boat came down.



Isolde and Jabari went north on the fifth of December, by road, in daylight, under Kaldo's seal.

Not to her father's house. To a small house in a valley further west that had been her mother's mother's, and had come down to her mother and from her mother to her, in a settlement her father had signed when he married and had never thought of since: a farmhouse with an orchard and a mill and forty acres, on land that was hers and not Sallow's, which Ferra had found in two days in the crown's register by reading every marriage settlement of Sallow's house for forty years. Her father could not touch it. Ferra had made sure of it, in writing, with a copy of the settlement on the mill's paper under the barony's seal, and a second copy in the crown's register beside the marriage lines.

Six of the garrison rode with them as far as the edge of the north, in Kaldo's colours, openly, on the capital road, so that every posting house between Kaldo and the north would see whose seal they rode under and say so. On the door of the coach, which had been Saban's and was now theirs, Abloh had painted, very small, beside the hinge, where only somebody looking for it would see it, the barony's arms.

Ferra walked down to the South Gate to see them go. Emeric walked with her. In the gate yard, with the coach waiting and Jabari already up on the box beside the driver, because he would not ride inside a coach, Isolde got down again, and came back across the yard, and stopped in front of Ferra.

"There was a man on the road," she said. "By the back wheel. When the lamp broke and the oil went up, I saw his face." She said it quickly, quietly, the way she had once told Emeric about the four greys. "Young. A scar through his left eyebrow, here." She touched her own. "A mouth that had been laughing before it all went wrong for him. He wasn't my father's. He wasn't Vane's. I'd know any of theirs." She looked at Ferra. "I'll know him again. Wherever he is. I wanted somebody to have it written."

Ferra took out her book, there in the gate yard, in the cold, and wrote it. Every word, in her small upright hand. Then she closed the book.

"It's written," she said.

"And if you ever need eyes in my father's house," said Isolde, "you have them."

Ferra looked at her.

"You said that about your aunt's," she said. "In February."

"I did." Isolde smiled her quick smile, and this time it did not go away. "I didn't have any eyes in my aunt's house. I had a maid who could be believed and a steward with a cough. I said it because it was the only thing I had to give you and I wanted to give you something." She put her gloved hand out, as a man would have, and Ferra took it. "This time it's true. My mother's in that house. And my maid. And my little brother and sister, who are going to grow up, and who were asleep on a bench in the hall the night the sword came off the wall, and who will be told about it every winter for the rest of their lives." She shook Ferra's hand once, firmly, the way Ferra had taught her at the counting room table. "And Bastian. Who'll remember all of it. I know my brother. He will."

She let go. She turned to Emeric.

"Thank you," she said. "For deciding something real." And she went back across the yard and got into the coach, and the door shut, and Jabari on the box lifted one big hand to them both, and the coach went out under the arch and away down the capital road between the six riders, toward the north.

Ferra stood in the gate yard and watched it go until it was round the bend.

"She cried on a horse in front of her maid," she said. "She told me. Over his letter." She put the book back in her pocket. "I've never cried on a horse." She turned to go back up the hill. "I've never been on a horse I didn't fall off."

"You rode behind me to the capital."

"That wasn't a horse," said Ferra. "That was you." And went up the high street, and did not look back to see whether he had heard it, because she knew he had.
