Chapter 187: The Chair

The packet came down on the crown post in the first week of the winter, on the rider's next run south.

It was addressed to Saban, and it was not from any of Saban's people. It was sealed in plain wax with no device, in a clerk's hand nobody in Kaldo knew, and Saban did not open it at the gate. He carried it up to the keep in his coat and did not open it at supper either, and sat through supper with it, talking about the price of wool with Bram, which he did very well and which Bram did not notice was being done to him.

He brought it to the long-room table after the others had gone up. Emeric was at the head with the day book, and Ward was in his chair by the door, and the fire had burned down to the part of the evening when a room gets larger.

Saban put the packet on the table and broke the seal and unfolded it.

There were two sheets of copy, close-written, in the clerk's hand. And folded round them, on a slip of the capital's grey paper, one line in a hand that Emeric did know, because he had stood in front of it twice and read it on a ruling a great many times more.

The roll is public. The charter filings are not, but a magistrate may read them. I have had both copied. I did not ask why. K.

Emeric looked at it, and then at Saban.

"You went to Keller."

"In the spring," said Saban. "While you were at court. You'd told me to keep looking, and I'd been looking on the coast road for a year and losing a courier past Milford every quarter like a fool. So I looked somewhere a courier doesn't go." He put his hand flat on the two sheets. "Every chartered house files its people with the crown's judiciary. Partners and factors. It's been the law since the old king's time, so that a man who's lent against can find out who's lending. The factor roll's public. Anybody can read it. Nobody ever does. It's in a room on Keller's floor with one clerk in it and the clerk was asleep."

"And you read it."

"An afternoon." Saban turned the first sheet round so that it faced Emeric across the table. "That's my copy of the part I wanted. Keller's clerk made it clean."

It was a column out of a ledger. House Ducatis, factors, by working name, with the year each was posted and the post, going back sixty years.

Emeric found it without looking for it, because it was there four times.

Vantic, posted to the coast house, in a year before Emeric's father was born. Vantic, posted to a salt works in the west, twenty years after that, died in post. Vantic, a house in the capital, eleven years. And the last line of the column, in the clerk's clean copy: Vantic, posted to the south, under the covenant of Kaldo. And the year.

He read it twice.

The house's factors work under house names, she had said, at the Western Gate, with the strongbox in the cart behind her, to Ferra, for the register. Vantic will do. And Ferra had written it down, and nobody had asked about it again, because it was a dull answer and a complete one. It had been true. Every word of it had been true. He had simply not heard what it meant.

It was not a name. It was a chair. There had been a Vantic before her, and one before that, and one who had died at a salt works in the west, and there would be one after her, and the house would hand the next one the same word the way it handed the next clerk the same stool.

He sat with that for a while. He had learned a good many hard things about how the world was run since he woke in this body, and most of them had been worse than this, and none of them had been colder.

"The second sheet," Saban said.

The second sheet was not public. That was what the slip meant. It was the charter filing: the house itself, as it had to declare itself to the crown every ten years. The name of the house. The seat. The principal, the head of the house, with his age and his style. And under him, set in by the clerk as the filing required, the family.

Three children.

The eldest son, and against his name the book he held: the crown's paper, and the high houses'. The second son, and against his: trade, the coast, the shipping. And the third.

A daughter. Livia.

Against her name, where her brothers had their books, the clerk had written nothing at all.

Emeric did not say the name. He read it, and put his finger on the empty space beside it where the book should have been, and left it there.

"There's no post against her," Saban said. "Because her post's on the other roll. Under the chair." He sat back. "A daughter of the house, at nineteen, taken on at the coast house under a working name, with a principal to stand for her who conveniently died. And posted, when she'd done her years at whatever they gave her before us, to the worst book in the south. Which was us."

"And the courier."

"The courier I got in the autumn." Saban had not touched the wine. He did now, and put it down. "I stopped trying to follow him and put a man at every crossroads west of Milford instead, each one to pick him up where the last lost him. He's good. He changes roads. But he goes to the same place every time. There's a harbour on the coast a day past Milford, a fishing harbour, nothing in it. A cutter out of the coast house puts in there on the Thursday tide, and he gives her the bag, and she goes back west on the same tide. One of her crew drinks. My man drank with him." He turned his cup. "There's a bag in her hand on that cutter every week, he says. Not only the quarter days, when her courier comes from us. Every week, from wherever she is, by whatever road, it's on the quay on the Thursday tide, and it goes west to the coast house. Not the capital. The capital branch holds our paper, and it gets her quarter's accounts by the House's own rider, the way a factor's supposed to send them. The coast house is where the family started. It's where the old man lives. He hasn't been to the capital in six years. Everybody in that harbour knows him. They call him the old man, as if there were only one."

"Every week," said Emeric.

"Every week. As long as that man's crewed her, and he's crewed her six years."

Emeric thought of the first year of the loan, when she had lived in Kaldo, and the letters going out of the Western Gate every week, and some weeks twice. He had seen them go himself, and taken them for diligence. A factor did not write every week. A factor wrote at the quarter and when something happened. A letter every week whether anything had happened or not was not a factor reporting to a house.

It was somebody being read.

"She's never given a term," said Ward.

They both looked at him. He had not moved in his chair by the door. He had his hands on his knees and he was looking at the fire, not at them.

"Not one," Ward said. "In seven years. I've sat by the door at every table she's sat at. A hired factor gives a little. A week on a date. A Copper on a rate. A clause he doesn't care about, to be liked, because being liked buys him next year. She's never given a Copper. Not on anything. Not on things that didn't matter." He looked at Emeric. "Somebody reads every one."

"You never said."

"It wasn't mine to spend," said Ward. "It wasn't anybody's. It was just true."

Saban folded the two sheets together and laid Keller's slip on top of them and put his hand on the pile.

"So," he said. "What do you want done?"

Emeric could see three things to do before Saban had finished asking, and he did not like that he could.

He could see them very clearly. That was the trouble with them. The House lent against his city and his works, a hundred and twenty Platinum and a second draw on the way, and the House was a family, and the family had a daughter it was grading on his book, and he now knew which of the family's hands the grades went to. A letter to the coast house, courteous, in his own hand, from the prince whose book it was. A word in the right ear in the capital about which of the three was doing the House's work in the south. Or nothing said to anybody, and the knowledge kept, and brought out across a table on the day the House pressed him for something he did not want to give, the way you bring out a card you have held all night.

Every one of them would work. He had spent ten years learning how things like that were done, from men who did them to him, and he had become good at it, and here it was, laid out on his own table on two sheets of clean copy.

And he thought of a yard, in his first year, with forty men in it. And a garrison clerk named Corliss who had held the provisioning line for eleven years, and a stack of paper with four independent confirmations on it, and a question asked in front of all of them because Emeric had been certain. He had been right about the paper. He had not been right about the man. Nobody had said Corliss's name at this table in a very long time, and nobody needed to. He had learned what it cost to move on a thing you could see, before you knew the thing you could not.

What he could see was a name, a chair, a cutter on the Thursday tide.

What he could not see was all the rest of it. Why a daughter of the largest lending house in the kingdom had taken the worst book in the south and walked it for seven years and never once said so. What the grades were for. What she wanted. Whether she wanted anything, or only wanted not to be put somewhere she had not chosen.

"Call your men off the coast road," he said.

Saban looked at him.

"All of them. The harbour too. The man who drinks." Emeric put his hand on the pile beside Saban's, not on top of it. "It's hers. When she wants us to have it, she'll say. Until then we haven't got it."

"We have got it," said Saban.

"Then we act as if we haven't. Which is harder, and it's what we're doing."

Saban looked at him for a long moment, the way he had looked at him in the autumn, when Ferra had said something about a case getting heavier. Then he took his hand off the pile.

"And these?"

"Ferra's box," said Emeric. "Tomorrow. At the table. They'll all hear it once, from me, and then nobody says it again."

Ward got up and put another log on the fire, which he did not usually do, and went out without saying goodnight, which he did not usually do either.

He told the table the next evening, after supper, with the boards cleared.

He told it the way Ward told a road: flat, in order, and short. The roll. The four Vantics. The filing and the empty space beside a name. The cutter on the Thursday tide, and the old man at the coast house, and a letter every week for seven years. He did not say the name. He said a daughter of the house, and let the table do the rest. And then he said what he had decided, which was that nothing would be done with it, by anybody, ever, and that it did not leave this room, and that if she ever chose to say it herself, whoever she said it to would hear it for the first time.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Armand spoke first. He had his hands folded on the head of his stick, and he said it to the fire, the way he said the weather.

"I met the father," he said. "Once. Forty years ago. He came to the Circle, a young man, to ask what a mage cost a lord by the year, all told: the pay, the lodging, the years he could work, the years he could not. We told him. He wrote it all down in a small book and thanked us and went away, and I thought at the time he was the first man who had ever come up those stairs who wanted to know the price of us instead of the use." He turned his stick a quarter turn on the flags. "I supposed he was going to lend against it. I never knew whether he did."

Bram said nothing at all. He sat with his big hands on the table and looked at them. After a while he said, "She walks the works. Every quarter day. With her book." He thought about it. "She asked me once how long a blow was in the converter, start to finish. I told her. She wrote it down." That was all he said.

Imani said, "Seven years of a letter a week," and did not say anything after it, and looked at her hands too.

Saban said nothing, because he had said it all the night before and had nothing to add to it, and Saban did not talk for the sound of it at this table, whatever he did at others.

Ferra had her book open in front of her, as she always did at the table, with the pen beside it. She had not picked the pen up while he talked. She did not pick it up now.

"It was never going in the book," she said.

That was all. Nobody said anything about it. Emeric put Saban's two sheets and Keller's slip on the table in front of her, and she folded them once more, small, and took the key off the cord round her neck and put them in the box, under the stones, and locked it, and put the key back.

Then Bram asked about the rail spur to the quarry, and they talked about that.

The frost came early that winter, and it came hard, and it caught the first quarter's second water tower with its base course set and its mortar green. Weekes came up to the long room with the news and the figure in the same breath. The course would have to come out and go back in the spring, and the draw the House had set against the tower in the second instrument was dated for the week after midwinter, when the tower was meant to be standing to the first floor, and would not be.

"A fortnight," Ferra said, working it. "If the draw moves a fortnight past the thaw, the tower's where the instrument says it is when the House's man walks it. If it doesn't move, we draw against a hole in the ground, and the House's man writes that down."

"Then we ask for a fortnight," said Emeric.

He did not go back to the papers. He sat looking at Weekes's figure, and it was not the tower he was thinking about. A tower's course could come out in the spring and go back in. The dock at the ridge foot could not. It would stand with the lower water against its gate on every tide, and with the sea let in on purpose every time a hull was floated in it, and lime did not set under water at all. It set in the air or it did not set. In a dock it would lie in the joints soft as cheese, and the first time they pumped the dock dry the sea would come back in through the walls.

"Ferra," he said. "The pumice that comes up on the Emberfell. How much of it do we sell?"

She did not look up from the figure. "Most of it. Bram keeps a few sacks for polishing."

"Stop selling it."

She looked up then. He was already writing.

Vantic came for the winter quarter day nine days before midwinter, in the House's carriage, with her small book and her clerk and her courier, and sat at the loan table in the counting room across from Ferra, and read Ferra's book.

Emeric came down for the part that needed him. He had come down for the part that needed him on most quarter days for seven years, and sat at the head of the table while the two women went through the instrument clause by clause, and answered what he was asked, and gone back up. He did it now. He sat at the head of the table with Ward in the chair by the door, and watched Vantic read, and found that he was watching her the way he had watched the roll: as a thing he had been looking at for seven years and had never once actually read.

She read the way she always read. Steadily, with her pen in her hand and her small book open at her elbow, copying what the House needed and nothing it did not. Her face was the face she kept for tables. She asked Ferra about the frost, because she had read about it in the book before Ferra could tell her, and about the tower, and Ferra told her.

Then she came to the draw.

"You'll want it moved," she said.

"A fortnight," said Emeric. "Past the thaw. So the tower's where your instrument says it is when your man walks it."

It was nothing. It was the smallest thing he had ever asked the House for. Any factor in the kingdom would have written it into the margin and initialled it and been thanked for it, and taken the thanks to the next quarter day as a small coin to spend.

Vantic looked at the clause for a moment.

"I can't give you that," she said. Pleasantly. In exactly the voice she used for the price of wool. "The instrument dates the draw. The date's the House's, not mine. I'll write for it." She wrote a line in her own book as she said it. "You'll have an answer by the spring quarter, and I expect it will be yes, because it ought to be. But I can't give it you across this table."

A week ago he would have heard a clerk, careful of her standing, doing her job. He had heard it on every quarter day for seven years. He heard it now for what it was: a woman who could not afford to spend one Copper of discretion, on anything, ever, because somebody at the far end of a Thursday tide read every word she wrote and would read this one too.

"Thank you," he said.

She glanced up at him. It was only a glance. He had said thank you on quarter days before. Something in how he had said it must have been different, because she looked at him for about as long as it takes to draw a breath. Then she looked down again, and went on to the paper works.

He did not say anything else. He did not look at Ward, and Ward did not look at him.

When she closed her book at the end of the morning, a folded sheet at the back of it slid a little way out, and she pushed it in again with one finger without looking at it. He caught the edge of it. A table, ruled small. Names down the side, and Kaldo the first of them, and columns across. He did not know what it was. He saw Ferra see him see it, and saw Ferra not say anything, and he did not ask.

Vantic went down the stair with her book under her arm and her clerk behind her. The carriage went out of the Western Gate on the road north, as it always did.

The courier went the other way.

Emeric went up onto the gate tower to watch him go. He did not know why he did it. He stood on the tower in the cold with his hands in his coat and watched a man on a bay horse with a bag on his saddle go out along the coast road toward Milford at an easy trot, not looking back, the way he had gone every quarter day for seven years. There was nobody behind him. There was nobody at the crossroads past Milford, or at the harbour, or on the quay on the Thursday tide. Saban's men had come home a fortnight ago and been paid and asked nothing.

The rider went over the first rise and was gone.

He had her name now. It was in a clerk's clean copy in the bottom of Ferra's box, under his mother's stones and a column of Armand's readings, in the dark, with the key on a cord round Ferra's neck. He had read it once, at the long-room table, with his finger on the empty space beside it where her brothers had their books.

He found, standing on the gate tower, that he did not want to say it. Not aloud. Not in his own head, even. It was the only thing she had in Kaldo that was hers and not the House's, and she had kept it seven years without spending it, and he was not going to be the one who spent it for her.

He went down out of the cold to see about the tower.
