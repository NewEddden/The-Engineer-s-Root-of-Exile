Chapter 204 - Yield

Strade wrote six lines to a letter and never more, and he wrote his tallies in the black.

Nobody had expected it. Least of all Saban, who had said in June that Strade would pour his case into the river the night of the dinner; and Strade had not poured it anywhere. He had used it. Not for letters; his letters went on writing themselves in the brown iron gall from Ambry's front shop, six lines at a time, all summer, and never once came through the knots. But on the fourth of August, at six in the morning, in the small square room with the fire laid and not lit, Strade sat down at his desk and opened a ledger and dipped his pen in the prince's ink; and the printer's bed behind Ambry's began to move.

"Because it's good ink," said Ferra, when she brought the first page out to Sethe's counting room. She laid it on the table. "That's all. He doesn't believe a word you say. He believes ink is ink. He looked through the bottle at the candle and saw it was the best black he'd ever seen, and he's a man who wastes nothing." She sat down. "So he uses it for the one thing in his house he wants to last."

It was a grid. That was all it was, to look at. A page ruled in Strade's own very neat hand, faint grey on the sized sheet, into columns; and at the head of each column a word; and down the left, dates, a line to a week; and in the cells, figures. Small, careful, upright figures, with no flourish on any of them.

Intake. Held against rent. Yield. Kept. Losses.

The page was July. The second page, which came off the bed an hour later, was June. Strade was copying his book. The third was May.

By noon there were nine pages. By evening, fourteen; Strade had worked all day, through his dinner, as a man works who has decided his old ledger is falling to pieces and wants a fair copy in an ink that will outlast him. The figures went back two years, a page a month, a line a week. And Ferra sat at the table in Sethe's counting room with all fourteen of them in front of her in a row, and her pencil, and her own book open beside them; and added.

She added every column, down and across. She added the rows. She found that Intake plus what had been Held the month before, less Losses, came out exactly to what was Held at the month's end, every month, without a single error in two years, because Strade did not make errors. She found that Yield, divided by what was Held, came out at nearly the same figure every month, a little over two. She found that Kept was always a little under a third of Yield.

She found everything a column could tell her. She could not find what any of it was.

"It's not paper," she said at last. It was past ten. She had not looked up for an hour. "It can't be paper. Nobody counts paper in ones and twos. Intake, three, in the first week of June. Two in the third. Losses, one, in the second week of May. Held against rent, fourteen, sixteen, fifteen." She put her pencil down. "Fourteen of what? Held against whose rent? What yields two of something a week for each one you hold?"

"Cattle," said Saban, from the window. "A man holds cattle against a debt. They calve."

"Not two a week." Ferra did not look round. "And Strade's never kept a cow in his life. He hasn't a field. He has a counting house and a small house with no garden." She looked at the grid. "I can add it. I can add it in my sleep. It's the neatest book I've ever seen." Her voice had gone very quiet. "I can't read it."

Emeric stood at the end of the table and looked at the fourteen pages and did not say anything at all.

He had a guess. He had had it since the second page, since the word Yield sat beside Held against rent and the numbers in the two columns stayed tied to each other, month after month, as close as a pulse to a heart. He did not like the guess. He liked it less the longer he looked at it, because it fitted, and because it came out of a book, a book from his other life about a war, and the camps in it, and what one kind of man had done with another kind when there was a use to be had out of them; and not out of Wayford. Not out of anything he had seen with his own eyes on this river.

His knowledge was pages. It was not ground.

"Ottoline," he said.



She came across the square at half past ten at night in a shawl over her nightgown, with her spectacles on her forehead and her hair in a long grey plait, because Ferra's clerk had knocked on her door and said the Chancellor would be grateful, and Ottoline Fesk had never once in four years refused the chance to see Ferra grateful.

She sat down at the table in Sethe's counting room without being asked, pulled the lamp toward her, and looked at the grid. She did not ask where it had come from. She looked at the column heads. She looked at the dates. She ran her finger down Held against rent, slowly, month by month, and stopped, and ran it down Yield.

"Strade's," she said. "Nobody else rules a line that straight." She did not look up. "What do you want to know?"

"What it is," said Ferra.

"I'll tell you what I've seen," said Ottoline, "and you can tell me what it is." She sat back. "I've a shop on the square, Chancellor. East side, two windows, a clock over the door that's stopped. I sleep over it, badly, I always have, since my husband. And for twenty years I've watched the Staple's carts go across the square under my window at night." She took her spectacles off her forehead and folded them in her lap. "Not the paper carts. The paper carts go by day, up the hill, with their loads covered and the drivers singing, and everybody sees them. I mean the night cart."

"One cart?" said Emeric.

"One. Small. Closed, like a baker's, with a door at the back. Two horses, not one, which is a lot of horse for a small cart. It goes across the square from the north, from the Staple house, at two in the morning, once a week, down the south road. And it comes back on the third night after, empty, the same way. I've timed it for twenty years. I'm a clockmaker." She put her finger on the Yield column. "Once a week. Four, five times a month. Look at your dates, Chancellor. The Yield's entered once a week. The day after the cart goes."

Ferra looked. She did not say anything. Her pencil moved down the dates, and stopped, and moved, and stopped.

"And it's heavy," said Ottoline. "Going. For its size. You can hear it on the cobbles. And light coming back." She put the spectacles back on her forehead. "And once, the winter before last, the driver got down to piss at the horse trough below my window, at two in the morning, in the snow, thinking nobody was awake, and left the back door of the cart on the latch. And a box fell out of it onto the cobbles."

Nobody in the counting room moved.

"Small," said Ottoline. "The size of a lady's jewel box. Iron-bound. Heavy; it hit the cobbles like a stone. And he came round the cart so fast, Chancellor. I've never seen a man come round a cart that fast. He picked it up in both hands and held it against his chest and looked at it all over, the way a man looks at a baby he's dropped, and put it back in, and shut the door, and stood there in the snow with his back against the door a long time, shaking." She looked at the grid. "Over a box. I thought it was jewels. I thought the Staple was sending its plate south to be sold, a box a week, the five of them selling their silver because the trade had gone bad. I thought that for two years." She put her finger on Losses. "And then in the spring the driver stopped coming. A new one came. A younger one."

"The old driver," said Emeric. "Did you know his name?"

"Everybody knew it. He drank at the posting house on the third night, when he came back with the cart empty. A south-road man. Grey, gone lame these last years, with a bad eye." Ottoline frowned. "Cobb."

Emeric stood very still at the end of the table.

Ferra's pencil had stopped. She turned the page under her hand, without being asked, to the second week of May; and there in the Losses column was the figure one, as there had been since the first; and beside it, for the first time on any page, in Strade's smallest and neatest hand, as if the man had felt for once that a figure was not enough: C., carriage.

Cobb. The courier on the south road whose name had sat in Wexler's ledgers at Kaldo eleven years before Emeric came, a sum every quarter as regular as rent, going back years; the man Petram had seen once with Garrin in a stable yard. The name he had set beside the things he had stopped expecting to resolve cleanly. He had looked for Cobb on the south road for seven years and never found a hair of him. Nobody had thought to look on the north one. And Cobb had been here, driving a closed cart across a square at two in the morning, once a week, for twenty years, with an iron box in the back that he held like a baby.

And in May, in Strade's neat hand, he had become a Loss.

"Go on," said Emeric. His voice sounded strange to him. "Please. Read the rest."

Ottoline looked at him over the lamp for a long breath. Then she looked back at the grid, and put her finger on the first column, and read it, slowly, the way she would have read a clock face to a child.

"Intake," she said. "Two, three, a month. New ones. Coming in." Her finger moved. "Held against rent. Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen. The ones they've got. Held, for what they owe; for rent, for debt, the way the old law lets you hold a man against what he owes you, in the north, if a magistrate signs." She did not look up. "The Staple has a magistrate. Everybody knows that. He dines with Placit." Her finger moved. "Losses. One, now and then. One in May. Gone." She stopped at Yield. "And this is what you get. From each one you hold. A little over two a week, Chancellor. Two of something, every week, from every one of them, that goes south in an iron box on a cart at night, so heavy a lame man shakes for a quarter of an hour in the snow when he drops it." She took her finger off the page. "And they keep a third. That's Kept."

She sat back.

"They're not counting paper," said Ottoline Fesk. "Or cattle. They're counting men." She looked at Ferra, and then at Emeric, and her sharp old face in the lamplight was quite still. "I don't know what you get out of a man that goes in a box. I'm a clockmaker. I can tell you it's heavy, and it's small, and it's worth enough that a man holds it like a baby." She stood up, and pulled her shawl round her. "You'll know what it is, Highness. I can see in your face you know what it is. You knew before I came in." She went to the door. "You didn't say. You let me read it. I'll remember that."

She went out. Nobody stopped her. They heard her go down the passage and out by the hall door, and across the square in the dark to her shop with the stopped clock, and the bell over her door ring once, and stop.

Ferra sat at the table with the fourteen pages in front of her and her hand flat beside them. She did not say anything. She was white to the lips.

"Stones," said Emeric.

She did not look up.

"You can draw a man's mana into a stone. Anybody can, who knows how. Mages, knights. A commoner, even, if somebody taught him. You put a stone against a man and you draw, and the mana comes out of him into it, and he's left with what an ordinary man has, which is very little, and he's tired, and he gets it back by morning; and you do it again." He was looking at the Yield column. "Every morning. Like milking. Two stones a week, from every man you hold, filled from a man's own life. And a stone filled is worth a great deal, to anybody, anywhere, and nobody asks where the charge came from, because a charge is a charge." He stopped. "They don't get it back forever. Not every morning, for years. Take it slower than a body makes it and the body grows to make more. Take it faster..."

"Losses," said Ferra.

"Losses."

She sat with her hand on the table.

"Fourteen men," she said, at last, very quietly, "held in Wayford, under your barony, against rent, for two years, by a magistrate who dines with Placit." She looked at the dates. "Two years at least. Strade's copying from a book that's falling apart. It's older than two years." She was quiet. "And Aster never knew. And I never knew. And you've held Wayford four years." She put her pencil down on the page, very carefully, so that it lay straight. "Where are they?"

"I don't know."

"Somewhere a cart can go across the square from at two in the morning," said Ferra. "From the north. From the Staple house." She stood up. "I'm going to bed. I'm going to lie in it and not sleep. In the morning I'm going to read every page that's come off that bed since June again, every one, for a word that I struck because I didn't know what it meant." She went to the door; and stopped, with her hand on it, and did not turn round. "Emeric."

"Yes."

"Don't come up," said Ferra. "Not tonight. I can't be anybody's tonight." And then, still without turning: "Tomorrow. Tomorrow I'll want you to. Tomorrow come up."



He went home on the Thursday, because he had said he would, and because the forge had a run waiting that had waited a month, and because there was nothing in Wayford he could do about fourteen men in a place he could not find but sit in Sethe's counting room and not sleep.

Imani was on the slip. No lamp.

She had said no lamp, and there was no lamp, and no bag; it was a clear night with the moon two days off full and the slip silver under it and the river loud. She was standing at the top of the slip in a dark dress that was not a ward dress, with her hair down, all of it, the long curls loose over her shoulders the way he had seen them only on the ship. Both hands empty. She did not say anything as he came down the gangway. She watched him come up the slip in the moonlight with her arms folded, and when he was near enough she unfolded them and took two fistfuls of his coat and pulled him the last step in, hard, and kissed him.

It went on for some time. Cade, on the ship, very carefully did not drop anything.

"Hello," said Imani at last, a little breathless, against his mouth.

"Hello."

"Both hands." She held them up between their faces in the moonlight, empty, and then put them both flat on his chest under his coat, inside it, against his shirt, and slid them round to his back, slowly, and pulled him against her. "See. Nothing to drop." She put her mouth against his neck. "You smell of Wayford. Sour rags and lime." She did not take her mouth away. "Ferra telephoned this morning. About Strade's pages. About what Ottoline read." Her arms went tighter round him under the coat. "You look like a man who's been lying in Sethe's bed in Sethe's smell for a week not sleeping."

"Three nights."

"Three's enough." She lifted her face. "Come up to the ward. Not the ward. My room. The back passage, past the linen press. Dray's wife's moved into the room at the end and she sleeps like the dead, she says she hasn't slept like it in nine years." Her hands moved on his back. "I've a bed. It's narrow. I don't care how narrow it is."

He stood on the slip with her hands under his coat and her body against his in the moonlight, and her face turned up, and thought, with great clarity and for some time, about the narrow bed past the linen press.

"The forge," he said.

Imani's eyes narrowed.

"There's a run," he said. "For the test turbine. The bearing races. It's waited a month. If I'm not in the cellar by three it waits another night, and Bram's been holding his lathe for it since..."

"Emeric." She did not take her hands out from under his coat. "It's two in the morning. On the slip. In the moon. With my hair down." She put her mouth an inch from his. "You're going to a cellar."

"Work first."

"Don't you dare."

"You said it. On this slip. A month ago. With a lamp. Work first, fun later. I was standing exactly here and you were standing exactly there with a leg on your apron."

"That was different. I had a patient." She pulled him closer by the back of his shirt. "I've been saying work first to you for seven years. You've never once said it to me. Not once. You don't get to start now, tonight, on this slip, with your hands..." She looked down. His hands had found her waist, under her hair, at the back, of their own accord, and were not doing anything about the forge at all. She looked back up at him. "With your hands where they are, saying cellar."

"The races," he said, and kissed her.

"Liar," said Imani into it.

It was a long time before he got to the cellar. When he did, at a little after three, he had a long scratch down the back of his neck under his collar that he had not felt being made and Bram saw at once and did not mention, very loudly, all through the run; and Imani's voice in his head, at the top of the slip, as he finally broke away down toward the keep with his coat open: "Tomorrow. Fun first. I'm writing it down. I'm telling Ferra. It's in the terms."



Armand took the quarter's band readings on the ninth, ten days late, in the long room instead of his own, because his room was too small for the heat and he said so.

Every band in Kaldo came to the long table in the morning, one after another, and sat in the chair beside Armand's chair with a wrist out, and Armand laid his own long old fingers on the bead and read it three times, with his eyes shut, and Osric wrote down what he said, badly, and was corrected. Emeric's six council bands. The four knights'. Tess's. The ordinary stones in the knights' bands, and the gauge stones, and the bead in Emeric's own; and then the people under them, the wrists, which was what Armand was really reading, and had been every year since the bands went on.

Emeric sat last. He put his wrist on the table, and Armand's fingers came down on it, cool and dry and light as paper, and stayed.

They stayed a long time.

"Well?" said Emeric.

Armand did not open his eyes. His fingers did not move on the wrist. He sat in his chair by the long table with the blanket over his knees and the morning light on the side of his old face, and read; and then he took his fingers away, and opened his eyes, and looked at Osric, and said a figure, and Osric wrote it down.

It was higher than the quarter before. Emeric could see it on Osric's page upside down. They were all higher. Every wrist at the table, every band, higher than in April, by a little; and the knights higher than the council, and Tess, who held almost nothing and always had, higher than either, by more than a little.

Armand looked at Osric's page for a while. Then he took the pen out of Osric's hand, gently, and wrote something at the foot of the page, in his own careful tower notation, small, that Osric could not read and Emeric could only half read upside down; and folded the page twice, and put it in the pocket of his blanket.

"Thank you," he said, to the table. "That's all. The twenty-ninth of October, and on time."

"Armand," said Emeric. "What did you write?"

The old man looked at him over the table with his pale clear eyes.

"A number," said Armand. "And a question." He folded his hands on the blanket over the pocket. "When I know the answer to the question, I'll tell you the number. Not before." He shut his eyes. "Osric. Take me to the window. The light's better there and the company's quieter."



Imani found him in the works at four that afternoon, at Bram's lapping bench, with the night's bearing races from the forge being lapped true one by one and set in a little shining heap, and his sleeves rolled, and grease to the elbow.

She stood in the works doorway in her ward dress with her hair tied up, and looked at him across the floor, at the bench and the lap and the heap of steel and Bram and the two apprentices and the grease; and then she crossed the floor without hurry, between the benches, to his side, and put her mouth to his ear, and said, so that only he could hear it, very low:

"Fun first. You promised."

"I didn't promise. You told me."

"Same thing." She put two fingers on the back of his greasy wrist, under the band, and left them there, and did not count. "The ward's quiet. Dray's wife has the linen. Nobody's dying." She leaned against the bench beside him, close, so that her hip was against his. "And Ferra telephoned at noon, and I told her I was going to take you away from your rings for an hour and she said, and I'm telling you exactly what she said: Good. Somebody should." She looked at him sideways. "She said it twice."

Bram, at the other end of the bench, put a race down on the bench with great care and said nothing, at length.

"Work first," said Emeric, without much conviction.

"You keep saying that." Imani took her fingers off his wrist and took the steel ring out of his hand, the one he was lapping, and looked through it, shining, the size of her palm, and put it down on the heap with the others. "Bram. How many does he need today?"

Bram looked at the heap. Then at the prince. Then, at length, at the far wall of the works, as if there were something very interesting on it.

"I've got enough," said Bram, to the wall.

"There," said Imani. "Enough." She took his hand, grease and all, and pulled. "The work's done. Bram says so. Bram never says so." She pulled again. "Come on."

He went. Out of the works, past the apprentices, who found their benches very interesting too, into the yard and the August sun, with Imani's hand round his greasy one and her pace quick and certain and her face set the way it was set going into the ward on a bad night, looking neither left nor right, toward the river path and the willows below the mill race where nobody went in the afternoon.

At the yard gate he looked back, once. Bram had come to the works door and was standing in it with his arms folded, watching them go; and as Emeric looked, the smith shook his head, slowly, and went back in to his bench, and Emeric could have sworn, though he would not have told anybody, that Bram was smiling.

They came back up the river path an hour and a half later, separately, by different gates, she first. Her hair was down again and there was a leaf in it that Dray's wife took out at the ward door without a word. Nothing had happened under the willows, Imani told Ferra on the telephone that night, that she would have to report under the second term; though it had been, she added, in her ward voice, a near thing, and she had had to tell him so twice, and the second time he had listened.
