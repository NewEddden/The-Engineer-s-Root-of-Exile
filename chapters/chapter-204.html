Chapter 204 - Eight to a Pod

The drawings gave way on the Tuesday after the quarter day, and they gave way because of the numbers.

Emeric had left them alone for two months. That had been the rule since the first night with the book: the signs first, the numbers first of all, and the drawings only when there was somewhere to stand. Now there was somewhere. He had the strokes, one to four, and the hook with its tail. He had Ferra's ruled sheets and the seventy-one signs on his slate and the eleven sounds a dead man with a bad tooth had written under them, which Ferra had carried out of the delve in her head and set down on paper within the hour, every one with a ring round it. And he had every page of the book on his own shelf now, because he had looked at every one of them with his own eyes, and he knew how to lay them all out at once, which was a thing he had not known before the day his brother sat down on the floor.

So on the Tuesday night he went in alone, and walked the rows of pages in the plain light, and stopped at the drawings, and made himself look at them the way Ferra looked at a column: not for what they were, but for what repeated.

There were four drawings on a page near the end of the book that the copier had drawn with more care than any other page in it. He had ruled a border round them, which he had done nowhere else. And in the border, in the carved signs, not in his own brown ink, there was a line of writing under each drawing, copied off whatever he had copied them from. The signs under the drawings had numbers in them.

The first drawing was a round thing, a little flattened, like a bean or a plump lentil, with a pattern drawn over its skin. Emeric had taken it for a carved bead the first time he saw it, a jeweller's piece, the kind of thing that hung on a cord. Now he looked at the pattern. It was a net. Fine lines crossing, making little six-sided cells all over the thing's surface, the way a honeycomb is made, or the way frost lays itself across a pane.

The second drawing was the same round thing, split along a seam, and out of the split a stem, and on the stem two halves of the round thing lifted up like a pair of cupped hands.

The third was a tree. Not any tree he knew. It was tall and not wide, with a trunk that the copier had drawn with straight edges, as if it were a thing cut from stone and not grown, and branches that went up close to it rather than out, and leaves he could not make anything of because the copier had not been able to either, and had drawn them as a haze.

The fourth was a single branch of that tree, drawn large, and on the branch, standing upright on short stalks, three long pods. And every pod had bumps along it, where the things inside pushed out its skin.

He counted the bumps on the first pod. Then he counted them on the second, and on the third, because he did not believe the first.

Eight.

He came up out of it in his chair with his hand flat on his own stone, the one his mother had put in his hands when he was small, and sat there.

Eight grey stones on a banquet table, in a box lined with velvet, in front of two hundred people, on the last night before he was sent south. Corbin's gift. Corbin smiling, the way Corbin smiled when he had thought of something in the afternoon and saved it for a room. Unchanging, Corbin had said, to the room, holding one up to the candles. Like our brother. And the room had laughed, because a Hollow Crystal was a stone that did nothing, that no runesmith in the realm could write into, that dealers sold to people who liked the look of them, grey and faintly warm, in sets. Always in sets. Emeric had heard that once, years ago, from somebody at court who knew about such things, that you bought the grey stones by the set, eight to a box, the way the dealers had always sold them, and nobody knew why eight.

Six of them were on six wrists now. One was in the gatehouse board, telling the wall where it was. One was in the pump house on the western rise, where it had been since the third year, watering the farm. Eight. His brother had given him, as a joke about a drunk, the whole of what one pod of that tree had held.

He found he wanted to laugh, and did not, because it was the middle of the night and Ward was asleep in the next room. He sat with his hand on his own stone instead. It was warm under his palm, as it had always been warm, as it had been in the nursery. It was bound to him and had been since his first winter in Kaldo, and it had not changed in all that time by anything he had ever been able to see.

Unchanging. He took his hand off it.

In the morning he took the book to the small room and showed Ferra the page, the real page, bound in its boards, with the four drawings on it and the lines of signs under them.

She looked at the drawings for a long time. She looked at the pods and counted the bumps, as he had, three times. Then she looked at the line of signs under the fourth drawing and put her finger beside it, not on it.

"That's the hook," she said. "And strokes after it."

"Yes."

"Eight?"

"If the hook is five," Emeric said. "And there are three strokes after it. If the hook is five."

She took out her ruled sheet and turned to the row where the hook stood with its ring round it, and looked at it, and did not rub the ring out. She drew a second ring round the first, which was a thing he had never seen her do. "Twice a guess," she said. "It could still be ten. It could be ten and three. It could be a word that means a pod." But she did not sound as though she believed any of those, and she wrote, beside the double ring, very small, eight to a pod?

Then she turned the page back to the first drawing, the round thing with the net over its skin, and he watched her see it. He watched her see exactly what he had seen in the night, and go still, and not say it, because she did not say a thing until she had it.

There was a drawing beside the first one that he had not told her about. It was small, in a corner, and he had not understood it himself until the night. It showed two more of the round things, side by side with the good one: smaller, flatter, with no net drawn on them at all. Smooth. One of them had a crack down its middle. The copier had drawn a line under them both, and written in the carved signs a word that did not appear under anything else in the book.

"Those are ours," Ferra said.

Emeric had known she would say it and still it went through him when she did.

Kaldo had a drawer of them. Every barony had. They were teaching stones, the flat grey pebbles every child with a spark of the gift was given to learn on, to pour a little into and feel it fill and let it out again; the stones he had held in his left hand on the night he first found the room, and found almost nothing in them but a jar and how full the jar was. Nobody wrote into a teaching stone. Nobody could. They were too simple for it, everybody knew, and so cheap the tower gave them away. There were three dozen of them in the ward's cupboard where Imani's students learned their first charge. There was a box of them in the old exchange for Orrin's children. Ferra had four in her own desk and used one for a paperweight.

"Failed seeds," Emeric said. "Seeds that didn't take. Dried up, or never filled. We've been buying them by the box for as long as there have been mages, and handing them to children."

"Not seeds," said Ferra. "You said seeds."

"I did."

"You don't know it's a seed."

"No," he said. "I don't know it's a seed. I know it's drawn beside a pod with eight things in it, and a thing that splits and sends up a stem, and a tree. And I know I have been holding one in my hand since I was a child, and that nobody in two hundred years of the tower has ever been able to write a word into one unless he was sitting in a place nobody else has found."

Ferra looked at him.

"Say it properly," she said. "When we've got it."

They got the label the next evening, as much of it as they were going to get, with Armand at the table because Emeric wanted a man at the table who would refuse to believe any of it.

It was the line of signs under the first drawing, the round thing with its net. It was short. Nine signs. Three of them were among the eleven the copier had put sounds under, which was more than any other line in the book could claim, and two of them were the hook and a stroke.

Emeric did not pretend it was more than it was. He laid it out on a clean sheet, sign by sign, with the copier's sound under each of the three and a question mark after each sound, and the number sign under the hook, and blanks for the rest, and they looked at it.

One of the copier's sounds was a sign that appeared, in the rest of the book, beside every drawing of anything burning. Fires, lamps, a thing like a kiln. Emeric had noticed that a month ago and given it a ring. Another was a sign that stood beside drawings of wind: a tree bent over, a sail. And between them, in the label, was a sign he had seen in only two other places in the whole book, both of them in the long careful copy of a single carved wall the copier had drawn near the front, and both times next to the same other sign, which appeared in that wall more than any other.

"Wakes," Emeric said, "or rises, or begins. In," and he put his finger on the wind sign, "air. Something about air. And this one," the rare one, "I think is a word for a lot of it. Heavy. Thick. Full." He moved his finger along. "And then the hook, and a stroke. And then a sign I think means a year, because it stands next to a drawing of a sun going round in a circle, twice, in the front of the book."

"You think," said Armand.

"I think."

"The sound under that one," Armand said, and pointed at the rare sign, the one Emeric had called thick, "is a sound the man guessed. He could not read it. He wrote down what he hoped it said. And you are building on his hope a word you want it to mean." He sat back. "I've sat on the tower's reading bench. I've watched better men than either of us read a meaning into a thing because the meaning was what they had come to find."

"Yes," said Emeric. "That's why you're here."

Ferra had written it out on her sheet, with the rings. She read it back to them in the voice she used for reading back a figure nobody liked.

"Wakes in thick air. Then a hook and a stroke, which is five and one, or ten and one, or something else. Then a sign that might be a year." She looked up. "It wakes in thick air, and then something happens in six years, or eleven, or one year and something, or two. That's the most it says. That's the most it can be made to say."

"That's the most it says," Emeric agreed.

Armand looked at the drawing of the round thing with its net for a long while.

"Thick air," he said at last, quietly, not to either of them. And Emeric knew exactly where the old man's mind had gone, because it was where his own had gone the night before. To a place three miles up, over the sea, where the air was so full of the medium that a man's hair stood up in it and a stone left out on a deck filled itself by morning. The band. Armand had been told about the band in the year of the Wayford nodes, and had sat very still at this table and said that the tower had written papers about it for two hundred years and been laughed at for all of them.

Nobody said the word. It was in the room anyway.

Ferra did the inventory at the end of that week, because she did the inventory of the strongbox at the end of every quarter, and she would have done it that week if the world had been ending.

She did it at the long-room table after supper with the box open in front of her and her scale beside it: Bram's small brass scale with the ivory pans that she used for anything that had to be weighed and entered, and had used for eight years, and trusted more than she trusted most people. Emeric sat at the head of the table because he always sat at the head of the table when the box was open. Armand sat at the far end with his hands on his stick because Emeric had asked him to.

She went through it in the order the book listed it, as she always did. The deeds. The relay key's halves. Keller's slip and Saban's two sheets in their fold, which she did not unfold. The discretionary tally. And then the stones.

There were two in the silk at the bottom. His mother's, the chipped one, the only one left of the pair that had come in the comb case from his father's treasury. And the third of the three grey stones Saban's dealers had sold him in the spring, one at a time, through three different hands, so that no dealer in the south should ever see a prince buy more than one of a thing nobody wanted. The first two had gone into the wall board the week they came. The third he had kept out, unbound, and put in the box beside his mother's, and not said why.

He had not said why in the summer either, when he had asked Ferra to take the two of them out of the box before each of hull two's flask runs, and put them in a small iron case with one of her own flat teaching stones, the paperweight, and send the case up with the flasks, and take it back out of the flasks when hull two came down, and put the two grey stones back in the silk. She had done it every run, all summer. She had written the case into the run manifest every time, as case, E.'s, 1, with no contents, and entered its coming and going in the box's book. She had not asked. He had known she was not asking in the particular way she had of not asking, which was a way of writing down the date.

She took his mother's stone out of the silk first, and turned it in the lamp to find the chip, as she did every quarter, to be sure it was the same stone.

She stopped turning it.

He saw her stop. He saw her bring it nearer the lamp and tilt it, and tilt it back, and put her other hand under it as if it might fall.

"Emeric," she said.

The chip was where it had always been, a small bite out of one corner, worn smooth as a pebble in a stream after however many years in a comb case in a drawer in his father's treasury, and then in this box. Except that it was not smooth. Across the broken face, very faint, so fine you had to hold it in the light at the right angle to see it at all, there were lines. Tiny crossing lines, grey on grey, making cells. Six-sided cells, over the whole of the old broken face, like frost on a pane.

Armand got up from the far end of the table and came round and looked at it, without asking to, over Ferra's shoulder. He did not touch it. He looked at it for a long time. Then he went back to his chair and sat down, carefully, the way an old man sits down.

Ferra put the stone on her scale.

She did not need to look in the book for its weight. She had weighed it every quarter since the day it came to Kaldo in its comb case, and she knew it the way she knew the date. She moved the rider along the beam until the pointer stood level, and read it, and did not say anything, and then she moved it back to zero and weighed it again.

Then she took out the dealer's stone and weighed that. She did look in the book for that one, because it had been bought in the spring and weighed once, on the day it came, and entered at its purchase weight under Saban's receipt. She put her finger on the line. She read the pointer. She read the line again.

"It's heavier," she said. "Both of them. Not by much."

"How much?"

She told him. It was not much. It was the weight of a few grains of wheat. It was more than the brass scale could be wrong by, and she said so before he could ask, because she knew the scale and he knew she knew it.

Then she took out her own teaching stone, her paperweight, which had ridden up in the iron case with the two grey ones on every run all summer, and weighed that, against the weight she had written for it in the spring on the day he first asked for it.

It was exactly what it had been.

"A control," said Armand.

He had not said anything for a quarter of an hour. Ferra had weighed the two stones three times each, and the teaching stone twice, and entered all of it, and Emeric had sat at the head of the table and let her, and not said anything either.

"Your paperweight rode the same nights in the same case," Armand said, "and did nothing. That is one control, and it's a good one. It is not enough." He put both hands on his stick. "You want to tell me that these grew because they went up in the air. I want to know that they did not grow because they sat in a box in a warm room for a summer, or because they sat in iron, or because the ship itself does something to a stone it carries that is nothing to do with where she carries it. I want the ship's own stones weighed. I want a stone that sat in the forge's chamber all summer, with a flask opened on it, weighed. I want a stone of this kind that never left the ground." He looked at Emeric. "And I want one that flew and is not in that case."

They did it over four days.

The ship's stones Ferra already had, because every gravity stone in the barony was weighed at its raising and entered, and Bram weighed the Hod's again for her in an afternoon. They had not changed by a grain. A lamp stone that had sat on the shelf in the forge's chamber since the spring, where Bram had opened two flasks in the thaw and watched them bleed away through the brick, had not changed. A teaching stone from the ward's cupboard that had never been out of Kaldo had not changed.

A grey stone that had never left the ground was harder. Every grey stone in Kaldo but the two in the box was bound to somebody, and working. On the fourth day Ferra went to the gatehouse and asked the watch to let her take the board's stone out of its seat for the time it took to walk it to the counting room and weigh it and walk it back, with the board dark for that time and Declan standing at the gate in person to be the board; and it had not changed. It had been weighed on the day Emeric bound it, years ago, and it weighed now what it had weighed then.

And then Ward took off his band.

He did it at the table without being asked, on the fourth evening, after Armand had said the last thing on his list out loud and looked at nobody. Ward had flown in the band more than anybody in Kaldo. He had flown in it on the first night the ship went up and on most nights she had gone up since, and his stone had gone with him every time, on his wrist, under his gauntlet. He unbuckled the band and put it on the table in front of Ferra and sat back.

She weighed it. It had not changed.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"His is bound," Emeric said at last. "It's working. It's been working since the day I gave it him. Every stone that changed was one nobody was using." He looked at the band on the table. "Maybe a seed that's being used doesn't grow. Maybe a seed that's got somebody in it can't wake. Maybe it's the gauntlet. I don't know."

"Maybe," said Armand.

Ferra wrote it. She wrote it as he had said it, all three maybes, and drew a ring round the whole of it. Then she gave Ward back his band, and he buckled it on without looking at it, the way he buckled on everything.

"Now say it," Ferra said.

It was late. Armand had gone up. Ward was at the door. The two grey stones lay on the silk on the table between Emeric and Ferra, with the lamp beside them, and the book open at the page with the pods.

He looked at them. His mother's, with its chip and the fine grey net across the old broken face. The dealer's, whole, a little heavier than it had been in the spring. Two grey stones that did nothing, that no runesmith could write into, that dealers sold eight to a box to people who liked the look of them.

"It was never a stone," he said. "It's a seed. It was always a seed. That's why nobody could write into it: you can't write into a thing that's alive and waiting to be something else. You can only get into it, if you happen to be sitting where I was sitting." He touched the dealer's stone with one finger. "And these two were waking. All summer. In the band. And nobody's ever seen one wake, because nobody's ever carried one three miles up and left it there."

Hollow, he thought. They were called Hollow Crystals, and had been for longer than anybody could say, and nobody knew why, because they were not hollow; they were solid grey stone all the way through, everybody knew that. Except that somebody, once, a long time ago, had called them hollow. As if somebody had once known there was a space inside, and something in it.

Ferra wrote it down. Not with a ring. She looked at the words for a while after she had written them, and then she did a thing she did not usually do: she turned the book round and pushed it across the table to him, so that he could see she had written it plain.

A seed. Waking in the band. Eight to a pod.

"How long," said Ferra. "To the pod."

"If the label says what I think it says, a year at the earliest. Once it's in the ground and has what it wants."

"And if it doesn't say what you think?"

"Three. Maybe more." He looked at the drawing of the tree. "I don't know what it wants. The man drew it in the sun, with leaves. That's light. I don't know how much. I don't know how thick the air has to be, or for how long, or whether there's anything else. I don't know if it needs water or hates it. I know it woke in the band in a summer, a little, a few grains' worth, in an iron case." He sat back. "Armand won't guess at all."

"Armand said," Ferra said, "that he won't give a figure for a thing nobody alive has ever seen grow, when there is no control for it anywhere in the world, and that if you want a figure you should go and find the other tree." She did not smile. "I wrote that down too."

"And you?"

She looked at the drawing of the pod for a while. Then she turned to a clean page, and ruled it, and wrote a heading, and under the heading three columns, one for each year.

"Three," she said. "I'll budget for three. If it's one, I'll be glad."

She put the dealer's stone back in the silk, and then, after a moment, his mother's beside it, and folded the silk over them and closed the box and locked it. Then she did not put the key back on its cord. She held it in her hand and looked at the box.

"The wall board's been a stone short since the spring," she said. "You bought that one for the board. It's why we paid for it."

"I know."

"It goes into the board, then. Tomorrow."

He did not answer at once. He thought of the pod on its branch, and the eight bumps on it, and a thing that woke in thick air and split and sent up a stem. He thought that there were two stones in that box that were waking, and that one of them was for a wall, and the other was the last thing of his mother's he had.

"Tomorrow," he said.

Ferra put the key back on its cord. She did not ask him where the air was that thick, on the ground, anywhere, in all the world, for a thing to wake in and grow. She did not need to ask. He could see her not asking it, and he could see that she knew he had been asking it himself since the Tuesday night, and had no answer yet at all.
