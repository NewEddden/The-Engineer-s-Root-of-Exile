Chapter 344 - Visitors

The people's train cost a copper, and Maeve paid it herself at a window at the junction, to a clerk who did not look up.

Her man had wanted the express the whole way, and had said so twice before he gave it up.

On the bench facing hers sat four miners from the Grayrock pits in blue overalls so clean the folds still showed. Double at the knee. A loop sewn on the breast for something she couldn't guess. They had their wives along, and one wife had a baby, and somewhere after the second halt the baby had been handed down the row to Maeve because its mother wanted both hands for a pie. It was asleep on her shoulder now, dribbling on a travelling dress that had cost more than the carriage.

The miner by the window caught her looking at his knees. "Best ones," he said. "The works give you two pair. You wear the old underground and the new to town, till the new's old." He smoothed one leg flat with his palm. "Then you get another."

In the capital you could tell a lord from a carter across a courtyard by the coat, and that was what coats were for. Here four men had come to town on their rest day in the same blue they went down the pit in, and were proud of the creases. Maeve didn't know yet whether she liked it. She thought she probably did, and that the capital would have laughed, and that the capital was wrong about most things.

The baby woke on the edge of the town, looked at her, decided against her, loudly, and went home down the row.



Kaldo's station had a girl at the barrier with a ledger.

She was perhaps sixteen, with ink on her middle finger, and she asked Maeve's name, and where she was staying, and for how long, and wrote each in its column without lifting her eyes.

"Maeve."

The girl wrote Maeve and waited, pen up, for the rest.

"That's all of it."

The girl ruled a small neat line after it, so nobody could put anything there later. "Staying?"

"With my brother. Up the hill."

"Name?"

Maeve told her. The girl looked up once, then wrote that too, and gave her a thin yellow slip with a number printed at the top in red and a tear-line across it, and tore off the stub and kept it. "Show it if anyone asks. Seven days. Bring it back here to renew."

Maeve went through the barrier turning the card over. Somewhere in this town there was now a book with her name in it, and a number, and her brother's name beside both. In the palace that was how things started. You were in somebody's book; then on somebody's list; then a polite knight stood all night outside your door for your safety.

She put the card in her pocket and decided to be cross about it later, when she'd eaten.



She saw it before she was off the platform.

They were waiting under the station clock, the three of them, with Waylon leaning on the next pillar along as if somebody had left him there. Emeric was in the middle. Ferra had her arm through his. Imani had her hand in his coat pocket, all the way in, and was looking at the engine's wheels with professional interest, and not one of the three seemed to know they were standing like that.

"Oh," said Maeve, a good six yards off. "It's both of you."

Ferra went red from her collar to the roots of a fringe she had never had before, evenly, all at once.

"Yes," said Imani, as she might have said it about a broken wrist.

"Good. I said so in the winter. Didn't I say so?"

"You said so in a letter," said Waylon from his pillar. "I didn't answer it."

"You don't answer anything." She looked at the two women properly. When she had last seen them they had looked their age and their work. Now Ferra's red hair was past her shoulders with that fringe on it, and Imani's hair went most of the way down her back, tied off with a strip of bandage, and they were both, as far as Maeve could tell, about twenty-five. "Waylon wrote that you'd all gone younger. One line. I came three weeks early to see if he'd started drinking."

"I don't drink," said Waylon.

"I know. That's why I came."

Then she put her arms round her brother's neck and held on, in front of the miners and the girl with the ledger, the way she always had.

It was wrong. Her chin had always gone on his shoulder; now it went on his collarbone, and only if she came up on her toes, and he was solid under the coat in a way he had never been, not as a boy and not in the years when he was mostly wine. Younger. The drinking years had gone out of his face like a stain out of linen. And under his eyes, two smudges the colour of a bruise going yellow.

"You look twenty-five," she said, "and as if you haven't slept since Lantern Night."

"Since the twelfth," said Imani.

"Imani," said Ferra.

"She asked."

"I didn't, actually," said Maeve. "But go on."

Ferra looked at the station clock as if it owed her money. Emeric said, "Do you want breakfast?" in the voice of a man who had grabbed the first thing in the drawer, and Maeve laughed so loud that a porter turned his head, saw who she was with, and went on with his barrow without the least interest.



The tram ran up the Gate Avenue on rails let into the street, a long green car with a pole on its roof that touched a wire, and no horse.

Maeve bent to look underneath for the horse, and found Waylon bent beside her.

"I looked in August," he said. "There isn't one. I asked Tess what pulls it, and she told me, and I've been sorry since."

Maeve took a window. The Gate Avenue went by: shopfronts of plate glass from the knee up, so you saw the bread before you smelled it; a printer's with the News pinned flat behind the pane and three men reading the same page through it; peonies in tin buckets. A boy on a bicycle passed the tram on the inside, which looked fatal, and nobody shouted at him.

The conductor came down the car with a ticket punch on a cord round her neck. She was a broad woman somewhere past fifty in a grey jacket with brass buttons, and she looked at each passenger as though they were a sum she had already done and found to be wrong.

"Copper."

Maeve paid, and the woman punched her a ticket and moved on to Emeric.

"Copper."

Emeric paid. Maeve watched him do it with open pleasure. Nobody in the capital had ever asked one of Harrold's children for a copper; it would have been like asking the river.

The conductor took in Maeve's skirt, which had Grayrock on it to the knee, and the baby's mark on her shoulder. "People's train."

"By choice."

"Hm." It was neither approval nor the other thing. "You'll want a shower after."

"A what?"

"Water from overhead. Hot." She held a hand flat above her own head and let the fingers fall. "Like rain, if rain had a fire under it. Every Standard home's got one. Mine has. I'm off at four. Third ward, two streets behind the stop, brass nine on the door. Two coppers for the tank."

And she went on down the car and took a copper off a carter who had been trying to look asleep.

"Who is that?"

"Prudence Fairley," said Ferra.

"She decides who lives longer," said Imani.

Maeve laughed, saw that neither of them was joking, and looked down the car at the grey jacket again. Prudence Fairley was telling a small boy, in exactly the same voice, that feet went on the floor and not on the seat, and the boy put his feet on the floor.



Kaldo Clothing Works was up an outside stair in an old sail loft that still smelled of tar under the new cloth. Fourteen sewing machines ran down one side under the windows, each chattering to itself like a bird in a box, and the noise came up through the boards into Maeve's feet. Down the middle ran a cutting table longer than the high table in her father's hall. Nobody at it looked up.

"Twenty-two hands," said Ferra. "Sixteen from the school. They go home at six."

A girl of about seventeen ran a whole side seam through her machine in the time it took Maeve's own woman in the capital to thread a needle. At seventeen Maeve had done cushion covers. Fourteen of them, for sofas nobody sat on.

A man came down the room without hurrying, long and quiet, with pins in his lapel and a tape round his neck, and looked at Maeve the way her riding master had once looked at a horse somebody was trying to sell him.

"Abloh," said Emeric. "This is my sister..."

"Arms," said Abloh.

Maeve lifted them. She had done it before she'd decided to, which nobody had managed since she was eight. The tape went round her and over her and down her in a dozen quick turns while Abloh called numbers to a girl with a slate.

"Twelve. Long in the back. Rides."

"How do you know I ride?"

He touched, very lightly, the inside of her knee through the skirt, where a lifetime of saddles had left its shape. Then he looked at the dress itself, the capital's best, sixty buttons up the back and a waist that had taken three fittings.

"Who made this?"

"A woman in the palace."

"She made it for you to stand in," said Abloh, and went to a rail.

What he brought back was green: a linen fine as a handkerchief, the green of a new pear leaf, with two straps no wider than a finger and nothing whatever for the arms, six buttons down the front where a woman could reach them herself, and a skirt to the middle of the calf. No boning anywhere. When he held it to the window she could see the light through it.

"What's this for?"

"Walking," said Abloh. "It's the end of May."

Behind the screen she got out of sixty buttons with Imani's help and into six on her own, and came out. The air from the windows went over her bare arms and shoulders, her mother's shoulders, the sword ones, that every dressmaker in the capital had spent Maeve's life hiding under sleeves. A princess out like this in daylight would be talked about in every house with a fire, and Maeve decided she would very much like to be.

"That's the sample," said Abloh. "For the window. There's no other in a twelve till June."

"No."

"I need it for the window."

"Then you'll have to take it off me in the street," said Maeve, "and I'll scream. What does it cost?"

He told her. It was less than the hat she had travelled in. She counted it into his palm, all of it, and he looked at the coins and then at her.

"Nobody pays the first price."

"I'm on holiday."

He did not quite smile. "Keep the hem off the bicycle chain," he said, and went back to his table. On the outside stair two girls coming up with a bolt of grey drill stood aside and looked her over, top to bottom, the dress and not her, and one said to the other, with deep resentment, "That's the window one."



The switchback stood in the tree belt outside the town wall, under the arches of the ring line's viaduct, and from a distance it looked like a fence that had lost its mind.

It was a long timber track on trestles that rose and dropped in three humps, each lower than the last, with iron rail on top in short lengths of every age bolted end to end, a winch platform at each end, and a car like a short open cart. A painted board said THE SWITCHBACK. ONE COPPER. NO STANDING. Under it, in another hand: No, it does not go faster if you lean.

"The rail crews' apprentices built it out of offcuts," said Ferra. "In their own time. It opened this month."

"What's it for?"

Ferra looked at Emeric.

"For going down," said Emeric.

Waylon had walked off under the second hump and laid his hand flat on an upright, the way he laid a hand on a horse's leg to feel for heat. A girl in a rail crew's grey coat with a grease rag through her belt came down off the platform and folded her arms at him.

"You can't stand there."

"This one's green." He knocked it with a knuckle. "That one too. The rest are seasoned. These two will move when they dry, and they'll dry by August."

"It's been checked."

"Check it again. I'm not saying it's bad. I'm saying August."

The girl looked at him for a long, unfriendly time. Then she took a pencil stub from behind her ear, wrote something on the back of her hand, and went back up her ladder.

"Ride it," said Maeve.

"No. It goes up and it comes down and you've paid a copper to end where you started." Waylon put his hands in his pockets. "I've been run off with by better horses than that."

So Maeve rode it with Imani on the front bench and Emeric and Ferra behind, and behind them a boy of nine who had plainly been on it before and wanted everyone to know.

The winch clanked them up a notch at a time. At the top it stopped, and the first slope, which had looked like nothing from the grass, went down like the side of a well. On the viaduct beyond, a train was coming across.

"Hold the bar," said Imani.

"Why?"

The apprentice let go.

Her stomach stayed at the top. The rest of her went down. The wind came straight through the linen, the trees went up past her the wrong way, the iron under the car shrieked at the bottom and she shrieked with it; and they went up the second hump with the train going over above their heads, so that for a stretch the whole sky was one long iron roar. At the top of the second hump the boy behind yelled with joy, and down they went again.

At the far platform she climbed out with her hair down one side of her head and her knees belonging to somebody else.

"Again."

"Nineteen yards on the first drop," said Ferra, a little pale. "I counted the uprights."

"On that?"

"I always count. It helps." She was holding Emeric's hand, and Maeve did not think she knew it.

Maeve rode it twice. When she came back round the second time, Waylon was in the queue.

Nobody said anything to him. He had been standing behind a girl of seven or eight who had climbed out, said "Again" exactly as Maeve had, and gone straight to the back of the line. Waylon paid his copper to the apprentice with the grease rag, took the front bench alone, and held the bar with his elbows out, the way he held a pair of reins.

He made no sound the whole way round. At the far platform he climbed out and walked back along the grass to them with a face the colour of a candle.

"Well?" said Maeve.

"It was fine," said Waylon.

He went on a few steps, then turned and called up to the apprentice. "Second hump. You can feel it go. Left side, where I said."

The girl wrote it on her hand under the first thing and gave him the short nod one tradesman gives another, and Waylon looked more pleased about that than about anything Maeve had seen him do in years.



The bicycles stood in a rack at the end of the tree belt, with a boy in charge of them and, naturally, a book.

He wrote her down by the number on her purple card, and the bicycle's number beside it, and the time. It was a frame two, for a lady her size: two wheels in a line with a seat between, and it would not stand up by itself, and she could not see why anyone expected it to stand up with her on it.

"It stays up because you're going," said Imani. "Like a hoop. If you stop, it doesn't."

"Then I won't stop."

"Everyone says that."

The ring path ran round the outside of the town under the viaduct's arches, flat and gravelled, with benches. Maeve tucked Abloh's hem under her, and Imani held the back of the seat and walked, then trotted, then ran with her hair flying and let go without saying so; and Maeve went ten yards on her own, and twenty, and then thought about it, and she and the bicycle went into the verge together at the first bend.

She had grazed one knee and the heel of her hand, and it stung like nettles. Imani came up out of breath, went down on her heels, and held one finger an inch above the knee without touching it.

The graze closed. It went pink, then the colour of the rest of her. The capital's physicians had bled Maeve and blistered her and once put a leech on her neck, and not one of them had ever done anything she could watch happen.

"Do that again."

"No," said Imani, doing her hand. "Don't fall off again."

The third time she had it. It came all at once, the way a young horse stops arguing with you: the thing under her stopped wanting to lie down and wanted only to go, and she let it. The arches went over, cool, then sun, then cool. She rang the bell at an old man on a bench who had done nothing to deserve it, and he lifted his hat, and she laughed out loud, a long way ahead of the others, with the window dress snapping round her calves.



One of Maren's girls kept a coffee house in the thickness of the South Gate arch, where the tram stopped: six small tables under the vault, a copper pot the size of a cauldron at the back, and the cups rattling every time a tram went through.

"You're in the draught there," said the woman at the counter. "Move in. Two coppers. Don't ask for sugar till you've tried it."

Emeric went to queue behind a carter, who did not let him go first, and Waylon went out to look at a dray horse at the stop. So it was the three of them under the arch, the way it had been on paper for five years and in a room only once, at the end of her father's high table on the night of the gold roses, for about as long as it took to finish a cup.

The coffee was black and bitter and smelled like bread left too long in the oven. Maeve drank half to be polite, then felt it arrive in her fingers and the backs of her knees, and stared at the cup.

"It does that," said Imani. "Have the other half."

She had the other half, and asked about the drawers.

"Filed by date," said Ferra. "Left-hand side of the desk. There's an index."

"Mine are in with my stockings," said Imani. "No index. I know which is which by the smell. You put lavender in yours the year your mother sent the soap."

Five years. Maeve knew that Ferra dated her letters top right and ruled a line under the date, that Imani crossed out so hard the pen went through, and the name of every cat on the castle hill. From the brother whose castle it was, in all that time, she had received exactly nothing.

"He did write to me once," she said. "I was fourteen. He was ten. Four pages about a pony, and the pony's name is spelled three different ways. That is my entire correspondence with the Duke of Kaldo. I've thought of having it framed."

"You kept it?" said Ferra.

"Of course I kept it. It's the only one." She said it lightly, and they took it lightly, which was what it was for, and the thing was said and put away.

Then she put the purple card on the table between the cups.

"Your girl at the station wrote me down."

"Everybody's written down," said Ferra.

"Yes. That's what I don't like. I've lived my whole life in a house where the list was always somebody's. Corbin had one. The Church had one. Father had three, and none of them matched. He didn't write to me for nine years," she tipped her head at her brother's back in the queue, "because a letter would have put my name in some book. He told me so the night they made him duke. He wasn't wrong about the books, only about me. And the first thing his town does is write my name in a book at the gate."

Ferra didn't answer at once. She turned her pen in her fingers.

"Anybody can read it," she said. "That's the difference. The station book goes to Saban's office first, and to the records hall at the end of the month, and anybody can ask for it there. You can ask who's read your page, and that's written down too."

"Anybody can read it now," said Maeve. "Who keeps the hall in twenty years?"

Ferra looked at her for a while. Then she opened her notebook and wrote something, small and quick.

"She's right," said Imani.

"I know she's right. That's why I'm writing it down."

"And Waylon's one line," said Maeve. "Can I have one?"

"A stone? Ask the Board," said Imani. "The conductor, and me, and three others, in the records hall with the doors open. They'll write down why, either way."

"Not by the crown, I suppose."

"Never the crown," said Ferra. "It has its own line."

"I'm not the crown. I'm the crown's sister."

"That's worse," said Imani cheerfully. "You're not ill, you're over eighteen and you can't buy it, so you'd be in the book behind a dyer with a bad hip. Prudence would give you a fair hearing. She'd enjoy it."

Half of Maeve was offended by the dyer with the bad hip, and the other half was not, and she could not have said which half was the better.

Waylon came back and sat, and Emeric behind him with two more cups and a twist of paper with something fried in it.

"Waylon," said Ferra, "how many horses could a great house keep, with a proper yard? A house the size of Highcairn, say."

"Never been to Highcairn."

"If you had."

Waylon thought about it the way he thought about everything, slowly and from the ground up. "Depends on the water. Forty, if the water's good and there's meadow. Sixty if somebody knew what they were doing. Why?"

"I was wondering," said Ferra, and wrote it down.

Ferra had never once wondered anything idly in five years of letters. Maeve filed that at the back, where she had filed things in the palace, and ate one of the fried things out of Emeric's paper, which turned out to be fish.



The inner moat went round the dome in a ring, with four bridges over it and willows on both banks, and you could hire a boat by the hour from a stage at the foot of the north bridge.

Emeric reached for the oars.

"Not you," said Imani. "You'll have them out of the rowlocks."

"I'm at none."

"You're at none on cups. I haven't tested you on oars."

So Waylon rowed, long and easy, and Maeve sat in the stern with her fingers in the water, and Emeric folded himself onto the bottom boards with his face to the sun and was asleep inside two minutes. Imani looked at him and then at Maeve and said nothing whatever, which was worse than anything she could have said.

The dome rose out of the island in the middle, tall and round, its glass so full of sky that Maeve could see nothing inside, only clouds and the willows upside down. A lawn ran from the glass to the water. Two knights stood at the far bridge doing nothing, in the way knights stand when they are doing a great deal.

"What's in it?"

Waylon did not miss a stroke. Ferra looked at the water.

"Work," said Ferra.

Maeve knew a shut door when she heard one; she had grown up in a corridor of them. Waylon, who had never kept a secret from her in his life, was keeping this one without visible effort. She let it be, for now.

Then a man came round the curve of the glass, on the lawn.

He was tall, and he walked the way a young man walks when he has nowhere he has to be, hands behind his back, face turned up a little. A plain blue shirt, sleeves rolled. His hair was dark gold all through. Maeve had time to think that he was a good-looking man and must know it, and then he turned his head toward the boat and the sun caught his eyes, and they were gold.

"That's Father," said Waylon, and pulled the next stroke.

Maeve stood up.

The boat rolled. Ferra grabbed both gunwales. Emeric woke on the boards and said, "What," and Waylon said, "Sit down," in the voice he used on a horse about to go through a gate it shouldn't, and Maeve sat, hard, and did not take her eyes off the lawn.

Her father came down the grass and stood under a willow at the water's edge and waited for them to drift in.

The last time she had seen him, in the capital, he had been grey at the temples and greyer in the face, and had stood up when she came in, slowly, the way old men stand who are pretending it costs nothing. This man looked about thirty. He looked younger than she was. He looked, she saw with a lurch that had nothing to do with the boat, like Waylon. One in the boat and one on the bank, they might have been brothers, with Waylon the elder.

"Maeve," said Harrold.

"You look like his brother."

"So I'm told. A woman on the tram asked me last week if I was the new man at the stables." He glanced at Waylon, who was holding the boat off the bank with one oar and looking at nobody. "I said no, that was my son. She said I'd got him young."

"You're younger than me."

"To look at." He crouched on his heels at the water's edge, which she had never once seen him do. "Not inside. Inside I'm exactly as old as I was, with the same opinions, and you'll be glad to hear I still think you should have married the Lisle boy."

"He has no chin."

"He had a great deal of land." Harrold looked at the green dress, the straps, her bare arms. "That's new."

"Abloh."

"It suits you. You'll be talked about."

"I intend to be."

"Good," said her father, and smiled; and that was the worst of it. She could not remember the last time he had smiled at her in daylight with nobody watching. He had gone somewhere without her and come back as somebody she would have liked very much to have for a brother, and had missed being as a father by thirty years. She was glad. She was so angry she could have put her hand in the moat to cool it.

"Has he shown you the switchback?"

"Twice."

"I've been on it four times."

Waylon's head came round. "Four?"

"Once in the front." Harrold stood, with no hand on his knee. "I'm wanted. I'll see you at supper. Waylon, your filly's been at the hedge by the north gate again; I put her back." And he went up the lawn and round the dome, and was lost in the sky in the glass.

Waylon rowed. After a while Imani, beside her in the stern, put a hand flat on Maeve's bare shoulder and left it there, cold even in the sun.

"Two weeks I've had," said Waylon eventually, to the water. "It doesn't get less strange. You just get used to it being strange."

"You might have warned me."

"You'd not have believed me." He pulled. "And I wanted to see your face."

She threw a handful of moat at him, and he let it hit him, and that was better.



At the stage, while the boat man argued with Ferra about the deposit, Emeric took a folded paper out of his coat and gave it to Waylon.

It was a drawing: a long box on wheels like a railway wagon, cut through to show the inside. Two stalls. Something in coils under the floor, like a carriage's springs but bigger. Padding along the walls.

"A horse box," said Emeric. "She hates trains."

Waylon looked at it for a long time. Then he went down on his heels on the boards of the stage, spread it flat, and put a stone on one corner and his knee on the other.

"The floor's wrong. It wants a slope to the back, or she'll stand with her weight forward the whole way and come off sore. And she'll want to see out. A slot here, with a bar, so she can't put her head through." He looked up. "Who drew this?"

"I did."

"Then you've never been in a box with a horse." Waylon held his hand out without looking. Ferra put a pencil in it. He began to draw, slowly and badly, over Emeric's clean line, and Emeric went down on his heels across the paper from him.

And there they were, Maeve thought, on the steps above them: the two of them on a floor. She had found them so a hundred times in the east nursery, Emeric with his blocks in towers and Waylon, four years younger, knocking them down, until one winter Waylon had started building stables round the bottom of the towers for his wooden horses instead, and Emeric had let him. She had not thought of it in twenty years. Here it was again, their heads nearly touching over one sheet, both wrong in different places.

She had the sense, for once, to say nothing.



Brass nine was a house in a row of houses that were all the same house.

The same brick, the same slate, the same green door with a step, all the way to the corner. Some had beans up on strings, and one had a goat. In the capital a house told you who lived in it before you knocked. These told you only that somebody did, and Maeve could not decide whether that was the fairest thing she had ever seen or the saddest.

Prudence Fairley was on her step at ten past four, still in the grey jacket, with a small brass key in her hand that had a number stamped in it. The lock was set into the door itself, a round brass face with a slot.

"Pin tumbler," said Prudence, turning it. "Ottoline Fesk's. Nobody gets in without this key."

"Somebody made the key," said Maeve.

"Somebody made you." Prudence pushed the door open. "The number's on my card and nobody else's. If I lose it I go to the works and stand in a queue and prove I'm me."

"And they keep a list of the numbers."

"Course they keep a list," said Prudence, with contempt. "How else would they know which was mine?"

Maeve did not trust it. She went in under it anyway, because she was filthy and the woman had said hot.

The wash room was at the back past the kitchen, small and white-tiled, with a drain in the floor and a pipe up the wall ending high over her head in a brass rose like the end of a watering can. Two taps. Prudence put a finger on one.

"Hot. The tank's in the roof, off the back of the range. Twenty minutes and it's gone, so don't stand there admiring it." She shut the door.

Maeve got out of six buttons and turned the tap, and the water came down on her head.

It was hot. Not the lukewarm of a palace bath that four girls had carried up two flights in cans, gone tepid by the fourth can, but hot, out of the air, for as long as she liked. Grayrock went grey down the drain, and the baby, and the night on the express, and the palace, a little; she stood with her palms flat on the warm tiles and laughed out loud at nothing. Then she stopped laughing and just stood.

Through the wall, a woman began to sing.

It was a kitchen song, the kind you sing over a pot when you think nobody can hear, a little flat, with a clatter in the middle as if a lid had gone on the floor, and a hiss under all of it like rain on a roof. Maeve turned the water off to listen, and the singing went on, and she turned the water back on, because she was not stupid.

When she came out at last in Prudence's towel the tank was cold. Waylon was asleep by the range with his boots out, Emeric was in the yard looking at where the pipe went into the roof, and Ferra and Prudence sat either side of the front room door, arguing without heat about a clock.

The singing came from a wooden box on the dresser with a brass horn on it like a great flower. On top of the box a black disc went round and round, and a needle on a bent arm rode it.

"Gramophone," said Prudence, without getting up. "That's Maren. They cut it through her kitchen hatch and she didn't know, and she didn't speak to the girl for two days. I bought it with my board money. First thing I ever bought I didn't need."

Maeve stood in the towel and listened to a woman who was not in the room sing over a pot she was not stirring. She had sung it once, months ago, and it had been kept, and here it was again exactly the same, the lid falling in the same place, like something folded up in a drawer.

The needle reached the middle and went round there, hissing. Prudence lifted it off.

"An hour and ten minutes," she said. "Four coppers."

"You said two for the tank."

"You've had two tanks. I'll wash cold now. I washed cold forty years before any of this; I'll not die of it."

Maeve paid her four, and then, on second thoughts, a fifth. Prudence gave the fifth back.

"Four. Do they have these in the capital?"

"No."

"Tell them," said Prudence Fairley, and put the kettle on the range for herself, since nobody else in her house seemed likely to.



They ate at Maren's, where Maren came out with a ladle to say Maeve had a good colour for somebody from the capital, and went back in without waiting to be thanked. Then they went up onto the town wall.

The wall walk was wide enough for a cart, and there were people on it in the long blue light: couples, a man with a dog, two girls from the clothing works who recognised the window dress and said so, loudly. Waylon had gone back to his horses. Ferra and Imani walked on ahead, arm in arm, and had stopped waiting for the other two.

Maeve had Emeric's coat on over the green, because the wind came up off the sea once the sun was down. It smelled of him and of machine oil and, very faintly, of squirrel.

The town went down the hill below them in its wards. To the west the works ran along the river, long sheds and chimneys with a little smoke standing straight up, and past them the cranes on the quays like herons. In the tree belt she could just see the switchback, black against the trees, with nobody on it.

Then the lamps came on along the Gate Avenue, a row at a time from the bottom of the hill to the top, with nobody walking along with a pole to light them. Then the next street, and the next. The windows went yellow in the rows of Standard houses, all the same and all lit, and the town came up out of the dusk under her.

In the palace, at this hour, a man would be going down the long gallery with a taper past her father's dark rooms and the dust sheets in the east wing.

"You could have written," she said.

Emeric had his arms on the parapet beside hers. He did not look round.

"I know."

That was all. He didn't give her the reason again; she'd had it years ago and told him then what she thought of it. He stood looking at his town, his face younger than it had any right to be and very tired, and waited for whatever she was going to do to him.

Maeve thought about four pages on a pony, spelled three ways. She thought about the purple card in her pocket, and the book it came out of, and the woman's voice in the box that had been kept and could be played again exactly the same; and how none of it was a letter, and how at fourteen she would have traded the lot for one.

Below them a tram went up the Gate Avenue with its windows lit, and its bell rang twice, small and clear, all the way up the hill.

"You can show me instead," said Maeve. "That's better."

He turned his head and looked at her.

"Tomorrow," she said. "All of it. The works and the port, and whatever Waylon won't tell me is in that glass thing. And I want to go on the switchback in the dark."

"They don't run it in the dark."

"Then ask them," said Maeve, and took his arm, the way Ferra had on the platform, and they went along the wall after the other two.
