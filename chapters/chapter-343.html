Chapter 343 - Garden Doors

Tamsy Brook had taken the whole front of her house off for the summer, and she was standing in the gap with her arms folded, daring the sky to try anything.

It was a narrow grey house on the square below the castle gate. In March it had had a door and two windows. Now eight tall panes of plate glass in oak frames hung on a brass track in their place, and every one had been folded back against the side wall like the leaves of a book, so that the room and the terrace were one floor. Six tables stood on the flags under a striped awning pegged to the castle's curtain wall, and over the wall leaned the old pear from the inner ward, in full leaf, with the fruit set small and hard and green all through it.

"Forty-one," said Ferra, looking up.

"Forty-one what?"

"Pears. On this side. There'll be more on the ward side; it sets heavier where it gets the morning."

"You can't have counted forty-one pears crossing the square."

"I counted them on the way down the hill," said Ferra, "while you were walking into the gatehouse lintel."

Tamsy unfolded her arms. She was a small square woman of about thirty with flour to the elbows and a cloth over one shoulder, one of Maren's girls from the days when Maren's kitchen was the only thing in Kaldo that could feed forty people at once. She counted the party coming across the flags the way Ferra counted pears.

"You said six and him," she said to Imani.

"I said six and him, and Tess, and Osric."

"Osric's two." Tamsy jerked her chin at the long table under the awning, two tables pushed together with a cloth over the join. "I've put you against the wall where the sun won't get in your plates. Nobody's wanted the inside since I took the doors off."

Bram had stopped at the end of the track and crouched on his heels to look at it. He ran his thumb along the brass rail, gently, the way he now did everything, and then along the underside of the nearest frame where the wheel sat.

"Who made these?"

"The glazing shop by the river. Frames and track from the works."

"Hm." He turned the wheel with one finger. "Wants grease."

"It's had grease."

"Wants more," said Bram, and went and sat at the far end of the table where he could see the track while he ate.



Tess and Osric came across the square a few minutes later on two bicycles, and every head on the terrace turned, because nobody in Kaldo was yet tired of watching bicycles.

Tess rode the way she drew, neat and a little faster than she needed to. Osric rode the frame three, which was built for a man his size and still looked like a toy under him, with his knees out and his face set and both hands on the bars as if it might bolt. He went round the fountain in the middle of the square in a very wide circle, well clear, and the whole terrace watched him do it.

"He's learning," said Ward.

Osric got off a full yard early and leaned the frame three against the castle wall beside Tess's with enormous care.

"Don't," he said to the table at large.

"I was only going to ask whether the fountain had recovered," said Saban.

"It's a fountain," said Osric, and sat, and his chair gave a long complaint under him and held.

The menu was on a board by the inner door in Tamsy's square chalk, prices in copper the way Maren had started it on the Gate Avenue, and under the prices, smaller: Maren says this is all wrong. Maren eats here every week. Pea soup with mint, the first of the peas. River trout in brown butter. Pork and apple pie. Lamb's liver and onions. Fried potatoes in a basket, with salt and vinegar on the table. Rhubarb fool. Coffee at two coppers, which was dear, and nobody at the table cared.

"Everything," said Osric, when Tamsy came. "Twice on the potatoes."

Tamsy looked at Imani, who nodded, and wrote it down. Imani ordered the liver and onions, and a second plate of it in a covered dish to go up the hill for Danner, who had been eating liver twice a day for twelve days and had sent word by one of the ward women that the kitchen boiled it and it squeaked.

"You're taking a patient his dinner," said Saban.

"I'm taking my patient his iron. It happens to be his dinner." She took a pencil out of her hair, where it had lived in her hair for a fortnight now that there was enough of it to hold anything. "The bone's coming on. The long bone's nearly down to where the elbow goes. Then the joint, and he's frightened of the joint, and he's right to be." She looked round the table. "Which reminds me. Breakages. This week's. Bram?"

Bram took his pipe out of his coat, looked at the canvas above him, and put it back.

"Tongs. Two." He considered. "A hinge. Mine. On my own door."

Imani wrote. "Saban."

"Fourteen," said Saban pleasantly. He produced a list from his breast pocket, folded small. "With what each cost. A cup, two cups, a pen, a pen, the sheet Ferra gave me, a chair back, a door handle, the clasp on my travelling case, which I'd had nineteen years, a lamp chimney, a pen..."

"That's three pens."

"Four. I didn't read you all of it. I'll bill the crown."

"You were last up. You're on the curve." Imani turned. "Ferra."

"None."

Imani looked at her over the pencil.

"One," said Ferra. "A teacup. It doesn't count, it was cracked."

"Everything counts. Ward?"

Ward said, "Two," and nothing else, and Arthur's strapped fingers were not mentioned by anybody at all.

"Emeric. You're the control."

"Nothing this week."

"He's been up longest," said Imani to the table, as if presenting a chart. "Five weeks. Eleven the first week, four, two, then none. That's the hands learning, not the body. The body's finished. Your hands still think they're the old ones, so they send the old push to a stronger arm, and the stronger arm does as it's told." She tapped the list. "If it were the body, the count wouldn't come down. It's coming down, in a straight line, in the order you woke. Saban's fourteen is exactly where it should be. It's beautiful."

"I'm so glad," said Saban.

Armand, who had not been asked, sat with his face up to the sun, eyes closed, his cardigan over the back of his chair. He had come out of his own stone more than a year ago.

"It took me a month to stop breaking teacups," he said. "My housekeeper bought me tin ones. I've still got them."

"Hold things like they're full," said Tess. "A cup, a pen, a door. Pretend it's full to the brim and you'll go slow without thinking." She glanced at Osric. "He held my hand like it was full for a week."

Osric turned a deep even red from the collar up.

The soup came, and the potatoes, a basket at each end and a third for Osric, too hot to touch, and for a while there was nothing at the table but eating.



The fork went halfway through Ward's trout.

He was cutting the fish with the side of it and telling Saban what the capital's mill-owners had written about the thirty-two hours, which was a great deal, when the fork folded down at the neck, quietly, the tines lying flat along the handle.

Ward looked at it. He laid it on the cloth beside his plate, bent as it was, took a clean one from the jar in the middle of the table, and went on cutting his fish and his sentence together.

Imani reached over, without a word, and wrote W. 3.

Tamsy, passing with the pie, picked up the bent fork, held it up to the light, looked at Ward, and took it away with her.

"She'll put that on the bill," said Saban.

"She'll put it on the wall," said Ferra. "Look."

There was a nail by the inner door, and on it hung a spoon twisted round once like a stick of barley sugar. Under it, in chalk: Rylan, and the date.



Bram had been saving it.

He had sat at the far end of the table through the soup and most of the pie with his elbows on the cloth, saying nothing, and every time Emeric looked up Bram was looking at him. No expression. The way he looked at a bar in the fire to see whether it was ready.

Emeric reached for the salt.

"Hm," said Bram.

It was not loud. It did not need to be. Saban put down his cup. Tess put her hand over her mouth. Armand opened one eye.

"That's for me," said Emeric.

"That's for you," said Ward.

"Ward told me on the stair," said Saban. "Before breakfast. He was still laughing, which made it hard to follow; I had to have it twice. Is it true you can't sleep?"

"It's true I didn't."

"Two nights running," said Imani helpfully, with her mouth full.

"Three," said Ferra, "if you count the first."

"He slept the first. He snored."

"I don't snore."

"Everybody snores," said Imani. "I've a list."

Ferra took a potato off his plate. Ward had his chin on his fist and his eyes on the pear, and the corner of his mouth was doing what it had done on the stair, and he was not letting it.

"There's a remedy," said Armand. "The great invention of civilisation. Separate beds."

"A bigger bed," said Tess. "That's the engineering answer. You can't put three in a bed built for one man in the old keep. It's a loading problem." She looked down the table. "Bram could make you a frame."

Everyone looked at Bram. Bram looked at Emeric for a long time, and then at his daughter, and then at Imani, and back at Emeric.

"Hm," said Bram.

"That's a no," said Ferra.

"That's a 'later,'" said Tess.

"I know my father's noises. That's a no."

Emeric put his face in his hands. Under the table Imani found his ankle with her foot, which was cold even in the sun, and left it there, and went on with her liver.



People came up to the table and went away again all afternoon, the way they did in Kaldo, where you could not sit anywhere long without somebody having something to tell you.

A girl from the Grayrock clinic in her half-day dress stood at Imani's elbow and said quickly that the boiling copper had a crack in it, and they had moved the linen to the second copper and boiled it twice, and was that right. Imani said it was right, and to tell the works, and to put the crack in the clinic's book with the date. The girl went away pleased, having looked at nobody else at the table once.

A woman came across with a child on each hand, two small girls in yellow pinafores pulling in opposite directions, and stood in front of Imani.

"I wanted you to see them in the light," she said. "You only ever saw them by a candle."

Imani put her fork down and looked at the twins properly, and they looked back at her, and at her hair.

"They've done well," said Imani. "Who cut this one's fringe?"

"She did."

"Good," said Imani, and the woman laughed and took them back to her table, and one twin looked back over her shoulder at Imani's hair the whole way.

Prudence Fairley came off the tram at the stop across the square in her conductor's jacket with her punch round her neck and walked in a straight line to Ferra, as though Ferra had been sent for.

"Two things." She held up two fingers. "Master Aubin took forty minutes at the last sitting over what 'healthy' means. I timed it. Then he wanted 'old' defined, and the steward wrote it all down, and we heard one case. One."

"Did you define 'old'?"

"I said old was when you had to ask." She lowered a finger. "And the pay. If it's by the sitting, he can talk all he likes. If it's by the hour, I want my forty minutes back."

"By the sitting," said Ferra. "And the sitting has a clock. I'll have the clerk hang one where Master Aubin can see it."

Prudence thought about this with deep suspicion. "Facing him?"

"Facing him."

"Then that'll do." She looked round the table at their young faces, and did not seem impressed by any of them. "You lot ate yours first, I read."

"Yes," said Imani.

"Hm," said Prudence, which made Bram look up, and she went back and caught the next tram as it pulled out, standing on the step.

"She doesn't like us," said Osric.

"She doesn't like anybody," said Saban, with approval. "That's why she's on it."



Saban had brought a goal to lunch the way he brought everything, folded small in his breast pocket until the moment came. When Tamsy came out with the rhubarb fool he turned his chair half round.

"What did the doors cost you?"

"Fourteen silver for the glass. Four for the frames."

"And to pay them back?"

"In sun, nine weeks. In rain, never. I shut them and it's a house again." She set down the glasses. "On the last fine day I turned every table out here four times. When it was a house I turned three tables twice. That's what they cost."

Saban nodded slowly. "The crowning's in June. There'll be a supper in the capital's hall after, two hundred covers, perhaps more..."

"No."

"I haven't asked anything."

"You're going to ask me to cook it." She folded her arms again, cloth and all. "I've the peas coming off the Milford farms in June and my doors open and every table full, and I'm not shutting them for a fortnight to stand in a cold kitchen in the capital cooking for two hundred lords who'll send the trout back because it's got its head on."

Saban took this well. He had been told no by men who owned whole streets. "Then I'll have the lords come here."

Tamsy looked at him, and at her six tables, and at the square with its fountain and its tram rails and the ice cart coming round the corner of the bakery with a string of children after it.

"I've six tables."

"They'll queue," said Saban, "which they've never done in their lives, and they'll talk about it for a year. You needn't answer now."

"No," said Tamsy, and went in.

"She'll do it," said Saban.

"She said no three times," said Ferra.

"She said no to the capital. And she told me her covers. People don't tell you their covers unless they want you to know how good they are."

Ferra wrote something in her notebook. Emeric did not ask what.



The ice cart stopped by the fountain. A small boy came away from it with a copper ice in a paper cup and stood at Armand's elbow, licking, until Armand opened his eyes.

"My brother says you're the old mage. But you're not old."

"Your brother is half right," said Armand, "which is better than most brothers."

"Make a spark."

Armand looked at the cloth, and at the canvas overhead, and at Bram, who lifted his new eyebrows. Then he held out his hand, palm up, and over the middle of it a white spark came and sat, no bigger than a pea, crackling to itself, blue at the edges.

"Bigger," said the boy.

"No," said Armand, and closed his hand. "That's the size it is today. Come back when you're taller."

The boy went back to the cart quite content, and could be heard telling the others that the old mage wasn't old and wouldn't do it bigger.

"You could have," said Tess.

"I am trying very hard, at my age," said Armand, shutting his eyes, "not to be the most interesting thing in the square."

The express's driver came by on his way home from the depot, a big grey man with soot in the creases of his knuckles, to tell Bram that the new whistle was too loud and the cows on the Milford road were running from it a quarter of a mile off, and was that good or bad, because the farmers had views. Bram heard him out and said "Hm," and then, unexpectedly, "Good," and the driver said the farmers wouldn't like that, and went off whistling.

Sela went by last, on her tricycle. She came round the fountain at a stately pace with library books in the basket and her hat tied on with a ribbon, and Tess half rose to watch the back axle, and Sela rang the bell twice and called, "Not falling off anything!" and went on toward the school without looking back.

Tess sat down. "The back axle's fine," she said, and then to Osric, quieter: "I was worried about the back axle."

"I know," said Osric.

Tess turned to Ferra, as if the axle had reminded her of something. "The shoot's put out two leaves."

Ferra put her fork down.

"In the dome. Not the sill. The sill's all dead." Tess took a potato. "The root's gone soft and the leaf's gone. The glass and the bit of pod haven't done anything. They're just sitting there."

"Two leaves," said Ferra.

"Small ones. Violet."

Ferra picked her fork up again and ate her trout, and laughed when the table laughed; but Emeric, beside her, watched her take the book out of her lap under the cloth and cross out one word in the clause and write four in its place, very small, and put the book back without looking at it.

"It hasn't rooted," he said, low.

"It's got leaves," said Ferra. "I'm not waiting for it to have roots."



Harrold came down from the gate at about three, which nobody had expected, in a plain blue shirt that fitted him.

The whole terrace watched him come. At the table nearest the square two old men in their best coats got halfway out of their chairs before they remembered, and sat down again, and looked at each other.

"Majesty," said the express driver, who had stopped at the fountain.

"Not since the fourth," said Harrold. "Harrold does. Or 'sir,' if you're stuck." He looked at the empty chair at the end of the long table, by Bram. "Is that anybody's?"

"It's yours," said Ferra.

He folded himself into it the way a young man does, without thinking where his knees went.

"She sent me out," he said. "She says I've been standing in her light for two days. I'm to sit in the sun and come back and tell her what everyone ate." He looked down the table. "In order."

"Soup, trout, pie, liver, potatoes, fool," said Osric at once. "Twice on the potatoes, sir."

Harrold looked at him with real respect. "She'll like that." He took a potato from the basket and ate it, and watched Emeric reach for the salt, and cleared his throat.

Emeric put the salt down.

"I said nothing," said Harrold. "I'd decided not to mention that they've salt on already. I'm going to be very restrained, now I've time. It'll be a great strain on me."

Bram said "Hm" beside him. Harrold turned and looked at Bram, and then at Emeric, and back at Bram, and something passed between the two fathers that Emeric did not like at all.

"Hm," agreed Harrold.



September came along the wall-top road from the inner ward at about half past three, the way it always came.

Emeric felt it before he saw it. Since the shed his sense ran far wider than it had: on a still afternoon he had the whole square without trying, every note at every table, the bakers in their back room, the bright cluster round him and Harrold's heat at the end, the tram coming up from the river a long way off. September was a pinprick in all of that, quick and warm and busy, stopping and going along the top of the curtain wall.

It went down the pear and dropped onto the awning with a soft thump that made the canvas bounce, ran down to the edge, and hung there by its back feet a foot above Ward's plate, looking at the potatoes.

Ward saw it. He did not move.

"Don't," said Ward to the squirrel, quite reasonably.

September dropped onto the cloth, took the largest potato on Ward's plate in both hands, and turned to go.

Emeric caught it.

He did it from his chair: his right hand went out and closed, and the squirrel was in it. He had not decided to. Before the shed he would have been two seconds late and fallen off the chair. Now his hand was there before September had finished turning, and only once it was there was he afraid.

Not of the teeth. He had seen Bram's tongs fold like wet paper and heard Saban read out four pens. He held the squirrel round its middle like an egg he had been told not to crack, with no more push than he would have put round a full glass, and held his breath as well.

"Not at the table," said Ferra.

September fought. It twisted like a wet rope and got its back feet against his wrist and kicked, and bit him hard in the web of the thumb. He felt the teeth go in. He did not close his hand.

"That's drawn blood," said Imani, leaning across with interest. "Don't squeeze it."

"I know."

"I've never seen you hold anything that gently. Hold everything like that and I'll stop counting you."

He brought it in against his chest and cupped his left hand over it, loosely, the way you shelter a candle. Its heart was going so fast it was one sound and not a beat at all. He did nothing about it. He let his hands be warm and kept them still and waited, and the table went on with its business round him, or pretended to, which in Kaldo was the same thing.

Tamsy came out with a hazelnut on a saucer. "If you're keeping it," she said, "it'll want paying," and put it by his elbow and went back in.

It took a long while to tire, and then it tired all at once: it stopped kicking and lay against his chest with its tail over its back and its sides going and its black eyes on the potato it had dropped. Slowly the heart came down. Not to anything slow; a squirrel's heart was never slow. But down out of terror into something that was only a squirrel's heart, quick and even. When it was there he laid two fingers of his left hand on its side, low, where the ribs came in, and went in along his own mana through the bead, into the pinprick, and asked.



It gave him everything.

A man, read, gave you what you asked for and kept back the rest, because a man had rooms and could shut the doors. September had no rooms. It did not know how to give a little. The whole of it came across at once, without words, the way a bucket comes over when you tip it, and while it lasted there was nothing in Emeric but squirrel.

The wall-top road, not as a picture but as places in an order, each with its feel underfoot: the warm flat coping by the gatehouse, the gap at the third merlon where the wind came round hard, the stone by the keep stair that rocked, that you went over fast and never stopped on. The pear, which was everything. The dome's glass, cold and unclimbable, an annoyance. The smell from Tamsy's chimney, which meant certain hours. The tram bell, which meant nothing, except that after it there were sometimes crumbs at the stop.

The hawk. A shape that was the hawk's shadow on the flags, with a feeling fixed to it that went through everything else and stilled the whole road: the sky as a lid, and the need to be under something, now.

And the caches, known as surely as his own name. Eleven hazelnuts, two acorns and the end of somebody's bun in the crack behind the gatehouse lamp bracket. Pear pips under the moss in the gatehouse gutter. Something hard and round in the lead of the keep's downpipe, a coat button, he thought, which September valued above all the hazelnuts together for no reason he could find.

There was no Emeric in it anywhere. It did not know what he was. He was a warm thing that had stopped it.

It was brighter in there than he had expected, and there was more of it. He could not tell whether that was this squirrel, or the air it had been born in, or only that he had never been inside a beast and had nothing to set beside it. Bettany's dense shelf came into his head, the rats a tenth heavier than their sisters, and he put that away for later.

He tried first what he wanted, which was leave Ward's plate alone, and there was nowhere to put it. September had places and things and going to them. It had no "not." Offering it the plate with "leave" in front was like asking someone to take the absence of a cup. Only the plate got across. He felt it land in September like a lamp lit in a window, warm and wanted and very near, and took it back hastily before the squirrel could go for it through his fingers.

So he gave it a place. He laid the keep end of the wall-top road on the table between them, the way he would lay a drawing in front of Tess: the rocking stone, the corner of the downpipe, the patch of coping where the sun hit last. He did not push. He set it down and waited, the way you hold out a question.

September took it. He felt it become a thing the squirrel was going to do, as plainly as a man nodding.



The square came back all at once, loud.

The bakers. The fountain. The tram, much nearer, grinding round the bottom of the hill. Two dozen people at six tables, and the six round him, and Harrold's heat, and Ferra's hand on his forearm, and her voice close by, finishing something.

"...meric."

He blinked at her. She took her hand away.

"That's twice," said Ferra. "I said your name twice. You didn't hear either."

"I didn't hear anything."

"No," she said, and looked at him in a way he could not quite read.

Saban reached across and set a small leather purse down beside the hazelnut saucer. Emeric looked at it. It was his.

"About a minute in," said Saban, before he could ask. "Your eyes were open. You looked straight at me. I took it out of your coat and counted it, eleven silver and some copper and a hairpin I expect belongs to somebody, and you didn't blink." He picked up his cup. "That's a lesson. Don't do it anywhere a man like me is sitting across from you, which is most places."

"Not two minutes, all told," said Harrold from the end of the table. He was watching Emeric as though he had seen something he recognised. "You went a long way off for two minutes."

Emeric opened his hands.

September sat on his palm a breath, sides going. Then it went: up his arm, off his shoulder, up the awning's pole in three bounds, into the pear, onto the wall, and along the wall-top toward the keep, stopping and going. At the far end, by the keep stair, it stopped on the patch of coping where the sun hit last, and sat up.

The whole table had turned in their chairs.

"It went," said Tess.

"It went where I asked it."

A hundred yards off, very small, September washed its face, both hands over the ears and down, several times, thoroughly. Then it came back. It came along the wall-top at a good pace, down the pear, across the awning, down the pole, and onto the table, where it walked straight past the hazelnut, took another potato off Ward's plate, and left.

Ward watched it the whole way with a fork in his hand. The fork stayed straight.

"I saw that coming from the keep," he said.

"Useless," said Imani.

"It went where I asked it," said Emeric. "I asked for the keep end. It went to the keep end. It can't hold 'don't.' It's got places and things, and once it's been where I asked, it's done, and after that it does what it likes."

"That," said Armand, with deep feeling, "is every student I ever had. Went exactly where you sent them, and the moment your back was turned they were back for the potatoes."

Imani had his right hand turned over on the cloth and was looking at the two small holes in the web of the thumb. She held a finger an inch off them and they closed, and the skin went pink and then ordinary.

"What's it like? In there?"

He told her, badly: the bucket and the lid of the sky and the rocking stone and the button. She listened with her chin on her other hand and asked two sharp questions, about the heart and whether it had known him, and wrote both answers on the back of her breakage list.

"It didn't know me at all," he said. "I was just a warm thing."

"Then it's like most people," said Imani, "the first time they meet you."

There had been squirrels like this at home, grey ones, in a park by a road whose name he had lost, that took chips out of the bins by the benches and were frightened of nothing. He kept that to himself. Ferra was looking at him again, and he thought she knew where he had gone, and that she would not ask in front of anyone.

The hazelnut was gone off the saucer. Nobody had seen it go.



Ferra had brought the envelope. Of course she had. It came out of her notebook soft at the corners and brown along the fold, the April crust and the August nut on the back in her small upright hand. She uncapped the seamed pen and wrote under the last line, holding the pen as if it were full to the brim.

14 May. Caught September. Asked it to go to the keep. It went. It came back. It took Ward's potato.

She looked at it, and at Ward, and added: Two.

"It's not a competition," said Ward.

"Everything counts," said Ferra, and drew a line under it.

The sun had gone round and came in under the awning now, across the cloth and the empty fool glasses and the spoon on its nail, and the shadows of the pear leaves moved on the table. Tess had her feet up on Osric's chair. Saban was writing a list on the back of the bill. Tamsy came out with the covered dish of liver, saw Bram looking at the brass track again, and put a pot of grease down in front of him without a word; and Bram said "Hm," and took it, and went to see about the wheels.

Harrold stood and stretched until his back cracked.

"I'll tell her about the squirrel," he said. "She'll want it in order."

"Tell her I got bitten."

"I'll tell her that first," said Harrold, and took two potatoes from the basket for the walk, and went off up to the gatehouse, ducking under the wicket a good six inches early, because he had learned in two days what his son had not in five weeks.



They walked up the hill in the long light, slowly, because Imani was carrying Danner's liver and would not be hurried with it, and because half the square wanted to stop them, and because none of them was in any hurry at all.

At the wicket Emeric ducked, in plenty of time. Ferra saw him do it and said nothing, and the not saying went on long enough to be a comment.

"You'll sleep tonight," said Imani. "You've had sun. And the read. That sort of thing tires you."

"Will I?"

"Probably not," said Ferra.

Imani laughed out loud in the gatehouse passage, where it rang, and the guard on the wicket looked round. Emeric walked on between them up the ring path toward the old keep, with his purse back in his pocket and a squirrel bite gone from his thumb.

Halfway up, without turning her head, Ferra said, "Emeric."

"I heard you that time."

"Good," said Ferra, and took his arm, and Imani took the other, and they went on up.
