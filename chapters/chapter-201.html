Chapter 201 - Counting Signs

By the week the river dropped, there were seventy-one signs on the slate.

Emeric had begun the count on the second evening after Zale came up from the gate, with the book open on the table in the small room at the end of the long room and a slate beside it, and he had not missed an evening since. Every time his eye found a shape on the page it had not found before, he drew it on the slate and gave it a number. That was all. He did not guess what any of them meant. He did not let himself guess, which was harder, because some of them looked like things: a hook, a ladder, a thing like a bird's foot with a bar across it, a square with a dot in its middle that his hand kept wanting to call an eye. He drew them and numbered them and moved on.

Ferra kept the other half of it. She had ruled a sheet the first night without being asked, a column for the page and a column for how many signs were on it and a column for how many of those Emeric had never seen before, and every night she added a line. The first pages had given them twenty new signs, and eighteen, and twenty-two. By the second week it was six a page, and four. This week it had been one, and then none, and then one again, a bent thing like a snail's horn that had hidden on the fortieth page in the corner of a line.

"It's flattening," Ferra said. She turned the sheet so he could see the shape the numbers made down the page. "Seventy. Maybe eighty. Not three hundred."

"Not twenty-six, either."

"Why twenty-six?"

"Any number round there." He had said it without thinking and now he had to think. "If it were letters, one sign for one sound, you'd run out of new ones in the first few pages. Two dozen, three at most. A people can't make more sounds than that with their mouths, not that they'd bother to write. If every word had its own sign you'd never run out. You'd still be finding new ones on the last page." He put his finger on her column. "This is neither. It's a sign for a piece of a word. A sound and the sound after it. Ka. Lo. Do."

Ferra wrote something in the margin of her sheet. He knew without looking that it was the three syllables of the barony's name, and that she had marked them as his guess and not as a finding.

There had been a man in his old life who had done this, or something like it. He could not remember the man's name. He remembered that he had not been a scholar at all but a builder of houses, a drawer of plans, and that he had done the whole of it at a kitchen table in the evenings after work, with a grid, because a grid was the only tool he trusted. Emeric had liked him for that when he read about him, a long time ago, in another world. He liked him more now, at a table in the evening after work, with a grid. There had been another man, earlier, who had been luckier: he had had a stone with the same words cut in three kinds of writing, one of which he could already read. Emeric had no stone like that. He had a book nobody could read, a brother who could read the notes in its margins badly, and a woman with a ruled sheet.

It was more than the builder of houses had started with.

The book had three things in it, and they had learned to keep them apart.

The first was the copying. Page after page of the signs, in rows, in a hand that was careful and not easy, the hand of a man drawing something he did not understand and did not want to get wrong. Sometimes there was a line of drawing round a block of them, the edge of a stone, a crack across it, a corner broken off, so that you could see the man had copied a thing carved in a wall and had copied the wall too. That was the part nobody could read. Armand had turned those pages for an hour on the night Zale came, with his spectacles on and then off and then on again, and closed the book and said he had handled books from every kingdom that had books and he could not read one line of that, and he did not think anybody in any of them could. Then he had asked to borrow it for a night and given it back in the morning and said the same thing again, more quietly.

The second was the margins. Wherever there was room, the copier had written round his copies in his own tongue, small and fast, in a brown ink gone pale. That was the part Zale could read, a little, because it was a tongue from the far side of the western water that he had picked up in ports over a great many years, and spoke, he said, like a sailor, and read like a child of six.

The third was the drawings. Whole pages of them, here and there, of things Emeric could not put a name to and had stopped trying to. He had decided the first night to leave them alone until the signs gave him somewhere to stand, and so far the signs had not.

So: numbers first. That was the other thing the builder of houses had done, or somebody had. A people who wrote anything wrote how many. He had gone through the book looking for short runs of the same mark repeated, next to drawings of things that could be counted, and he had found them. A stroke, two strokes, three, four, standing beside what might have been jars, or baskets, or stones. After four, a different sign, a hook with a tail, and then the strokes again after the hook.

"Five," Ferra had said, when he showed her. "Or ten."

"Or twenty. Or a word that means a great many."

"Or a word that means and some more besides," said Ferra, "which is what I write when Saban gives me a count from a warehouse." She had put the hook in its own row on her sheet, and drawn a ring round it, and a ring on her sheet meant a guess.

Zale read the margins from the window seat, because the window seat was the place in the small room nearest the door.

Emeric had noticed that in the first week and said nothing about it. His brother came into a room the way weather came into it, easily, all at once, bringing the smell of outside, and then sat down where he could see the way back out. At meals in the hall he took the end of the bench. On the wall walk he stopped at the stairheads. He did it without looking as if he were doing it, which meant he had been doing it long enough that he had stopped knowing. Half a lifetime at sea, and ports with no friends in them, and a court before that with Corbin in it. Emeric supposed you learned to sit near doors.

He read with his finger under the line and his lips moving, and then said the line aloud in their own tongue, slowly, trying words and throwing them back.

"'My hands are too cold to hold the pen,'" he read. "Or the pen is too cold to hold. One of them. 'The boatman,' something, 'twice the price he said on the quay, and I paid it, because the,' I don't know that word, 'because the tide.' Something about the tide." He looked up. "He got cheated by a boatman. I like him already."

"Sure?" said Ferra.

"Of the boatman? Certain."

She made a stroke beside the line. A stroke was sure. A dot was half. A ring was a guess. She had explained the marks to Zale on the first night and he had laughed and said she was the first clerk he had ever met who wrote down how much she believed him, and she had said she wrote it down for everybody, he was only the first to notice, and Emeric, at the other end of the table, had looked at his slate so as not to look at either of them.

The copier had been a man. That was the first thing the margins gave them, and Emeric had not known until he had it how much he had needed to know it. His hands had been cold. He had had a tooth that hurt him for a month in the middle of the book and then, on a page further on, did not, and he did not say why. He had been cheated by boatmen, more than one. He had been hungry, and had written down, once, with what Zale said was plain joy, what he had eaten when he stopped being hungry. Whoever had cut the signs he copied, the man who copied them had been a man with a tooth.

And he had come from a long way off. He counted his days in the margins the way sailors did, days out from a place whose name Zale could read and none of them knew, days past a light he called the last light, days of open water after it with nothing written at all. Zale had sat with that page a long time.

"That's west," he had said. "Nothing on Saban's charts goes that far. Nothing on anybody's. The ports where they speak this, the ones I know, they talk about further west the way we talk about the moon." He had put his finger on the unknown name. "He's from past there."

Ferra had given that a dot.

The rest came slower. Near the front of the book there was a page where the copier said he had found the signs in his own country first, cut into a hill above a river, on a stone that the shepherds there used for a wall. Zale was sure of the hill and the shepherds and not sure of the river. The copier had copied the stone. Then, some years later, from the look of the ink, he had seen the same signs again, on a thing a trader carried: a small thing, Zale thought, the word was the word for something you hung round a neck. And the trader had said where it came from.

"An island," Zale read. "In a," he frowned, "a narrow sea, a channel, between two shores. Where the ground," and he stopped, and read the line again, and laughed, not happily. "Where the ground grows glass."

Emeric looked up from the slate. On the table between them, beside the book, lay the pouch Zale had brought with it, open, and in the pouch the shard crystal he had bought for next to nothing in tavern after tavern along the northern coasts, off Kesrathi sellers who would not say where they got it in front of strangers. Long pale splinters, some clear, some smoky, with a feel to them under the sense that Emeric had not yet found a word for. Not like the stones Nevin cut. Rawer. As if they had been broken off something that was still growing.

"The sacrifice ground," Zale said. "That's what the sellers called it."

"Sure?" said Ferra.

"Of what he wrote? Half." Zale shut the book on his finger. "Of what the Kesrathi call it, sure. They said it the way you'd say the name of a place where your uncle drowned."

There was one more word from those pages that Emeric found himself writing on the edge of the slate, away from the signs. The copier used it for the people who had cut the stones. Zale tried it three ways and threw each back. "The earlier ones. The ones from before. Not the old ones, he's got a word for old and it isn't that." He shrugged. "The ones before."

Ferra gave it a dot, and did not say anything, and neither did Emeric.

It was two margins a night, some nights three. Zale was not slow because he was stupid. He was slow because the tongue on the page was a written one and he had learned a spoken one, and every line was a sum he had to work, with half the numbers missing.

On the Thursday Ferra did the arithmetic, which Emeric had been not doing for a week.

"There are three hundred and some margins," she said. "At this rate, it's spring." She did not look at Zale. "And Fletcher says the gales are due."

They were. The first autumn blow came up the coast every year within a fortnight of the equinox, give or take, and sat on it, and after that the sulphur boats went home when they could and came when they could and nobody sailed for pleasure. Zale had come up the Emberfell lane at midsummer. He had meant to go back down it before the weather turned; he had said so the first night, easily, the way he said everything, and Emeric had heard what was under it, which was that a man who had been gone half his life had not come home to stay.

Emeric looked at the book, and at the slate, and at his brother in the window seat by the door with his finger under a line he had been reading for a quarter of an hour.

There was a faster way. There had always been a faster way.

Ward did not like it. He said so in the passage outside, with the door shut.

"No," he said. It was the same flat voice he used for reporting a gate, which was the voice he used for everything, and it was the whole of his first answer.

"He's reading at two pages a night. He sails in a fortnight."

"Then he sails with two pages a night." Ward stood with his back to the wall and his arms folded. "Six people know what you do in there. Six, in all the years you've been here, and every one of them you'd watched for a year first. You've watched him for six weeks."

"He's my brother."

"So's the other one." Ward let that sit. "He's going out of this kingdom on a sulphur boat, alone, to Kesrathi ports, asking questions about a place Kesrathi don't like questions about. If somebody puts him on a table, he's got every page of that book he's ever read to give them. That's bad. With this, he's got you." He did not raise his voice. "A thing like that in a prince's head. The Church hears it, they won't trouble with a court."

"There's nobody in the world who could check it," Emeric said. "If he told it, it would be a story about his brother, from a man who collects stories."

Ward looked at him for a moment as if that were an answer he would think about later and did not think much of now.

"And everything you've ever seen's in that room," he said. "On the shelf. You told us so yourself. The first day."

"I know where it is. I'll stand in front of it."

"And if he turns round?"

"He won't. The book will be in front of him." Emeric heard how that sounded and said the rest anyway. "Ward. If he goes with half a book in his head, he'll go further to find the other half than I would ever send a man. That's what he is. I'd rather he went knowing what he was looking for. And I'd rather it was the whole of what we can give him, and not two pages a night."

Ward did not answer that for long enough that Emeric thought he might not.

"He's your brother," he said at last. "That's the reason. The rest is the case for it." He unfolded his arms. "I'll be on the door."

Emeric told him in the small room with the shutters closed, and Ferra at her end of the table with her band on her wrist and her hands flat on her ruled sheet, and Ward on the door.

He did not tell it well. He had told it six times in all his years in Kaldo and never once to somebody who had known him before, and he found that it came out worse for that, not better. That there was a place he could go, in his own head, and take people. That time went differently there. That anything a man had seen with his own eyes was there with him, the way he had seen it, and could be looked at again as long as he liked. That the six people Zale had eaten with every night for six weeks had all been there, and some of them went on their own now. That it was how he had done most of what Zale had walked round Kaldo looking at.

Zale listened to all of it from the window seat. When it was finished he was quiet for a moment, and then he laughed, the easy laugh, and it did not quite come off.

"You're serious."

"Give me your wrist."

Zale looked at Ferra, as if she might tell him it was a joke. Ferra looked back at him and did not. After a while he came and sat in the chair beside Emeric's, which Emeric had set there for him with its back to the wall and its arms, because both bodies wanted to be held by something when the rest of them went, and put his wrist out on the table the way a man puts his hand out to have it read, half mocking, half not.

Emeric put his fingers on it and reached.

It was like leaning on a door and finding somebody on the other side. Not pushing back. Only there, and not moving, with his weight set. Emeric felt it the moment he touched it and knew exactly what it was, because he had felt it once before, years ago, at another table, with a young woman who had been a little frightened and had not known she was holding on.

He could have pushed. He had never pushed a man who did not want to come, and he did not know what it did to them, and he had decided at that other table, years ago, that he would not find out on somebody he loved. He let go.

Zale was looking at him. "Well?"

"You held on."

"I didn't do anything."

"I know. You held on anyway." Emeric kept his fingers where they were. "You have to want to come in. That's all it is. You don't have to do anything, you have to want it."

"I don't generally want to go anywhere I can't see the way out of." Zale said it lightly, and it was the least light thing Emeric had heard him say in six weeks.

"You can see the way out of this one. You can leave whenever you like. The first time one of the six came in, she looked round for about half a minute and walked out again on her own, to see if she could. Then she came back."

Zale thought about that.

"And if I don't like it?"

"Then you leave, and you sail with two pages a night."

His brother looked at him a long moment, and at the window, and at the door with Ward outside it. Then he laughed again, differently, and sat back in the chair.

"It's like going over the side at night," he said. "You don't decide to. You just stop holding the rail."

Emeric reached, and there was nothing on the other side of the door at all.

The plain light.

He had not brought his brother to the room above Kaldo. He had thought about it and decided against it in the passage with Ward, because the room above Kaldo had too much in it now, too many years of it, and none of it was Zale's to see. He brought him to the first place instead, the place before he had built anything, which was nothing but light, grey and even, like a morning with high cloud, and floor enough to stand on.

Zale stood on it.

He did not say anything for a while. He looked at his hands, and turned them over, and looked at the light, and then down at the floor, and then at Emeric, and his face did several things one after another that Emeric had last seen it do when they were both children and Zale had fallen out of the apple tree in the east court and not yet decided whether it hurt.

"Where," he said, and stopped.

"Nowhere. My head. Yours, a bit, now."

"I'm sitting in a chair." Zale said it carefully, like a man testing a plank. "I can feel it. I can feel your hand. And I'm here."

"Both. It gets easier."

Behind Emeric, where he did not turn to look at it, was his own shelf. It was always behind him in here, the way a man keeps his desk on the wall he turns his back to when company comes, and he felt it there the way he always did: a lifetime of looking, the whole of it, down to the smudges. He stood where he was, and kept his feet where they were, and did not turn round. It cost him more than it should have. He had known it would.

Beside Zale was Zale's own. It had come in with him, as everybody's did, and Emeric did not look along it. It was not his, and not looking was the only thing he had ever been able to promise anybody about it. He looked only where he meant to, which was at the end of it nearest his brother's hand, and there the book was.

Not one book. Every page of it that Zale had ever turned, and he had turned them all, many times, in all the years of carrying it. Each one was there the way he had seen it: this one by a candle in a ship's cabin, yellow at one edge, with the shadow of his own thumb across the corner; this one in hard noon light on a quay with a ripple of salt stain through it that the real page did not have yet; this one crooked, half-seen, over a tavern table.

"Lay them out," Emeric said. "All of them. Side by side."

"How?"

"Want them laid out."

Zale looked at him as if he were being made fun of. Then he looked at the shelf and frowned, and the pages came off it. Not all at once and not neatly; some of them came twice, from two different nights, one by candle and one by day, and some came upside down. But they came, and lay in the grey light at the height of a table, in rows, the way no bound book had ever let anybody see it, every page of it at once.

Zale made a sound that was not a word, and then said a word Emeric had not heard him use since the stable yard.

Ferra came in through her own door.

She did it quietly, the way she did everything in here now, and it still made Zale jump backwards half a pace and put his hand to a sword he was not wearing. She stood at the end of the rows of pages with her hands folded in front of her, exactly as she had sat at the table, and looked along them the way she looked along a column.

"Nothing we write in here comes out," she said to Zale. "So I'll remember it."

"You'll," Zale began, and looked at Emeric, and at her, and whatever he had been going to say he decided not to. "Right," he said instead. "Right." He looked down at the nearest margin, and put his finger under the line out of habit, and began to read.

It was an hour. Emeric felt every minute of it, the way he always felt the room when there was more than one other person in it: not in time but in weight, a steady draw on him, by the head and by the work, like carrying a child up a long stair. Outside, he knew, the candle on the table had barely moved.

Zale was not faster in here. He was the same sailor with the same child's reading. But there was no candle guttering and no Ferra waiting with her pen raised and no fortnight to the gales, and above all there was every page at once. When he did not know a word on one page he could go and find it on another, three rows over, where it stood in a line he did know, and come back with it. When the copier used the same phrase twice, forty pages apart, Zale could stand between them and read them together. By the second quarter of the hour he had stopped putting his finger under the line.

The island came clearer. The trader with the thing round his neck had come from a coast of dry hills, and had said the island lay off it, across water a man could row in a day, with a fort at each end of it that belonged to two different kings and a middle that belonged, the trader had said, to whoever was on it. The copier had gone. It had taken him most of a year and he had written very little in that year, and what he had written was about money. He had got there. He had found the glass that grew out of the ground, and men digging it who did not want him to see them dig. And under their digging, somewhere, he had found the signs again.

"'A way down,'" Zale read. He was on one of the last pages. It was one he had seen by candlelight, and the candle had been bad, and the margin was half shadow. "'There is a way down under the,' something, 'and the signs go down with it, and I will follow them in the,' morning, it must be morning. 'If this is the last,' something. 'If this is the last page.'" He stopped. "That's all. The rest of the page is his drawing of a door."

Nobody said anything.

Then Ferra, at the end of the row, said, "Here," and they went to her. She had been walking the copyings while Zale read the margins, looking at nothing but the rows of signs, the way she looked at nothing but a column when she was checking a sum, and she had found something neither of them had seen at a table in six weeks because at a table you could only see two pages at once.

Under some of the signs, not many, in the same pale brown ink as the margins, the copier had written small marks of his own. Sounds, in his own tongue's letters. A syllable under a sign, and a query after it.

"He was guessing," Ferra said. "Like you. He was trying to say them."

Zale read them off. There were eleven. He did not know what half of them were meant to be, because they were only sounds, but he could say the sounds, and Ferra said them after him, one by one, and Emeric understood that she was putting them away somewhere in her head beside the seventy-one on his slate, in her columns, with a ring round every one.

It was not the stone with three kinds of writing on it. It was eleven guesses by a dead man with a bad tooth.

It was more than they had had an hour ago.

Zale sat down hard on the floor of the small room.

He did it the moment they were out, before Emeric had taken his fingers off his wrist, as if the chair had not been where he left it, and he sat there on the boards with his back against the leg of the table and his knees up and breathed. Ferra was already writing. Her pen was going down her ruled sheet faster than Emeric had ever seen it go, and she did not look up, and she did not stop.

Emeric looked at the candle. It had gone down about the width of a fingernail. His head ached along the back, the way it always did when he had carried company, and his mouth was dry.

"How long," said Zale, from the floor.

"About a minute."

"Not that." His brother looked up at him. "How long have you been able to do that?"

Emeric could have made it smaller. He did not.

"Since my first winter here."

Zale did the sum. Emeric watched him do it, and watched the sum come out, and did not like watching it.

"When I was in Velmoor," Zale said slowly, "somebody told me in a tavern that the king's third son had been sent to a plague town to drink himself to death quietly, and was making a good start. I laughed. I remember laughing. I thought, that's Emeric, he always did do what he was told." He looked at the boards between his boots. "I thought about coming back. For years I thought about it. I thought, somebody ought to go and see about him, and there's nobody else who will." He laughed, short. "And you were sitting here. Doing that."

"Not the first year. I didn't know what it was for, the first year."

"Who else knows?"

"You've eaten with all of them."

"Six," said Zale. He had counted them at table without being told, the way he counted the doors. "And now me." He got up, slowly, using the table's edge, and stood, and did not sit back down in the chair. "I don't like it," he said. "I want you to know I don't like it. I don't know yet which part."

"That's fair."

"It isn't fair at all. That's rather the point." But the easy look came back over his face, most of the way, as if he had put on a coat. He looked at Ferra's pen going down the sheet. "Did we get it?"

"We got the island," said Emeric. "We got a way down. We got eleven sounds." He looked at the pouch on the table, and the splinters in it, pale and smoky, broken off something still growing. "And we got where your sellers are getting those."

Emeric knew the island by another name, which he did not say. Verandel's Claim. He had read it in the charter as a boy, in the long form of the kingdom's founding that princes were made to copy out, a line about a claim across the water in the name of the first king, and he had not thought about it since, because nobody had. A charter was a charter. Everybody in the capital would tell you the channel island was a rock with a fort on each end and nothing in the middle worth a quarrel, and had been telling each other so for as long as there had been a capital.

Nobody in the capital would pay Kesrathi sellers' prices for a pouch of crystal from a rock with nothing on it. Nobody would have to. The Kesrathi were selling it cheap in taverns, a splinter at a time, to anybody. Which meant there was a great deal of it, and they did not know what it was either, or did not care. And somebody over there was digging.

He said none of that. He said, at supper, in the long room with the six at table and Zale at the end of the bench, that he would like his brother to go back out the way he came, by sea, along the coasts where the Kesrathi sold, and buy more of what they were selling, as much as he could carry without looking like a man who wanted it, and listen. To what they called the place. To who went there and when and who came back. To anything anybody said about a way down.

"And if I can get there?" said Zale. He said it at once, before Emeric had finished, and Emeric saw that he had been waiting to be asked it and had already answered it himself.

"Then be careful there. It's somebody's."

"It's everybody's, from what the man said. Whoever's on it."

"That's what makes it somebody's."

Saban, across the table, looked up from his plate at that, and down again, and Emeric knew there would be a conversation later about which ports, and which sellers, and what a Kesrathi merchant's face did when a stranger asked the wrong question twice. Ferra counted a purse out at the end of the table after supper, in Rands, twice, and wrote it in the book under a line that said what it was for without saying what it was for, and gave it to Zale, and made him sign for it. He signed with a flourish, and she looked at the flourish, and gave the line a dot.

He was glad to go. He did not pretend otherwise and nobody asked him to.

"Six weeks is the longest I've slept in one bed since I was twenty," he said on the stair that night, going up. "I was starting to worry I liked it." At the turn he stopped and looked back down at Emeric. "I'll write. Somebody always goes somewhere a letter can get from."

He sailed four days later, on the morning tide, on an Emberfell boat.

She was one of the sulphur boats going home, and she went home heavy: not with sulphur but with Kaldo's own blue stone off the ridge, broken for the kiln over the sea, stowed along her bottom for ballast because an empty boat rolls. The boatmen still thought a prince who sent rocks across the water to be cooked was a strange thing and a good joke, and said so, and took his money. Zale went up her side with his sea bag on his shoulder and the pouch on his belt, empty now. He did not take the book. He had brought it as a gift, and he said so on the quay when Emeric offered it back, and said that he had read it so often he could not sleep without seeing it anyway, and that a thing nobody could read was better off with somebody who might. It was in Ferra's box, with her ruled sheets and the slate.

It was barely light. Out over the lower water a mist lay on the flats, and south of the quays, over the fold of land, the white lights on their masts above the yard still burned, hard and steady, the way they burned all night now. Zale looked at them from the rail.

"What's that?"

"The dock. They work nights."

His brother nodded as if that were the most ordinary thing in the world, and in Kaldo that summer it was. He did not ask anything else. He was already looking the other way, down the channel, toward the sea, with the look of a man who has been indoors too long.

The boatmen cast off. The tide took her, and the sweeps went out, and the boat went down the lower water slowly into the mist with her ballast in her and her one passenger standing at her stern, not by the rail but by the ladder, where a man could get off quickly if he had to. Near the channel's mouth he lifted one hand without turning round, the way he had lifted it over his shoulder leaving the stable yard when they were boys and he was going somewhere Emeric was not allowed.

Ward stood on the quay beside Emeric until the boat was gone.

"He's carrying it now," Ward said.

"I know."

"Not the book." Ward watched the mist where the boat had been. "The other thing."

"I know," said Emeric again. He did. He knew exactly what his brother was carrying down the coast into the Kesrathi ports in his head, and who would hang a man for it, and how little either of them could do about that now, and he had known it in the passage outside the small room with Ward's arms folded, and he had done it anyway.

Out on the water, faint through the mist, somebody on the boat laughed at something, loudly, and he knew the laugh.
