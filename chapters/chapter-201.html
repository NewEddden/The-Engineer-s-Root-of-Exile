Chapter 201 - The Mask

Ferra had written her conditions before he had finished saying what he wanted.

She laid them on the long table on the third of May on a single sheet in her own hand, in front of everybody, and did not read them out; she put the sheet in the middle of the table, squared to its edge, and sat back, and let them read it for themselves while Emeric was still explaining how a stone hears a stone.

He had got as far as the knots. He had Bram's figures in front of him, from a fortnight of evenings on the river bank below the works with the stoppered jar out of the cold press and a reel of wire and Tess's sounder: that a line of the black, wet, carried through one wall for twenty yards and no further, faint; and that a listener stone, if it was told to, could do more than click. It could hear the pulse and give it again, louder, to a second stone wired beside it, like a man at the back of a hall repeating a speaker's words for the people outside. Two stones bound together with wire. A knot. And a knot could hear a knot, if you told it to, a long way off; Bram had had one hearing another across the river at the mill, three hundred yards, on a wet evening, with a sheet of the black drying on a board between them.

"So you put a knot near the man writing," Emeric had been saying. "In his chimney. On his roof. Anywhere within twenty yards of his desk. And it hears him write, and gives it to a knot a little way off, and that knot gives it to another, and at the end of the line..."

And Ferra had put her sheet on the table, and he had stopped.

Everybody read it. Ward read it standing, over Saban's shoulder. Imani read it with her chin on her fist. Vantic read it once, quickly, and then again, slowly, and put her finger on the third line and kept it there. Armand had it read to him by Osric, who read it badly and was corrected twice.

The ink net. Five conditions. F.

1. A written warrant for each house listened to, signed by the Holder and entered in my book with the date and the reason. No warrant, no listening.

2. My clerks sort and read. Saban's men never hear a raw page. Only what I flag.

3. Anything that doesn't touch the Staple is struck the day it is read and never copied. Grief. Love. Debts between friends. Their children's letters. Struck.

4. Nothing heard goes before a court. Not one word. It tells Saban where to look. The case is made from what can be got openly, or it is not made.

5. It ends when the Staple ends. On the day the five are taken, every knot is drained, and I enter that it was done.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"The fourth," said Saban at last. He did not look pleased. "If nothing it hears goes before a court, Chancellor, I've a man reading five men's letters and nothing he can do with them but go and look under a stone where they said they buried something."

"Yes," said Ferra.

"And if he finds nothing under the stone?"

"Then we know where not to look." Ferra did not move. "And they never know we were listening. And the next time a lord in Randillia is hanged on a judgment out of this barony, nobody stands up in the capital and says Kaldo reads men's letters as they're written, because nobody in the court ever heard a word that came off a knot." She looked at Saban. "That's what the fourth is for. Not for them. For us. For the next ten years."

Saban looked at her for a long breath. Then he nodded, slowly, like a merchant who has been shown the second page of a contract.

"The third," said Vantic.

She still had her finger on it.

"Their children's letters," she said. "Struck. Never copied." She did not look up. "Who strikes them?"

"My clerks."

"Who read them first."

"Who read them first," said Ferra. "Somebody has to. You can't strike a letter you haven't read; you don't know what's in it." Her voice did not change. "I'll choose the clerks. I'll choose the ones who can read a man's letter to his dying daughter and strike it and not carry it home." She paused. "There aren't many."

Vantic took her finger off the line. She did not say anything else. Emeric, watching her, saw her look at Ferra across the corner of the table, a short level look, the kind one clerk gives another who has just said aloud the thing about the work that clerks do not say; and Ferra did not look back, and did not need to.

Imani said: "And if what's struck is a crime?"

They looked at her.

"A letter that doesn't touch the Staple," said Imani. "A man writing to his brother that he's beaten his wife again, and she's at his sister's, and he means to fetch her back." She had her chin on her fist still. "Struck. Never copied."

"Struck," said Ferra. "And then I'll find a reason that isn't a knot to send somebody to his sister's house. And I'll enter that I did, and why."

"That's not in the five."

"No," said Ferra. "It's in me." She looked at the sheet. "If I wrote down every case, there'd be fifty conditions and nobody would read past the sixth. There are five. Anything the five don't cover, I answer for, by name, in the book." She put her hand on the sheet. "That's what the F is for."

Imani looked at her a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and took her chin off her fist.

Emeric signed the warrants that afternoon. Six of them; one for each of the five houses, and a sixth, which Ferra wrote and he signed, for the Staple's counting house on the square at Wayford. Not for Lisle. Ferra would not write one for Lisle, because there was nothing in Sethe's boxes with Lisle's name in it, and no warrant without a reason; and Emeric, who wanted one for Lisle quite badly, did not argue, because she was right, and because he wanted it for no better reason than that Lisle had been careful.



The difficulty was the ink.

It was all very well to put a knot on a man's chimney. The knot heard only the black. It heard Kaldo ink boiled on a charged stone, and nothing else on earth; and nobody in Wayford wrote with Kaldo ink, because Kaldo ink stayed in Kaldo, and nobody in Wayford wrote with a grey ink in any case. They wrote with iron gall, like every clerk in the north, from Ambry's jars.

"Then we give it to them," said Saban, "and they won't use it."

"Why not?"

"Because it comes from you." Saban spread his hands on the table. "Highness. These are five old houses who paid for four years to keep you a drunk in a ruin, and who paid forty Gold to have you put in a boat. They know you're not a drunk in a ruin. They know who's got four baronies and an iron road. If the Holder of Kaldo sends each of them a fine bottle of a new black ink with his compliments, they'll pour it in the river, and then they'll wonder why he sent it." He shook his head. "A gift from a man you're afraid of is a thing you look at very carefully."

"And a gift from a man you're not afraid of?"

"You use it," said Saban. "Without thinking. Because it's good ink and it was free." He shrugged. "But they're afraid of you. They'd be fools not to be."

Emeric sat at the long table and thought about his brother on the slip in the mist, with his sea bag on his shoulder.

Corbin's waiting for the drunk. Everybody who's afraid of you would sleep better if they found him. You might remember that, one day, when you want somebody to sleep.

"Then they won't be afraid of me," he said.

He told them.

It was not much of a plan, said aloud. That was its virtue. He would go north again, openly, as he had gone in April, as the Holder of Wayford come to see his paper works; and he would ask each of the five houses for dinner, as a baron did; and at each dinner he would be the man they had paid four years to keep him. Older. Sober enough to have learned to add, and greedy enough to have learned to want. A prince who had found out at the junction that there was a fat trade going on his own road that he had no share of, and who had come up the hill with a smile and a full cup to ask, as one gentleman to another, how a man might get a share of it. Who would talk too much at dinner about his father, who had never valued him, and his brother, who had always had everything, and Kaldo, which was a ruin he had made something of with his own money and nobody had ever thanked him for. Who would drink too much of their wine.

And who would leave behind him at every dinner, as a gentleman did, a gift. A case of bottles. The new black ink from his own mill, very fine, that he was so proud of that he could not stop talking about it. Engraved with each house's own arms. From the vain, greedy, harmless younger son of a king, who had been handed four baronies by a court that did not know what else to do with them, and wanted to be liked.

"They'll use it," said Saban slowly, "if they think you're a fool."

"They've thought I was a fool for twenty years. It's not much of a step."

Ward, at the window, said nothing. Bram looked at his hands. Armand, in his chair, opened both his eyes, which he did not do often at the long table, and looked at Emeric for a long while with an expression that was not quite approval.

Ferra did not say anything either.

She sat at her place with her pen in her hand and her book open in front of her, and when he said the word drink, she looked at him. One look. Across the table, quick, and then down at the book again; and in it, for the length of a breath, he saw her see him at a table in a stone house four hundred miles away with a full cup in his hand and his old smile on, the one she had met in the first summer at the trestle under the castle wall, and had watched him take off, piece by piece, over seven years, so slowly that neither of them had noticed it go.

She wrote something in her book, small. He did not see what. She did not look up again until the table rose.



Bram and Tess built the suction pads in the works in nine days, because the knots had to go up onto roofs and into chimneys at night, and nobody could climb a Wayford roof at night in the ordinary way without being heard by every dog in the town.

He told Tess about the black on the first morning. He had meant to wait longer, and found that he could not ask her to build a thing for a reason she was not allowed to know; and she sat in her chair at the end of the bench in the works with her data book on her knees and listened to all of it, the size plate and the pulse and the sounder in the loft, without moving, the way she went quiet when something mattered; and at the end she said, before Bram could:

"Wet Kaldo ink warms a spent stone near it. Stops when dry."

"Yes."

"I wrote it on my slate. In the cellar. The morning of the wage forms." She looked at the bench. "And rubbed it out at night, with the rest, because I didn't know what it was." She was quiet. "Three years."

"Neither did I."

"You'd have worked it out. If I'd kept it." She opened her data book to a clean page and wrote the date at the top, and under it, small, in her careful hand, the same line again, word for word, and did not rub it out. Then she looked up at Bram. "What are we building?"

A suction pad was a brass cup. That was all it was, to begin with: a shallow round cup the width of a man's palm, spun on Bram's lathe from sheet brass, with its rim turned and lapped flat on the surface plate until you could not get a hair under it on glass. Round the rim, a ring of the Duvrenne pitch from the bottom of the still crew's copper, warmed and pressed on and trimmed, soft and black and tacky, that sealed the cup to whatever it touched the way a wet hand seals to a pane. And in the back of each cup, set in a little brass boss, an air stone, told to pull.

Press the cup to a stone wall and tap the boss, and the stone pulled the air out of the cup through a pinhole, and the cup clung. Not by magic; by the weight of all the air in the world pressing on the back of it with nothing in front to push back. Emeric drew it for Tess on her data book, the cup and the wall and the arrows of the air, and Tess looked at the drawing and said it was the same as a child's leather sucker on a string, that the children in the dock quarter stuck to the school's window when Sela was not looking, and he said it was exactly that, and she looked at him as if he had wasted her time.

Four cups to a set. Two on the boot soles, strapped under the instep. One on the off hand, on a leather mitt with a loop for the fingers. And a spare, for the knee, on a strap. Tap a boss and the cup took hold; tap it again and the stone let the air back in through the pinhole and the cup let go, with a small sharp pop like a cork coming out of a bottle, that you could not do anything about. Bram tried. He tried a little leather flap over the pinhole and a second hole and a softer pitch, and every one of them still popped.

"It'll pop," said Bram, in the end, grudgingly, as if the cup had won an argument with him. "Every time you let go. Nothing to do about it." He looked at the cup in his hand. "And it'll not hold on anything that isn't sound. Not on rough brick, not on old mortar, not on moss. The pitch wants a face. A good dressed stone, or slate, or glass." He put it down. "You'll want to look before you put your foot down."

"Wayford's houses are dressed stone," said Emeric. "The Staple house is. Saban looked."

"Then look," said Bram, "before you put your foot down."



He tried them on the keep wall the first night, over the canal ring, because the keep was the only dressed stone in Kaldo high enough to fall off, and the canal ring was the only thing under it soft enough to fall into.

Ward stood on the bank below with a lantern and a watch in his hand. Tess sat at the top of the bank beside him in her chair with her data book open on her knees and a second lantern on the chair's arm. Osric had come because he had been told by Ward to bring the rope, and had stayed because nobody told him to go.

The first thirty feet were easy. Emeric went up the keep's north face in the dark like a fly up a window: right foot, tap, hold; left hand, tap, hold; left foot, release, pop, up, tap, hold. The stone was cold and smooth and sound under the pitch, and the cups held as if they had grown there, and he thought, at thirty feet, with the canal ring black and still below him and the lanterns small, that this was not so hard, and was pleased with himself.

At thirty-two feet he put his right foot on a face of stone that was not sound. A patch of old lichen, flat and grey and invisible in the dark, that he did not look at before he put his foot down.

The cup did not hold. He felt it not hold; the pop, wrong, too soft, and his weight going onto nothing, and his left hand's cup tearing off the wall with a crack as everything he had swung out from it at once, and the cold air, and the black water coming up.

He did not use the gravity. He thought about it, all the way down, for the whole of the fall; he could have braked it with the Drop, a little lift and the rest taken in the legs, and come down into the canal like a man stepping off a cart. And Ward was on the bank with a lantern, and Tess, and Osric, and a garrison man at the river tower who could see the canal ring from his post; and the keep wall at night was not the place.

He hit the water flat on his back with a noise like a door slamming.

When he came up, coughing, with weed on his face, Ward was looking at his watch.

"Four minutes and a half," said Ward. "From the bank to the water." He looked up at the wall. "Thirty-two feet. Tess, write it."

Tess was writing it.

The second time he got to forty feet, looking down at the face before every step with the lantern Osric had tied to his belt, and the off hand's cup let go of its own accord, because the pitch on it had gone cold and stiff on the first swim and nobody had thought to warm it again. He knew as it went. He had time to say something to the wall that Tess did not write down.

"Two," said Ward, when he came up. "Forty feet. Five minutes." He held out the end of the rope. "Out. Osric's turn."

Osric went up the wall. Osric had asked to. He had stood on the bank through both falls with the rope coiled over his shoulder and his eyes on the wall and the wet black shape of the prince climbing out of the canal ring, and when Ward said his turn, he had already had the boot cups strapped on for some time.

He came off at twelve feet. He came off at twenty, and at nine, and at twenty-six, and at fifteen, and once, at six, so close to the bottom that he hardly got wet, and stood up in the canal ring in water to his waist with a frog on his shoulder, and Tess laughed out loud, the first time Emeric had heard her laugh out loud in a month. Every time he climbed out, she wrote it down. The height, and the time, and which cup, and why, in her square hand, with a little drawing of a man in the margin by each one. He did not look up the bank at her while she wrote. He looked at the wall. And every time he came out of the water, he looked at the wall again, and asked Ward if he could go back up.

"You have been in that moat six times," said Ward, at last, at midnight.

"Seven, sir. With the frog."

"Seven." Ward looked at him, wet to the hair, with weed on his collar and the cups on his boots. "His Highness has been in it twice."

"Yes, sir."

"His Highness is going to Wayford," said Ward. "You are not." He looked up the bank, at Tess in her chair with her data book and her lantern; and then back at Osric, steadily, for a breath longer than he needed to. "Why are you climbing that wall?"

Osric stood in the light with the water running off him and opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"To test the cups, sir," he said.

"To test the cups," said Ward. He looked at Tess's data book, at the page of little men falling in its margins. "They are well tested." He coiled the rope. "Go to bed. Both of you. All three."



Imani was at his door at half past midnight with her bag, because the garrison man at the river tower had told the watch, and the watch had told the ward's night nurse, and the night nurse had known exactly whom to wake.

She sat him on the edge of his bed in his shirt with the lamp turned up and looked at him all over, the way she looked at a man carried in off the road, and found the things he had not noticed: the skin off the heel of his left hand where the cup had torn away, a cut along the jaw from something in the canal ring, a graze the length of his forearm, green at its edges with weed. She cleaned them without speaking, briskly, with the brown spirit that stung, and he hissed at the jaw, and she held his chin still with two fingers and went on.

"Osric came off seven times," she said.

"Six. And once with a frog."

"Osric came off seven times," said Imani, "and he hasn't a mark on him. The night nurse looked. He went past the ward door wet to the hair, singing." She dabbed the jaw. "You came off twice. And you look like a plate somebody dropped down a stair."

"Osric didn't fall forty feet."

"Osric didn't fall forty feet because Osric isn't a fool who goes to forty feet the second night." She put the cloth down and turned his left hand over and looked at the heel of it, the raw place, and her mouth went tight. "You didn't stop yourself."

He looked at her.

She did not look up from the hand. She said it to the hand, very low, so that it went no further than the bed.

"You could have. I know you could have. I've watched you go light on the ship's deck in the middle of a stride, when you thought nobody was looking." She took the pot of salve out of her bag and opened it. "And you didn't, because Ward had a lantern and the river tower can see the canal ring. So you hit the water flat, from forty feet, twice, and took the skin off your hand, so as to look like a man who can't." She put the salve on the raw place with one finger, gently, much more gently than her voice. "And next month you're going to sit at five men's tables and look like a man who drinks."

"Yes."

"You're very good at looking like things you aren't," said Imani. She wound a strip of linen round the heel of his hand, round and round, and tied it off, and did not let go of the hand when she had. "I don't know if that's a good thing. I've not decided."

She sat on the edge of the bed beside him with his bandaged hand in both of hers, in her lap, and looked at it.

"Ferra hates it," she said. "The drinking. She hasn't said so."

"I know. I saw."

"Everybody at the table saw. She'd not want you to know that." Imani turned his hand over, palm up, and laid two fingers on the inside of the wrist below the band; not to count, he thought, this time; only to lay them there. "We both met what was left of the drunk, you know. In the first summer. I patched him. She kept his books." She was quiet. "She watched you put him down, line by line, in that book of hers. She won't like watching you pick him up again."

"It's only for the dinners."

"I know that. So does she. It's not the point." Imani took her fingers off his wrist and stood up, and closed her bag. "Go to sleep. I'll not have you go north with a hand that turns. Not with that weed in it." She went to the door, and stopped, and looked back at him on the bed in his shirt with his jaw dabbed brown. "And when you're at their tables. Don't drink their wine."

"I'll have to drink something."

"Then drink it slowly," said Imani, "and come home and let me look at your eyes." And went.



Bram boiled the six batches on the eleventh and twelfth of May, in the ink kitchen behind the mill with both doors locked and the size plate under every pot; and every batch had its own measure of the grey dust in it, weighed on Harl's little beam, from one pinch to six.

That was the whole trick of it. A listener stone could not tell one thing from another; it could only tell how much. But more dust drank more off the stone, and pulsed harder, wet; so a line of the six-pinch black pushed the needle on the office board six times as far as a line of the one-pinch. Six boilings. Six strengths. A knot on a house's chimney would tell the office that somebody in that house was writing; the needle would tell it whose bottle the pen had been dipped in. If Hollin's steward borrowed Placit's ink, or Strade gave a bottle to his brother, the office would know.

A radio, he thought, standing in the ink kitchen with his hand on the warm iron. Six stations, told apart by how loud they were. And he had not been able to build a radio in Kaldo yet, and would not for years, because the valves and the wire and the crystals were all a long way off; and here it was, in a pot of grey dust on a fire stone, the whole of the idea, from a mistake he had made on a morning with no sleep.

The bottles came up from the glass works on the thirteenth. Twenty-four of them, four for each house: squat, heavy, clear, with ground stoppers and a band of plain glass round each shoulder where the engraver at the works had cut, one house to a case, the arms Saban had drawn for him off the doors of the five houses in April. Placit's tower. Hollin's horn. Dray's three bars. Vennor's wheatsheaf. Strade's key. And for Lisle, who was not on any warrant and to whom, Ferra had decided, a gift could still be sent because a gift was not a warrant, a stag.

Tess filled them in the loft with a funnel, each case from its own crock, with the crocks marked and the cases marked and the marks written in her data book against the pitches; and checked every one, twice, with Bram's sounder, before she put in the stoppers.

"If I get one wrong," she said, not looking up from the funnel, "you'll hear Hollin and think it's Placit."

"Don't get one wrong."

"I don't," said Tess.



He went north on the fifteenth by the morning train, with the cases on the flat wagon at the back in a crate of straw, and Ward and Saban and two of the garrison, and his good coat with the four baronies on it.

At the railhead, before he mounted, he took off the coat and put on another.

It was the one he had brought down from the capital seven years ago, in the carriage, the one with the gold lace on the cuffs and the stain on the left breast that had never come out. Ferra had kept it. She had kept everything from the carriage, in a press in the counting room's back room, listed: one coat, gold lace, stained. He had asked her for it on the fourteenth, and she had gone and got it without a word and given it to him over the counting room counter, folded, and had gone back to her book; and he had not been able to tell from her face anything at all, which was how he knew.

It still fitted across the back, a little. It was loose at the waist. He had not had a waist like that since the capital. He stood at the railhead in the cold morning in a drunk's coat over a body that Ward had been running up and down the keep stair for four months, and did up the buttons, and felt the old shape of it settle on his shoulders like a hand.

Ward looked at him for a long breath.

"Highness," said Ward.

"Yes."

"Stand badly," said Ward. "Your back has been straight since January."



Placit dined at six on the twenty-second, in a long panelled room at the top of the stone house in the fork of the roads, above the chain, with the weathervane creaking on the ridge over their heads in the spring wind.

He was old. Older than Emeric had expected from the square hand; seventy, perhaps, small, very upright, with a white beard cut close and pale eyes that did not move much, at the head of a table laid for six. His son was there, and his son's wife, who said nothing all evening, and a cousin, and the Staple's chief clerk from the counting house on the square, a soft man with clean hands who smiled at everything. Vennor was to dine on the twenty-fourth, at his own house by the weir; the other three were to dine early in June. They had sent their answers to the Holder of Wayford's invitation in a single day, all five, on the same paper, which was Pell's grey letter paper, and Emeric had read all five answers at Sethe's old desk and known exactly what they meant.

They had talked about him. They had agreed together what to say. They were waiting to see what he was.

So he showed them.

He drank Placit's wine. Not much of it; slowly, as Imani had said, a mouthful at a time with a long pause between, and every mouthful he let them see go down. He let his voice go loud at the second course, and louder at the third. He talked about the iron road, and how much it had cost him, and how nobody in the capital had ever said a word of thanks. He talked about his father. He had not meant to say as much about his father as he said; it came very easily, once it had started, which frightened him a little, sitting there in the drunk's coat with the lace at his wrists; how easily it came, and how much of it was true. He talked about Corbin, who had always had everything. He talked about Kaldo, a ruin, a ruin when he came, and look at it now, and did they know what he'd had to spend, his own money, his own father's gold, and had anybody in this kingdom ever once said well done?

And then, at the cheese, with his elbows on the table and his cup in his hand, he said the thing he had come to say.

"Your junction," he said. "On my road."

The table went still. Placit, at the head, did not move at all; his pale eyes rested on Emeric's face and stayed there.

"I paid at it," said Emeric. "In April. Two Silver and three. Did you know? My own paper. My own cart. On my own road." He laughed. It was the old laugh, the one from the capital, loose and too loud, and he heard it come out of his own mouth in the long panelled room and did not know, for a breath, whose it was. "I'm not complaining. Don't think I'm complaining. A charter's a charter. My father's grandfather signed it, or somebody's did." He waved the cup. "But a man does wonder, my lord. When he's spent seven years and all his father's gold making a barony that pays, and then he goes up a hill in the barony he's been given, and finds a trade on it that's paid very handsomely for two hundred years. And he doesn't have any of it." He leaned forward, confidentially, too far, and slopped the cup. "A man does wonder how another man might get a little of it. As a friend. A partner. A small one. A silent one." He sat back. "I'm very good at being silent. Ask anybody at court."

Nobody at the table said anything.

Then the chief clerk from the counting house laughed, softly, pleasantly, with his clean hands folded on the cloth; and Placit's son laughed too, a beat after; and Placit, at the head, smiled, a little, for the first time that evening, in his close white beard.

"Highness," he said. "We must certainly talk."

The ink went into the case on the sideboard at the end of the evening, four squat bottles with the tower on their shoulders, with a long fond speech from the Holder of Kaldo about his mill, and the black, the best black in Randillia, he'd made it himself, they'd never see a finer ink in their lives, they must use it for everything; and Placit thanked him for it gravely, and the chief clerk took one of the bottles out of the case and held it to the lamp and admired it, and the son's wife said nothing at all.

Ward was waiting at the foot of the stair with the horses, in the dark, under the creaking weathervane.

Emeric came down the steps carefully, as a man comes down steps who has had a great deal of wine, one hand on the rail, and got onto his horse at the second try, and rode down the hill toward the town at a walk, swaying a little in the saddle, past the chain and the shed and the post with its board of figures, until the stone house was out of sight behind the trees.

Then he sat up straight.

Ward rode beside him in the dark for a while without saying anything.

"Well?" said Emeric at last.

"The son believed you," said Ward. "And the clerk." He looked ahead down the dark road. "The old man smiled."

"I saw."

"He did not smile," said Ward, "until you asked for money."

They rode on. Below them on the hill, Wayford's lamps were going out one by one along the river, and the vat houses were dark, and somewhere a dog was barking at nothing; and in the long panelled room at the top of the stone house behind them, Emeric knew, the case of black ink stood on the sideboard where he had left it, four bottles, with their stoppers in, dry, silent, waiting for somebody to want to write a letter.

"Vennor on the twenty-fourth," said Ward.

"Vennor on the twenty-fourth."

He took the lace cuff of the drunk's coat between his finger and thumb, as he rode, and felt it, the old stiff gold thread, and did not let go of it until they reached the square.



Vennor was a small man in a small house by the weir, with the river loud under his windows and a wife who talked enough for both of them, and he was afraid.

Emeric saw it in the first minute, on the step, in the way Vennor's hand came out to be shaken and went back again too quickly; and he saw it all through the soup, in the way Vennor laughed at things a beat after his wife did, and looked at the door whenever a servant came through it. He had very small handwriting, Ferra had said, reading his bundle in the boxes. The smallest of the five. A man who writes that small, she had said, is a man who does not want to take up room on the page.

So Emeric took up all the room at the table. He was louder than he had been at Placit's, and drank a little more, slowly, and talked about his father until Vennor's wife ran out of things to say about her own; and somewhere about the second course he saw Vennor stop looking at the door. The fear went out of the little man's shoulders as it went out of a horse's when it has decided the thing in the hedge is only a sack. He laughed at the right moment. He refilled the prince's cup himself.

At the end of the evening, when the case of bottles with the wheatsheaf on their shoulders had been carried in and admired, Vennor's wife wanted to know if the black was really as black as His Highness said; and before anybody could stop her she had sent for a pen and a slip of paper, and unstoppered a bottle, and dipped, and written on the slip in a round hand, for a trial, the date of the next dinner, which was to be at Hollin's.

The black went down shining on the paper in the lamplight. Vennor bent over it with his wife, and said it was very fine, very fine indeed, and the prince was too kind.

Emeric sat at the table with his cup in his hand and his sense open, and felt it. Faint, across the cloth, a yard from his hand: the low slow note of the black, pulsing, stroke by stroke, as the round letters went down; and then going quiet, letter by letter, as they dried. There was no knot on Vennor's chimney yet. There was nobody on the other end of the line. Nobody heard it in all the world but him.

"Very fine," said Vennor again, happily, and corked the bottle.

"I made it myself," said the Holder of Kaldo, a little too loudly, and held out his cup to be filled.
