Chapter 67 - Out by Everything

The dock quarter wanted to go home.

It said so at the pay trestle on the first Saturday in June, one family at a time, and it said so at the north well, and it said so at the door of the keep, where a delegation of net-menders stood in the gate yard with their hats in their hands and asked, very politely, when the prince meant to let them build. They had been living in other people's houses for the best part of a fortnight. The houses were sound and dry and had doors with numbers on Ferra's list, and the people in them were grateful, and wanted to go home.

Ferra priced it.

She walked the burned quarter with the carpenter and the mason for two days, lane by lane, and came back with a page. Timber: there was none in the barony that was not either growing or already in a roof, and the baron's woods would give poles and not beams. Thatch: the reed beds below the old works would cut in August and not before. Lime, stone, nails, labor. A hundred and some houses, small, as they had been. She put the figure at the bottom and drew a line under it and did not say anything about it, which was how he knew it was bad.

He went down to the gate yard and told the net-menders so. Not yet. Not before the reed was cut, not before there was timber. The oldest of them, a man with a face like a walnut and hands like the nets he mended, heard him out, and then said that their grandfathers had built the dock quarter the first time without any prince and without any timber but what came down the river, and they could do it again, and they did not need the barony's leave, only its carts. He was not wrong. Emeric gave them the carts. By the end of June there were six houses standing on the black ground below the bridge, made of salvaged posts and old sailcloth and whatever the river brought, with people living in them, and Ferra wrote them into her book under a heading of their own and did not price them.

The forty who had been kept on at the wharf were still kept on, because they had nowhere else to be kept, and Ward had them clearing the burned lanes with carts, down to the ground, so that whatever was built next would be built on something. The barony paid them by the day. The barony paid the garrison, and the households, and the school's bread, though the school was shut, and Imani's ward, which was full, and the coast men's last days, and the burials. It paid Bram for the iron in the breastplates. It paid the potter for the jars, and the cooper's journeyman for the casks that were standing in his shop full of spirit with nobody to sell them to.

Nothing came in.



The spirit was the only thing Kaldo made that anybody wanted, and there was nobody to want it.

Grayrock's ore barges had not come down the river since the year turned and were not coming now, and if they came they would not be buying. Milford's carter had not come back since the levy went home. The salt boatman came up at the end of June with the month's salt and took four casks down to the coast for what the coast would pay, which was not much, because the coast had heard about the war, and nobody there wanted casks from a town that might not be standing in the spring, and three of his four casks came back up again at the end of July because nobody had bought them.

Emeric tried selling next year.

It was an old idea, a forward contract, and a good one, in a world that had banks and courts that would enforce a promise across three baronies. He drew up a paper with Ferra at her table: next year's spirit, and next year's soap, at a fifth under this year's price, to any buyer who would pay half of it now. Hale wrote it out fair, eight times, with the seal. It went out by every road Kaldo had. Down the river to the coast by the salt boatman. West to Milford by a rider. North by the road to Wayford and the market towns on the south road, where Saban's buyers were. The riders came back with nothing in their saddlebags.

Nobody said no. That was the worst of it. Nobody in any of the towns had said the price was wrong, or the spirit was bad, or the soap. They had read the paper and handed it back, and said that it was a handsome offer and that they would think about it, and some of them had given the rider a cup of something, and none of them had put a name to it. A plague town, a month out of a war, with a prince who had been sent south in disgrace, offering to be paid now for spirit it would make next year, if it was still there next year to make it.

One answer came back. It came in August's first week, on a carter's flat wagon, in the hand of the Milford carter, who had been told to give it to nobody but the prince and who had been looking forward to the cup of spirit.

It said: Milford takes twenty casks at next year's price. Half paid. It is not what you need.

It was signed P.

The half was in a bag under the carter's seat. Ferra counted it at her table and wrote it into her book on a line of its own, and then sat looking at the line.

"It's not what we need," she said.

"No."

"He's right." She did not look up. "It's about what we need for a week."



She had a date. She had had it since June, he thought, and had not shown him, because she had been waiting for the contracts to change it, and they had not.

She showed it to him at her table on the evening the carter came: the barony's whole year on two facing pages, coming in and going out, and at the foot of the right-hand page, under the line, a date. The last Saturday in August. After that the trestle at the exchange door would open on a Saturday morning, and the households would stand in their line with their children, and there would be nothing in the bag.

Under the date she had written the ways out. She had done it in the same small hand, and numbered them, because that was what you did with a sum that would not come out: you wrote down every way of making it smaller and priced each one.

One. The garrison on half pay.

Two. The forty from the wharf let go.

Three. The households' pay cut by half, or stopped, while the school was shut.

Four. The seed grain sold to Milford, at Milford's price.

Five. All of them.

Each had its figure beside it, and the new date it would buy. The last line bought until the middle of October.

He read it twice. Then he put his finger on the third line.

"No," he said.

"I know you'll say no. I've written it down because it's there." She put her pen down. "It's the biggest line on the page. If you won't touch it, the others don't reach."

"Then the others don't reach."

"Then nothing reaches." She said it quite flatly. "Then it's the last Saturday in August, and the bag's empty, and I'm the one sitting behind the trestle."



Hollis came to the trestle on the next Saturday, as he came every Saturday, for his grandchildren's pay.

He took it from Ferra's hand and counted it, slowly, coin by coin, standing at the trestle with the line waiting behind him, and put it in his pocket. Then he did not go. He stood to one side, at the end of the trestle, with his hat in his hands, and waited until Emeric came out of the exchange door.

"They're saying in the west side you can't pay it past August," Hollis said.

Emeric did not ask who was saying it. In Kaldo it would have been everybody, and nobody, by now.

"They're saying wrong," he said.

Hollis looked at him with his small, still face. Then he looked at Ferra, behind the trestle, who had not looked up from her book and was not writing anything in it.

"I said I'd hold you to it," Hollis said.

"I know."

"I'm not here to hold you to it. I know you'll pay it." He turned his hat round once in his hands. "I came to see your face when you said it. That's all."

He put his hat on and went off up the lane. Emeric did not know what his face had looked like. He suspected Hollis did.

Ward came up to the line room that evening, where Emeric was not doing anything, and stood in the doorway.

"The garrison will take half," he said. "Until the spring."

"Who told you?"

"Hollis," Ward said. "And the west side." He did not come in. "I asked them. Nobody said no."

"I'm saying no."

"Then I will tell them so." Ward turned to go, and stopped. "They asked me first," he said. "Before I asked them."

He went out.



He went down to her table that night because he had seen her hand on the book at the trestle, not writing, and he had never seen it do that.

Bram was not there. He was at the powerhouse, on Harl's stool, as he was every night now. The stove was out, because it was August. The lamp was lit on the table, and the book was open under it, at the two facing pages and the date, and Ferra was sitting in front of it with her hands flat on the table on either side of it.

She did not look up when he came in.

"I've been through it four times," she said. "Since supper. Every line. I thought I'd made a mistake somewhere, because I'd never had a page come out like this, and I couldn't find it, and then I thought that was the mistake, thinking there had to be one." Her voice was perfectly level. Her hands on the table were not quite still. "Every line on the page is right. And the page is out. By everything."

He sat down across from her, in the chair that had been mended.

She looked at the page. Her face was white, and the freckles stood out across it, and she was holding her mouth in a way he had seen once before, on the wall, in the white light, the night the lamps came on, and had not known then what it meant either.

"I don't know what to do," Ferra said.

He had never heard her say it. He had heard her say she did not know a thing, often, because she would not say more than the page said, and she said so to his face as readily as to anyone's. He had never heard her say she did not know what to do. It went into him like the cold off the river.

He did not tell her it would be all right. He did not know that it would, and she would have known he did not know, and it would have been the first lie he had ever told her.

"Show me," he said. "From the top."

She looked at him.

"From the top," he said again. "Read me every line. I'll write them on another sheet, and you tell me if I've got them wrong."

So she did. She read him the barony, line by line, coming in and going out, every copper of it, in her plain level voice, and he wrote each one down on a clean sheet with his new-skinned hand, still stiff, in letters a child would have been ashamed of, and she watched him do it, and corrected him twice. It took most of an hour. Somewhere in the middle of it her hands stopped moving on the table. Somewhere in the middle of the going-out page she reached across and took the pen out of his hand, without saying anything, because his writing was too bad to read. She did it slowly, with her fingers along his, so that the pen would not drag where the skin was new, and went on writing the lines herself while she said them, and he let her.

When they got to the bottom it came out the same. Of course it did. It was her book.

But it was a sum now. It was not a wall. It was a number at the bottom of a page with a line under it, and a date, and the number was a debt the barony did not have the money to pay, and that was a thing that happened to baronies and to men and to shops in every world he had ever lived in, and there were things that were done about it.

"Say it," Ferra said.

"We borrow."

She was quiet.

"Against what?" she said at last. "We've got nothing anybody will lend against. You saw the contracts. Nobody will lend to that."

"Then we find somebody who lends to things nobody else will."

"At a price."

"At a price." He looked at the page, at the third line, the households' pay, the biggest line. "I'd rather owe money," he said, "than owe that."

She looked at the third line too. Then she looked at him, across the lamp, until he began to wonder what she was seeing, and he did not look away.

"All right," she said, and turned the page, and ruled a new one.



Saban came in the second week of August, two months early, with one cart instead of four, because he had heard about the war in Wayford, and then about the fire, and had come to see whether there was anything left in Kaldo to trade with.

He sat at Ferra's table on the first evening with his hat on his knee, and drank the cup of spirit she poured him, and listened to the whole of it without interrupting, the contracts and the date and the third line. When it was done he set the cup down and considered the wall above Ferra's head.

"There's one house that lends against a thing like this," he said. "Ducatis. In the capital, and on the coast. They lend against what a place will be, if they think it will be anything. Nobody else does. They price it."

"They'll own us," Ferra said.

"Maybe." Saban considered the wall again. "The owning is in the terms. Terms can be read." He looked at her. "You can read."

Ferra did not answer that. She looked at Emeric.

"Yes," Emeric said. "Write to them."

He said it as if he were ordering lime, and Saban, who had heard him order lime, gave him a look that said he had not been fooled, and drank the rest of his spirit, and said that he would carry the letter himself, as far as the coast house, because a letter from Kaldo to anybody had a way of not arriving, these days, and he would like to see this one arrive.
