Chapter 67 - Red-Handed

Ward noticed it on the eighth day and said so on the eighth evening, which meant he had spent a day making sure.

"There's a party three hours behind us and they've been three hours behind us since the fork."

"Coincidence."

"We stopped a full day at Wayford because of the axle. They stopped a full day somewhere too." Ward was checking a girth he had already checked. "Nobody keeps a three-hour gap over two hundred miles by accident. You keep a three-hour gap on purpose, and there's only one reason to want one, which is that three hours is close enough to reach and far enough to be somewhere else when it's found."

So they knew for the last three days of the road, and they did not talk about it very much, and Ferra kept the case on her knees and did not put it in the boot.

Sethe's reasoning was sound and it was out of date by two years, and there was nobody in the world whose job it was to tell him so.

He had worked out that the case against him was a prince's paper and a prince's mouth. That is what such a case had always been. A holder brings a grievance, a holder brings his own books, a holder stands in the room and says what he says, and if the holder is not there the grievance goes back into a drawer with everything else that has no one behind it. Remove the man and you remove the matter, and on the road a dead prince is bandits, and in the capital a dead prince is a scandal that stalls for a year and then quietly does not resume.

What he did not know, because he had never been told and had no way of finding out, was that a magistrate had spent two years holding a standard over the thing precisely so that it would not depend on the man who brought it. Dated against other people's records. Paired, with fates attested by strangers. Lodged with a court, by number, in a register, four months ago. The case had an author and it no longer needed one.

Killing Emeric in the third week of that summer would not have ended the matter. It would have added a second one, better documented than the first, with the beneficiary named on the front page.

Nobody had explained this to Sethe because there was nobody whose office it was to explain it, and because the men he paid to advise him were paid to advise him about the world as it had been for four hundred years.

They came in on the seventeenth day and took a lodging in the third ring, in a stone house off a yard, with a flagged passage running from the stair to the back door.

It happened in that passage at about the ninth hour, with the light gone.

Five men. Knives and two short clubs, no swords, which told Ward everything he needed before anybody moved: swords are for soldiers and knives in a passage are for men who expect it to take under a minute and want to be walking out of a yard at the end of it.

They came in the back door while two of them came down the stair, which was competent, and it was the last competent thing that happened to them.

Emeric had been going the other way with a lamp in his hand and he did three things in the order he had been doing them for seven years.

Water, pulled thin across a flame. Both lamps in the passage went out and the one in his own hand went out and the passage became a stone box with no light in it at all, and he had done that one so many times in a room nobody had ever seen that his hand had finished it before he had decided to.

Moving air, second, into the narrow part by the stair, which knocked the two men on the bottom steps off their feet and put one of them into the wall with his head, and which did not kill anybody, and which had never once killed anybody.

And stone, third, a slab of the passage floor raised a hand's breadth and dropped, under the three coming from the back door, in the dark, while they were running.

The whole of it took perhaps four seconds. On the bank above the lane, four years earlier, the same three had taken him most of a morning to deliver and had cost him everything he had. In a passage, against five men, at nine feet, he was finished before the first of them was on the floor, and the difference was not power and was not talent and was six more years of doing three things and never a fourth.

Then Ward came through the dark and did what Ward did.

He had been waiting four years for something to be simple, and this was simple. It was not a fight and there is no honest way to write it as one. Three of them were on the ground already. He killed two in the passage and one in the yard, and he broke the arm of the fourth in a way that was not necessary and was deliberate, and he took the fifth alive by putting him face down on the flags and kneeling on him until the man stopped trying, and the fifth was the youngest and had not got past the doorway.

Ferra came out onto the stair with a lamp she had lit herself and looked at the passage for a moment.

"Anyone ours?" she said.

"No."

"Then I'll send for the watch, and I'll want the names of every man in this house who saw it, before they talk to each other."

She had them written down inside eleven minutes, with the hour, in her own hand, and made each of them put a mark against his own line, and it was that sheet and not any of the bodies that did the work the following afternoon.

The chamber was the same room.

Emeric had been in it twice and it had not changed either time: the long table, the high windows, the chairs chosen by a man who did not want anybody comfortable. No gallery. Nowhere to put one.

Keller was five years older and looked it and did not stand.

Sethe came in eleven minutes before the hour, which was correct, with an advocate he had retained three months before he needed one, and he had slept, and Emeric noticed that and remembered noticing the same thing about a different baron in the same room and did not say anything about it.

He was greyer than the man on the field had been. He had the build of somebody who had been heavy and had stopped eating properly. He looked at Emeric once on the way to his chair, without any particular expression, the way you look at a piece of furniture that has been moved.

He answered the matter and he lied, and the lie was good.

The crossing's sorting warrant was held by a crown officer, not by Wayford and not by him. He had no authority over the man, had never given him an instruction, and could produce his own household roll to show that the fellow had never been in his employ.

The payments in his household book to that name were nine Gold a quarter for the upkeep of the ferry approach and the stabling of the post's relay horses, which was a charge Wayford had carried for forty years and which his father had carried before him, and the sum had not changed.

He had never seen a letter belonging to the barony of Kaldo. He had never asked anybody to look at one. He regretted the losses on the southern road, which he understood to be considerable, and observed that the southern road had been badly policed for a generation, and that his own correspondence had suffered from it, and that he would welcome the court's attention to the matter.

As to Milford. The region had banditry. He had lost stock himself in two of the last four years. He had no knowledge of the burning of any barn and had been at Wayford the entire week.

It took him about twenty minutes. It was fluent and it was internally consistent and it was the last useful thing he did.

Keller did not argue with any of it. He put four things on the table, one at a time, and let each of them sit there while the advocate read it.

The first was the crossing's own toll book.

It had been produced by the crossing under compulsion in the ordinary course of the matter, five months earlier, by the same clerk who had written to eleven waypoints and frightened two men into honesty. It was not a postal record. It was a revenue record, kept because the crossing levied on weight, and it recorded every bag that came onto that ground and every bag that went off it, with the weight at each end, because the levy was assessed on both.

On eleven dates across three years a bag had come onto the crossing at one weight and gone off it lighter, by between two and nine ounces, with no entry anywhere accounting for the difference. On thirty-one other dates there was no discrepancy at all.

The eleven dates were the eleven dates.

"That is a clerk's arithmetic," the advocate said. "Scales differ. A bag gets wet."

"A bag that gets wet gets heavier," Keller said, and moved on.

The second was the crown post's lodgment register, and it was not interesting, and that was the point of it.

Fifty-one entries in a year, in the hand of a clerk at a southern gate, each one stamped and dated, each one giving a weight and a seal device, each one matching a receipt in the Kaldo bundle. The clerk had been asked, by letter, whether he had ever met or corresponded with anybody from the barony of Kaldo about anything, and had replied that he had not, and had added, apparently to be helpful, that he issued about four hundred such receipts a year and that nobody had ever queried one before.

Keller read that line out. It was the only moment all day when he appeared to be enjoying himself, and it was very brief.

The third was a single letter.

It had been found in the capital, in the southern correspondence office, by the clerk with the folder, in the course of indexing four bags of material that had arrived all at once. It was dated eighteen months earlier, inside the window. It had arrived at the time, alone, and had been filed and never answered, because nothing from that desk was ever answered.

Kaldo's records said the item had been sent and never acknowledged. Kaldo's records did not say it had never arrived, because Kaldo had no way of knowing that any of them had.

It was an unremarkable report about a toll and a school. Somebody had opened it, read it, judged it harmless, and sent it on, and had done a careful and professional job of closing it again.

Keller put it beside the bundle and then put beside that a small wooden box with four wax dies in it, each with a date written on the underside in Bram's hand.

Every one of the fifty-one Kaldo seals bore a nick in the shoulder of the device, a degree or so off the vertical, of the kind a die-cutter leaves when his hand slips finishing a shoulder.

The seal on the letter that had arrived did not.

It was a good seal. It was, in every other respect, an extremely good seal. The device was right, the depth was right, the wax was the right wax. A man had cut a new die from an impression and had cut it well, and in cutting it well he had removed a flaw, because no craftsman alive reproduces another man's slipped shoulder.

"The irregularity was cut deliberately," Keller said, "by a smith in Kaldo, eighteen months before this letter was opened, for this purpose. I have his statement and I have the dies and I have the dates on them. I mention the deliberateness because it will be put to me that it is a coincidence, and I would rather dispose of that now."

The advocate did not say anything.

The fourth thing was the one nobody in Kaldo had asked for.

The court had written to House Ducatis, because a court that wants to know what a thing cost writes to the people who count. The house had answered in nineteen days, which Keller's clerk described in the margin as prompt, and the answer was a certified extract from the book of the house's resident factor at Kaldo, countersigned by a partner in the capital office, lodged in the ordinary form by the factor herself as any factor would lodge it.

It ran three years. It was a short column at the back of a working book, under a line about tolls, with dates beside every figure. Carriage paid twice for items sent once. The doubled postage heading, entered as an operating cost against no asset. The tolls at the crossing, itemised, quarter by quarter. Down the side, in a hand nobody in the room had seen before, a running total.

It was the price of the thing, kept in date order for three years by a creditor's agent with no interest whatsoever in the barony of Kaldo winning anything, who had begun the column before there was a case, for her own purposes, and who had never once mentioned it to the borrower.

Ferra looked at it for rather a long time.

Keller had the door opened at the middle of the morning and Paxton came in.

Emeric had not known he would be there. Nobody from Kaldo had known. He had been summoned by the court in the ordinary way six weeks earlier and had told no one, because the summons had not instructed him to tell anybody and he was not a man who volunteered.

He came in with his hat in his hand, in a good coat that had been mended, and sat down where he was told to sit, and Sethe watched him cross the room with the expression of a man watching something arrive that he has no category for.

He gave the court four things and he gave them the way he wrote letters, which was as an inventory.

First, the four nights, with the head count, the barn's load and the value of the wool.

Second, that he had entered into commercial agreements with Kaldo five years earlier and had stood with Kaldo since, and that he had told nobody in the world about the second part except the holder of Kaldo and three of his people. Not his own steward. Not his own hall. Nobody in Milford. He had kept it that way on purpose and he stated the purpose plainly, which was that a man who is believed to be on your side is worth more than a man who is known to be on somebody else's.

Third, a bundle of letters in Sethe's own hand, going back four years, which the court had asked him to bring and which he had brought. In every one of them, down to and including one written six weeks before the four nights, Sethe wrote to Paxton as an ally, on the assumption that Milford was Wayford's, in the ordinary confiding tone of a man writing to somebody on his own side.

And fourth, that after the four nights the letters stopped, and there had not been one since.

Keller asked him one question.

"Between the last of those letters and the first of those four nights, did you say or write anything to anybody, in any barony, that would have told the holder of Wayford that Milford stood with Kaldo?"

"No."

"Did anyone in your household?"

"I'd have to say I can't swear to every man I employ. I can say nobody in my household knew it. You can't carry what you haven't got."

Keller turned to the bundle and read out eleven lines from the second page of a report written in November by the holder of Kaldo, addressed to the crown, lodged at a southern gate on the fifteenth, never delivered, stating that Milford should be understood as aligned with Kaldo and not with Wayford.

Then he sat back and did not say anything else about it, because there was nothing else to say about it, and the room had four days in it.

The three men came in after the midday break and they were not impressive and they were not supposed to be.

Saban's road had found them eleven months earlier, sideways, through a woman who kept an inn on the western circuit and a carter who owed him money, and Saban had done nothing with them except establish where they drank. The court had done the rest four months ago, quietly, through the coastal magistrate, and had offered each of them separately the version of the arithmetic that involved living.

They were labourers. Two of them were brothers. They had been paid four gold between the three of them to burn a barn on a night, by a man they could describe and could not name, who had met them twice at an inn and who had paid half in advance in a yard.

They did not know whose barn it was. They had not asked. One of them said, when Keller put it to him, that he had assumed it was an insurance matter, which was the only thing said in that room all day that made anybody laugh, and the laugh was one short unpleasant sound from somebody at the back.

What they gave the court was the date, the inn, the yard, the description, and the coins, two of which the younger brother had kept because they were struck from a mint he had not seen before and he had thought they were worth keeping.

The man they described was in Sethe's household roll, which Sethe had produced himself that morning to prove that the crossing warrant-holder was not on it.

Emeric was asked six questions all day.

Whether he had written the November report. Whether he had written it himself. Whether the fold sequence had been altered at any point in the year. Whether he had ever spoken to the clerk at the southern gate. Whether he had instructed anybody to approach the three men. Whether he had been in the passage at the ninth hour on the previous evening.

He answered all six in under a sentence each and was not asked to elaborate on any of them.

Ferra answered perhaps forty. The index was hers, the log was hers, the fold sequence was hers, the four years of dispatch dates were in her hand, and she answered the way she did everything, which was flatly and without any interest in whether the answer helped.

Somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, between the dies and the coins, Emeric worked out that he was the least necessary person in the room.

The toll book was the crossing's. The lodgment register was the crown post's. The seal was a smith's and a forger's between them. The cost column was a creditor's. The dates were a bookkeeper's. The four days were a cattleman's, and the four gold were three frightened men's, and every one of those sources had produced what it produced for its own reasons, in its own books, without reference to him, and half of them had never heard of him when they wrote it down.

Remove him from the room and the afternoon proceeds exactly as it is proceeding.

That was the thing. That was the entire thing he had been building since the bucket line and the burial rows and the census, which was systems that did not depend on the man who had made them, and here it was, finished, working, in front of a court, and he sat in a chair at the side of a room with nothing in his hands and found that it felt considerably worse than he had expected it to.

He did not say that to anybody. He was fairly sure Ferra knew, because Ferra glanced at him once during the coins and then did not do it again.

Keller ruled at the end of the afternoon and it took him under three minutes and he read it the way a man reads a list back to confirm he has it right.

Sufficient evidence of the systematic interception and destruction of crown post, over three years, at a crossing held under the respondent's authority and maintained out of the respondent's household. Sufficient evidence of the use of matter so obtained, specifically the moving of men and fire against the barony of Milford in consequence of a document the respondent had no right to read. Theft from the crown's own revenues, noted and subordinate.

Then he set the sheet down and took up a second one, which had been written that afternoon.

"There is a further matter, opened today, and I have taken it today because the witnesses are in this building and one of the men is in a cell across the street with a broken arm and has been talking since the sixth hour."

He read it.

Sufficient evidence of treasonous conspiracy, specifically the financing of an attempt upon the life of a prince of the blood.

Death, by crown order.

He said the last part twice and the second time he said it to Emeric, in the same room, in the same order, in the same words he had used in that room five years earlier to another baron about the same prince, and not one person present remarked on it, and the narration will not either.

Sethe did not make a speech.

Emeric had half expected one, because the last man had made one, and the last man's had been the worst forty seconds of his life and had stayed with him for five years.

Sethe sat for a moment after the ruling with his hands on the table. Then he asked the court whether his household would be held in the matter, or only himself, and whether his steward's production of the roll that morning would be taken as complicity, and whether there was a form in which his daughter's settlement, which had been made four years earlier and lodged, could be distinguished from the estate.

Keller answered all three. The household, no, except the named man. The steward, no. The settlement was a separate question for a separate office and he would direct that it be asked.

Sethe said thank you, and sat down.

He was not defiant and he was not broken and he did not look at Emeric at any point, and what he had the appearance of, sitting there, was a man who had been comprehensively out-administered by people who kept better books than his and who had understood that some hours ago and had been waiting since then for the procedure to catch up with the fact.

They came out into the corridor a little after six, the three of them, and stood there while the building emptied.

Emeric was not holding anything. Ferra had the case. Ward had his hat. There was nothing to sign, nothing to carry, nothing to take home, and nobody had asked them to come back in the morning.

Ward said that Paxton had gone out the other door twenty minutes ago and had not waited, and Emeric said he would write to him, and Ward said that Paxton would hate that, and Emeric said he would write to him anyway.

Ferra said that she wanted the index back before they left the city, because it had gone into the court's hands as an exhibit and an exhibit is a thing that goes into a room and does not come out, and she had four years of work in it and no copy of the last eleven months.

They talked about that, in a corridor, for some minutes, because it was a real problem and because it was the only problem any of them had.

Nobody said anything about the three baronies.

Grayrock had a widow holding it for a daughter under a crown licence. Milford had a man who had just spent in one morning the only thing he owned that his enemy had not priced. Wayford, as of three minutes past five that afternoon, had a holder under sentence of death by crown order and a settlement that was going to a separate office.

Somewhere above them in that building, men whose names Emeric did not know were going to have to decide what happened to all three, and would begin thinking about it within the week, and would want it disposed of cheaply.

Nobody in that corridor had thought about it for one second, then or on the road home.
