Chapter 167 - Early

The beds were behind the old barn beyond the foundry, where they had been for two years, under a thatch on posts with the sides open to the wind, and in the second week of the thaw Emeric went out to them before breakfast with a spade and a clay dish and did not tell anybody why.

They did not look like much. They had never looked like much. Long low heaps of earth and stable muck and ash and lime rubble and the sweepings of every privy on the island, bought at a Copper a basket off the handcart for two years, laid in rows under the thatch and turned every month by two old men from the lower water who were paid to turn them and did not ask what they were for. The boys on the island called them the dung houses. Ferra's book called them the beds, behind the old barn, and beside that, in her hand from two years ago, Three years.

He went down the second row to the end that had been laid first, and knelt, and scraped at the face of the heap where it had dried in the wind.

It was there. A fine white bloom on the dark earth, like frost that had forgotten to melt, in the cracks and on the edges where the air got in. He scraped a little of it into the dish with the edge of the spade, and took it to the foundry, and put a pinch of it on a coal in the smallest hearth, and it hissed and spat and went up in a small yellow flare that made the boy at the bellows jump.

He did it twice more, from two other rows, and got the same.

Then he went and found Ferra.

She came out with him after breakfast, with Hobb and her book, and walked the rows in her pattens with her skirts held up out of the muck, and looked at the white bloom where he showed it to her, and did not touch it.

"You said three years," she said.

"You said three years. I said two or three."

"You said three to the table. I remember it. I wrote it while you were saying it." She had the book open on her arm. "Two years and a season."

"To the season," he said. "Not to the day. There's more to do before it's anything you can use. But it's there, and it's there in every row."

She looked at him, and then at the heap.

"Why?"

So he told her. He had put off telling her for two years, because it had not mattered until it worked, and he told her now standing in the thatch's shade with the old men watching from the end of the row. The white stuff did not come out of the dung by itself. Something had to make it, something too small to see, that lived in the earth and ate what the dung gave off and left the white stuff behind it. Left to themselves, the beds would have had to wait for it to find them, and grow, and that was the three years. He had not left them to themselves. When they were laid he had sent to Grayrock for two carts of the white crust off the old spoil heaps by the sealed shaft, which had lain there in the rain for a hundred years going white, and he had had it dug into every row. Whatever made the white stuff at Grayrock had come with it. It had not needed to find the beds. It had been in them from the first day.

"You knew that when I wrote three," Ferra said.

"I thought it. I didn't know it."

"You let me write three."

"Yes."

She looked at the page for a moment. Then she held out her hand to Hobb for the pen, and he gave it to her, and she wrote under Three years, in the same small hand, Yielded at two, to the season, on the Grayrock crust. H.R.H. let this book say three. And the date. And she gave the pen back to Hobb and closed the book.

"I would rather it said two," she said. "Next time."

"Next time I'll let it say two."

"Next time you'll tell me what you think and not what you know," she said, "and I'll decide which to write." She gathered her skirts. "How much?"

He told her that too, as near as he could. What the rows would give at the first working, and what they would give by the autumn, and what they would give every year after that if the handcart kept coming and the old men kept turning. What happened between the white bloom and the black grain in a cask he did not describe to her and she did not ask; it happened in a shed beyond the beds with a copper and a row of tubs, and two men who had been Colm's glasshouse hands worked it, and Ferra paid for it under a line in the book that said the works beyond the beds. The chlorate jar was in the smoke house under the lake wall with Ward's lock on it, a mile off. It stayed there.

"Enough for what?" she said.

"For a range. For the summer." He thought about it. "And after that, more than a range."

She did not write that down.

The barrels came off the boring mill.

Bram had built the mill in the winter to Emeric's drawing, in a lean-to on the mill race below the converter, off the same wheel that drove the beater and the converter's blowing engine by turns. It was an ugly thing. A long oak bed, iron-strapped, with a carriage that ran along it on a screw, and a spindle at one end turned by the wheel through Jorrin's gears, and a bar coming off the spindle with a cutter at its tip, hard steel, ground to a shape Jorrin had filed eleven times before Emeric let it go. A barrel was forged round a mandrel at the anvil, from a strip of the converter's steel wrapped and welded, and came to the mill rough and crooked inside, and was clamped to the carriage, and the carriage was screwed along the bed slowly into the turning bar, and when it came off the other end it was a tube with a hole down the middle that you could look through at the sky and see a clean round circle.

Emeric had not touched it. That was the point of it.

The first lathe in Kaldo had come out of the Mana Forge in his second year, in a night, bed and spindle and all, true to a hair, because there had been no other way to get a true thing into a valley that had never had one. Every machine since then had been made on that lathe, or on a machine that lathe had made, or on a machine made by one of those. Bram called it the ladder. You climbed it one rung at a time and every rung was a little less magic than the one below, until at the top there were machines that Jorrin could build out of iron and oak with his own hands from nothing but the one under it and a drawing, and that nobody would ever need a prince for again.

The boring mill was a long way up the ladder. Jorrin had built half of it himself.

He watched the fourth barrel come off the bed with Jorrin and Bram, and Jorrin held it up to the light from the door, and looked down it, and handed it to Bram, who looked down it, and handed it to Emeric.

It was straight. It was not perfect. There was a faint waver in the circle at the far end where the bar had chattered on a hard spot in the weld. It would throw a ball well enough at a hundred paces and anywhere at all at three hundred. There was nothing spun into it. The inside of it was smooth as a kettle.

"You could make these in Wayford," Bram said. "If Wayford had the steel."

"You could make these anywhere they had a smith and a river."

"That's what I said."

"I know." Emeric gave the barrel back to Jorrin. "That's why."

The rifle was in the armoury in the keep, in its oilcloth, in a locked case, with the horn flask that had been empty since the spring before last beside it. One barrel, one night. Ferra's line was still in the book above everything that had come after it, and he had read it again the week before when the first barrel came off the mill, to be sure of it. That barrel had taken everything he had for a night and nobody on the earth could make another, and it had put a ball in a man's leg a hand below where he had aimed. These were nothing. Anybody who got hold of one of these and a boring mill could make a thousand of them, and would, and there was nothing in them that anybody would need to steal from him to do it.

He had spent twelve years trying to build things other people could build without him. It was strange to find that the one he minded least was this one.

The charges were Bram's.

Emeric had said one sentence about it at the table in the winter, which was that the ball had gone low in the Staple's yard because the charge had not been the charge before it, and that no man was going to stand in a line and fire a thing whose charge he had to trust to the hand that poured it. Dray had a limp now, in the north, if he had anything. Emeric had not stopped thinking about the leg.

So there was a measure. Bram had made it on his standard, the same standard the rail lengths and the stone weights and the surveyors' rods came off: a little brass cup on a handle that held, struck level, one charge, and a set of weights, and a balance under glass in the powder house beyond the beds, where a girl from the finishing room weighed every tenth cup and wrote the weight on a slate. The powder went into paper tubes rolled on a wooden former and tied off at one end, with the ball rammed in on top and the tube twisted shut over it. You bit the twist off, and poured a pinch in the pan, and the rest down the barrel, and the ball and the paper after it for wadding, and rammed it home. Two old women on the island rolled the tubes on the long table in the paper works' back room, and did not ask either.

The paper was the paper works' third sort, the straw stock, the cheap yellow sheet that nobody wrote on if they could help it. Hobb had brought him the order to sign. It came out of the same week's making as the ink works' wrapping and the finishing room's pen papers, off the same vats, and Ferra had put it in the same column, and he had looked at the column for a while before he signed it.

Armand had opened his room on the ground in the last week of the winter.

He had not told anybody but Ferra, and Ferra had told nobody, and Emeric had found out the way he found out most things about Armand now, which was when Armand came into the pattern store in the first week of spring on a steady morning with a book under his arm and a stone in his hand and sat down at the writing bench without asking.

"I should like to try a cell," he said. "For the new hull."

Emeric looked at him.

"I can reach it now," Armand said. "Here. Not only in the band." He did not look pleased about it. "It took the winter. You may ask Ferra how many nights. I would rather you didn't."

"How?"

"The same way the smith did it, and the physician. I stopped trying to picture a room and built something I knew." He put the stone down on the bench. It was a plain lift blank for hull two's second rail path, one of forty Emeric had cut in the winter. "I built the tower study. Not the room. The study. The books in the order I had them when I was forty. It took me a month to get the third shelf right. When the third shelf was right, the door was there."

Emeric sat down across from him.

Nobody had written a cell but him for seven years. It had been the thing about the ship that frightened Ferra most, and she had said so at the table more than once: that everything that held her up was written by one man, and if that man died there would be a ship that could not be repaired. Bram had written the first cell that was not Emeric's in the autumn, a plain lift cell for hull two's keel rack, after he opened his room out of the boiler. It had taken him four days and he had not let anybody watch. It worked.

Armand wrote his in one morning.

Emeric watched him do it, because Armand asked him to. He watched the old man's hand lie flat on the stone and not shake, and his eyes close, and his face go still and grey and intent the way it went when he was reading someone in the east room. It took most of an hour. At the end of it Armand took his hand off and sat back and breathed as if he had carried something up a stair.

Emeric put his own hand on the stone.

It was a cell. It was not Emeric's cell. It was laid out the way a mage of the Circle would lay out a working, with the structure first and every part of it named before it was used, like a lecture with the conclusion in the first sentence; where Emeric would have left a gap to be filled in by use, Armand had filled it in advance, carefully, and signed it. It was heavier than it needed to be. It would hold a rail of lift stones true under load for as long as anybody wanted. There was nothing wrong in it at all.

"Well?" Armand said.

"It's good."

"Don't be kind to me, I'm too old."

"It's good," Emeric said. "It's slow. It's better built than mine."

Armand looked at the stone on the bench for a long moment. There was no pleasure in his face. Emeric had seen a great many expressions on it over twelve years and had learned to read most of them, and he had seen this one only once before, the day before Wat's burial, when Armand had told him that his study had come out the way he had hoped it would not.

"The third," Armand said.

"Yes."

"I have spent my life," Armand said, "telling boys that the Circle's way was the only way this could be done. That it had been proved. That I had proved it. Forty-one of them are on your flat with shovels, because I was wrong about that." He put his hand on the stone again, lightly, the way one touches a thing one has made and does not like. "Put it in the rail. If it fails, I want to know."

"It won't fail."

"Then I should like to know that too," Armand said, and took his book and went out, and Emeric heard his stick on the stair all the way down.

The range was beyond the beds, on the rough ground between the barn and the old quarry bank, a long strip of grazing the barony had bought off a lake farm in the winter for more than it was worth. Bram's men had built a butt of turf and sand at the far end, taller than a man, and set posts in front of it at fifty paces and a hundred and two hundred, and Hobb had written the range in the book and Ferra had crossed it out and written the lower field, because she said there was no reason the whole island had to know what it was from the page.

Emeric gave it to Enno on the first morning.

He had thought about it all winter, and had not thought about anybody else. Enno was forty-some now, and grey in the beard, and had been the worst man in the garrison once, a long time ago, drunk on duty and in the lock-up twice in one season, and Ward had nearly turned him out. Then there had been the war, and Renner on the road with his hand raised, and Enno on the gate tower with the air rifle across the parapet and one shot in it, and after that Enno had never been drunk again. Nobody had ever told him to stop. He had just stopped. He was the only man in Kaldo who had killed another man at a distance with a thing he aimed, and he had never talked about it, and he was the only man in Kaldo whom Emeric had ever seen watch a low shot and know exactly what it was.

He had asked to fire the rifle once. Emeric had said no.

"I want twenty men," Emeric said to him at the butt, "who can do this in their sleep by midsummer. Load, fire, load, fire. Not aim. Not yet. Load and fire the same way every time until their hands do it without them."

Enno looked at the barrels on the trestle. Twenty of them, stocked and locked, with Bram's stamp on every lock plate and a number burned into every stock. He picked one up and turned it and looked down it the way Jorrin had, at the sky.

"No twist in it," he said.

"No."

"It'll throw wide past a hundred."

"It'll throw wide past fifty," Emeric said. "At fifty, twenty of them at once won't."

Enno put the barrel down and looked at him.

"I was going to say something," Enno said. "At the board. In the yard, that time. You saw me."

"I saw you."

"I was going to say, you won't know that thing for a year." He looked at the trestle. "A man doesn't know a weapon until he's fired it a thousand times. The air rifle, I had it two years before I'd trust it past the wall. You fired yours three times and then you took it to Wayford. I was going to say, give it to somebody who'll shoot it every day." He shrugged, a small movement. "Then I thought, it's not mine to say. And you'd already written it down."

Charge, not hand. Four left.

"This time I'm giving it to somebody who'll shoot it every day," Emeric said.

"I heard." Enno picked up the barrel again. "Twenty."

"Twenty to start."

"And after?"

"After," Emeric said, "we'll see what the beds give."

He drilled them that week and the week after, on the lower field, in the mornings, and Emeric stood at the end of the line for three mornings and then stopped going, because he could see that Enno did not want him there and that Enno was right.

They were not soldiers. Enno had asked for men who had never held a sword, and Ward had given him men off the rail works and the camp and the island, and two from the fire company's first watch, who had asked. A carter. A lime-burner. Two brothers from the eel traps. A clerk from the Grayrock tally house who was short-sighted and could not see the butt at two hundred paces and could load faster than any of them. Enno did not teach them to aim. He taught them to stand. Where to put their feet. How to hold the piece so that it came up to the same place against the same shoulder every time. How to bite the tube, and pour, and prime, and ram, and return the rod, and come up, in the same order, at the same speed, with his voice counting it, and then with his voice not counting it, and then with his voice counting it faster.

They did it without powder for four days. On the fifth day they did it with powder and no ball. On the sixth, with ball.

Emeric went down on the sixth, because Ward asked him to.

He stood behind the line with Ward and Osric and Declan, and Imani, who had come because Ward had asked her to come and had brought her bag and one of her students and a jar of goose grease, and Ferra, who had come because she wanted to see what the book was paying for. The twenty stood in a line at fifty paces from the butt with their feet where Enno had put them, and Enno said make ready in the flat voice he used for all of it, and twenty hands went to twenty pouches, and the smell of the torn paper and the powder came back on the wind.

They fired together. Not quite together. A ragged roar, one long noise with a few short ones at the end of it, and a bank of white smoke that rolled forward off the line and hung there, and through it the butt at fifty paces with the turf jumping in a dozen places and the post in front of it split down one side.

Ward did not flinch.

Emeric was watching for it and it did not come. Ward stood with his hands behind him and his face grey and still and watched the smoke go, and said nothing, and when Enno's voice came again, load, he watched the twenty hands go to the pouches again, and his face did not change at all.

They fired four times. The fourth time the man at the end of the line, a big fair lad off the rail works called Willet, did not fire. His lock snapped and the pan flashed and nothing happened.

Enno had told them what to do if that happened. Hold the piece up, muzzle to the butt, count ten, then lower it. Willet had been told. Willet held it up and counted, and it was the longest ten of his life, and on seven he did what everybody does, which is tip the piece and look at the pan.

It went off at nine. Not the barrel; the charge in the barrel had caught at last from the smoulder in the touch-hole and the ball went into the ground a yard in front of the line with a sound like a dropped plank, and a jet of flame came out of the touch-hole sideways across the back of Willet's left hand where he had it cupped round the lock.

He dropped the piece. He did not yell. He stood and looked at his hand.

Imani was there before anybody else moved. She had the hand in a bucket of cold water from the butt's trough before Willet had finished looking at it, and the student had the jar open, and she held the hand in the water and looked at it and said, without looking round, "Who told him to cup it?"

"Nobody," Enno said.

"Then who told him not to?"

Enno did not answer.

"It's the back of the hand," Imani said, to Willet, in a quite different voice. "It'll blister. You'll keep it. Leave it in the water till I say." Then, in the first voice, to the line, to Enno and Ward and Emeric and all of them, "Show me what they were told."

Enno showed her. The ten count. Muzzle to the butt. Hands clear of the lock.

"Hands clear of the lock," Imani said. "Is that written?"

"It's said."

"Said to twenty men who have never held one. Said once." She took Willet's hand out of the water and looked at it and put it back. "Write it. Everything that can burn them, write it. And put it where they can read it, and read it to the ones who can't." She looked at Emeric for the first time. "And somebody write where these are carried. Because I will not take a ball out of a child in a kitchen because a man came home from this field with one of these loaded."

"Nobody takes them home," Enno said. "They're racked here."

"Tonight they're racked here," Imani said. "What about when there are two hundred?"

Nobody said anything.

Ferra wrote it that afternoon, at the long-room table, with Enno and Ward and Imani at it and Emeric at the end. It was short. She read it out when she had finished it.

No piece to be carried charged within any wall, roof or gate of the barony, or on any road of it, save on the lower field and by order in the field. A charged piece is a piece with powder in it, whether or not it has ball. Every piece to be drawn or fired off before it leaves the lower field, and the drawing or firing seen by the man in charge of the field and marked on the slate. A man who carries a charged piece where this forbids it is dismissed.

"Dismissed," Ward said.

"Bram's rule," Ferra said. "It's the same shape as the watcher's. It works."

Imani read it over Ferra's shoulder and said, "And the hands," and Ferra added a line under it for the lock and the ten count and the hands, and Imani read that too, and went out without saying anything else, and Emeric thought she had not looked at him in that room since the autumn except when she had to, and that today had been a day when she had to.

It went up on the barn door on the lower field the next morning in print. Nevin had set it the night before and pulled forty. Willet read it standing in front of the door with his hand in a linen bag full of grease, and Enno read it to the ones who could not.

Osric found him on the lower field at dusk, on the eighth day.

Emeric had gone down alone, after the others had gone in, to look at the butt. He did not know quite why. He stood in front of it with his hands in his coat and looked at the turf, which was chewed to the sand in a band across its middle about the height of a man's chest, and at the posts in front of it, which had been renewed twice in eight days. The line had fired eleven times that afternoon. Enno had them loading in the time it took to say a short prayer, and he said they would do it in half that by the summer.

He heard Osric on the grass before he saw him. Osric came and stood beside him and looked at the butt too.

They stood there for a while. The light was going over the quarry bank and the smoke from the afternoon still lay in the hollow by the beds, thin and grey and sour, drifting a little toward the lake on the last of the wind. Osric had been at the line all afternoon with Ward. He had said nothing all afternoon. He was a good knight and a fair man and the best of Ward's three at the ship's levers, and Emeric liked him more than he had ever said, and he had been very quiet.

"Highness," Osric said.

"Yes."

Osric looked at the butt for a while longer before he said it. When he did, he said it plainly, the way he said everything, the way he had once told Emeric that the ship's forward rack was half a turn out and he would not take her up until it was fixed.

"What is a knight for, Highness," he said, "when Enno's twenty can do that?"

Emeric did not answer.

He stood beside Osric and looked at the turf chewed down to the sand in a band at the height of a man's chest, and he thought about the chamber, which had put tracery to Osric's shoulder and made Ward what he was, and about a carter and a lime-burner and a short-sighted clerk who had never held a sword and had been standing where Osric was standing four hours ago, and he did not answer, and Osric did not ask again.

After a while Osric said, "Goodnight, Highness," and went back up the field toward the barn in the dusk, and Emeric stayed where he was, and the smoke from the afternoon drifted off the lower field and over the beds, and lay there in the dark on the white bloom in the rows.
