Chapter 167 - Dead Air

The turbine came out of the forge on the fourth of November in two pieces, a disc and a ring of blades, and Bram turned the disc over in the window light, twice, and then said it was the first thing the forge had made that frightened him.

It was not big. The disc was the size of a cart wheel's hub, of the converter's best steel, and the blades were a ring of small curved cups round its rim, sixty of them, each one shaped like the inside of a spoon and set at its own careful angle, every one the same as every other to the width of a hair. That was all. A wheel with cups on it. A boy with a knife could have whittled one out of wood in an afternoon, and it would have looked the same.

It would not have done the same. That was what frightened Bram.

Emeric had explained it at the bench, with a pencil, the week before. The engines Kaldo had were piston engines, every one: steam pushing a piston down a cylinder, and the piston pushing a rod, and the rod turning a crank, in, out, in, out, a heavy thing going backwards and forwards and having to be stopped and started again every stroke. The turbine did not go backwards and forwards. It went round. Steam came out of a nozzle, a jet of it, faster than anything Bram had ever seen move, and hit the cups on the wheel's rim, one after another, and the wheel went round, and round, and round, faster than any piston engine in the world could turn a shaft, smoothly, without a knock, as a mill wheel goes round in a stream; except that the stream was steam, and the wheel was small, and it went round so fast that its rim, Emeric said, would be moving faster than a crossbow bolt leaves the string.

"And if a blade comes off," said Bram.

"It goes through the casing. And the wall. And whoever's behind the wall."

Bram had looked at him. "Then why are we making it?"

"Because one day it'll make all the light in four baronies," said Emeric. "Not this one. This one's to learn on."



It went onto the blast furnace's blower at the works, because the blower was the one machine in Kaldo that wanted to turn fast and steady all day and did not care if it stopped for an afternoon. Mark 3 steam, off the new steel boiler, the one with Tess's tested gauge on it, into a nozzle Bram had turned and bored himself, onto the forge's disc in a casing of iron plate an inch thick that Bram had made, on a shaft in two of the forge's ball bearings, and the shaft through a gear to the blower's own shaft.

It ran. It ran with a sound nobody at the works had heard from any machine: not a beat, not a knock, not the chuff and clank of every engine since the first; a high steady singing whine that went up through the floor into the soles of the feet and set the windows humming. The blower turned faster than it had ever turned on the old engine. The furnace roared.

Bram stood beside the casing with his hand flat on it, feeling it, the way he had stood beside the rolling mill the first day with his hand on the frame. He stood there for most of an hour.

"Blades and discs," Emeric said, beside him, under the whine. "The forge makes those. Every turbine there ever is in Kaldo. Nobody else could make a blade like that, and a blade that's wrong kills people." He was looking at the casing. "It never makes the boiler. Or the tubes. Or the plate. You make those. With your lathe and your rolls and your rivets and your hardness tester and your gauge, the way you made that one."

Bram did not take his hand off the casing.

"Because the boiler's the thing that's everywhere," he said, after a while. "In every shed in four baronies. And if the forge makes it, it's only ever in four baronies." He looked at the casing. "And if I make it, any smith I teach can."

"Yes."

Bram nodded slowly. "Then I'll want to make blades too," he said. "Not as good. By hand. To learn." He took his hand off the casing at last. "In case."

It seized on the tenth day.

Not the blades. The bearing. The forward bearing on the shaft, the one nearest the steam, went from a whine to a scream in the space of a breath at four in the afternoon, and the boy on the blower shut the steam off as he had been taught, and the disc ran down, slower and slower, for a quarter of an hour, with the bearing grinding all the way, and stopped.

Bram had it apart by six. The balls were good, the forge's, round to a hair. The races were good. What had killed it was the oil.

It was the oil every bearing and every axle and every piston in Kaldo ran on: rapeseed, pressed at Harl's old mill, clean and pale and good. And at the heat of the bearing next to the steam it had gone, in ten days, from oil to varnish. A brown sticky lacquer, baked onto the balls and the races and into every corner of the bearing's cage, thick enough to scrape off with a thumbnail; and when there was enough of it, the balls had stopped rolling and begun to drag.

Bram held up a ball between his thick fingers in the lamplight, with the brown lacquer on it like toffee.

"Plant oil," said Emeric. "It gums when it's hot. All plant oils do. It's fine for an axle or a lathe. Not for this." He took the ball. "This wants an oil from the ground. Rock oil. Refined. It doesn't gum." He put the ball down on the bench. "We haven't got any."

"Then the turbine waits."

"The big ones wait. Till there's rock oil." He did not say where rock oil was or how far off it was, because he did not know yet. "This one runs on the blower with a man changing the bearing oil every second day and scraping the bearing every week. To learn on. And when there's oil, we'll know what we're doing."

Bram looked at the brown lacquer on the bench for some while.

"I'll start the blades," he said.



The knights went into dead air in the second week of November, over the river at night, with Tess's count stone.

It was not a dead zone. Nobody in Kaldo was going to burst a stone over the river to make one, not even for practice; a real dead zone cost a stone worth a year of a man's wage, and stripped every working stone for half a mile, and would have put the K Discovery's matrix to sleep in the shed and the town's wall lamps out with it. The count stone was Tess's answer. A small air stone on its own cell in a box clipped to a knight's belt, wired to both hearts of his suit by a pair of copper leads, with a key in the box's lid that Tess kept. Turn the key, and the box cut both hearts at once, as if the stones in them had died; and counted, aloud, by a little air whistle in its lid, three notes, one, two, three. And on the third note, if nothing else happened, the watchdog's bellows went slack, and the springs that Bram had tried against the forge's reference threw the cape-wing open.

A suit with two dead hearts was a suit with no lift and no push and no heat, over a river at night, with a man in it. The cape was all he had.

"You glide," Sorrel told them, on the bank, in the dark, with her limp and her cropped hair and her arms folded. She had flown her own cape-wing off the cliff above the Mere eleven times in the spring, and into the Mere at the end of every one, because a wing with nothing behind it does not land. It only stops falling. "You don't flap. You don't kick. You don't reach for anything. You lie on it. Like lying on a bed. You let the wing do it, and you steer with your heels, a little, and you look where you want to go and not where you're going to hit. And when you're a man's height off the water you put your heels down and your arms up, and let it stall, and drop in." She looked at the four of them in their black plate. "And if you hit the river, you've got cork in the padding and two of Rylan's men in a boat. You'll live. You'll be wet."

Ward went first. Tess gave him the key on the bank, and he went up on his lever over the middle of the river, high, two hundred feet and more, until he was a black shape against the stars with the shed's lantern hung at his belt so the boat could follow him; and turned it. The whistle went one, two, three, and he fell, and the cape opened behind him with a crack like a sail filling, and he fell a while longer with it open, which Sorrel had told them he would, and then the wing bit and he came out of the fall and lay on it, and slid down the dark air over the river in a long flat slant toward the far bank, and put his heels down over the shallows, and dropped in to the waist, and stood up, and walked out.

Declan went second, and did it as neatly as he did everything until the very end, when he put his heels down a beat early, a good twelve feet up instead of a man's height, and the wing quit under him and dropped him flat. He hit the shallows on his front with a slap they heard on the near bank. He was up before the boat could reach him, wading out bent over with one arm across his ribs, and when Ward asked, he said, "Fine," and did not straighten up for a while.

Osric went third. He did everything Sorrel had told him not to do. When the whistle went he reached for his lift lever, which was dead; then for his stick, which was dead; then, when the cape threw, he kicked, both heels, hard, as a man kicks in deep water, and the cape folded at the back and the front of it took the air like a scoop and stood him on his head, and he went into the river upside down with a noise like a cow falling off a bridge.

The boat had him in a quarter of a minute. He came up on his back in his cork, spouting, and lay there in the river with his violet helm full of water, and the far bank, where Ward and Declan stood, did not make a sound. Then Declan made one. It was very small.

"I kicked," said Osric, from the river, to the sky. "She said don't kick. I heard her. I kicked anyway. Something in a man kicks." He was carried to the bank in the boat. "Again," he said, when he had been helped out of it, with the river running out of his plate. "I want to do it again. Now, before I'm frightened of it."

He did it four more times that night. He went into the river twice more. On the fifth he lay on the wing and slid down the dark to the far bank and came down in the shallows on his feet, and stood there with his arms out from his sides, not moving, as if he were afraid that if he moved it would turn out not to have happened.

Emeric went last. He had not been going to. Tess said a man who had drawn a wing ought to fall under it, and handed him the box, and he clipped it on.

It was strange, when the whistle went. He had known what would happen and it was strange anyway: the hearts going quiet, all at once, the steady warmth of the stones behind his breastbone and between his shoulders gone out like a pair of lamps, and the plate's weight coming back onto the harness in a breath, and him falling. One. Two. And in the falling, before the third note, his own gravity came up in him without his asking for it, the way a hand comes up when a man trips, to take the weight off; and he stopped it. Held it down. Let himself fall. Three. The springs threw the wing, and the air took it, and he lay on it and slid down the dark over the river with nothing holding him up but cane and silk and Sorrel's angle of the battens.

He came down in the shallows too fast, went to one knee in them, and got up with the river pouring out of his plate.

Sorrel was on the far bank when he waded out. She looked at him in the dark with her head on one side.

"You nearly didn't let it," she said. "At two. You went stiff." She had not seen anything. She could not have; she had no sense of his, and there had been no glyph, because he had not let it get that far. She had seen a man go stiff in the air. "Next time don't. Lie on it."



The steam failed on the same night, an hour later, because he had decided it would not.

He had thought about it for a month. Ward's dead-air drill was for a suit with its hearts dead, coming down where it could. But a man coming down where he could, at night, onto a bank somebody else was holding, would want not to be seen coming. And there was a river, and a night, and he was fire.

Fire on water made steam. It was the first thing every child with the faculty learned not to do, at the edge of a well, because it hurt. Heat the water hard and fast, the way he had heated the wet cakes in the balloon's basket, and it went up in a white cloud as thick as wool. A cloud a man could not see through. And air would move a cloud. Air was not his; it cost him three times what fire did, and he was clumsy with it, Sorrel said, the way a man is clumsy writing with his other hand. But it was only a push. He had pushed the paper planes. He could push a cloud.

So when the four knights had gone back up on their levers, high over the river wall, to come down again under their capes, and Ward stood on the far bank in the dark with his arms folded to watch for them, Emeric knelt at the water's edge on the near bank, below the wall, and put his hand flat on the river.

The steam came up. It came up with a roar, a great white boiling wall of it off a stretch of the river as long as a barge, rolling up into the dark as thick as the snow on the north faces of the tiles, and the river under it hissed and spat. He felt the cost of it, which was nothing; fire was his. He held it, and made more, and it rose and spread on the still air, a white bank between the wall and the far bank, and high above the wall the first knight's whistle went and the cape threw.

Emeric put his other hand out, and pushed the air.

He meant to push the cloud. Toward the far bank, slowly, a moving wall of white with the knights coming down behind it; the shape of it was in his head as clearly as the line he had laid over the shed for Ward. He pushed. The air went. Not slowly; he did not have slowly in him with air, he had only hard, and it went hard, a gust out of his palm across the water like a door slammed open in a gale, and it hit the cloud in the middle and went through it.

The cloud came apart. All of it at once. It tore into a hundred white rags that went streaming away over the river on the gust, thinning, in every direction, and were gone in the time it takes to say it, and the river lay open and black under the stars, and over it, in plain sight, against the white wall of the town, Declan was coming down under his black cape, slowly, like a bat across a lamp.

On the far bank, Ward raised one arm, and pointed at him.

Emeric knelt at the edge of the water with both hands still out, and the steam still curling off the river where he had boiled it, and looked at the empty air where the cloud had been.

Two things. Each of them had worked. The steam had come up, thick, exactly as he had wanted it, and the air had gone out of his hand, hard, exactly as he had told it to; and together they had done a thing neither of them did, and the thing was that Declan was seen from a hundred yards off by a man with his arms folded.

Sorrel came down the water stair behind him, slowly, on her bad leg. She stood on the bottom step and looked at the river.

"You blew it away," she said.

"Yes."

"You pushed it. Like a man pushing a door." She came down onto the stones beside him. "Air isn't a door. Air's a river. You don't push a river. You lean into it at the top, where it's already going, and you let it carry the thing you want carried. A breath. Not a gust." She looked at him in the dark. "Who taught you air?"

"Nobody. A book."

"It shows," said Sorrel. She sat down on the step with her bad leg out in front of her. "I'll teach you air," she said. "In the spring. When I've time. You'll hate it. You'll want to push." She looked across at Ward on the far bank, who had lowered his arm and was standing, waiting, the way Ward waited. "And then you'll stop wanting to, and you'll be able to put a cloud anywhere you like on a still night, and walk the whole of Ward's lot across a river under it, and he'll not see one of them." She stood up. "Not this year," she said, and went up the water stair.



He showed her the other thing a week later, because she asked what else he had been pushing.

A pebble. That was all. In the works yard, at dusk, with nobody else there, a round grey pebble off the river bar in the palm of his left hand, and his right hand in front of it, cupped, the way a child cups a hand round its mouth to call across a field. He had thought about the crossbows in the armoury rack, and about the ink drop he had pushed across a page in the first winter, and the paper planes; and about a funnel. Air going into the wide end of a funnel came out of the narrow end fast. A funnel of air in front of a pebble, made of nothing, with no barrel and no stone.

He did it. The glyph came up off his right palm, air, pale and wobbling, too big, and the air went into the funnel he had made of it and out of the narrow end hard, and the pebble went off his left palm across the works yard like a sling stone, and hit the wall of the coal store, and broke off a corner of a brick.

Sorrel looked at the brick.

"Again," she said.

The second one went into the coal heap. The third went over the wall into the river. The fourth broke another brick, a hand from the first. His head ached, the steady push behind the eyes that air always cost him; he could have done perhaps ten more before he wanted to sit down.

"It's a sling," said Sorrel. "With no sling." She picked the broken corner of brick up off the yard and turned it in her fingers. "A crossbow does that. Better. Every time. And any boy in the levy can wind one."

"A crossbow needs a bow. And a string. And a smith, and a bowyer, and a quiver of bolts." He looked at the pebble in his palm. "This needs a pebble."

Sorrel thought about that. She looked at the brick.

"Air shot," she said. "That's what it is. That's what you'd call it, if you called it anything." She put the corner of brick in her pocket. "Spring," she said. "I'll teach you to lean, and then you'll hit what you're looking at." And she went away across the dark yard toward the mill, limping, with the broken brick in her pocket.



She came back on the Thursday, in the rain, with the corner of brick still in her pocket.

He was at the bench under the works' north window with the turbine disc in front of him and a sheet of Kaldo paper beside it, working out how much the blades would stretch at full speed, and she took the brick out and put it on the corner of his sheet to stop it curling, as though that was what she had kept it for.

"The cape doesn't fly," she said.

He put the pencil down. "It got four men across a river last week."

"It got four men down. Slower than they'd have come down without it. That's not flying, that's falling with manners." She pulled a stool out from under the bench with her good foot and sat on it, with the bad leg out straight. "Eleven times off the cliff at the Mere. Eleven times in the water. A cape's a gull with its wings nailed open. It can't go up. It can't do anything but come down a bit further off than it would have."

"You're an air mage," he said. "You could stay up all afternoon."

"I could stay up an hour and be sick in a bucket after. And then some priest would stand on the tower steps and say there, you see, flight's for mages, the way God meant it." She wiped the rain off her face with the back of her wrist. "Gulls aren't mages. Swifts sleep up there. I've watched a heron go two miles off one push and never move its wings. I want a wing like that. Cane and cloth and glue. Nothing in it a dead zone can stop and nothing a priest can call anything but what it is. I want one a girl off a farm can fly."

The rain went on against the window. Out in the yard a man was shouting at somebody about a barrow.

He knew too much, was the trouble. He knew the man with the whirling arm in a garden and the boy he'd sent across a valley in a carriage with wings, and the German with his hill of turf and his two thousand jumps who had broken his neck on the last one; two brothers with a box of fans and a set of tables; the shape of a wing cut through like a loaf, and the numbers for it, and the long road after that, to engines and to things he had no business remembering in a works yard in the rain. And a prince who had come south with a box of books and a bad name would know none of it. Every sentence he gave her would be a sentence somebody asked him about, later. Sorrel would be the first.

He turned the sheet over and drew.

A straight wing, long, thin, with its two ends tipped up a little so that from the front it made a very shallow V. A stick behind it for a body. A small flat wing at the far end of the stick, for a tail, and a small upright fin on that. A seat under the wing's middle, and a weight in the nose, which he drew as a box and labelled it: stones, or lead, or a fat man.

Sorrel looked at it for a while.

"That's a paper plane," she said.

"It's the one the class folded. Big enough to sit in." He wrote the word under it. "A glider."

"Glider," she said, trying it. She didn't seem to mind it. "That's all?"

"That's where it starts."

"You know more."

"I know what crosses a long room."

She looked at him with her head on one side. Then she looked back at the drawing, and he watched her decide not to have that argument today.

"Fine," she said. "Then I'll ask. Why are the ends turned up?"

"What does a paper plane do if you let it go tipped over on one side?"

"Rolls back level. Most of them. The bad ones go into the wall." She frowned at the V. "Because the low side's flatter to the air, so it takes more of it." She did not wait for him to say yes. "All right. It only goes down, though. That's the cape all over again. How does it go up?"

"It doesn't," he said. "Where does the air go up?"

She opened her mouth to say something short, and stopped.

"Up the face of a hill," she said slowly, "when the wind's on it. You can lean on it at the top of the Mere cliff in a west wind, I've done it with a sheet." She was looking at the window now, not at him. "Over a ploughed field on a hot day, not over a wet meadow. You can see it shake over the furrows. And under the flat-bottomed clouds in summer, the fat white ones. I've felt it twice in the cape, coming down, like a hand under the back. Didn't last. I didn't know what to do with it." She looked at him. "You'd go round in it. Wouldn't you. Like a hawk does. Round and round in the one place, going up."

"You'd know better than I would," he said, and meant most of it. She had been in the air more than he had, the old way. The air told her things it had never told him.

"How far does it go, then? For every foot it drops."

"How far does a gull go for every foot it drops? How far does a crow?"

"A gull goes for ever. A crow flaps." She thought about the two birds, and he could see her seeing them, one wing long and narrow, one short and broad. "So you want it long and thin."

"Why?"

"I don't know." She said it as though it annoyed her. "Do you?"

"Build two small ones," he said. "One long and thin and one short and fat, the same weight, the same cloth. Throw them off the keep on a still morning and count paces to where they land. Then do it again. Write it down."

"You answer every question with a question. Like a priest."

"A priest has the answers."

That got half a laugh out of her, which was more than he usually got. "What slows it down?"

"Put your hand out of a coach window."

She held her hand up between them, flat, palm down, and then turned it on its edge, and then flat again. He watched her feel the coach that wasn't there.

"Everything that isn't wing," she said. "Every stick, every strap, every bit of the body hanging down in the wind. It's all something you're dragging through the air for nothing." She looked down at herself on the stool. "Including me."

"Lie down in it."

"Like the cape." She nodded once. Then she reached over and tapped his wing with one finger. "You've drawn that flat."

"Have I?"

"A bird's isn't. I've had a gull's wing in my hands. A dead one. It's curved, the top's round and the underneath goes in, like a spoon." She looked at the turbine disc on the bench between them, at the sixty little cups round its rim, and then back at him, sharply. "Like those."

He said nothing at all.

"You know why," she said.

"I know it's worth finding out."

Sorrel folded the sheet in four and put it inside her coat. She left the brick where it was.

"How long?" she said, getting up.

"Years. If you do it properly."

"I've been doing it improperly off towers since I was twelve," said Sorrel. "I can do properly." She went to the door, and stopped there with her hand on it and the rain blowing in past her. "And when it flies. When I want it up with no hill under it and no hot field and no cloud. What then?"

The disc sat on the bench. Sixty cups, every one at its careful angle. Bram had said it frightened him.

"Then you'll want something that pushes," Emeric said.

"A stone."

"Not a stone."

She looked at him for a long moment from the doorway. Then she grinned, which he had never once seen her do, and went out into the rain, and left him with the turbine and his numbers and the corner of a broken brick on his sheet, and the sense, which he did not like, of having said a word more than he meant to.
