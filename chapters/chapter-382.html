Chapter 382 - What Could Go Wrong

The list had been started in July on a single sheet, and by the ninth of February it was forty-one sheets in a box file with a strap round it, and Ferra carried it into the long room on the Monday morning under her arm like a ledger nobody wanted to audit.

"Every way it goes wrong," said Emeric. "That anybody's thought of. Out loud, before we go, so nobody's surprised."

"We'll be surprised," said Saban.

"We'll be surprised by something that isn't on the list. Not by anything that is."

That had been the rule since the heartbeat, in September: say the faults before anybody builds them in. He'd said it so often since that Ward had started saying it for him, and Tess had started saying it before either of them, and the list had grown like a hedge. A dead zone under a hull over land. Over sea. Over the K Discovery with the relays aboard. A cartridge spoiled at depth. The Challenger's latch failing. A storm in the Band with the necks open. Fire in a hold. A man overboard at night. Fever aboard. Rats. The bleeding sickness in somebody who'd passed the card. The thick water thicker than Zale's run had found it. Ilenne's people saying no. Ilenne's people saying yes and meaning something else. A hull lost. Two. The relay chain broken behind them with nobody to ride it up.

And the small ones, which Imani had insisted went in as well, because the small ones were what killed you when you were busy with the big ones. The heads backing up in a sea. The laundry not drying for a fortnight and a whole crew in damp wool getting sick. Burial, if it came to that: a weighted canvas and the words Doran had written out, and nobody had wanted to write that line and Imani had written it anyway.

They went through it all that week. They drilled what could be drilled.

The abandon-ship packs were Tuesday, at the yard, on the K Amara at her moorings with all three hundred of her people aboard and the knights' aprons empty, and Osric with a bell and a watch.

Every soul aboard every hull had one. It hung on a hook at the head of their own bunk, not in a hold, not in a locker, where their hand would find it in the dark. Canvas, the size of a loaf, with a strap. Inside: a flask of water, two days' rations in a tin, a whistle on a cord, a small lamp, a knife, a float that blew up by mouth, a mask with a cartridge in it in its tin sleeve and its wax, and the chute, the Well's rule, folded on top, because a hull that went down over land went down from a height. Ferra had rationed the cartridges for the packs the way she rationed everything, by counting them twice and then once more at night.

Osric rang the bell at a quarter past ten without warning. Long, short, long, short, which was the abandon. And three hundred people who'd been told it might come that week and not when came up out of the K Amara's decks with their packs on their backs in four minutes and fifty seconds by his watch, and stood at their stations along the rail in their lines, and he walked down the lines and looked at every one of them.

"Strap," he said to one. "Under the arm, not over. Over, it comes off in the water." To another: "Where's your mask?" "In the pack, sir." "Show me." It was. To a third: "Your chute's upside down." It was.

And at the end of the line, by the after rail, where the home crew had come aboard for the drill because one day they'd be crewing hulls five and six, when the yard had them off the slips, Tolliver.

Osric stood in front of him for a long time.

Tolliver's pack was on his back, the right way up, strap under the arm. His mask was in it. His chute was right. And the clip on the safety line at his belt, the one every man at the rail was supposed to snap onto the jackline before he did anything else, any time the bell went, was hanging loose at his hip, unclipped, swinging slightly in the breeze off the bay.

"Tolliver," said Osric.

Tolliver looked down at it.

"Oh, for..." he said.

"Yes."

"I'd clipped it. Sir. On the way up. I'm sure I'd..." He stopped. He looked at the jackline, which was a yard away along the rail, and the clip, which was not on it, and his face did a thing Emeric had seen it do at a fire, at a drill, on a fireboat, and once on the wall at the South Gate in a high wind, which was the face of a young man watching himself be a fool and unable to stop it.

Osric took a pencil out of his pocket and the hull's roll off its board and found a name on it, and drew a line through it, slowly, so Tolliver could watch.

"You were down for hull five's first crew," he said. "When she's off the slip. Out past the islands and back. You're not now."

"Sir..."

"A man who doesn't clip on in a drill in the bay," said Osric, "doesn't clip on in a gale in the Band. And I'll not be writing to your mother." He gave the pencil back to his pocket. "You'll be on the walls. You'll be very useful on the walls. They're not going anywhere."

Tolliver stood at the rail with his clip in his hand and looked at it. Then, very carefully, as if it might bite, he snapped it onto the jackline.

"There," he said.

"There," said Osric, and went on down the line.

Mira ran the knights' net drill on the Wednesday, on the ridge above the Milford road, with a squad of eight in the MK3s and the snow coming sideways.

Three failures, one after another. Ward had set them; she wasn't told the order. The first was an antenna. Halfway up the ridge Ward stepped out from behind a rock in his old plate and snapped the two brass rods off the helm of the squad's second man, Corin, with a pair of shears, and stepped back behind the rock; and Corin's voice on the squad plate went from clear to a hiss to a sound like a man talking from under a blanket two rooms away.

"Corin's short," said Mira, on the squad plate. "Three hundred yards, maybe less. Corin, close up. You're on my shoulder till the top." She didn't look round. "Bren, you're his voice past that. Anything he needs to send, he sends to you and you send it on."

The second was the operator. At the top of the ridge, Ward came out again and touched Bren on the shoulder, which was the sign, and Bren sat down in the snow with his hands in his lap and was dead.

"Bren's down," said Mira. She knelt by him for a moment. Then she took the code sheet out of the pouch on his thigh, his copy of Ferra's month, and put it inside her own plate. "I've the sheet. Corin, you're on my shoulder still. Kell, take the company plate. Report in to the line room on the hour as us. Not as Bren. As the squad."

The third was the net. Halfway down the far side Ambrose, five miles away at the heartbeat board with Ward's sealed order open on the desk in front of him, turned the key on the western drum and the southern one together, and the whole of the line room's traffic over the Milford road went quiet. Not round. Gone. The hole too big to go round.

Kell keyed the company plate and got nothing. He tried command. Nothing. Then he looked at Mira.

"Emergency," he said. "Do I?"

"Is anybody dying?"

"No."

"Then no. Emergency's empty." Mira stood up out of the snow. "We're on our own. We knew we might be. We've a squad plate that reaches each other, and a road, and our feet." She pointed down the ridge at the Milford road, a dark line in the white a mile below. "We walk to the road. We walk to the first house on the road with a mast on its roof, which is the steward's post at the Hollow, two miles south. And we use their set. Through whatever drum's still up." She looked at Corin, who still had his hand cupped over his snapped helm as if the rods might grow back. "Or we walk home. It's eleven miles. I've done it."

They walked to the Hollow. The steward's post had a set, and a crank, and an old man who'd been told the knights might come and had the kettle on, and they rang the line room through the northern drum, a hundred miles round, and Ambrose said, from very far off, "Squad two, I have you. Report," and Mira reported, and that was the drill.

"Good," said Ward that evening, in the yard, which was all he ever said, and then, after a moment, to her alone: "The sheet. Off his body. Before you did anything else."

"That's what you'd have done."

"That's what I'd have forgotten to do the first time," said Ward. "I lost a sheet that way. In the war before this one. A man died over it." He looked at the snow. "Good," he said again.

Then he gave her the crate.

It had been standing against the armoury wall all day under a tarpaulin, and the squad had been walking past it and pretending not to look. Ward pulled the tarpaulin off and opened the lid, and inside, packed in sawdust in rows, were magazines. A great many of them. The ordinary steel boxes every knight in the yard carried in the self-loader, but with the base plates changed: some smooth, some with two notches filed across the bottom, some with three, some with one deep cut, some with a single round hole drilled through.

"Item nineteen," said Ward. He had the sheet in his coat; he didn't need to take it out. "A dead zone over a squad with nothing in their magazines but stones. Emeric wrote that one in August and I've been sitting on it since." He picked up a three-notch magazine and turned it in the lamplight. "Everything you've got with a chip in it is a lump of lead in a dead zone. The lightning round. The spike. Fine on a Tuesday. Useless on the one day you need it. So he's had Fennick and Tess give every round a floor."

"A floor," said Bren.

"Something under it that works when the stone doesn't." Ward thumbed a round off the top of the three-notch magazine and held it up. Its tip was painted yellow. "Stun. There's a barb on the front and a battery behind the barb, a little dry cell of Tess's the size of a thimble, and a coil. It goes in, it stays in, it bites for five seconds. No stone. Then on top of that, if you've the chip version, the lightning chip behind the barb does what the spike round always did, harder." He put it back. "Dead zone, you've still got the five seconds."

Kell was looking at it with the face of a man who knew where this was going. Kell was the densest knight in the squad, and everybody in the yard knew the story of the first spike round, the one that had failed on an elite before the air battle, because a dense body fought outside mana off at the skin.

"It's not mana," said Ward, to him. "It's a battery. Your blood doesn't know the difference between this and a cart horse touching the fence on the Milford road. Want to see?"

"No, sir," said Kell.

"Good lad," said Ward. "Bren."

Bren held his hand out, because Bren always held his hand out. Ward didn't fire it at him. He took a dart out of a tin, a bare one, barb and cell and coil, and pressed the barb against the meat of Bren's thigh through his trousers, and pushed.

Bren sat down on the gravel. He didn't decide to; his legs did it for him, all at once, and he sat there for five seconds with his mouth open and every muscle he had pulling the wrong way, making a noise like a kettle, and then it stopped and he was just Bren, sitting in the yard.

"Oh, no," he said, quite calmly.

"Five seconds," said Ward. "That's a long time to a man with a knife. That's all it's for. It isn't for killing and it isn't safe. A man falls on his head from five seconds, he's dead of it." He held out a hand and Bren took it. "So you aim at the body and you're on him before the five are up."

The blue ones were the batons. Mira knew those already; everybody had heard about them. A round with a fat rubber cap on it where the bullet ought to be, on a light charge of powder, so the gun hardly kicked. In air mode it took no powder at all: the selector had a new stop on it now, a soft stop, filed and marked in white, below the ordinary one, and on the soft stop the crystal pushed the round out at a lob a man could almost watch.

Ward had them hang a sack of wet sand on the old post at twenty yards, and Mira put a baton into it on the soft stop. The sack swung and thumped and hung there with a dent in it the size of a fist.

Then he had them move the post to five yards and do it again, on powder.

The sack split. The sand came out of it in a slide onto the gravel and the rubber cap went on through the canvas and smacked off the armoury wall behind.

Nobody said anything.

"Legs and belly," said Ward. "Never above the belt. Never inside twenty yards. If he's inside twenty yards you put the gun up and use your hands. That's not a rubber ball. It's a fist off a horse. Anybody who forgets that kills somebody's son in a market and we all hang for it." He looked along the line of them. "Say it."

"Legs and belly," they said. "Never above the belt. Never inside twenty."

The smooth base was ball: the killing round, the one they'd always had. The single deep cut was new and the tips were painted black, and Ward didn't hand those round. He held one up so they could see it. A small hard core in it, he said, the same stuff as the big rifle's on the K Amara, made smaller. It went through a breastplate and the man behind it. It went through a hull plank. "It won't do anything to the big thing," he said, "so don't think it will. A thing the size of a man, though." He put it back in its row. "Those you're issued five of, counted out and counted in."

The drilled hole was the green ones. Tag rounds. They burst against whatever they hit and left a splash of green dye and a smell like crushed fennel that didn't wash out for a week, and in the dye, if you had the chip version, a dust so fine you couldn't see it, from one of Armand's sense stones ground to flour, that anybody with a good sense could follow for a day afterwards like a lamp left on in a window. Mira put one into the armoury door, and the door smelled of fennel for the rest of the winter.

"That's for the one who runs," said Ward. "You don't shoot him. You mark him. And you let him run home, and then you know where home is."

Then he put the yard lamps out.

It was full dark by then, a February dark with the snow still in the air, and they stood in it in a line with their rifles and their pouches full of mixed magazines, every kind together, and Ward walked up and down behind them calling, and they had to find the right one by touch and change it and say what it was.

"Batons."

Two notches. Left pouch. Change. "Batons," said Mira, and heard six others say it with her, and one say "Stun."

"Bren," said Ward, in the dark.

"Sir."

"What've you got?"

A pause. A scrape of a thumb on steel.

"Three notches, sir."

"And what's three notches?"

"Stun, sir."

"And what's the man in front of you doing?"

"Throwing stones at a window, sir."

"Then you've just put a barb in a boy for throwing stones. Change it."

They did it for an hour. Less-lethal on the left hip, killing on the right, always, on everybody, so that a hand in the dark went to the right side of the body before the head had caught up with it. By the end of the hour nobody was getting it wrong, and Bren was getting it right faster than anybody, which Mira suspected was the shock.

"Good," said Ward, when the lamps came back on. He looked at the crate. "Every knight in the fleet does this before we sail. You teach it." That was to Mira. "Not me. You."

"Me, sir?"

"You found the sheet on a dead man before you did anything else," said Ward. "You'll teach them to find a magazine in the dark." He put the tarpaulin back over the crate. "And Bren can be the dead man again. He's good at it."

The relay drills were the Wednesday too, at the Well, in the afternoon, and Tess ran them, and Emeric hated all three.

The first was a ride cut off mid-chain. He'd said it at the Well on the twenty-seventh of December, the first night: if the relay died while he was in it, he didn't know where he ended up. Maybe home. Maybe nowhere. Tess had said that a thing you didn't know was a thing you found out, carefully, before it found you out somewhere a thousand miles from a bed.

So he sat on the Well's deck with his back against the drum and his palm on the plate, with Orla Garran on one side of him and Ward on the other, and went: the Well, BR-01, the northern drum, out over Grayrock and the cold hills. And when he was in the northern drum, a hundred and fifty miles off, Tess, on the speaker in the line room, said "Now," and Ambrose ordered BR-01 quiet behind him.

The road behind him went out.

He'd expected to feel it as a drop. Or a dark. Or the long fall of a delve gone wrong, the cold coming up the stair. It wasn't any of those things. He was in the northern drum, looking down at the Grayrock winding gear in the low winter sun, and the next moment he was on the Well's deck with his back against the drum and his heart going like a hammer and his palm slipping off the plate, and Orla Garran's hand on his wrist.

No between. No falling. Like a lamp put out in one room and lit in another. He'd been taken out of the far end and put back where he'd started, all of him, the way a picture goes out of a magic lantern when you pull the slide and is still there on the slide, whole.

"Where were you?" said Orla.

"Here," he said. "I'm here." He looked at his hand. "It sent me home."

"It sent you home," said Tess, in the speaker, very quietly, and he heard her let out a breath five miles down that she must have been holding since the morning. "Write it down. Ambrose, write it down. A ride that loses the road goes home." A pause. "If home's there."

"If home's there," he agreed.

The second was the body. He didn't know it was coming; that was the point of it. He went out again, the Well to BR-01 and back, a short one, eleven seconds, the first ride's length; and while he was gone, Orla Garran got up from beside him as Tess had told her to that morning, and walked to the lab's far end and stood with her back to the deck and looked at a cloud. And Ward, who'd been sitting on his other side, got up too, and walked away along the rail to the drum rail's end; and came back, slowly, from behind, with a wooden knife from the barracks' practice rack in his hand, and stood over the king's body sitting on the deck with its eyes shut and its palm on the drum, and laid the wooden blade along its throat, gently, under the jaw, and held it there.

Emeric came home into a body with something cold under its chin.

He didn't move. He opened his eyes. Ward was looking down at him.

"Eleven seconds," said Ward. "I'd have needed two."

Nobody said anything. Orla had turned round from her cloud, and her face was grey.

"That's the one I don't like," said Ward. He took the knife away and put it in his belt. "Not the road. The road sends you home. Tess says so, and I believe her. This." He tapped the deck beside Emeric's leg with his boot. "You go out, and you leave this sitting on a deck with its hand on a drum. And anybody who's with you only has to look at a cloud." He looked at Orla, and his face softened very slightly. "I told her to. She hated it."

"I did," said Orla.

"Two," said Emeric. He got up, slowly. "Two by the body. Always. Never one. And neither of them looks away for anything." He looked at Ward. "And not you. You'll be on the K Amara. When I do it out there, it'll be off the K Discovery in the Band, with the hull parked and the guns cold, and two knights sitting on the deck either side of me with their swords out, and they'll not be the ones who drew lots for it, they'll be the ones I picked."

"Write it," said Ward.

"I'm writing it," said Tess, from five miles down.

The third was Tess's own, and she came up for it on the Hod's tender with her stick and a crate, because it couldn't be done from the line room.

The crate held one of the eleven deciders from her shelf in the Motion Shop, a spare, cut and keyed and locked, with its own little radio and its own stone, in a drum the size of a milk churn. Orla wheeled it out onto the deck on the trolley, into the Band, three yards from the Well's own drum, and Ambrose woke it from the line room, and it beat: tok, in the speaker, a fresh neighbour on the chain, and the Well's drum beat back to it.

"Now," said Tess, "I'm going to try to get in."

She put her palm on its plate. Her own note. Fine and regular, like a ruled snowflake, and not one of the two that Tess herself had cut into every lock on every drum on the chain.

She went to go in.

Emeric felt it from where he stood, a yard off: the small sharp shut of a door, the way you'd feel a door slam in a house from the far end of a corridor. The spare's stone closed. Its radio stopped mid-beat. Its pin held where it was on the trolley. And up the chain, a single word, the hit word, to the Well's drum, and on from the Well to BR-01, and on from BR-01 to every drum over the kingdom; so that in the speaker on the lab bench, a second later, nine beats came in at once, out of time with each other, tok-tok-tok-tok, the neighbours shouting.

Then quiet.

Tess took her hand off the plate. She was a little pale.

"That's what it does," she said. "That's all it does."

"Ambrose," said Emeric. "What's the board say?"

Ambrose's voice, very small. "Spare's gone. Last word first, so the board says hit. Nobody hears it now. If I didn't know it was on the Well's deck I'd say something had knocked it out of the sky."

"Exactly like a dead one," said Tess. "From outside." She looked at the drum on its trolley, dark, with her handprint on its plate. "I knew it would. I built it. I still wanted to see." She sat down on the crate it had come in. "I put my hand on it," she said, more quietly, "and it shut on me like I was the Church."

"That's what you built it to do."

"I know what I built it to do." She looked up at him. "It doesn't make it nice."

Imani's check was the same evening, at the ward, at seven, and it was routine, and it was on her list for everybody.

Every voyager. Every one of the eight hundred, before the end of February. Ten minutes each, in the small room at the back of the ward with the good lamp: Imani or Bettany or one of the senior nurses with the card in front of them, pulse and lungs and eyes and teeth, the Band hours read, and for the ones who were going furthest and staying longest and would have the least help if something went wrong, her sight. Imani did those herself. The six. Zale. Maeve. Ilenne, whom she'd been checking every week for more than two years and knew better than her own face. Armand, who complained.

The king was the last of the day. Ferra was the one before him. She'd gone through in her ten minutes and come out and sat on the bench in the corridor outside to wait for him, because they were going to walk up the hill together afterwards and eat something, if Maren let them.

He sat on the end of the bed in his shirt. Imani listened to his chest. She looked at his eyes with the lamp. She pressed his wrist and counted. She wrote. Then she put her pen down, and pulled her chair round in front of him, and sat, and put her hands on either side of his head, lightly, above the ears, the way she'd held it a hundred times, and looked.

He knew what her sight did. He'd never been able to describe it and neither had she. She said she saw what was there the way she saw a bruise under the skin, or a bone set badly: the shape of a thing, where it was full and where it was thin. In a body it was mana, how it lay, where it ran clean and where it pooled. And in the head of a delver, who carried a house in there with rooms in it, she said she saw the rooms. Not what was in them. Their shape. Where they were lit.

She looked for a long time. Longer than usual.

He'd done this with her every quarter since the summer. He knew the length of it. He felt the seconds go past the length of it, and then more seconds, and he kept still under her hands, and didn't ask.

"Emeric," she said at last.

"Yes."

"There's a room I don't know." She didn't take her hands away. "Not a room. A place where a room was." She frowned. She was choosing words with great care, the way she did when she had to tell somebody something about their body that they would remember her saying for the rest of their life. "Everything else is the way it always is. Lit. Full. The places where you keep things." Her thumbs moved a little, above his ears. "And here, near the old part, the part from before you were seven. A place with an edge. The edge is all there. The walls are there, the shape's there. You can see where it went and how big it was." She stopped. "And there's nothing in it."

He sat very still.

"It's new," said Imani. "It wasn't there in September. I'd have seen it." She took her hands away, slowly, and put them in her lap. "When did you lose something?"

He didn't answer.

"Tell me something," she said. Gently. "Anything. Tell me something about Corbin when he was small."

And he went to.

He knew there'd been things. He knew it the way you know a word is on the tip of your tongue: he could feel the place where it was. A stair. The stair outside his mother's rooms in the tower at the palace, before. A small boy on it. Corbin, small, three or four, on the bottom step, and himself on the step above, and something they'd done there, a game, or a quarrel, or nothing much, an afternoon. He knew there'd been an afternoon. He knew there'd been more than one. The palace years, before the long hall and the steps and the dark; the few good ones. He knew they'd happened. He knew he'd had them. He'd taken them out and looked at them, now and then, in the long room at night, when Corbin was being Corbin at the foot of the table and he needed a reason not to hate him.

He reached for one. And it was there, the shape of it, the step and the light on the step and the size of a small boy sitting on it. The frame. Every edge of it.

And nothing inside.

He reached for another. The same. A shape. A size. The certainty that something had been there, warm, his. And nothing, nothing at all, the way a picture frame is nothing when someone's taken the picture out and hung the frame back on the nail.

He sat on the end of the bed. Imani didn't move. She'd seen his face change.

"Oh," he said, after a while. It came out very small. "That's what it took."

The door to the corridor was open a hand's width. It had been open all along; it always was, at the ward, so a nurse could hear. Ferra was standing in it. He didn't know how long she'd been standing there. She'd heard Imani's question, at least. She'd seen him answer it, which was worse, because he hadn't said anything.

She looked at him for a long moment. He watched her read it. She could read a ledger upside down across a table in the time it took most people to find the first line, and she'd been reading his face for seventeen years, and she read it now: the stair, the boy, the frame with nothing in it, and the reason for the frame. Not the leash; she'd known about the leash since the first of December, she'd written its line in her own hand. The cost of it. The one he'd said, at the long table, he didn't know.

"Corbin," she said.

"Corbin," he said. "When he was small. The palace. The good bits." He looked at his own hands. "I know I had them. I've got the size of them. I can't find what was in them."

Ferra came into the room and shut the door behind her.

He argued it once, on the Thursday, in the long room, after the list. He argued it badly, and he knew he was arguing it badly while he did it, and they let him.

They'd gone through the forty-one sheets in the morning with Tess and Osric at the table as well, and the last of it in the afternoon, when Tess and Osric had gone back to the Works and it was only the six. He'd read his own last item himself, from his own sheet, the one he'd added that week after talking to Armand.

"A ring burst scales," he said. "Everybody's seen a small one. A cut ring on a spell in a fight, the flash, the two mages on their backs. A mid spell cut wrong and the thing goes off between them. We've all seen it." He looked at Armand. "I asked how big it goes. If you cut a ring on something enormous. Something the size of a dragon's breath, or bigger. Nobody knows. Nobody's seen the top of it."

"Nobody's been fool enough," said Armand.

"So it's on the list. If it happens out there, we'll be the first to see it. And probably the last." He put the sheet down. "That's all I've got."

And then Imani, who had been sitting very quietly at Ferra's side the whole afternoon, took a single sheet out of her own bag and put it at the bottom of the pile, face up, and pushed it across the table to the middle.

It had one line on it, in her hand.

42. The king. A place gone near the old part, since September, the shape of it kept. What it took: his memories of C. as a boy, the palace years. He knows they happened. They're not there. Cause: the leash (1 Dec). Will it take more: unknown. I.

Saban read it upside down and was quiet. Bram read it and put his big hand flat on the table. Ward read it and looked at Emeric for a long time and then looked away, at the window. Armand read it and closed his eyes, as if he were somewhere else for a moment, in the tower, sixty years before, watching old men find out what they'd lost by accident.

"Take it off," said Imani.

She said it without heat. She'd been against it on the first of December, against it in plain words; she'd said it was the Church's trick and that he'd be the one who told Corbin it was a check, and that was worse. She hadn't said it since. She'd done the check delves with him and sat with him on the Thursdays and never once said I told you.

"Take it off," she said again. "You can. You burn it. You'll not get them back, I know you'll not get them back. But it's not taken anything else yet that I can see, and I'd sooner it didn't have the chance."

"No," said Emeric.

He'd known he was going to say it. He hadn't known how badly he'd say what came after.

"It's done," he said. "That's the thing. It's paid. Whatever it took, it took. Burning it doesn't give it back. So it's not costing me anything now, it's not, the cost's gone, it's in the past, and what's left is what it's for. Which is the same as it was in December." He could hear himself. He went on anyway. "Nothing's changed. The Church still wants in. He still knows how it feels from inside better than anybody, and that's why he's in the chair, and that's why it's on his ankle. If it was right in December it's right now. The only thing that's different is I know the price." He stopped. "And the price was mine. Nobody else's. It's not his. It never touched him."

"It touched him," said Ferra.

He looked at her.

"You lost the reason," she said. "You told me that yourself, last night. You kept those afternoons so you'd have a reason not to hate him. When he was being him at the foot of the table." She didn't raise her voice. "So now you've got the decision without the reason. You'll still not hate him. You'll still say yes to him in the chair. You'll just not know why, any more, and he'll see it in your face and not know either."

"That's not a reason to take it off."

"No," said Ferra. "It's not."

He'd expected more. He'd expected all of them: Imani, and Armand with what he'd watched in the tower, and Saban's dry objection that a thing you couldn't audit was a thing you couldn't trust, and Bram's slow shake of the head. He'd got his argument ready for all of them and it was a bad argument, he knew it was, a man explaining why a bridge that had cracked should be left standing because the crack was already there. And he waited for them to take it apart.

Nobody did.

Imani looked at him for a long time across the table. Then she reached out and took her sheet back from the middle of the pile and put it, face up still, on top of the forty-one. Number forty-two. On the list, with the dead zones and the rats.

"Then it's on the list," she said. "And you know what it costs. And it's yours." She put her hand flat on it. "When you burn it, if you burn it, it'll be because you've decided to. Not because I found it. I'd like that to be true."

"It's true."

"Good."

Ferra wrote in the book. She turned it round so they could see, the way she always did.

12 Feb. Item 42, I.'s hand. Cost of the leash found: his memories of C. as a boy. He keeps it. Argued it badly. We let him. F.

He read it. "That's not fair."

"It's written down," said Ferra. "That's different."

He went up the keep stair at eleven that night, past the second floor, and Corbin's door was shut, with light under it, and the scrape of a chair, and the small dry sound of a man turning pages, the word book probably, which Corbin had been learning at night since January in a hand so careful it was almost painful to watch.

Emeric stopped on the landing. He'd stopped there in December, on the night of the check, and had a feeling he'd meant to remember something and couldn't think what, gone like a name, and it hadn't seemed to matter.

It mattered now. He knew exactly what it was. He stood on the stair outside his brother's lit door and reached for the stair outside his mother's tower, the bottom step, the small boy, and found the frame, every edge of it, and nothing inside.

He didn't knock. He thought about it. He thought about what he'd say, and found that he didn't know what you said to a man you couldn't remember being small with, about a thing he didn't know you'd done to him; and that the only true thing was the thing he couldn't say.

The pages went on turning on the other side of the door. After a while he went on up.
