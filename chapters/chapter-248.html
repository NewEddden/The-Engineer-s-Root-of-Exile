Chapter 248 - The Crown Book

Saban came back on the evening train sixteen days after he went, with the cold of the capital still in his coat and a look Emeric had seen on him only once before, at the border ford, in the physician's cart, when he had woken up and asked whether it would mark him.

He did not go to the table room. He went to the counting room next door to it, where he kept his own desk under the high window, and sat down at it in his coat, and put his hands flat on the desk in front of him, and stayed there. Ferra found him there half an hour later when she came through for the wool book, and came and fetched Emeric without a word, and Emeric went through and sat on the corner of the next desk and waited.

"I found it," Saban said.

"Grier."

"Grier's paper. All of it." He did not take his hands off the desk. "I'll tell it in order. It's better in order."

He had gone up with the man in grey, Wald, on the first train, as far as Wayford, and by the hired carriage the rest of the way, and Wald had not said a word to him for five days except about horses. At the capital's gate they had parted without arranging to meet again. Saban had taken a room over a saddler's in the upper market, where he had lodged before, and gone out that evening to the chancery lanes.

"Everyone knew Grier had voted," he said. "Everyone knew he'd turned. It was all anybody was talking about in the lanes, after the king. There was a song about it in one of the wine-houses by the second night, not a good one. That was no use. What I wanted was the men he owed."

He had listed them at the inn after the elevation, from four taverns in one night, and he had not written the list down, because he had not needed to. Three of the lending houses. The vintners' company. Two of the old earls. His own steward. A man in the upper market who sold saddles.

"I started with the saddler," Saban said, "because I was lodging over him." Something that was nearly a smile went over his face and did not stay. "He's owed eleven Gold Rand for a hunting saddle and three bridles, four years. He's been owed it so long he's stopped minding, and he tells anybody who'll listen, and I've listened before. I asked him how Lord Grier's account stood. He said it stood paid." Saban turned his head and looked at Emeric for the first time. "Paid in full, in the first week of the new year, in coin, by a man he didn't know, who wanted a receipt in Grier's name and took it away with him. The saddler thought it was the best thing that had happened to him in four years. He'd told the whole street."

The steward, the same. Four years' wages, the suit pending before the Crown's table, withdrawn in the first week of the new year, the wages paid, by a man the steward did not know. The vintners' company, the same. The two old earls would not see Saban, which was only to be expected. But the lending houses kept clerks, and clerks drank, and the clerks of the two houses that had won judgment against Grier before the Crown's table and never been paid drank in the same wine-house under the chancery arches, and one of them had a sister who had married a Kaldo wool factor, and was glad to see a man from the south.

"Both houses sold Grier's paper," Saban said. "In the week after the council's first sitting. Not a quarter of it, not half. All of it, judgment and all. They'd have sold it to anyone for a tenth of its face; they'd written it off years ago in their own books. They were paid its face. Every Copper." He moved one hand on the desk, a very little. "And the third house, the one that hadn't gone to court yet. The same. The same week. Its face."

"Who bought it?"

"The same buyer. All three." Saban was quiet for a moment. "Ducatis."

Nobody said anything. Ferra, who had stayed by the door with the wool book in her arms, did not move.

"That's not strange in itself," Saban said. "Ducatis is the biggest lending house in the kingdom. It buys up paper all the time, bad paper most of all, cheap, and waits, and squeezes, and gets a quarter of it back and calls that profit. If Ducatis had bought Grier's paper for a tenth of its face I'd have thought nothing of it. Ducatis bought it at its face, which nobody does, and then it didn't squeeze." He turned his hand over on the desk, palm up, empty. "It wrote it off. The whole of it. The clerk's cousin is a runner at the Ducatis branch in the capital, the one by the exchange, and she says the paper came in and went straight upstairs and never came down to the floor at all. Straight upstairs to the high desk. And the next thing that came down was an order to send Lord Grier a quittance, in full, sealed, by the House's own rider." He closed the hand. "A quittance in full. That's a House telling a man, in writing, that he owes it nothing and never did. Between the council's first sitting and the vote. Grier had it in his hand a week before he said aye."

"Upstairs," Emeric said.

"The high desk. At the capital branch the high desk is the book for the crown's paper and the high houses. The lords' accounts. The Crown's own. It isn't touched by the floor clerks." Saban looked at him, and it was the look from the physician's cart again. "It's held by the eldest son. It always has been. You know that. You had Keller copy the filing."

Emeric knew that.

He had known it for two years and more: a filing in the judiciary, three children of the House, and against the eldest son's name the book he held, the crown's paper, and the high houses'. Against the second son's, the trade and the coast and the shipping. And against the daughter's name, where her brothers had their books, nothing at all.

"I can't prove it," Saban said. "Not from where I stand. I know the paper was bought, and I know it was written off, and I know Grier had a quittance in his hand and turned. I know it went through the high desk. That's four taverns and a runner's cousin. It won't stand up anywhere. To stand up it would have to be the book itself, the line in it, the day and the sum. And the House's books are the House's. No court in the realm can open them without the House's leave, and the House won't give leave, and I can't get into the room." He took his hands off the desk at last and put them in his lap. "And even if I could. Even if the line was in front of me. It would only say the House bought a lord's paper and forgave it. A House can forgive whom it likes. It wouldn't say why."

"It would say who paid the House," said Ferra, from the door.

Saban looked at her.

"Nobody buys three houses' paper at face and forgives it," Ferra said. "Not even Ducatis. Not for a man like Grier. Somebody paid the House to do it, or the House did it for somebody it wanted to please, and either way there's a line on the other side of the page. There always is. A book balances." She shifted the wool book in her arms. "Find what came into the high desk the same week, and from whom. That's your why."

"And that's the book I can't get into," Saban said.

Emeric sat on the corner of the desk and looked at the two of them, and at the high window with the dark in it, and at nothing.

He knew one person who could get into it.

He wrote to her that night, by the House's own courier, who was lodged at the inn by the north gate as he always was between quarter days, because the House kept a man in Kaldo now the way it kept a room at the junction in Wayford. It was not a quarter day. It was six weeks off one. He wrote four words, because he found he could not write more without writing the thing he could not write, and signed it with his initial.

Please come. Not business. E.

She came in three days, on a Tuesday, alone, in the House's small carriage with no clerk and no courier, the way she had come once before, on a Tuesday, in the rain. She came up to the table room in her plain dark travelling coat and her gloves, with no book under her arm, which he had never seen, and sat down across the table from him without being asked, and took her gloves off, and laid them together on the table, and looked at him.

"Your grace," she said.

He had asked Ferra to stay, and Saban. He had thought about it for most of the three days and decided that he would not do this alone in a room with her, because then it would be a thing between the two of them, and it was not; it was a thing between Kaldo and her House, and she would want it said in front of witnesses who kept books. Ferra sat at the end of the table with hers shut in front of her. Saban sat beside Emeric with nothing.

It was not a quarter day, and the guest rooms had not been aired, and she had said at the door that the carriage would wait. Emeric had lain awake over that and been glad of it. The last time she had slept under this roof, her window had lit in the minute after the dome let go; and there was an old man under the glass now, drawing on it day and night, that nobody outside this table knew was there.

Emeric told her.

He told her the way Saban had told him, in order, and he did not soften any of it, because she would have known if he had, and would not have forgiven it. The saddler. The steward. The two houses that had won judgment and been paid at face. The third. Ducatis. The high desk. The quittance in full by the House's own rider, a week before the vote. Grier, pale, saying aye before his name was finished. One voice. The king.

She listened to all of it without moving. She had her hands folded on the table in front of her, on nothing, where her book should have been. Her face did not change at any point, and it was a face he had watched across a loan table for seven years, and he knew what it looked like when it was keeping still on purpose.

When he got to the high desk, her hands tightened on each other. That was all.

When he had finished, she did not say anything for a long time.

"You know whose book the high desk is," she said at last.

"Yes."

"You've known since the winter before last." It was not a question. "The filing. You told me. Three children, and a book against each of the sons' names." She looked down at her folded hands. "And you've sent for me, not on a quarter day, to tell me that my eldest brother's book bought a vote against the king."

"Saban thinks it did. He can't prove it."

"No. He can't." She said it without any weight on it, as a figure. "Nobody can prove it without the book, and nobody can see the book but the House." She lifted her eyes to his. "And you've asked me here because I'm the House."

He did not answer that. He could not think of an answer that was not either a lie or cruel, and he would not give her either.

She looked at him a moment longer, and then past him, at the window, where the snow was coming down past the lamps in the court, slowly, in the windless dark.

He could see it on her. He could see her doing the sum, because he had watched her do sums across a table for seven years, and she did them the way Ferra did, all at once and then again, slowly, to be sure.

The proof of the bribe was the proof that her House's crown book had bought a vote against the king. There was no way to hold the one without holding the other. And held in the right hand, at the right table, it was the thing she had never had and could never otherwise get: a line in her brother's own book, in his own clerk's hand, that showed the man who held the crown's paper had spent the House's money buying a lord's vote to put the king in the Church's keeping. Laid in front of the partners, and the old man at the coast house who listened and never said anything, it would end her brother. It would end him as the eldest son, as the holder of the high desk, as anything but a man who had done a thing a House does not survive. It was the only weapon anybody in her family had ever been handed against him, and it had been handed to her.

And it was the House. It was her House's name on a line that bought a verdict against the sovereign. The day it came out, in any court, in any hall, the House of Ducatis was the house that had bought the king's fall, and every noble house in the capital with paper at the high desk would take it away by the spring, and the crown's paper with it, and there would be no high desk left for anyone to hold, her brother or her.

It was a succession weapon, and it was the end of the thing she would succeed to. The same line did both.

She sat with her hands folded on the empty table and did the sum, and he watched her, and did not help. He did not know how.

"I'll give you the route," she said.

She said it quite suddenly, when the silence had gone on long enough that Saban had begun, very slightly, to shift in his chair. Her voice was the voice she used for the price of wool, dry and easy and even.

"Not the book. Nobody takes the book out of the House. The route to the line in it." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table, the way Saban had laid his on his desk. "The high desk is copied every week for the coast house. Every line in it. The copy goes on the Thursday tide in a locked case, and it's read aloud at the coast house at the old man's table, the way all the House's books are read. It's copied on the Wednesday night, at the capital branch, in the room behind the high desk, by one clerk, who has copied it for thirty years and never been wrong. He lets a partner's factor sit with him on the Wednesday if the factor's own house's account is being drawn in the same sitting, so the factor can see his own lines go into the copy. It's a courtesy. It's older than the House's charter." She looked at Saban. "Kaldo's paper is held at the capital branch. Kaldo is a partner's account. Mine. Its factor is entitled to sit with the copyist while Kaldo's lines are drawn, on any Wednesday, and nobody will think anything of it, because nobody ever has. The factor sits at the copyist's elbow for an hour. The book is open on the desk. And the copyist carries the quarter's totals forward every Wednesday from the quarter's first page, because that's how the coast house wants them, so every page of the quarter so far goes past under his hand before he writes a line of the week's."

"And a factor who can read a page once," said Saban slowly, "and keep it."

"I don't know anything about that," Vantic said. "I don't want to." She did not look at him. "I'll give you a letter to the branch, in my hand, naming you my factor for the Kaldo account for one sitting. I've never named a factor for it. I've drawn it myself for seven years. It'll be noticed, and it'll be noticed as a thing I did, and that's the price of it to me, and I'll pay it." She turned back to Emeric. "That's the route. Whatever's in this quarter's pages, you'll have seen it. What came into the high desk and from whom. After the spring quarter day the quarter's closed and the pages go to the coast, and then I can't help you, and I won't try again."

"Thank you," Emeric said.

"Don't." She said it sharply, and then, at once, in the plain voice, the voice he had heard once before in this room, on a Tuesday, in the rain: "I haven't said the rest."

He waited.

"I've come to this table on every quarter day for seven years," she said. "I've priced you and refused you and lent to you and refused you again. I've never once asked Kaldo for a thing that was mine. The books you gave me were for the House. Everything I've ever asked you for was for the House." She held his eyes. "This is mine. This is my term. The first I've ever set you."

"Name it."

"It never goes to any court." She said it clearly, once, the way she said a figure she would not move. "Not the line, not the copy, not anything you take from the copy. Not to the council. Not to the Crown's table. Not to Keller, however much he'd like it. Not in any hall in this kingdom, ever, in any form, by you or anyone you send. Whatever you use it for, you don't use it there." She paused. "And the House hears it first. Not from you. From me. At my next Reading, in front of the partners and in front of him, in my own words, as the worst numbers my book has ever held."

Ferra, at the end of the table, had her pencil in her hand. She did not open her book.

"The partners audit each of us in front of him," Vantic said. "You know that. I told you. Every few years. And he listens, and he doesn't say anything, and there's only one thing he ever asks." She did not look away. "Whether we'll say it went badly before anyone makes us. That's the whole of the Reading. It's not whether the numbers are good. Any of us can bring good numbers. It's whether a child of the House will stand up and say the numbers are bad before the partners find it out for themselves. My brothers have never once had to. Their numbers are never bad." She laid her hand flat on the table, where her sheet had lain once, folded in the back of a book. "These are the House's worst numbers. In my brother's book. Bought a vote against the king. I'll say so. I'll stand up at the Reading and say it before any partner can find it, and before any court can make me, and before you or anybody can use it to make me. I'll say it went badly." Her voice did not change at all. "That's my term. It's the only way I can hold it and still have a House to hold it in."

Nobody said anything.

Emeric looked at her across the table, and thought about the card she had just put into his hand and then taken most of the way back out of it, and found that he did not mind at all, because he had never been going to play it. He had known that since Saban said Ducatis in the counting room with his hands flat on the desk. He did not want to win anything more in a court. Kaldo's honest paper was in a court already, laid out in three rows on the Crown's table, and it had put his father in the Church's keeping. He would not lay another honest line on another table and watch what it did.

"Agreed," he said.

She looked at him for a moment longer, as if she were waiting for him to add a term of his own. He did not. She nodded once.

At the end of the table Ferra laid her pencil down beside her book, exactly parallel to it, and did not open the book, and did not write anything, then or after. Emeric saw her not do it. He had seen her not write things down before, perhaps a dozen times in all the years, and every one of them had been a thing that was never going in any book. This was the first time he had seen her decide it while the other person was still in the room.

Vantic wrote the letter to the branch at the table, in her own hand, on Kaldo paper, and signed it with her whole name, which he had never seen her write. She sanded it and folded it and gave it to Saban, not to Emeric, and Saban put it inside his coat.

Then she put her gloves on, one and then the other, and stood.

"You'll want to be quick," she said. "The Reading's in the autumn. I don't know what else will have happened by the autumn. I'd like there to be something left to tell them about."

"We'll be quick."

She went to the door. At the door she stopped, with her hand on the frame, and did not turn round, the way Waylon had not turned round in the long passage with his hand on the frame, and for a moment Emeric thought she was going to say something else.

"It's a strange thing," she said, to the door. "You've spent the whole of this winter learning that honest paper can be used against the man who keeps it. I've spent my whole life learning that it's the only thing I have that can't." She was quiet a moment. "I think we've both been right."

She went out. He heard her on the stair, and then in the court, and then the small carriage going away under the snow toward the north gate, and then nothing.

Nobody at the table said anything for a while.

"Wednesday," Saban said at last. "Any Wednesday before the quarter day." He put his hand on his coat, where the letter was. "That's six weeks. I can be in the capital inside one."

"You'll see the quarter," said Ferra. She was looking at her closed book. "Every line that went into the high desk and every line that went out of it, since the winter quarter day. The quittance will be there, or the sale. So will whatever paid for it. A book balances." She lifted her eyes to Emeric. "But it will be in names. Somebody's man paid in something on a day in the first week of the new year, and the book will give his name and not his master's. You'll need to know whose man he was before you see it, or you won't know it when you do."

"Grier knows who," said Emeric.

Ferra did not answer. She did not need to. She had heard him say the next thing before he said it, and so had Saban, and so, he thought, had Ward, who had been standing by the door the whole time and had not moved or spoken once, and did not now.
