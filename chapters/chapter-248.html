Chapter 248 - The Counting-House

The copyist's name was Tolley, and he was older than Saban had expected, and kinder, and he did not look up once in the whole hour.

The room behind the high desk at the Ducatis branch by the exchange was small and warm and smelled of lamp oil and sealing wax and old paper, and it had one window, shuttered, and one desk, and on the desk under a green-shaded lamp lay the book. It was a great thing, bound in black calf gone brown at the corners, the height of a man's forearm and as thick as his wrist, and it lay open on a slanted board with a ribbon in it, and Tolley sat before it on a tall stool with a worn place on the seat and a clean book beside it for the copy. There was a second stool at his elbow. A factor's stool. Saban sat on it with Vantic's letter lying on the desk in front of him, where Tolley had laid it after reading it twice, and his own hands folded in his lap so that nobody could say afterwards that they had touched anything.

"Kaldo," Tolley had said, reading the letter. "The lady's own account. I've never had a factor for the lady's account. She draws it herself." He had looked at Saban over his spectacles for a moment, mildly, and then folded the letter and laid it down. "Well. It's her account. Sit, Master Saban. You'll want to see your lines go in. They'll come in their order."

And he had begun.

He began, as the lady had said he would, at the quarter's first page. He turned the ribbon back to it, carefully, with the flat of a bone folder, and ran his finger down the column of the first week, and wrote the week's total in the clean book, and turned the page. Then the next. He carried the totals forward, page by page, from the winter quarter day, as the coast house wanted them, so that the old man at the table there could hear the quarter entire each week and not have to add it himself. Every page of the quarter passed under his hand before he came to the week's.

Saban did not read them.

He had learned that at the border ford, in a quartermaster's tent with ten chests and the burning started, with his pulse going like a child's: you did not read. You opened the room, and you built the counting house, the high desk and the stool with the worn place and the ledger shelf and the brass rail for your cuffs, and the lamp last, green over the desk, and then you sat in the green light and waited. You waited the way you wait for a slow clerk to bring a roll up from the cellar. You did not reach for it. It came, and you took it, and looked at it until it was whole, and got down off the stool and put it on the shelf.

It was easy, here. That was the strange thing. It was the easiest it had ever been. The room he built was nearly the room he sat in: a high desk, a worn stool, a green lamp, an old man turning pages. He sat in both of them at once and the pages came to him in both, and in the one he put them on the shelf, and in the other he sat with his hands in his lap and watched Tolley's finger go down the columns, and neither room knew about the other.

The first week of the winter quarter. The second. Crown paper; the treasury's drawings, the chancery's, the household of the king. The high houses, one after another, lords whose names he knew from the benches and whose debts he knew from the taverns. Then the week of the winter sitting, the week the cardinal laid his paper on the Crown's table and the king stood up, and nothing in it but the ordinary business of a House that lent to a kingdom.

Then the week after.

Tolley turned the page. Saban sat in the green light and waited for it, and it came, and it was quite short.

"Two lines," he said.

It was late on the Wednesday night, in the miller's room at the mill on the river, with the fire laid again by Wald and burning, and the shutters shut, and the four of them round it: Emeric, Ward, Rylan, and Saban, who had walked the three miles out from the city in the dark after the sitting because he would not trust it to a cart, and was still in his coat. He sat on the bench where Grier had sat, and held his hands to the fire, and his hands were quite steady and quite warm, and he did not look at any of them. He was looking at the shelf.

"The third day of the new year," he said. "Under the household of the lord prince, the heir's household account, which is crown paper and draws on the high desk and always has. Paid in, by J. Rudd, under-purser, on the household's warrant, in coin and bills on the treasury. A sum." He said the sum. It was a great deal of money. Emeric had not known there was that much loose money in any household in the kingdom, and did not doubt for a moment that Saban had it to the Copper. "And against it, on the same page, the same day, not carried into the household's own account at all but held on the high desk's side, under a mark I didn't know, which is the eldest son's own mark for a thing he keeps in his own hand. Held against purchase."

"And the second line."

"The fourth day of the new year. The high desk's side. Under the same mark. Purchased, of Hendry's, of Cowle's, of Pellam Brothers, the paper of the lord Grier, at face. Three sums, and their total, and the total is the sum Rudd paid in the day before, less the House's fee. Then, under it, the same hand: Written off in full. Quittance by the House's rider." Saban stopped. "That's all. Two lines. The House took the prince's household's money on the third and spent it on the fourth buying up Grier's paper at face and forgiving it. It balances to the Copper, less the fee. The fee's the House's usual."

"Whose hand?"

"The high desk's clerk's. A good hand. A secretary's." Saban's mouth moved. "The same hand, I'd say, near enough, as a note Grier had at his door. I'd want to see the note to swear to it. I've only seen it through you."

Nobody said anything.

"It holds," Saban said. "The proof. It holds. Grier's note, Grier's date, Rudd's face at Grier's door and at the fountain. Rudd's name on the heir's household account paying in, the day before. The high desk buying Grier's paper at face with it, the day after, and forgiving it. It holds all the way from the prince's household to the aye." He looked into the fire. "And there isn't a line in it anywhere with the Church's name on it. Not one. Not in the book, not in the note, not in the quittance. I looked. I went back through the whole quarter on the shelf, twice, while I walked out here. The close doesn't bank at the high desk. It doesn't bank anywhere I can see. It isn't there."

"It never is," said Ward, from the window.

Emeric looked at him. Ward did not turn round.

Emeric sat with it for a long time after the others had gone down to sleep on the sacks below.

The proof held. He had known it would hold since the mill stair, since Grier's shelf and the badge in silver thread under a cloak, and he found now that it gave him nothing at all. He had it. It could never go into any court. He had promised that across his own table, to a woman who had put her name to a letter to get it for him, and he would keep it; and even if he had not promised, there was no court left in the realm to take it to, because the man whose household had paid for Grier's aye was the lieutenant of the realm and kept the courts in his father's name. The proof held and it was useless, and it was the most dangerous thing in the kingdom, and it was loose.

That was the thing he sat with. It was loose.

Not the line. The line was in the House's book and on Saban's shelf and nowhere else, and would stay there. But a proof leaves a wake behind it the way a boat does, and this one had been going through the city for three weeks. A man from Kaldo in the chancery lanes, asking about Grier's debts in four taverns. A saddler telling the whole street his account was paid. A clerk's cousin at the branch by the exchange who had talked to a man from the south about the high desk. Grier, who had not been out in three weeks, going to a vintner's funeral and coming home at dawn in a cart. And today, in the room behind the high desk, the lady Livia's own account drawn for the first time in seven years with a factor at the copyist's elbow; which Tolley would not mention to anyone, and which would be in the week's copy anyway, in Tolley's own careful hand, drawn in the presence of the lady's factor, Master Saban, on its way to the coast house on the Thursday tide.

None of it was proof of anything. All of it was a wake. And the man at the high desk, whose own mark was on the two lines, would see it before the week was out, because it was his book and his House and his sister, and he was very good at the craft.

And then Corbin would know that somebody had been at the thread.

He would not know who. He would not know how. He would know that somebody had been asking about Grier, and he would know that the somebody had come from the south, and he would ask himself the question Emeric had asked himself on the ship: where, in the capital, could a man from Kaldo have found a door?

The palace.

Emeric saw it all at once, the way he sometimes saw a machine: not the parts one after another, but the whole of it moving. The king was in his own rooms in the palace, in the Church's keeping, with two priests at the door. But the palace was a house full of people, and not all of them were the Church's, and not all of them were the lieutenant's. There was a household in grey in it that put every man in the kingdom in his place at every sitting and had no name and no roll. There were the king's own people. There was Kassim. The palace was the one place in the capital where Corbin could not be sure who was holding which door; and the finding said the king was committed to the lords spiritual at such place as they shall appoint, and the lords spiritual had not yet appointed one, because nobody had needed them to.

Corbin would need them to now.

He would move the king. Out of the palace, out of the city, out of any house where a door might be held by somebody who was not his, to a place the lords spiritual would name at once when the lieutenant asked, because they had been waiting to be asked. A Church house. Somewhere with a wall round it and nobody in it but the Church's own. And once the king was inside that wall, nobody from Kaldo or anywhere else would ever reach him again, and nobody would ever need to answer for what happened to him there, because he would be in the keeping of the lords spiritual, for examination, as the council had found.

There would be a road between the palace and the wall. There always was.

He sat by the miller's fire until it was low. Then he went down and woke Ward, and told him, and Ward listened with his eyes shut, lying on his sack, and at the end of it said only, "When?"

"Soon. He'll want it done before the lanes have any more to talk about. A week. Perhaps less."

"Then we go home tonight," Ward said, "and come back ready."

The carrier's bag came into the gatehouse at New Kaldo three days later, with a single letter in it in the cipher that two men knew.

Ward brought it up to the table room unopened and read it there, by the fire, slowly, the way he had always read the king's letters, with his lips moving very slightly on the cipher and his finger under the line. It was the first letter that had come down that road from the king's end since the winter. It was not in the king's words. It was in Kassim's. Emeric could tell that from Ward's face before Ward said anything, because Ward had read the king's letters for thirty years and his face did a particular thing when he read them, and it was not doing it.

"Thornhythe," Ward said.

Emeric knew the name. Everybody did. The mother house of the northern brothers, a day and a half up the north road from the capital, in the hill country, behind a wall that had been built to keep out the old kings' tax-men and had never once been breached by them. The lords spiritual had appointed it on the second day of the week. The king would go on the fifth. By the north road, in a closed carriage, with the Church's own at the door of it.

"Who rides with him?"

Ward read on. "Forty of the lieutenant's household knights. The carriage. A second carriage for the Church." He looked up. "The cardinal goes himself. In the king's carriage. For the honour of it."

Nobody at the table said anything.

Ward went back to the letter. There was a second part to it, below a line, in a different ink, as though Kassim had added it later, after he had sealed the first and opened it again.

"Eighty," Ward said. "Not forty. He's doubled it since. Twenty more of his own, and twenty of Aldane's horse, the day before they go." He was quiet a moment. "Kassim says the lieutenant has had every man in the king's own household questioned. Every one. The grooms, the cooks, the men on the stair." He turned the sheet over, as though there might be more on the back, and there was not. "Kassim says nobody has asked the lady's household anything at all. They set the council hall for the lieutenant's first sitting on the fourth day, the same as they always have. He thanked them for it."

Emeric looked at the letter in Ward's hand.

He never suspects Maeve. It went through him like cold water. Corbin had smelled the thread, and he had done exactly what a clever man does who smells a thread: he had gone looking for it where the king's people were, and doubled the escort, and asked Aldane for horse, and shut every door he could see. And he had walked past the one he could not see, because it had never in his life occurred to him that it was a door. His half-sister in grey at the foot of the gallery stair, who had never once been on anybody's side. She set his hall for him. He thanked her.

"He suspects a rescue," Ferra said.

"He suspects me." Emeric took the letter from Ward and laid it on the table and did not read it; he could not read the cipher. "He suspects the south. He'll have men on every road out of Kaldo, and he'll find nobody on any of them, because nobody's going by road." He looked at the table. "He's doubled it for a company coming up the north road on horses. He hasn't doubled it for anything else, because there isn't anything else he can think of."

Ward went down to the works that night.

Emeric went with him, because Ward asked him to, which Ward had never done about anything that concerned his own kit. They went through the dark yard to the pattern store, where there was a light in the high window as there always was, and Bram was at the long bench with the lamp over him and an injector in pieces on a cloth, and he looked up when they came in, and saw Ward's breastplate under Ward's arm, and put the injector down.

Ward laid the breastplate on the bench in front of him, face up.

It was the same black plate it had been for years, with the lions at the shoulders and nothing on it anywhere to say whose it had ever been; and in the middle of it the heart, behind its pane, where Ward's deciding stone sat alone, with the two brass levers either side. And beside the deciding stone, under the pane, inside the iron, the second seat: a small round socket with a narrow neck from it to the deciding stone, and a door at the breast that opened by hand, and in the socket, where a stone would go, a plug of iron screwed down tight and wired, so that nothing could go in by accident. It had been there since the summer Emeric reworked the suits. Ward had looked at it for a long time that day, and said that'll be a while, and put his hand on the left lever, not to move it, only to know where it was, and said he would keep them for the while.

He put his hand on the left lever now, the same way, only to know where it was. Then he took it off.

"Take them out," he said.

Bram looked at him. Then he looked at the plate, at the capped seat and the levers, and then at Ward's left wrist, where the band sat, as it always sat: a band of iron with a stone set in it, one of the six, closed round his wrist with a clasp.

"It won't go in as a stone," Bram said. "The seat's for a stone. That's a band."

"I know."

"They're one piece. The stone's grown into the iron. You'd not get it out of the band without breaking one or the other." Bram said it flatly, as a fact about metal. "The master got his out with a penknife, and tore the seat doing it, and he'd nothing left to lose. You have." "So the seat takes the band. Clasp and all. Round, not a socket; a cradle, with a gate over it, so it goes in on your wrist and comes out on your wrist." He was already looking at the plate as though it were a drawing. "And the neck to the deciding stone wants to come round to meet it where it sits. And the door wants cutting bigger." He put his hand flat on the plate. "Tonight?"

"Tonight."

Bram did not ask why tonight. He did not ask anything. He looked at Ward for a moment longer across the bench, and then he pulled the lamp down on its chain, and took up a turnscrew, and began.

It took him until past midnight. Emeric sat on a stool by the forge and watched, and did not help, because Bram did not want help with a plate he had made, and because he found that he wanted to watch it done. The wire came off the capped seat. The plug came out, and Bram held it up to the lamp for a moment, a small plain turned thing, and then put it in his apron pocket instead of on the bench. The levers came out one after the other, with their linkages, and lay on the cloth beside the injector, two brass levers with the brass worn bright at the grips where Ward's hands had been on them for years. Then Bram cut. He cut the door wider, and filed it, and fitted a cradle of iron into the seat where the socket had been, a curved bed the shape of the inside of a wrist, with a hinged gate over it that closed with a catch; and he brought the neck round from the deciding stone to meet the cradle, and tried it with the master's band from the drawer, the one with the torn seat and nothing in it, and adjusted it, and tried it again.

Ward stood at the bench the whole time and did not sit down.

When it was done Bram put the pane back, and wiped the plate with a rag, and turned it round on the bench to face Ward, and stood back.

Ward put the breastplate on. Bram did the straps; Ward let him. Then Ward opened the door at the breast, and put his left forearm across his chest, and laid his wrist in the cradle, band and clasp and all, so that the stone in the band sat against the neck and the neck ran to the deciding stone; and closed the gate over it, and the catch went home with a small clean sound.

He stood there with his arm across his chest like a man with his hand on his heart.

Emeric felt him go in. It was very small, and very quick: a weight in the room that had not been there, and then was, a few paces off, like a door opening in the next room of a house. Ward's eyes were open. He was not looking at anything. Under the pane the deciding stone did something that Emeric felt rather than saw, the way you feel a horse gather under you before it moves.

Ward came up off the floor of the pattern store the width of a hand, and stayed there, level, without a lever anywhere on him.

Then he came down again, gently, onto both feet, and opened the gate, and took his wrist out of the cradle, and stood looking at the plate for a while.

"For the while," he said.

That was all he said about it, then or ever. Bram swept the two brass levers off the cloth into a drawer, with the plug, and shut it, and went back to his injector.

The last thing Emeric did before they went was stand at the glass.

He had done it every night for five weeks, at the end of the night, with the lamps out and the screens up. He did it now with his coat on and the others already going down to the lake. Inside, Imani sat on her stool at the old man's head, awake, with her band on her wrist and the tree's faint light on her hands.

He opened his sense to the dome and felt for the draw.

It was there. It had been there every night, slow and patient, running down out of the apex toward the pallet by the middle bed like water toward dry ground. Tonight it was slower. Not much. Enough. The hollow round the old man was shallower than it had been, the way a dip in a sheet eases when the weight on it lightens; and when Emeric looked at Ferra's column in the gauge book on the shelf, the column agreed with him. Four cartridges a day for a month. Three, last week. Two, since Sunday.

It was the first sign of anything. He did not know what it was a sign of.

He tapped on the glass, once, and Imani looked up. He put his hand flat on the panel, and then pointed down, at the pallet, and made the small easing gesture a man makes over a gauge that is falling slowly and in the right direction. She looked at the old man for a long moment, and then back at Emeric, and nodded, once.

He had told her already, at the table, that whatever happened she was to send nothing north. Not a rider, not a letter, not a lamp from the ridge. If the old man woke, he would still be awake in three days. If he did not, there was nothing a man on the north road could do about it.

He went down to the lake.

They went up from the lake on the night before the fifth day, in the dark, with the moon three days off new.

The Discovery carried nine of them: Emeric, and Ward and Osric and Rylan, in their suits; the four of Rylan's men from the mill; and Saban, because Saban knew the north road from the capital to Thornhythe from a wool buyer's saddle, every mile of it, and had put it on the shelf. Fletcher flew her, because Osric was wanted on the road. She would not go near the road any more than she had gone near the city. She would put them down at night in the hills a long way off it, on the far side of a ridge, and go back up into the band, and wait, and come for them when Ward's lamp told her to.

The ship was only the way to the road. The road was theirs, and they would take it on the ground.

In the long cabin, going up through the cloud with the frost beginning on the glass, Emeric sat in his coat with his hands between his knees. At his left wrist, under the cuff, on its cord, the bead lay against the skin where it had lain since the winter of the Wayford net: round, a little smaller than a knuckle, smooth and grey, with the names of fifteen knots folded away inside it and whatever it had been before the forge folded away under those. It had been his mother's once, in a comb case in a drawer in his father's treasury. It had been the root. He had made it into something else with his own hands before he knew what it was, and put it on a cord, and never once taken it off.

Nobody looked at it. Nobody ever did. It was a thing he wore. He sat with the frost growing on the window beside him and his mother's stone in his coat and her other stone on his wrist, and the Discovery climbed into the band, and turned north.
