Chapter 248 - Winter Garden

Emeric came into bay three a little after the bell with a board under one arm and a wooden box under the other, and put both down on her bench on top of the carbide drawings without asking, which meant it was going to be a long morning.

The board was a plank of deal with brass screws along it. On it, screwed down, a cell from the battery room, a small lamp in a porcelain cup, and a switch, the plain kind they put in the new houses on the west side: a brass lever on a slate base.

"What's that," he said.

Tess looked at it. Then at him.

"A switch."

"What does it do?"

"If this is the lesson, I'll go and weld something." She reached out and pushed the lever over. The lamp lit. She pushed it back. The lamp went out. "It does that."

"How many things can it say?"

She didn't answer straight away, because he had asked it the way he asked when the answer was small and the thing behind it wasn't. Two. On and off. A switch didn't know half. You couldn't push a switch a little and get a little light; that was what the rheostat in the station was for, and it was a different animal. A switch said yes, or it said no.

She said so.

"Yes or no," he said. "True or false. Some people write it one and nothing." He drew on her chalk slate, a stroke and a ring: 1 and 0. "Same thing. It's just a way of writing down whether the light's on."

"All right." She waited. He didn't go on. He opened the box.

Inside were more switches, a coil of the waxed wire Abloh's room made for the line, and a second lamp, and on top of them, wrapped in a rag, a relay out of one of the boxes on the telegraph road, the brass kind they'd taken out when the grey went in. She knew it by the scratch on its base. She had put the scratch there.

He gave her one more switch, and the wire, and sat down on Bram's stool, and said nothing at all.

So she put the second switch on the board in a line with the first, the current going through one and then the other on its way to the lamp, the way you'd put two cocks on one pipe. She knew before she touched them what it would do, and she did it anyway, all four ways, because knowing and seeing weren't the same and Bram had hit that into her at twelve. Both off: dark. The first on: dark. The second on: dark. Both on: the lamp.

"Both," she said.

Then she undid it and put them side by side instead, two pipes into one tank, and ran it the four ways again. Only both off left it dark.

"Either."

She had written them down without thinking about it, two columns of strokes and rings and a third column for the lamp, four rows each, because that was how she logged the hydrants: every valve, every way it could be set, and what the far gauge read. When she looked up he was looking at the slate, not at her.

"That's called a truth table," he said. "The first one's AND. The second's OR."

"Those aren't names. Those are words."

"They're both."

She looked at the board. The lamp sat in its porcelain cup, lit, because she'd left both levers over. And she thought, the lamp's deciding, and then at once, no, it isn't. The lamp didn't decide anything. It lit when it was told to. The wiring was what decided, and it decided the same way every time, and it decided without anybody standing there.

"If this and this," she said slowly, touching the levers, "then the light."

"Say the first word again."

"If." She frowned at him. "What?"

He wrote it on the slate, IF, under the table, and didn't explain it, and she let it sit there because she could feel it was bigger than she had time for.

"What about one or the other but not both," she said, mostly to herself, and then answered it before he could, because she'd already built it and forgotten she had. "The back stair at the hospital. Imani's night nurses wanted the light from the top and from the bottom, so there's a switch at each end, and whichever you throw, it changes. If they're the same, it's dark. If they're different, it's lit." She stopped. "That's a question too. Are they different."

"Exclusive or," said Emeric. "XOR."

"That's a terrible name."

"I didn't pick it."

"Who did?"

He didn't answer that, which was his way, and she'd decided long ago it was out of one of Armand's books and that it didn't matter.

The thing that did bother her was the other way round. A lamp that was lit when the lever was off. She turned the switch over in her hand. You couldn't make a switch say the opposite of itself. You could wire it backwards, but backwards was still the same question.

She picked up the relay.

She had it on the board in a minute, the switch working its coil, the lamp on its contacts, and when she put the last screw down the lamp came on, with nothing pressed, and she said a word Bram would have fined her for and started looking for her short.

"Look where you put the lamp," Emeric said.

She'd put it on the back contact, the one the spring held closed while the coil was dead. She threw the switch; the coil pulled the arm over; the lamp went out.

She sat back.

What held her was the relay itself, a lever moved by current instead of a hand. Which meant one answer could throw the next question. Which meant you could hang one of these off another, and another off that, and nobody had to stand at the board at all.

"How many of these," she said, "to make something that does anything?"

"More than you'd think."

"A thousand?"

"More than that."

She looked at the one relay in her hand, which had taken a man a week to wind, and felt the size of it, the way she'd felt the size of the line road the first time she walked it with a bag of insulators. It could be walked. It would take years.

She tried the adding on her own while he was still sitting there, because she wanted something to come out of the morning that was hers. One lamp for the answer, one switch for each number. Nought and nought, dark. One and nought, lit. Nought and one, lit. One and one: dark. That was the stair switch exactly, and it was wrong. One and one was two.

"It's broken," she said.

"Is it?"

"The lamp's out. One and one isn't nothing."

"How do you write two with one lamp?"

She opened her mouth and shut it.

That was where Osric came in with the basket, because it was noon and Maren had packed it for two since the war and there was no use either of them pretending she hadn't. He put it down on the end of the bench where the carbide drawings weren't, and looked at the board with the two lamps and the relay, and asked what it was.

"Questions," said Tess. "Yes or no. This one's both or nothing."

"Like the gate," said Osric, unwrapping the bread. "On the wall. Friend or not, and the word or not. If he's a friend and he's got the word, he comes in. Friend with no word, he waits. Word with no face, you call the sergeant." He tore the loaf. "Both, or the gate stays shut."

Tess looked at him.

"Say that again."

He said it again, slower, the way he'd said it to every new boy on the wall for ten years, and she wrote it on the slate in her square hand, Osric's gate, beside the table, because it was a better way to say it than any she'd had.

And then she saw what the dark lamp had been trying to tell her. One and one put the first lamp out, and that was right, because the first lamp was the ones. What it needed was somebody to say that both had been on. Both, or nothing. Osric's gate. A second lamp, beside the first, that only lit when both were on.

Two lamps. Counting in twos, where everybody she knew counted on their fingers.

She didn't know yet how far that went. She could feel that it went a long way.

"Binary," said Emeric, getting up off Bram's stool, and wrote it at the bottom of the slate. "That's tomorrow."

"Can it remember?" she said, before he got to the door. "If it can only answer what you're asking it now, it can't do anything for Abel. A hand has to know what it was doing a moment ago."

He stopped. It was the face he made when somebody got somewhere before he'd meant to take them.

"Next month," he said.

When he'd gone Osric gave her the heel of the loaf, because she liked the heel, and sat on the stool he'd left warm, and ate, and didn't ask what any of it was for. She wrote ABEL in the margin of the slate, small, and then, under the truth table, a fifth row nobody had asked for, with a question mark in the lamp's column.



Osric had a speech.

He had been polishing it for a week. Everybody at the long table knew he had, because he had been seen polishing it: on the wall at night, moving his lips; in the yard, between drills, standing by the water butt with his eyes on nothing; and once, by Declan, who said so to nobody but Rylan, who said so to everybody, in the stable, to a horse.

He stood up with it that evening, at supper, after the soup and before the mutton, with his cup in his hand.

The long room was full, as it was full most evenings that spring, because the dome was going up in the ward below its windows and everybody who had anything to do with it ate at the long table so as to go on arguing about it afterward: the six, and the knights, and Tess at the table's end in her chair beside Osric's place, and Maren in and out with the dishes, and Saban, and Rylan with his feet on the rung of Declan's chair. Osric stood up among them with his cup, and the table, which had been arguing about glass, stopped.

"I've something to say," said Osric. "To all of you. As you're all here." He cleared his throat. "As you know, I've served in this castle since before His Grace came south, and in that time I've come to have the greatest respect for every person at this table, and for one of them in particular, whose skill and whose courage and whose..."

"Sit down," said Tess.

Osric looked down at her.

"Sit down," said Tess, "before you get to the part about the stars. I've heard you practising the part about the stars through the wall of bay three for four days." She put her own cup down on the table, and looked down the length of it, at all of them, in her flat measuring voice. "We're walking out. Properly. Since February." She picked her cup up again. "That's all."

Osric sat down.

Nobody looked up.

That was what he would remember afterward, Emeric thought, watching Osric's face: not that nobody laughed, but that nobody looked up. Bram went on cutting his bread. Armand went on looking at the fire. Ferra went on reading a list beside her plate. Down the table, the knights went on eating their soup, every one of them, with tremendous concentration.

"Since the fire drills," said Ward, from the door, without turning his head.

Osric opened his mouth.

"She checked your suit," said Emeric, mildly, to his own plate, "in the middle of the works, the morning we tested them, with forty men watching. She didn't check anybody else's. She came across the whole yard on the motor to do it."

"Nobody carries a drafting board for a woman by accident," said Imani. "Up four flights of the hospital stair. In the war. Twice."

Maren put the mutton down in front of Osric, and then in front of Tess, and stood back with the dish on her hip.

"I've packed his dinner for two since the war," said Maren. "Every day. Bread and cheese and an apple each, and the heel of the loaf on her side, because she likes the heel. Nobody ever told me to." She looked at Osric. "Nobody told me to stop, either."

Declan said nothing at all. From Declan, at that table, it was a speech.

"We were careful," said Osric, faintly.

"He was careful," said Tess.

Rylan took his feet off the rung of Declan's chair and sat up.

"Saban," he said. "The book."

Saban sighed, and reached inside his coat, and brought out a small fat notebook with a strap round it, and undid the strap.

"There's been a pool," said Rylan to the table, cheerfully, "since the suction pads. Since the falls off the keep wall. When they'd say it out loud, in front of everybody. Everybody in the garrison's in it, and half the works. A copper a guess. Saban's held the stakes." He looked at Osric with great affection. "Two hundred and eight coppers, last count."

"Two hundred and twelve," said Saban, turning pages. "There were four more after the carbide." He ran his finger down a column. "Nobody had this week. Nobody had this month. Most of the garrison had last summer, and lost. The works had the war, and lost. Your cook," he looked up at Maren, "had midwinter, and lost." He found the line. "One guess for this week. This evening, in fact. At supper. Paid in on the twenty-third of February."

The table looked at him.

"Whose?" said Rylan.

Saban looked at the line a while longer. Then he looked up the table, mildly, at the master smith, who was still cutting his bread.

"Master Bram's," he said.

Bram buttered his bread.

"Paid in," said Saban, "at four in the afternoon. On the twenty-third. Of February." He did not look at Osric. He did not need to; everybody at the table who had been in bay three on the twenty-third of February, at about half past three, with the carbide going, was already not looking at Osric. "A remarkable guess."

"I've known her a long time," said Bram, and ate his bread.

Ferra laughed.

She laughed harder than anybody, and longer, with her hand flat on the table; and when she could, she put down her list and explained to Osric and Tess, at length, kindly, with examples, exactly how a secret was actually kept. You did not change what you did. You did not stand by a door in the snow with your hands behind you. You did not carry anything up any stairs. You kept to your routine, and you let people see exactly what they had always seen, and you never, ever let anything show on your face at a table. She went on for some time. She was very good on the subject. Imani, beside her, listened to about half of it, and then got up from the table quite suddenly and went out of the room, and was heard on the landing, for some while, making a sound like a cough that went on.

Bram looked at his younger daughter down the table for a long breath, and then at his elder, who was still explaining; and then went back to his soup, which he had not yet finished, though the mutton had come.

Emeric kept his face perfectly straight. It was the hardest thing he had done that year.

Osric sat beside Tess with his ears the colour of the carbide furnace's door and his speech in his pocket, and when the table had gone back to the glass, and the mutton, and Rylan was collecting his stake from nobody, he leaned over to Tess, under the noise, so that only she would hear; and Emeric, two places down, heard it anyway, because he had his sense open and Osric's note was louder than his voice.

"You know you could do better," said Osric. "Than me. You know that. Everybody at this table knows it. The best head in the works, and a garrison knight who learned his letters at twenty-eight off you, on a slate, in the dinner hour." He turned his cup on the table. "You could do a great deal better."

Tess did not look at him. She cut her mutton.

"I could do a great many things," she said. "I'm not doing them. I'm eating my dinner." She put a piece of mutton in her mouth, and chewed it, and swallowed it, and then turned her head and looked at him, at last, in the measuring way. "You taught me to throw a rope. I taught you to read. Nobody's keeping count of that but you." She went back to her plate. "Stop keeping it."

Osric looked at her for a while.

Then he stopped keeping it. Emeric felt it go, in the note: something loosened, like a knot let out of a rope. Osric picked up his knife and fork and began, at last, to eat his mutton, and did not say anything else all evening, and did not need to.



The dome went up in the inner ward in three weeks of March, in the east half, where the herb beds had been since before the keep had a duke.

It had been meant to be stone. In the plans for the new city that Linus had drawn and Ferra had costed in the autumn, the middle of the inner ward was to be a round hall one day, a rotunda, with a dome of stone on it like the domes of the capital's great church, at the centre of everything; and its ring of footings had been laid out in pegs and string in October. He took the pegs up in February. Not the plan. The plan stood; the stone dome would come, one day, bigger, over everything. But the thing that went up first in the middle of the pegs was not stone.

It was a ring of steel on the ground, from Bram's works, fifty feet across, bedded in mortar on the old herb beds' stone edging; and on the ring, a dome of glass.

Not glass panes in a frame of timber, like the glasshouse at the ice works. A dome made of triangles. He had drawn it in January on one sheet, and Bram had turned it round twice on the table and then asked how a man was meant to build a thing that was all corners; and he had shown Bram how: that every triangle in it was one of three shapes, no more, and every strut was one of three lengths, and every joint where the struts met was the same iron hub with the same bolts, five arms or six. You built it from the ring up, a row of triangles at a time, each one leaning on the ones below it, and it held itself up as it went, without a scaffold, because a triangle could not be pushed out of its shape without breaking one of its sides. And when the last triangle went in at the top, the whole thing was so strong that Bram could stand on it, and did, at the top, on the last morning, in his apron, with the town looking up at him from the lane through the gate.

Geodesic. He did not say it. He called it the glass room, as Ferra had, and the town called it the winter garden from the second week, because it was in a garden and it was glass and it was winter, and that name stuck.

Every triangle was glass. Ivo's glass, poured and fused flat in the works' annealing oven, the same glass as the forge's tube and the vials and the flasks, because glass held mana and nothing else on the ground did. Each triangle sat in the iron frame on a bead of soft pitch, and was clamped down by an iron strip over its edges, and was sealed over the strip with more pitch, until the whole dome was a single closed skin from the ring to the top hub, with nothing in it that could let what was inside get out.

At the entrance, on the side toward the keep, there was not a door but two. A little iron room built out from the ring, a box the size of a privy, with a door in its outer wall and another in its inner wall, and a rule, painted on the outer door in Ferra's square capitals: ONE AT A TIME. Nobody would walk into the dome from the ward with the air inside it what it was going to be. You went into the box, and shut the outer door behind you, and stood there while the air in the box became the dome's air, and then you opened the inner door. And on the way out, the same, the other way. A pass-box. Bram had made it from the drawing of the ship's hatch.

And through the ring, low, on the north side, an iron collar, a thick iron sleeve with a threaded mouth outside and a valve inside, that a cartridge from the vial rig could be screwed into from the ward; and when it was screwed in and the valve was turned, what was in the cartridge went into the dome, and stayed there.

Imani's beds went in the last week. Herbs: the ward's feverfew and its comfrey and its foxglove, a bed of each, and others she would not name to anybody yet; she had asked for the beds before he had finished drawing the dome, and had said, when he told her what would grow in that air, that she knew perfectly well that anything grown in it would be mana medicine and not medicine, and that she would not dose anybody with a leaf of it until she knew what it did, and that the only way to know was to grow it. So the beds were true. If anybody in the town asked what the glass room in the castle ward was for, it was Imani's garden, and they could go and look at her foxglove through the glass, and it was.

The screens went up round it on the last day: tall frames of canvas on poles, painted grey, standing round the dome a few feet off its glass, which could be drawn across at night on rails so that nothing inside the dome could ever be seen from the keep's windows or the lane. The town thought they were for the wind.



Armand went up for the number on the twenty-eighth.

He went up in the Hod, at night, from the reeds at the lake's far end, with Ned at the helm and Imani counting the hours; and he went up because he would not let anybody else do it.

"It's a reading," he said, in the long room, when Emeric had asked him not to, and Imani had asked him not to, and Ferra had looked at him and said nothing. "One reading. Of how thick the Band is, at its middle, by a man who has read the thickness of mana for sixty years with his own sense and knows what he's feeling. You can't do it. You've the sense, but you've no measure; you've never felt the Band against anything but itself. I've felt the tower's deep room, and the capital's high altar on a feast day, and the old mine at Grayrock with the shaft open. I can tell you what the Band is against all of them." He folded his hands on the blue book. "That's the number the tree wants. And it's mine to give."

So he went up. Three hours, Imani's cap, with his old hand flat on the Hod's open hatch at the top of the climb and his eyes shut and his lips moving; and he came down at four in the morning grey and shaking and very pleased with himself, and was carried up the keep stair, and before he would let anybody put him to bed, he made Ferra bring her gauge book.

He gave her the number. It was not a number anybody else in the world could have given her, because it was not a reading off a gauge; it was a man's sense of the Band's thickness, put against a scale that existed only in his own head, of every thick place he had ever stood in, and turned into a figure on Ferra's ruled page by an argument between the two of them that lasted twenty minutes, with Armand in his chair in his coat and Ferra on the stool beside him with her pencil, and Imani at the door trying to get them both to stop.

At the end of it Ferra drew a line across the page, in ink, at the figure, and wrote a word beside it.

DOME.

"There," said Armand. "That's the line. Not a hair over it. Your Vey's jar was a warning, and I'm too old to be warned twice." He looked at Emeric. "You'll want to go over it. To see it go faster. You'll stand in that ward with a cartridge in your hand and the seed in the earth and you'll want to pour."

"I know."

"Then she'll stop you." Armand nodded at Ferra. "That's why it's her line, and not mine. I'll be dead. She'll still be here with her book."

Ferra said nothing. She looked at the line, and capped her ink.

Ferra's line, the long room called it from then on. Armand had meant it to be called that, and nobody ever called it anything else.



He went up to Milford on the sixth of April, on the morning train, to bring the first of the trainees down.

Grayrock's had come down the week before, by the ore train, two wagons of them, and been housed: thirty-one men and women from Bryda's list, smiths and masons and a pump man and two nurses and a teacher, and at their head, with her own carpet bag and a box of ruled books, Ilsa Renner, with ten clerks of her own choosing, who were to learn Ferra's methods in Ferra's counting room for a year and go home and teach them in Grayrock's pit office; Ilsa had gone straight up the hill to the counting room without unpacking, and Ferra had put her at the side table and given her a column to check before she had taken her coat off, which Ilsa had said afterward was the warmest welcome she had ever had.

Milford's list was longer, and Paxton had asked for somebody from the castle to fetch them, because, he wrote, half of them had never been on a train, and the other half had only ridden on the grain wagons, and he wanted them to arrive believing they were wanted.

So Emeric went. And Rylan came with him, because Rylan had been asked by the new class for three weeks to go to Milford and look at something, and Rylan would not say what.

They came into Milford halt at nine. The halt was a platform of sleepers and a goods shed and a water tower, and a lane going up from it between hedges toward the village and the bench fields above; and above the bench fields, on the upland, where the ground rose toward the grey hills and the wind came over them off the west all year, there were wind pumps.

Six of them. Lattice towers of iron strap, bolted, the height of three men, each with a wheel at its top of thin iron blades set at a slant, like the sails of a little mill, and a vane behind the wheel to keep it facing the wind; and under each tower, a pump, and a trough, and a pipe going off downhill under the turf toward the cattle grounds and the dairy. They had gone up in March, by Bram's drawings and Paskin's footings and Milford's own smiths, the first of Milford's wind; the first of a great many, if Emeric had his way, and he meant to. Five of them were turning. The sixth stood still against the sky, its blades motionless, while the five round it whirred.

Hollis was in the field below them.

He was in the strip at the top of the bench fields, the one nearest the halt, with a seed bag over his shoulder and his old felt hat on and his trousers rolled to the knee, walking a furrow; and every second step he put his hand in the bag and took out a kernel of something gold and pushed it into the ridge with his thumb, and covered it with the side of his boot, and went on. Behind him, two young men in shirtsleeves were doing the same in the next two furrows, much worse.

The island corn. Zale's, from the second chest, half of it, from the sack in the dry store; and in the strip beside it, the beans that would not cook soft.

Hollis saw them coming up the lane and did not stop. He finished his furrow to its end, and turned, and then stood with the bag over his shoulder and watched them come.

"You've come to look at my strip," he said.

"I've come for Paxton's trainees."

"They're at the inn, being fed. Paxton's been feeding them since six." Hollis looked at the strip. "It's early. It's a fortnight early. I'd not put that seed in for another fortnight if it were mine and I knew it, because it comes from a hot country and the ground's cold yet. But it's not mine, and I don't know it." He put his hand in the bag and looked at what came out. "So I'm putting a strip in early, and a strip in on time, and a strip in late. And then I'll know." He looked at Emeric from under the felt hat. "One strip, mind. Not the field."

"One strip."

"Ferra said half the seed in the store."

"Ferra says half the seed in the store about everything."

"She's right about everything," said Hollis, and pushed another kernel into the ridge with his thumb.

The two young men in the next furrows were Paxton's nephews.

Emeric had known they were coming, because Saban had sent him the list with two names marked and a note beside them in Saban's small hand: steward's sister's sons; one has spent three years at Milford's inn tap and the other three years fetching him home from it; take both or neither. He had taken both. Paxton had sent them up to Hollis on the first morning instead of down the line with the others, because, Paxton wrote, they had better learn to put something in the ground before they learned to do anything else, and Hollis was the only man in Milford they were frightened of.

One of them was walking his furrow the way Hollis walked his, slowly, watching his thumb, covering each kernel with his boot and looking back along the ridge every ten steps to see that it was straight. The other was talking. He talked to his brother, and to the hedge, and to a crow on the hedge, and his ridge wandered.

Hollis watched them both for a while without saying anything.

"The quiet one," he said. "He's good. He's got hands." He did not say anything about the other.



The sixth wind pump had stopped on the Sunday, Hollis said, and nobody could see why. The wind was the same up there as at the other five. The wheel was free; a boy had climbed up and turned it by hand. It turned, and stopped.

Emeric went up to look, with Rylan, and Hollis came up behind them with his seed bag, because Hollis did not let anybody look at anything in Milford without him.

The pump stood on the upland in the wind with its blades still, and the five round it whirred and clanked and the water came out of their pipes into their troughs, and the sixth trough was dry. He stood at the tower's foot and looked up at it for some time.

The vane was the trouble. He saw it after a while. The vane at the back, the flat iron tail that kept the wheel turned into the wind, had been bolted to its arm a little off true, a few degrees; not enough to see from the ground unless you were looking for it. So the wheel did not face the wind square. It faced a little across it, and the wind that came over the hill came at the blades at a slant they had not been made for, and pushed them as hard one way as the other, and the wheel sat between the two pushes and did nothing.

He could have climbed up and loosened the bolt. He did not, yet. He put his hand up instead, open, toward the wheel, and asked the air.

Air was not his. It cost him three times what it cost Sorrel, and Sorrel was the only air mage he had ever learned anything from; she had told him once, standing on the keep's leads with her cape-wing furled, laughing at him, that the wind was not a thing to be pushed. Guide it, she had said. Don't fight it. It's going somewhere already. Find where, and stand beside it, and lean.

The wind was going somewhere already. It was coming over the hill out of the west and going across the upland and on toward the sea, and all he did was find it, as it went past the stopped wheel, and lean on it, a little, so that it came at the blades square and not at the slant: a breath of it, bent, the width of the wheel.

The wheel turned.

It turned slowly at first, a blade at a time, and then the wind had it, and it ran up, and the pump under the tower coughed, and clanked, and coughed again, and the pipe at the trough's end gave a gasp and a gulp of brown water and then a steady stream.

He held it there, the bent breath of wind, for the count of thirty, while his head started to ache behind the eyes. Then he let it go.

The wheel ran down. It turned slower, and slower, and stopped, and the stream at the trough's end went to a trickle and to nothing.

"That's what's wrong with it," he said to Hollis. "That's the angle the wind wants to come at it. Look at the vane." He pointed. "It's out. Your smith's put it on a little crooked, so the wheel's not looking straight at the wind. It wants straightening on its arm, a hand's width at the tail. I've just shown you the wind it would get if it were right."

Hollis looked at the vane for a long while. Then at the wheel. Then at Emeric, with his head on one side.

"You could have just said," said Hollis. "Instead of the trick."

"I could."

"But you wanted to see if you could do it."

"Yes."

Hollis nodded slowly, as if that were the most reasonable thing a man had ever said to him, and turned to the quiet nephew, who had come up the hill behind them without being asked. "Go and tell the smith his vane's out a hand at the tail. Tell him I said. Don't tell him the duke said." He looked at the boy. "He'll mind more if it's me."



He asked them on the walk back down to the halt. Hollis, and Farris, who had come up from his own fields to see what the duke wanted with the wind pump, and Hester Mull, who had come out from her dairy with her sleeves rolled to the elbow because Paxton had sent a boy, and who stood in the lane with her arms folded and her chin up as she had stood in her dairy door the first day Ferra had walked in on her.

"A book," he said. "Of what you do. All three of you. How Milford farms. The strips first, and the rotation, and the clover, and the dairy, and the cheese cellar, and the cattle on the clover, and why. Written down, so that a man at Grayrock or Wayford who's never seen Milford can read it and do it."

Farris looked at Hollis, and Hollis looked at Hester, and Hester looked at Emeric.

"We can't write," said Farris.

"You can talk. A clerk from the school will write it as you talk. And where there's something you know and can't say, I'll write it, and you'll cross it out if it's wrong."

"You'll get things wrong," said Hester.

"I'll get a third of it wrong."

"More," said Hollis.

"Then cross out more." He looked at the three of them. "Milford's farming lives in your hands. Grayrock can't copy hands. Wayford can't. Nobody can copy hands. They can copy a book."

Hester unfolded her arms.

"Whose name goes on it?" she said.

"Yours. All three."

Hester looked at him a long breath, with her chin still up.

"Then we'll do it," she said. "Over the summer. Not in the spring; in the spring we're sowing." She looked at Hollis. "And he'll want his strip in it."

"My strip goes in first," said Hollis.

"Milford's Book," said Farris slowly, trying it. "The Milford Book."

Nobody had a better name. It stuck.



Rylan showed him what the new class had asked him to look at on the way down to the halt, from the lane, without stopping, with one long arm.

It was the land below Milford's upland, to the south and east: the long rolling country between Milford's bench fields and the river, which was not Milford's and not Kaldo's and not Grayrock's, exactly, but lay between all three, common grazing and scrub and old sheep walks that nobody had ploughed in a hundred years because nobody could agree whose it was to plough. The line ran along its edge. From the halt a man could see a long way across it, green and empty, to a low ridge in the middle distance with a spring under it, where Rylan said there had once been a drovers' inn, and was not now.

"They've outgrown the yard," said Rylan.

"The class?"

"The class. And the garrison under it. And the trainees from the holdings, now; half the terraces by the yard are full of masons from Grayrock who want to know why there are boys falling off the keep wall into the canal every morning." He looked across the green country. "I've forty in the new class. Thirty at the old drill. Ward wants another forty in the autumn. And there's no room to run them. They run in the street. They jump off the wall into the canal because there's nowhere else with water deep enough. They do their cold water in the river in front of the washerwomen." He looked at the low ridge. "They need a ground. Their own. With room to run, and water, and a wall to fall off that isn't the keep's, and somewhere to sleep that isn't a mason's attic."

Emeric looked at the country between the baronies, the long green emptiness of it, with the line along its edge and the river beyond.

"Here," he said.

"Here?"

"Between all four. Not Kaldo's. Not anybody's, quite. Reached by the line from all of them." He looked at the ridge, and the spring under it. "A knights' ground. A base. Its own wall to fall off. Its own water. Its own beds." He was quiet. "And things in it later that I don't want inside any town's wall."

Rylan looked at him sideways.

"What things?"

"Later."

Rylan looked at the ridge a while longer. Then he took a stub of chalk out of his pocket, and leaned over the halt's fence, and marked a cross on the top rail, facing the ridge, so that anybody standing there after would see what he had seen.

"I'll tell Ward," he said. "He'll say not yet."

"He'll be right."

"He's always right," said Rylan. "It's very tiring."



The train down was a goods train, because the coaches Ferra had written to Grayrock's wagon shop for in February were not built yet and would not be till summer; so Milford's trainees rode down to Kaldo the way everybody who had ever ridden on the line had ridden on it, in an empty grain wagon, on plank benches laid across it from side to side, under a canvas hood on hoops to keep the smuts off.

There were forty-four of them, on four wagons, with their bundles at their feet and their best coats on, and most of them had never been further from Milford than the market at the halt. They sat on the planks very straight, holding the benches' edges, and when the engine at the front gave its whistle and the wagons jerked and began to roll, a girl at the end of the first plank gave a little scream and then put her hand over her mouth and laughed; and after that they were all laughing, and holding on, and looking out under the canvas's edge at the bench fields going past, faster than any of them had ever gone in their lives.

Emeric rode with them, on the end of the last plank of the last wagon, with Rylan beside him; and somewhere past the first mile a man across the wagon from him, a big quiet smith from the village with his hands in his lap, asked him whether His Grace often rode on a plank in a grain wagon.

"Every time I come to Milford."

The smith thought about that.

"There'll be seats, they say," he said. "In the summer. Proper seats, in carriages, with windows, for people. The steward said."

"There will."

"Then I'm glad I came now," said the smith, and held on to his plank, and looked out at Milford going by under the canvas with an expression of great content. "Nobody'll believe me after. That I came down on a plank, with the duke."



They were housed in the new terraces below the works by nightfall, in the rooms Ferra's office had found for them, two to a room, a tap on every landing; and in the morning they went to their places: the smiths and the masons to Bram's shops and the open works, the pump men to the treatment works and Hedda's beds, the carpenters to Fletcher's yard, the teachers to Sela, the clerks to Ilsa at Ferra's side table. And the four nurses on Milford's list, with the two from Grayrock's, went down the hill to Imani.

Emeric took them down himself, because he was going that way, and because the ward was the one place in Kaldo where a trainee from the holdings was most likely to turn round and go home on the first morning, and he wanted Imani to know he had brought them.

She took them through the ward at a walk, all six, without stopping, the way she took anybody through it: the cutting room, where the year's instruments lay in their new rows on the tray, every one bright; the wards, the beds, the night stair with Tess's two switches; the dispensary and its boxes of cards; and then the back room, which smelled of cedar shavings and something under the cedar that one of the Grayrock nurses put her hand over her nose at.

"Rats," said Imani, and opened the door wider so that they could see.

It was a long low room with a window at the end, lined on both sides with wooden cages on shelves, each cage with its bed of shavings and its water bottle and its small grey occupants, and a card on each cage in Imani's hand. A nurse Emeric knew, Bettany, a broad calm woman with a pencil behind her ear, was going along the shelves with a scoop of oats, filling each cage's dish and writing on each cage's card.

"They're for blood," said Imani to the trainees. "We're finding out what happens when you put one creature's blood in another. What goes wrong, and when, and why. We've been at it since last spring. It's their second year. Bettany keeps them." She looked at the six faces. "You'll take turns. Everybody does. If anybody's afraid of rats, say so now, and you can be afraid of them and feed them anyway."

Nobody said so.

On the way out, in the dispensary, she showed them the register: the long book of names, every name in Kaldo she had typed, with its letter beside it and a card in the box for each, A or B or AB or O, the four the clumping test on the white dish could tell apart. It was thick, now. A year's work. She opened it and turned the pages for them so they could see how many names, and Emeric, at her shoulder, saw what she saw as she turned them: the castle's pages, and the kitchen's, and the works', crowded, every line filled; and the dock quarter's pages, thin, a few names and then white paper.

"They don't come," said Imani, when the trainees had gone up the stair to the ward and he was still standing there. She shut the book. "The dock quarter. I've had Bettany down there twice. They say a cup of blood's a year off your life. Somebody's grandmother said it, and it's been said ever since, and nothing I say down there weighs a feather against a grandmother." She put the book back on its shelf. "They'll come when one of their own needs blood and there's none of his letter in the book. Then they'll come. I'd rather it wasn't that way."

"So would I."

"I know," said Imani. "It will be anyway."



It snowed on the ninth of April.

It came in the night out of a clear cold sky that had no business snowing in April, a late heavy wet snow that lay on the new green in the ward and the blossom on the old pear by the gate and on everything else, and by morning the inner ward was white again, as it had been at midwinter; and in the middle of the east half of it the winter garden stood with snow on every one of its glass triangles, a dome of white, with the green showing through underneath where the snow had slid off the steeper panes near the ring.

Ferra came down from the counting room in the middle of the morning, with her book under her arm, and stood at the window of the long room and looked at it for some time.

Then she put the book down on the window seat, which she never did, and went down the stair.

He followed her. He had been at the long table with the dome's charging drawn out in front of him, the cartridges and the collar and the rate, and Ferra's line across it in red, and he left all of it.

She was at the pass-box when he came out into the ward, standing in the snow with her hand on the outer door.

"It's not charged," she said, without turning round.

"Not till Friday."

"Then it's only glass." She opened the outer door. "One at a time. It says so on the door."

She went in, and shut the door behind her, and he heard the inner door open and close; and then he went in after her, and stood in the little iron box with the snow melting off his boots onto its floor, and shut the outer door, and opened the inner one.

It was warm.

That was the first thing. The glass had held the sun of the two days before, and the snow over it had held the glass's warmth in, like a lid; and the air under the dome was soft and still and damp, and smelled of turned earth and wet stone and Imani's comfrey. The second thing was the light. It came through the snow on the panes, every triangle of it, white, soft, without any shadow anywhere, as if the whole sky had been laid over them like a sheet; and under it Imani's beds lay in their rows, green, every leaf, the foxglove's rosettes and the comfrey's great hairy tongues and the feverfew's little yellow buttons, green under a white sky in the middle of the snow.

In the middle of the dome, where the beds stopped, there was a circle of bare earth, raked, the size of a cartwheel, with a ring of white stones round it.

Ferra was standing at its edge.

He came and stood beside her. Neither of them said what the circle was for. Neither of them needed to.

The snow on the panes over their heads shifted, somewhere toward the top of the dome, with a soft sliding sound, and a slab of it went down the curve of the glass to the ring and dropped off into the ward, and where it had been there was a triangle of grey sky, and a little more light.

"It's beautiful," said Ferra.

It was the second time in a month she had said it about something he had made. He did not say anything about that.

She put her hand into his.

They stood in the green under the white, in the warm, with the snow going down the glass round them in soft slides, and the bare earth in front of them with its ring of stones; and after a while she turned to him, and put her other hand flat on his chest, inside his coat, over his shirt, and stood close, the way she had stood at the counting room table in February when he had told her where he was going before he went.

"A quarter of an hour," she said. "That's all I've got. I've a column on the table with Ilsa that won't add."

"A quarter of an hour."

"Then it's yours," said Ferra. "And then it's the column's."

He kissed her in the winter garden with the snow on the glass, slowly, with her hand flat on his chest, and the light round them white and soft and coming from everywhere; and he felt her smile against his mouth, and felt her heart under his hand, which was not steady, not even slightly, and which no cup on earth would have needed to tell him was not steady. Her hair had come loose at the back. He put his hand in it. She did not stop him. She leaned into him in the warm, under the snow, and kissed him back as if the column on Ilsa's table had never been ruled, and the green round them smelled of earth, and somewhere over their heads another slab of snow went down the glass and let in another triangle of sky.

At the end of the quarter of an hour, exactly, she stepped back.

Her face was pink to the ears and her hair was down at the back and her mouth was red, and she put her hair up again with three pins out of her pocket, by feel, without a glass, in about the time it took to say it.

"Work," she said.

"Work first."

"No. Fun first, this time." She looked at the circle of bare earth in its ring of stones, and then up at the white sky through the glass, and then at him. "I wanted to see it once like this. Before Friday. Before there's anything in it but us and Imani's foxglove." She went to the inner door. "After Friday it's the tree's."

She went out through the pass-box, one at a time, as the door said, and he heard the outer door open and close, and stood alone in the green under the white for a breath, by the ring of stones.

Then he went out too, and up the stair, back to the cartridges and the collar and the rate, and Ferra's line across them in red.
