Chapter 248 - The Road

The Coldharrow cut was a mile and a half of road let down through a ridge of grey rock between two shoulders of hill, where the north road left the vale and climbed toward Thornhythe. Saban had put it on his shelf years ago from a wool buyer's saddle, on a wet day, going up to the northern brothers to buy their clip. He had never thought of it since. He had brought it out on the ship and laid it on the table in the long cabin like a page, and Ward had looked at it for a long time, and said, That one.

From where Emeric lay in the snow on the east shoulder, above the narrowest part of it, he could see the whole of it in the last of the light. The road came up out of the vale on the south, and entered the cut between two walls of broken rock, and ran along the bottom for a mile with the stream beside it, and at the narrow place below him the walls came in so close that two carriages could not have passed, and the rock leaned out over the road on the west side in a great split slab, and the stream went under the road in a culvert and came out the other side. Then the cut widened again and climbed away north toward the pass, and the dark.

Carriages in a road below a pass, and men on both slopes above it.

He knew the shape. He did not remember it; he remembered nothing from before his seventh birthday, and not much from the year after. But he had been told it, the bare shape, the way a prince is told a thing about his mother that the whole court knows, and he had carried the shape since without ever once letting himself look at it. He looked at it now. He did not like what he saw, and he did not look away.

Ward lay a few paces off along the shoulder, in his black plate with the cape furled under him and the helm on, and had not moved in an hour. He was looking at the road. He had been looking at it since they came over the ridge in the grey before dawn and he said that one again, aloud, to nobody. Emeric did not ask him what he saw there. He thought he knew.

They had been in the snow since before light. The Discovery had put them down on the far side of the ridge in the dark, on her legs in a high sheep-fold three miles from the road, and gone back up into the band before the east was grey, and would not come down again until Saban's lamp told her to. Saban was on the ridge crest behind them now with the lamp under his coat and a flask and his teeth chattering, and he would stay there. He was not a man for a road.

They had placed themselves in the forenoon, while the road was empty.

Rylan had spent an hour at the narrow place with the slab. He had gone down to it in his plate on the pads, the brass cups at his boots and his off hand drawing the air out of themselves with their small stones and holding him flat against the rock face like a fly on a wall; and he had put a breach stone into the split behind the slab at the top, and a second one lower down, pinned, and wired the pins together to a cord that ran up the rock to where he lay now, on the west shoulder, with the cord round his wrist. Then he had gone two hundred yards back down the cut, to where the south end narrowed a second time under a shelf of rotten rock above the culvert, and done the same there.

A breach stone did not blow. It struck. The whole of its charge went into one point, in one blow, like the fist of something very large. Rylan had opened a strongroom door with one at the Staple. Two of them in the back of a split slab would put the slab in the road. Two more under the rotten shelf would put the shelf in the road behind it. And whatever was on the road between the two falls would be alone.

That was the whole of the plan. Saban had said, on the ship, that it was a very short plan. Ward had said a short plan had fewer places to go wrong. Nobody had said what else it was, though Emeric had seen Osric look at the page of the cut for a long moment and then at Ward, and had seen Ward not look back.

Osric was on the west shoulder with Rylan, and Rylan's four men were in the rocks at the south end, above the second fall, where the horses would be. Emeric and Ward were on the east shoulder, over the slab.

The light went. The snow came on again, fine and slow, out of a low sky. Somewhere to the south, down in the vale, a long way off, a horn blew.

They came up the cut with lamps.

The vanguard first: twenty knights of the lieutenant's household riding in pairs, their lamps on poles, the light going up the rock walls on either side and making the snow in the air into sparks. Then a gap. Then the king's carriage, closed, black, with four horses and a coachman and a groom on the box, and a priest of the close in black on the step at either door, holding the rail, riding the step the way the Church rode everything, at the door. Four knights round it. Then the second carriage, the Church's, with the cardinal's arms on the door and two more priests in it. Then a gap. Then the main body, sixty strong, strung out down the cut and out of it into the vale, Corbin's household knights and Aldane's horse among them, their lamps going away behind in a long wavering line like a string of beads drawn through the dark.

Emeric watched the vanguard go under him. He counted them. He could not help it.

The vanguard's rear pair came level with the slab, and passed it. The king's carriage came up behind them into the narrow place, its lamps swinging, the horses' breath smoking, and he could hear the wheels on the frozen road now and the coachman talking to his horses under his breath.

He opened the crack.

It was the thing he and Ferra had done under the lake, and Ward and he had done at the cave mouth on the lamp and the four pulls because it would not go through rock: a hair's width held open between his room and another's, not a door, a crack, sound only, while the body went on doing whatever it was doing. Ward's room was a few paces off along the shoulder. Emeric felt it there, the weight of it, the way you feel a man in the next room of a house; and felt the crack open between them, and felt Ward on the other side of it, holding his end, from his own side, with his wrist in the cradle under his breastplate and his band seated against the deciding stone.

Now, Emeric said, into the crack.

On the west shoulder, Rylan pulled.

The slab came down.

It did not fall so much as leave. There were two flat sounds, very close together, like two great doors struck once each, and the whole face of the split rock on the west side stepped out into the air over the road and hung there for a moment in the lamplight with the snow on it and then went down across the road between the vanguard's rear pair and the king's lead horses with a sound that Emeric felt in the shoulder under him before he heard it. The lamps ahead went out, or went under. A horse screamed. And two hundred yards back down the cut, a heartbeat later, the same two flat sounds, and the rotten shelf over the culvert came down behind the Church's carriage onto the road and into the stream in a great roar of rock and white water, and the main body's lamps stopped in a long jostling line behind it, and the shouting began.

Between the two falls, in the narrow part of the cut: two carriages, eight horses, four knights, four priests, a coachman and a groom.

And a king.

Emeric went down the slope.

He did not fly. None of them flew. They went down the rock on the pads and the last of it on their feet, in their plate, and it was the plate that mattered and not the wings. Four men in black iron with lions at their shoulders came down out of the snow on both sides of a road below a pass, and the four knights round the king's carriage turned their horses to meet them in the lamplight, and they were good men, every one of them, knights of the rite with the frost-tracery to their elbows and a weight in their arms that no ordinary man had, and they had been told there might be a rescue and had not believed it, and they came on anyway.

And at that moment, in the carriage, somebody opened.

Emeric did not feel it. He was not delving; he had the crack open to Ward and nothing more, and the crack was sound only. He felt Ward feel it.

He felt it through the crack as a thing that happened on the other side of a wall: a stillness, very sudden, in the room a few paces off. Not a man falling. A man stopping. Ward's weight in the next room going absolutely still, the way a hound goes still in a hedge, the way a man in a dark house goes still when he hears a board move downstairs that he has heard move once before, twenty years ago, and has listened for every night since.

Ward had his band in the cradle and his room open, and a man in the carriage below had opened his own, a dozen paces off, for the first time within Ward's reach in all the years since Ward could reach at all. And on the shelf in Ward's room, beside the passage, there was the channel; and in the channel, up the slope in the hooded men, there was a shape with an edge and a weight, the way a man's hand is his own and a stranger could tell it from any other hand on a page. Ward had never been able to read it. He had felt it in his teeth on the day his queen died, and carried the feel of it twenty years without a word for it, and learned to delve, and put it on his shelf, and looked at it there, and still not been able to read it, because a thing on a shelf is only a thing on a shelf until you have something to lay it against.

He had something now.

That's him, Ward said, into the crack. The channel.

It was the fewest words Emeric had ever heard him use for anything. Four words, flat, the voice he reported a road in. And in them, a whole column in a ledger turned over and added from the other end. Twenty years of Aldane. Twenty years of I believe the evidence and I don't believe him. A duke on the first bench who had put his hands on a woman in grey in a passage and been put on his face for it. The inquiry that came back clean. The man Ward had watched across every hall for twenty years, waiting. All of it, gone, in one breath, off a road below a pass, because a shape on a shelf had found its hand.

Emeric already knew.

He knew in the same breath, before Ward's words had finished coming through the crack, and he knew it not through the crack at all. He knew it with the other thing, the sense that had come to him in the hold of a boat with his hands tied behind him, the long slow reach that listened and put nothing into anything; which he had never once let out inside the capital's walls, and had not let out tonight, and which came out of him now without being asked, the way a hand comes up to catch a thing thrown at the face. It went down the slope ahead of him into the carriage, and found the man there, and the man had a beat in him. Slow and even and underneath everything, the way two strings tuned a hair apart will beat when they are struck together, rising and falling against each other on a rhythm neither has alone. He had heard it once, on the deck above a hold, through the planks, and walked it to the hatch in his head and back, and filed it with a name a guard had given away and a voice off a ledge, and carried it ever since, and laid it once against a man face down in the ash of a strongroom and found that it was not him.

It was here. It was in the carriage.

And at his wrist, under the cuff, on its cord, the bead answered.

Not both strings. One. The bead lay against the skin of his wrist, round and grey and a little smaller than a knuckle, and it had been the root, in a comb case, hers, before he made it into something else in the forge; and one of the two strings in the carriage beat, and the bead beat with it, very faintly, the way a glass on a shelf will sing when the right note is struck across the room. The other string, it did not know. The other string was the man. The one it knew was not.

He did not have a word for how he knew what the one it knew was. He did not need a word. The bead was hers, and it knew its own.

Ward meant the mountain channel. Emeric heard the Deep Channel. Two channels, twenty years and a coast apart, and one man in both of them, on a slope above a road and on a deck above a hatch, with something in his hand.

He did not stop. He did not say anything to Ward. He went on down the slope into the lamplight, with his sword out, toward the knight on the grey horse who was coming for him.

It was short. Afterwards, on the ship, he could not have said how long. The four knights were good, and they had capacity in their arms, and they were on horses; and the four who came down the slopes were in plate that would turn a lance, and had been in the band so many nights that they had more in their arms than any rite had ever put there, and were on their feet on a road they had chosen, in the dark, with the snow in the knights' eyes and the lamps behind them. Osric put the first down with the flat of a shield and a horse's own weight. Rylan put the second down and did not let him get up. Ward took the third off his horse with one hand on the rein and one on the man's belt, and threw him into the stream, and did not look to see where he went. The fourth rode at Emeric, and Emeric stepped inside the lance the way Ward had taught him twenty years ago on a practice floor in the old king's house, and took the horse's bridle, and turned it, and the knight came down on the frozen road on his back and lay there.

At the south end, Rylan's four men had the carriage horses by the heads, and the coachman and the groom face down in the snow with their hands on the backs of their heads, because they had been told to be. The priests on the steps had got down. They stood by the wheels with their hands folded and did not move, and nobody touched them.

Beyond the falls, the shouting. Lamps coming up over the rock at the south end, a few, and men with them, climbing, slowly, on the broken stone in the dark. The vanguard's lamps on the far side of the slab, going to and fro. There was not much time. There had never been going to be much time.

Emeric went to the carriage door and opened it.

There were two men in it.

His father sat on the far side, in a travelling coat with a fur at the throat, with his hands on his knees. He looked older in the lamplight than he had looked on the dais at the elevation; the beard was white and the face under it was grey, and he had been sitting in a closed carriage since the morning with the man opposite, and it showed. He looked up at the black helm with the lions at its shoulders in the carriage door, and his face did nothing at all.

"Who are you?" the king said. He said it loudly, in the voice for the door, so that it went out of the carriage and up the cut and to every man on the road who could hear it, and to the priests by the wheels. "I will not go with you. I am in the keeping of the lords spiritual."

And he stood up, as he said it, and came across the carriage to the door, with his hand out, and put his hand on his son's iron arm, and gripped it, hard, and did not let go.

The other man had not moved.

He sat on the near side, facing where the king had been, in the red, with a travelling cloak over it, and a great deal of white hair, and his hands folded in his lap. His right hand was closed. It was closed on something, and the left hand lay over the right, as a man's hands lie in his lap in church. He was smaller than he looked on the east bench, and older, and he looked at the helm in the carriage door with an expression of entire and courteous attention, as a man looks at a person he has met before, and liked, and is interested to see again.

He was an arm's length away. The beat came off him like heat off a stove. And at Emeric's wrist the bead sang, very faintly, to the thing in the closed right hand.

"Your grace," said the cardinal.

He said it pleasantly, in the level voice that put its weight on no particular word. He did not say which of them he meant.

Emeric did not answer. He did not reach into the carriage. He did not do any of the things that went through him, in the time it takes to breathe in, like a string of lamps going past in the dark. The king had his hand on his arm. The king was the job. He had come up the north road in the snow to bring his father out of a carriage, and his father was coming out of it, and there was a man on the far side of the vanguard's fall with a lamp who was nearly over the rock, and another, and another.

He took his father out of the carriage.

Behind him, up the slope, Ward had taken one step toward the carriage door. Emeric felt it through the crack; felt Ward's whole weight lean toward the man in the red, twenty years of it, the way a tree leans before it goes. And felt him stop. Not because Emeric said anything. Ward stopped himself, on the second step, with his hand on his sword and his wrist in the cradle and his room wide open, and stood in the snow, and did not take the third.

The king, Ward said, into the crack. It was all he said.

They had meant to carry him up the east slope on the pads, between two of them, slowly, the way you carry a sack up a rock face. There was not time. The first of the vanguard were over the slab now, three of them, sliding down its face into the lamplight with their swords out, and the main body's lamps were at the top of the south fall, and a crossbow bolt went past Emeric's helm and struck sparks off the rock and went away up the cut.

Osric took the king.

He did not ask. He came across the road in three strides, and put his arms round the old man in his fur, the way Ward had once put his arms round a boy of seven in a road below a pass, and said something into his ear that Emeric did not hear; and his cape came open behind him with a crack like a sail filling, the battens going out stiff on either side in the lamplight, and the stones in the iron cups at the backs of his calves went up to a roar that filled the cut from wall to wall, and he went up.

He went up the east slope in one long rising leap that was not a leap, with the king held against his breastplate, over the rock and the snow and the heads of the men on the road, and over the shoulder at the top, and was gone into the dark and the falling snow in less time than it takes to say it.

It was the only time any of them flew that night. It was seen. Every man on the road saw something. A shape going up off the road and over the rock in bad light, black, with something spread out behind it like wings, or a cloak, or smoke, and a noise like a furnace door; and then nothing, and the snow. The men on the road stood where they were with their lamps and their swords and looked at the place on the ridge where it had gone. None of them would ever agree afterwards about what they had seen. Every one of them was sure of it.

The rest of them went up on the pads, the way they had come down, fast, with the crossbow bolts going past, and the knights on the road behind them too slow in the dark and the snow to follow up rock that had no path on it.

At the top, on the shoulder, Emeric turned round and looked back down.

The cut was full of lamps now. The vanguard over the slab, the main body over the fall, men everywhere in the narrow place between, round the empty carriage and the Church's carriage and the four knights on the road, two of whom were getting up. The priests by the wheels had not moved.

The king's carriage was moving.

Rylan's men had let the horses go when they went up the slope, and the horses had stood in the traces with the rock fallen behind them and in front of them and the lamps and the shouting all round them, and the coachman had got up off the road and back onto the box, and now, as Emeric watched, somebody on the road took the lead horse's head and turned it, and the carriage came round in the narrow place with its wheels grinding on the stone, slowly, and began to go back down the cut toward the south fall and the main body, at a walk, with a knight at either door.

The carriage door was still open. Emeric had not shut it. In the lamplight, through the open door, as the carriage turned, he could see the man inside.

He had not moved at all. He sat as he had sat when Emeric opened the door, on the near side, facing the empty seat where the king had been, with his hands folded in his lap, the left over the right, and the right closed on the thing it was closed on. He did not look out. He did not look up at the ridge. He sat in the lit carriage going away down the road below the pass with his hands in his lap, and the beat went away with him, slow and even, thinning as it went, the way it had thinned on the water in the dark years ago when a boat rowed away from a hold with a prince in it.

At Emeric's wrist the bead went quiet.

Carriages in a road below a pass. Men on both slopes. Knights round the carriage, and a man in it with his hands in his lap, and a woman's son on the slope above, this time, instead of the woman.

Beside him on the shoulder, Ward stood and watched the carriage go, and did not move, and did not say anything, and did not take his hand off his sword until the lamps had gone round the bend by the fall and there was nothing to see but the snow.

Then he turned, and went up toward the ridge crest, where Saban had the lamp out from under his coat and was swinging it, slowly, three times, toward the sky.

In the carriage, going down the cut, the cardinal sat with his hands folded, and did not look out of the window, and thought about what he had felt.

One of them he had felt before. On a deck, on a night on the coast, through the planks of a hold, very close; he had known since that night whose son it was, and what the son could do, and he had been careful ever since not to open within reach of it, in any hall they shared. He had opened tonight because he had needed to find, among four men in iron in the dark, which one it was. He had found it. It told him nothing he had not known.

The other he had never felt in his life.

It had been a knight's. He was quite sure of that; he had stood at the eight places round the frame in the chamber under the cathedral for more years than any priest living, and he knew what the rite put into a man the way a miller knows flour. A knight, made by the rite, marked to the elbow, with a room of his own and a door he could open. There was no such thing. There was no record at Ostrel of any such thing, in all the years the rite had been kept.

There would be now. He sat with his right hand closed in his lap and began, without hurry, to compose in his head the report that would go to the holy father, which he would not write down.

The Discovery came down out of the snow onto the high sheep-fold on her legs a little after the second hour, and took them up.

Osric was already aboard. He had come down to the fold with the king in his arms before any of them, and the king was sitting on a bench in the long cabin with a blanket over the fur and a cup of something hot in both hands that he was not drinking, and Fletcher at the levers, and the frost beginning on the glass. Nobody had told him where he was. He had not asked.

Emeric came in last, and took his helm off.

His father looked at him. Then past him, at the cabin: the long room of oak and iron with the lamps in their cups and the frost on the windows, and the floor under his feet that had begun, very gently, to rise; and at Ward, coming in behind with the snow on his lions; and at the helm in Emeric's hands. He looked for a long time, and his face did what it always did, which was nothing.

He did not say anything. Emeric did not either. There was nothing either of them could say in that cabin, with nine men in it and the band coming, that would have been the right volume.

The Discovery went up through the cloud into the band, and turned south, and the frost thickened on the glass; and the king sat on the bench with the cup in his hands and looked out of the window at the dark, and at the stars coming out above the cloud, very many of them, very close, the way no king of Randillia had ever seen them.

After a while he put the cup down on the bench beside him, carefully, and folded his hands in his lap, and closed his eyes.

Emeric sat across from him with his helm on his knees and his mother's stone in his coat, and watched him, and at his wrist the bead lay against the skin, round and grey and quiet, and did not sing.
