Chapter 70 - The Fifth

The first quarter day fell on the last Tuesday in September, and Ferra paid it at the trestle in the back room of the exchange at nine in the morning, because the terms said the quarter day and did not say the hour, and she had decided that nine was an hour nobody could call late.

She counted it out in piles of ten, as she and Vantic had counted the House's money in a fortnight before, only now it was going the other way. A twenty-fourth of what Kaldo owed. Emeric sat on the end of the bench by the door and watched it go into the bag in front of the House's rider, one pile at a time, and found he was pricing each pile as it went. That one was the roof of Harl's mill. That one was the timber for a dozen houses on the black ground across the river. That one, and the one after it, was most of a winter's coal for the powerhouse, if Kaldo had had a powerhouse that burned coal, which it did not.

Vantic wrote the receipt. It was the shortest receipt he had ever seen: the date, the sum, the quarter, the House's name and her own, five lines in that even hand, with nothing in it that could be read two ways. Ferra blotted the last line of her book. Vantic waited until the blot was dry before she signed beneath it, and then she turned the receipt round on the trestle and slid it across, and Ferra read it twice, and put it in the book at the page, and closed the book on it.

The rider tied the bag and carried it out to his horse. Nobody said anything until the hooves had gone up the lane.

"Ninety-one more of those," Ferra said, "if we let it run."

"We won't let it run."

"You said that in August." She was already ruling the next page. "I've written it down both times."



Saban's autumn round came in a fortnight early, with two carts this time instead of one, because the roads north of Wayford had stayed dry all September and he did not trust them to stay that way.

He sat at Ferra's table that evening with his hat on his knee and gave them the prices first, as he always did, in the order he cared about them. Soap was holding on the coast. The scented bar was holding better than the plain, which he had told them it would. Spirit was not moving anywhere south of the river mouth; the coast was still drowning in its own, and a cask from a barony nobody had heard of four years ago was a cask nobody needed to open. Paxton's twenty casks were promised against next year's still, half paid, and Paxton had asked after them in a way that meant he expected them on the day.

Then he set the cup down and gave them the news, which he priced the same way.

"Wayford's baron asked after your wall."

Emeric looked up.

"At the posting house. Sethe, his name is, if you've not had it. Sethe of Wayford. He was dining in the long room when I came through, which he does on a Thursday, and he had me over to his table because I'd come up from Kaldo, and he asked me three questions about your wall in the time it took to eat a pie." Saban turned the hat round once on his knee. "How high. Whether it was true it was lit all night. Whether the lights went out when the river dropped."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him it rained a great deal in Kaldo in the spring." Saban drank. "He laughed. He's a man who laughs at the right places."

Ferra wrote the name down. Emeric watched her do it, and watched her write the date beside it, and the word Thursday.

"And Grayrock's widow has a writ," Saban said.

"From whom?"

"From the crown. Who else writes writs?" He shrugged. "She holds the hill in trust for her girl, by the king's word, and she's selling her ore upriver and east now, to anybody who isn't us. That's why you've seen no barges since the year turned. It's not spite, or not only. She's got a writ, and a writ's a thing a woman can stand on, and she's standing on it."

"When did it come?"

"Before midsummer, I heard. She wrote north after the burying and had her answer inside the season." Saban looked at him. "Why?"

Emeric did not answer. He was looking at the shelf over Ferra's stove, where the page she had ruled in March of last year was kept, with its two lines and its column headed answered, and he was thinking that he had written north about the same war, three times, in the same month the widow had.

"Any word for us?" he said. "From the north. On your way through."

Saban picked up his hat and put it on his other knee.

"Were you expecting some?"



Ferra reckoned the fifth on the Friday.

The terms said a fifth of the year before's net revenue, and the year before, by the terms, was the year that ended on the day of signing: the spring before the war, the war itself, the burned quarter, the summer after with the town eating its seed. She did the whole year from her book in one long column and drew the line under it, and Vantic did it again from her own book in a column of her own, and they turned the books round and agreed, again, to the copper.

Neither of them looked pleased about it this time either. It was a small number.

A fifth of it was smaller.

Emeric had a list. He had begun it in July, the week after the terms were read, on the back pages of Armand's notebook, every work he meant to build and what he thought each one would cost, and he had kept it the way a man keeps a tooth he can't stop touching. A steam engine, so the lamps did not live and die by the river. Ovens to make coke, so Bram could stop burning the barony's woods to charcoal. A blast furnace. A slipway at the dead port. Glass. A cement kiln, to make something better than lime. He read the list to them now, from the top, with his figure beside each, and Ferra wrote the fifth at the foot of the page in her own hand without being asked.

It would have bought most of the slipway. Or a third of the engine. Or none of the furnace.

He looked at it.

"I said two years," Ferra said. She did not say it kindly. She had not said it kindly in August either.

"You did." He turned the notebook round so that it faced her. "You also said, unless I meant to tell you a new pipe was a repair."

She looked at him, and then at the list, and her pen stopped where it was.

"Running works are untouched," he said. "Repairs are untouched. Everything the barony had on the day of signing, it can keep and mend and work as hard as it likes. It's only the new that's capped." He put his finger under the first item. "So what's new?"

"The engine's new."

"The engine's new," he agreed. "Harl's mill isn't. It stood on the day of signing. It just hasn't got a roof."

Ferra looked down the list. She looked across the trestle at Vantic, who was writing something in her own book and gave no sign of having heard any of it.

"Rulings are asked in writing," Ferra said.

"I know."

"Then go away and let me write them."

He went away. When he came back with the lamps lit, there was a single sheet on the trestle in Ferra's small plain hand: four questions, numbered, each one a line, and under each a space the width of a line. Ferra slid it across the trestle. Vantic read it once, the way she read everything, all of it at once, and put it in her case without a word, and did up the strap.



The answers came down the hill in the morning, before the first bell, on the same sheet.

The House's form was written across the top of it in Vantic's hand, with the date. Under each of Ferra's questions there was one line, and no line was longer than it had to be.

The mill at the race wall stood on the day of signing. The restoration of its roof is repair of a work of the barony and falls outside the limit.

The engine described did not exist on the day of signing. It is a new work and falls within the limit.

The houses on the far bank are the property of their builders and not works of the barony. The House has no ruling to make.

Timber from the barony's woods has produced no revenue in the period covered by the clerk's book. Timber given from those woods for the houses above is carried at nothing, at the barony's choice.

Ferra read it standing up, by the window. Then she read it again, and Emeric watched her stop at the last line, and knew why. At nothing, at the barony's choice. It was what Vantic had written, three weeks before, of the households' pay. She had chosen to write it again, here, about trees, and nobody in that room thought it was by accident.

Ferra did not mention it. She folded the sheet in half and put it in her book.

"She's right about the engine," she said.

"I know."

"You'd have argued."

"I would have lost." He reached for his coat. "I'd rather lose to her on one line than on four."



The gate yard was full of net-menders, which it had been most mornings since the fire.

They came over the east bridge at first light and stood about in the yard with their hands under their arms and waited to be told no, and when nobody told them anything they went back over the bridge and raised another wall on the black ground. There were six houses over there now, and the beginnings of a seventh, built out of whatever the river brought down and whatever the fire had not been able to finish. In August Emeric had stood in this yard and told them not yet: not before the reed was cut, not before there was timber. The old man with the face like a walnut had heard him out, as he heard everybody out, and then explained that his grandfather had built the dock quarter the first time without a prince and without timber, and that he did not see why the second time should be any different.

He was there again this morning, at the front, where he could hear.

Emeric told him the barony's woods were open to them. Not to sell from. To build with. They could cut what they needed for their own roofs and their own walls, and the barony would put a man on the woodward's path to mark which trees, so that nobody took the oaks that held the bank, and that was all.

The old man heard that out too.

"We started cutting Thursday," he said.

Behind him somebody laughed, and stopped.

"In the baron's woods," Emeric said.

"The old baron's. He's dead." The old man considered him. "Is there still the fine? For cutting?"

"No."

"We'd have paid it." He said it without any heat at all, as a matter of record, the way Ferra said things. "It's a fair fine, for a man's trees. We'd have paid it in nets, over the winter. I had a figure."

"Keep the figure," Emeric said. "Use the trees."

The old man nodded once, as if a bargain had been struck that he had already struck by himself on Thursday, and turned round and said something to the men behind him in the quick flat speech of the far bank that Emeric still only caught half of. They went off toward the bridge in a body. Two of them were carrying axes they had not taken the trouble to hide.

He stood in the yard after they had gone and looked across the river at the black ground. The six houses. The seventh, with its ribs up. The smoke of a cooking fire going straight up in the still air. It occurred to him that the barony had just given away a winter's timber at nothing and that this was the first thing he had done since August that had cost Kaldo less than he had expected.



Ward found him on the gate stair at dusk.

The wall was dark above them. Ward had put the wall out again for the low water on the last night of August, all of it but the lamp over the South Gate, which burned now in its hood over their heads, a little yellow, with a faint high whine in it when the machine down at the race caught and slipped and caught again. The river had not risen. It would not rise, Tess said, until October, and she had a column that said so.

"The two in the guardroom," Ward said.

Emeric had not forgotten them. He had simply arranged, every day since June, to be busy with something else when he went past the guardroom door.

"Grayrock's not written about them," Ward said. "Not once. Not when the body went home. Not since." He looked out over the gate yard, where the last of the light was going off the cobbles. "Four months they've had the barony's bread. A man at the door with a bow, day and night, and a man to watch the man. Their hands tied but to eat. They'll not last the winter like that. Neither will the man at the door."

"I know."

"Then choose." Ward said it flat, as he said everything, with no weight on any word. "Feed them. Free them. Or hang them."

Below them in the gate yard a boy was leading a horse across to the trough, and the horse would not be led, and the boy was talking to it in a low furious voice about what it would get if it did not come. Emeric watched them until the horse gave in. Then he went down the gate stair to the guardroom door, and the man with the bow stood up when he saw who it was, and Emeric told him to sit down again, and opened the door himself.
