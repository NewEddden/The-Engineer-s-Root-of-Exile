Chapter 70 - The Suitors

There were eleven horses in the yard at Grayrock that were not Grayrock's, and Saban counted them before they had dismounted.

"Four good," he said, low, while a groom took his bridle. "Three hired. Two I'd not give you much for. And those two by the trough came up from the coast, and the saddlery's better than the horses."

Emeric looked. The two by the trough were a matched pair of bays, sleek and fine-legged and wholly unsuited to a hill road, and the saddles on them were tooled leather with silver at the pommels.

"Whose?"

"Nobody's, yet." Saban handed his reins over. "A factor rides a horse like that when he's been told to look like his house has money and hasn't been told how long he's staying."

They had come up the river road in a day, the three of them, with no banners and no escort, which had begun as a precaution four years ago and was now simply how he travelled. He had come to settle an administration. There was the ore contract to go over, and the road, and the tally house Bryda had emptied and filled in the autumn, and underneath all of it the question Ferra had marked with a small cross in the margin of the Grayrock book, which was what exactly Bryda was administering now, and under whose hand. He had meant to spend two days on it. He had not expected a queue.

He saw the whole of it at dinner, and he saw it the way he had learned to see a column, by what was in it and what was not.

There were nine men at Bryda's table besides her household and his own. A minor lord from the east with a holding the size of a large field, who had come with a gift of a clock for the hall. A second son of a house on the northern road, called Garvey, who was handsome and knew it and had brought a hawk. Two cousins of somebody Emeric had never heard of. An older man, a widower, who spoke well and slowly about the importance of steadiness in troubled times. The factor with the bays, who represented a coastal house and represented it very well, and whose coat Saban had priced from across the room in a single glance. And three others, whose names Emeric heard and filed and did not trouble to remember, because by the end of the soup he had understood that it did not matter which of them was which.

They were all perfectly pleasant. Nobody was rude. Nobody was menacing. Nobody drank too much or said anything that could not have been said in front of a bishop. The widower asked after Bryda's health. Garvey asked after the hawk's. The coastal factor admired the plate. Every one of them was correct, and several of them were charming, and Emeric listened to them for two hours and noticed that not one of them had asked a single question about Grayrock.

Not about the seams, or the shafts, or how deep they ran. Not about the men who worked them, or how many had come home from the war, or how the town was eating. Not about the road, which Kaldo's money kept mended and which carried every lift of ore that left the hill. Not about the tally house, or the clean- out, or the mine captains, or the river. They talked about the weather and the hawk and the clock and the excellence of the mutton. They talked around the hill the way men talk around a woman they are all hoping to marry, which was precisely what they were doing.

They wanted the hill. That was the whole of it. The mother or the daughter was the door.

Bryda sat at the head of her own table and kept them all fed and all talking and all perfectly content, and gave not one of them anything, and did it so well that he could not see the join.

"How long?" he said, afterward, in the counting room, with the door shut and a lamp between them.

"Since the attainder." She did not need to think. "The first came a month after. The Crown's confirmation was read in the hall in the summer, and they read it too, and they understood what it said better than I did at first." She sat with her hands folded on the table. "It said I have no instrument."

He waited.

"I held Grayrock in trust for Ilsa. That was the arrangement after Renner. Ugly, but it was mine, and it was clear. The attainder voided the trust. It took Renner's line out of the succession, which means Ilsa is no longer an heir to anything, and the trust had nothing left to hold." She said it in the same voice she used for a tally. "What I have now is a confirmation in a hall. Resident in the Crown's interest, pending disposition. And then the disposition was to you." She looked at him levelly. "I am the widow of an attainted baron, living in a house that belongs to a prince, on a hill that belongs to a prince, administering it on terms nobody has written down. I can refuse a man this month. I can refuse him next month. I cannot refuse him forever."

"Why not?"

"Because eventually one of them will find a way to a claim that runs through my daughter," Bryda said. "A settlement, a prior promise, a cousin's grant, some old paper of Renner's father's that says the upland grazing goes with the girl. There's always a paper, if a man looks hard enough and has a lawyer who's hungry. And when he has it, there is no law in this kingdom that puts a widow's preference above it. None." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat. "So I delay. I feed them and I thank them for the clock and I tell them it is too soon after my husband, and it is working, and it will go on working for a while."

"And then?"

"And then it won't." She did not look away from him. "Delay is the only tool I've ever been allowed, Your Highness. I'm very good with it. It runs out."

He did not see Ilsa until the next morning, and then only because he went looking for Bryda and found her daughter instead, at the window of the long gallery that looked down on the yard.

She was nineteen. He had not seen her since the afternoon in the yard four years ago when she had asked him what happened to her if her mother died, and he had half expected the same girl, and she was not. She was taller and quieter and stood very still, and she was watching the yard below, where Garvey was showing his hawk to the coastal factor and three grooms.

She knew he was there. She did not turn round.

"Eleven this season," she said. "Four for my mother. Seven for me." She watched the hawk lift off Garvey's glove and circle and come back. "The seven are more polite. The one with the hawk has offered the upland grazing as a wedding gift. He doesn't own it. His brother does, and his brother doesn't know."

"How do you know?"

"I wrote to his brother." She turned from the window then, and looked at him, and there was nothing in her face at all, neither bitterness nor bravery, only the level attention of somebody reading a price she had already checked twice. "I'm worth about four hundred acres of good grazing and a claim on the high seam. The clock was an insult. I've told my mother so."

She went past him down the gallery. She had been doing that sum, he understood, since she was fifteen, since the day her father came home in a cart, and she had got very good at it. He stood at the window a while longer and watched Garvey's hawk come in to the glove, and nobody else was there to say anything about any of it, and nothing was said.

His first instinct was wrong, and he acted on it before he had looked at it properly, which he had been trying for eight years to stop doing.

He found Bryda in the tally house at midday, going through the week's lifts with the new clerk, and waited until the clerk had gone.

"I can end it," he said. "I'll write to them. Every one of them, and their fathers, and their houses. A letter from the holder of Grayrock saying the widow and her daughter are under his protection and he'd take it amiss to have them troubled." He heard how it sounded and went on anyway. "It'd be over in a fortnight. None of those men wants my attention."

Bryda set down her pen.

"No," she said.

He had expected her to thank him. He had half expected her to refuse, out of pride, and be talked round. He had not expected the no to come so fast, or so flat, as though she had worked out the answer long before he had worked out the question.

"A woman who is left alone because a prince told men to leave her alone," she said, "holds her seat at the prince's pleasure. Every one of those men would know it by the end of the month. My hall would know it. The captains would know it." She closed the tally book. "And I would know it. Every order I gave after that, I'd be giving because you'd said I could. That's not a seat. That's a lease."

"It would stop them."

"It would stop them coming to me," Bryda said. "It would start them coming to you. The next man who wanted this hill wouldn't bring me a clock. He'd go to Kaldo and talk to you about my daughter, over my head, as a thing you'd taken into your keeping, and you'd be the one deciding." She looked at him with something that was very nearly patience. "I'm refusing your protection, Your Highness, because your protection is exactly how it's done."

He heard it. It took him a beat too long, and she watched him take it, and did not help.

He had been thinking of her, he understood, as the woman minding the chair. He had been thinking of her that way for four years, since the first evening at her table when he had had the ore contract drawn to bind the holder of Grayrock and not Bryda by name. A caretaker, holding a seat warm until the girl came of age or some man married into the hill. And his instinct, the moment he saw men at her gate, had been to protect a caretaker, which was to say to put his own hand over the chair and keep it warm for her. It had not once occurred to him to ask what she would need in order to hold it herself.

He did not say any of that. He did not yet have it in words. He had only the shape of having been wrong, and the woman across the table watching him find it.

"Then what do you need?" he said.

"I don't know yet," said Bryda. "Neither do you." And she opened the tally book again.

The mine captain was the other thing, and it was smaller, and it was not.

He saw it on the second afternoon, walking the lower workings with Ward and a lamp and the captain of the east shafts, a broad grey man called Tolley who had worked Grayrock's seams for thirty years under Renner and Renner's father. They came to a gallery that ran off to the left, down a slope, with fresh spoil at its mouth and the marks of new timbering.

"That's closed," Emeric said. He had the tally-house books in his head. "The steward closed that in the autumn. The seam's thin and the roof's bad."

"It's closed," Tolley agreed, and did not look at the gallery.

There were men working in it. Emeric could hear them, the steady knock of picks somewhere down the slope, and see the glow of their lamps on the wet rock.

"Then who's in it?"

"Men with families," Tolley said. "The seam's thin. It isn't empty." He met Emeric's eyes with the flat, unhurried respect of a man who has decided in advance how much trouble he is prepared to be in. "The baron would have worked it."

The baron had been dead four years. Tolley did not say which baron he meant, and did not need to. Emeric understood, standing there in the damp with the knock of the picks coming up out of the dark, that the refusal had nothing to do with the seam. It was about the order, and about the hand that had signed it, which was a woman's, and Renner's widow's. Everybody in the hall knew that. Probably everybody in the gallery did.

Ward was looking at him, waiting to see what he would do.

He did nothing. He could have ended it there, in the gallery, with one sentence and Ward at his shoulder. He could see precisely how, and precisely what it would cost Bryda if he did: it would be his order that closed the seam, not hers, and every man in Grayrock would learn which of the two of them could make a captain obey. He had just been told, in plain words, what protection was.

"Let's see the east face," he said, and they went on, and the picks went on behind them.

He did not propose anything. That was new, and he noticed it.

For eight years, whenever he had seen a thing that was broken, he had arrived at the fix before he had finished looking, and usually the fix had been right, and sometimes it had been right about the machine and wrong about the people standing in it. Now he sat in the guest room at Grayrock on the last night with the window open on the sound of the river and the head-frames black against the stars, and thought, and did not reach for anything.

He thought about a widow with no instrument. About a girl who had priced herself at four hundred acres and a claim on the high seam, and had written to a man's brother to check the figure. About a captain working a closed seam because the woman who had closed it was not a man. About a hall full of pleasant men with a clock and a hawk and a very good coat, every one of them waiting, correctly and courteously, for the delay to run out.

Saban came up late, with the account of which suitors had paid their lodging and which had not, and set it on the table, and looked at Emeric sitting by the window with nothing in his hands.

"You're thinking," Saban said.

"I am."

"It suits you less than you'd expect." He sat down on the edge of the other bed. "She's got a season. Two, if the widower's patient and the hawk man's brother doesn't write back."

"I know."

"And after that," Saban said, "one of them finds his paper, and marries the girl, and owns the hill you've been buying ore from, and everything that's under it."

Emeric did not answer. Below the window the river went on down towards Kaldo in the dark, carrying nothing, and somewhere in the hill a gallery that was closed was full of men and lamps, and he sat and looked at all of it until the lamp in the room burned down, and did not reach for anything at all.
