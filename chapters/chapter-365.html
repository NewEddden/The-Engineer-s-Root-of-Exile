Chapter 365 - Full

Imani cut the last notch on the eighteenth of August, at ten past seven in the morning, and then sat on the rim of the tank with the nail in her hand and didn't say anything for long enough that Ilenne looked round.

The dial was a brass disc the size of a saucer, screwed to the tank's iron frame where Ilenne could see it from the water. Imani had marked it in sixths on the day the guest came into the Cradle, two of Armand's to six, and had cut a notch beside the first mark that same night with a nail from Fletcher's tin, because she'd wanted something on the wall that moved. There were a great many notches now. They went round the dial close together, every second day and then every third, the top of the cup filling slowest, a year and nine months of them, and the last one sat a hair past the six.

"There," said Imani.

Ilenne looked at the dial. Then at Imani. Then she reached up out of the water and put one wet finger on the last notch, very lightly, the way you'd touch a sore place to see if it still hurt.

"Six," she said.

"Six. And a scratch." Imani put the nail in her pocket. "That's the scratch. You can have the scratch for free."

She'd thought, for some months, that she'd cry when it came, and had been mildly dreading it. She didn't. She felt light and slightly stupid, the way you do at the end of a night shift when somebody tells you the patient in the corner bed has gone home on her own legs and you'd been bracing for the other news so long that you don't know where to put your hands. She put them in her lap. Ilenne went on looking at the dial.

Then, instead of sinking back to wait for her fish as she had every morning for a year and nine months, Ilenne put both hands on the tank's rim, beside Imani, and came up out of the water in one long movement, and stepped over the rim onto the iron floor, and stood there dripping on her own legs in the early light from the Cradle's high windows, with nothing in her face at all, and looked down at the tank as if it were a bath somebody else had run.

"Bed," said Ilenne.



The bed was Fletcher's idea and Fletcher's carpentry, and he had been building it in his head, he admitted afterwards, since about February.

It was in the house on the bay. The house was his idea too. It stood on Kaldo's south shore below the lower ward, on the end of the row of pilots' houses where the shingle came up to the garden walls, and it had been a pilot's house until the pilot retired to his daughter's at Milford in the spring, and Fletcher had taken one look at it and gone to Ferra with a drawing. Two rooms down and two up, slate roof, a garden wall to the shingle with a gate in it. And at the back, where the pilot had kept a boat shed, a pool: a long stone basin let into the rock below the house, filled by the sea on every tide through a grating in the wall and emptied by it again, so that it was always salt and always cold and always clean, with a roof over half of it and a door from the kitchen. Fletcher's men had built the pool in four weeks. He'd built the bed himself, at night, in the Cradle's back shop, of ash, long, because she was long.

She moved in on the nineteenth. It took one cart. Her board, her cloak, her shell box with the few things in it that were hers, the second cloak Abloh had made her that she wouldn't wear yet, a sack of the cook's dried fish that the cook had sent without comment, and Imani's card. Zale came with the cart, on his stick, and sat in the kitchen and drank Fletcher's tea while she went through the house touching things. She touched everything. She touched the walls and the doors and the hinges of the doors and the window glass and the bed's long ash foot. She went down the steps into the pool in her shift and lay on the bottom for twenty minutes while Zale counted, and came up and sat on the edge, and said, "Cold. Good."

She slept in the bed that night. Imani slept downstairs in the kitchen chair, which nobody had asked her to do and which she said was for the bed's sake, in case it collapsed. It didn't.

In the morning Ilenne came down the stairs with her hair standing out from her head in every direction and her eyes half shut, and stood in the kitchen doorway looking at Imani in the chair, and said, slowly, with great feeling, a word in her own language that Imani didn't know and Ferra later wrote down, after some argument, as soft.



The dry came on the fourth day, and they found it the way you find most things about a body, by its going wrong.

She'd been out of the pool since the evening of the nineteenth. Not on purpose. She'd showered in the pilot's tin bath every morning, fresh water out of the city mains, and she'd been out in the city every day and come back late and tired and gone straight to the long bed, and on the morning of the twenty-third Imani came round to the bay house at eight with the card and found her at the kitchen table with her head in her hands.

The sheen was gone off her. Not the colour; the sheen, the faint moving pearl under the grey-white that had come back over the summer as the notches went round. She'd gone chalky, like a shell left too long on a sill. Imani crouched by her chair and looked at the slits under her ears, which were lying shut the way they did in air, and they looked tight. Tight was the only word. Like a seam that had been sewn a size too small.

"Head," said Ilenne, into her hands. "Here." She pressed the heels of her palms into her eyes.

"How long?"

"Yesterday. Small. Today..." She made a fist and opened it. "Big."

"Have you been in the pool?"

"Bath."

"Every morning?"

"Every morning. Bath." Ilenne took her hands away from her face and glared at Imani, quite suddenly, out of nowhere, with the slate starting down her arms. "Bath, bath, bath. I say bath. Why you ask, ask, ask..."

She stopped. She heard herself. She sat back in the chair and looked at her own arms going dark, and then at Imani, and Imani saw her understand that the temper was part of it. Ilenne put her hand across her eyes, which was the sign for slate, for anger, and then took it away, carefully, and put it flat on the table palm down instead.

"Sorry," she said, in Kaldo.

"Don't be. That's a symptom. That's going on the card." Imani got up. "Pool. Now. An hour, and I'm sitting on the edge."

It took forty minutes. At twenty the slits had eased. At thirty the headache had gone, and at forty the sheen had come back up under the grey as if someone had wiped a cloth over a dull pane, and she came up the pool steps into the kitchen with the water running off her and the temper gone and made Imani a cup of tea, badly, with the leaves in, to say so.

So that was the rule, and they wrote it down. Imani wrote it on the card in her own hand and Ferra copied it into the book that evening, under the fresh-water and salt-water lines that Ilenne had more or less explained in July with her hands. Fresh water carried her. A bath or a shower or a day of rain carried her a good while. But it didn't hold in the film on her skin and in her gills the way the sea did, and by about the fourth day, the fifth at best, she went dry whatever she did, and the only cure was salt: an hour in the pool, or a night in it, or the sea. Fresh water is a drink. Salt water is a meal, Imani wrote, and added a new line at the bottom of the shade card, under slate and the near-black and the deep blue, in her small slanted hand: Dry. Chalky, dull. Gills tight. Headache behind the eyes. Short-tempered (not her fault). Salt, an hour.

"Four days," said Ferra.

"Four this time." Imani tapped the card. "She's six. Five days at six. The body's still finding where it put things. It'll go longer as she settles; at home, on the dry side, she says, they'd go a week and think nothing of it. I want her to try for a week before Hollow Night and see." She put the card away. "And I want a soak in that pool every third night until then, whatever she says, so we stop learning it this way."

"She'll say something."

"She said it this morning," said Imani. "I've had it."



The city days were the part nobody had planned for, and they were most of it.

Saban had given out one instruction, at the long room table, in July, and it had been short: no story. No cousin from the coast, no widow from Grayrock, nothing anyone would have to remember. A tall woman in a grey cloak with her hood up walked about Kaldo, and anybody who asked was told by anybody who knew that she was a guest of the castle's and that was that. A town of Kaldo's size, Saban said, had a tall woman in a hood in it somewhere every day of the week. What it didn't have was a story with a hole in it for people to put their fingers in. So she didn't get one.

She took the tram the first morning, on her own, all the way up from the shore stop to the market cross and back down again, without getting off, twice. Then she took it up a third time and got off.

She went to the market and bought a peach and a length of blue ribbon and a small steel knife from the cutler, and paid for all three herself, out of a purse of coppers and silver that Ferra had given her on the island with a slip of paper inside it in Ferra's hand that said Wages: sea watch, Harbour Board, from 1 Aug. She'd looked at the slip a long time. Then at Ferra. Then she'd said the word that Zale said was her people's word for spending, the wages word, and put the purse inside her shift, against her chest, and kept it there.

She went to the coffee house under the arch, and sat at the back with her hood up and a cup she didn't drink, because coffee tasted to her like burnt kelp, and watched the room for an hour. She went to Maren's and ate a whole plate of Maren's smoked fish and potato and asked for a second, and Maren, who had been told nothing by anybody and had guessed most of it, brought her the second and stood over her while she ate it with her arms folded and a look of grim satisfaction.

"You're the first person in this town who's ever eaten that the way it wants eating," said Maren. "The rest of them pick." She took the plate. "Come Thursday. I do eel."

Ilenne came Thursday.

On the Sunday Wren came round to the bay house at nine with a bicycle. It wasn't her own; it was one from the Free Wheel rack on the shore road, borrowed on her card, with a basket on the front and the seat as high as it would go. She had a theory that a woman with legs that long would find a bicycle easier than most people did, because she'd be able to put her feet down.

It wasn't true. Ilenne fell off it eleven times on the shore road between the pilots' houses and the fish quay, and twice into the shingle, and once, at speed, very nearly into the sea, which Wren said afterward would have been the most ridiculous way for her to drown that had ever been imagined. The ninth time she fell off she lay on her back on the road with the bicycle on top of her and the front wheel spinning and laughed so loud, with her head back and the click under it, that two women came out of the end house to see. On the twelfth go she went all the way to the quay and back without stopping, her knees up round her ears, and got off at the bay house gate, and stood holding the handlebars, breathing hard.

"Wheel," she said.

"Bicycle," said Wren.

"Wheel." She looked down at it with deep affection. "My wheel."

"It's the Free Wheel's. You can't have it. It's got a number on."

Ilenne looked at the number. Then at Wren. Then, without a word, she wheeled it in through the garden gate and leaned it against the wall of the bay house under the kitchen window, and went inside.

Ferra paid the Free Wheel for it on the Monday. She put it in the book under Wages, because she said there wasn't a better column.



The ship went on the twenty-eighth of August, from the island, before it was light.

Bram had put the sixth vein in on the last Monday of August with Orrin's new glass. He fitted it himself, as he'd said he would, and then flew her twice round South Sentinel with Zale at the binnacle and nobody else aboard, and landed her on her four pegs on the shingle and got out and said, "She'll do," and went home. That was all anybody heard about it from Bram. From Zale they heard a good deal more, most of it about the louvres.

Imani went. That had been settled early and without much argument. Somebody had to be there who could look at Zale if the thick air got into him again, and look at Ilenne if anything happened at the wall that nobody had thought of; and of the people who could, Imani was the one Ilenne would let put a hand on her in the dark. Noll went, and the older rigger, whose name was Hesk, and a second rigger off the Hod. That made six.

Emeric didn't.

He'd meant to. He'd assumed it, all through August, in the way you assume you'll be at your own wedding. On the evening of the twenty-sixth, at the bay house kitchen table, with the chart out, Ilenne had looked at him across it and said, "No," and then, while he was still working out what she'd said no to, "My say." She tapped her chest. "Not Emeric say. Not Zale say." She looked at Imani. "Imani look. Only look."

"I want to be there," said Emeric.

"Yes," said Ilenne, as though that settled it, and in a sense it did.

He stood on the shingle at South Sentinel at half past four in the morning in the dark with Ferra's arm through his and watched the ship lift. She went up off her pegs without a sound, on six veins, the louvres down her flanks catching the lamp on the pier as she turned. She came round slowly into the west and went away over the reef, low, and was a darker place against the dark for a while, and then wasn't anything.

"You're holding my arm too hard," said Ferra.

He let go a little.

"Not that much," she said, and put it back.



It took them the best part of two days to reach the wall water, and Imani was sick for most of the first day, which she said was undignified and Zale said was traditional.

They went low, at a few hundred feet, the way Zale had gone in the spring, over a sea that turned from Kaldo's grey-green to a blue Imani had never seen and then, late on the second afternoon, to something else. She saw it first in Ilenne. Ilenne had been at the rail since noon. She stood there with her hood down and the wind in her hair and her hands on the rail, and as the afternoon went on she brightened. It came up in her the way it had come up in the pool at the bay house, the sheen under the grey, only more, and more, until in the low sun she was the brightest thing on the ship, pearl and silver, like the inside of the reckoning shell.

Then Imani felt it herself. The air had changed. It had got heavier, somehow, without getting any warmer, the way the air in a room gets heavy before a storm, and she found herself breathing it more slowly without meaning to, the way you'd sip something strong. She opened her sight a crack and shut it again quickly. Everything was louder. The sea was loud. The sea under them was full.

"There," said Zale, at the binnacle. His voice had gone careful. "That's it. That's the start of it. That's as far as I take her." He pressed his lips together. "I've an ache behind the eyes already, and I'll thank you not to look at me like that, Imani, I said I'd say."

"Bring her down."

He brought her down to a hundred feet, and then to thirty, and held her there over the long heavy swell with the louvres whispering. Hesk put the ladder over the lee side, weighted at the foot, the same ladder Zale had gone down in April with the shell in his teeth. It hung down into the sea and the sea took the end of it and lifted it and let it go.

Ilenne took off her cloak and gave it to Imani to hold.

She stood at the rail in her shift with her hands on the ladder's top rung and looked down at the water. She didn't move for a while. Imani held the cloak and didn't move either, and found that she had stopped breathing the heavy air at all and had to start again.

She could go, Imani thought.

It came to her quite clearly, standing there with the cloak over her arm. Ilenne was at her six. This was her water, or the start of it. Under that swell, somewhere west, a thousand miles or less, was her mother's door, and nothing stood between her and it now but the sea, which she could breathe, and her own legs, which the sea here would let her trade back for what she'd had. Nobody on this ship could stop her. Nobody on this ship would try. Emeric wasn't here. Imani hadn't asked him why he'd agreed to stay behind, and she understood now that he must have seen this too, all of it, from the shingle at half past four in the dark, and let her come anyway.

Ilenne went down the ladder.

She went down it quickly, hand under hand, and at the bottom she didn't let go. She hooked one arm through the last rung and let the swell come up round her to the shoulders, and Imani saw the change go through her under the water, a long ripple from the hips down, and her legs were gone, and then she was floating there at the foot of a rope ladder in the open sea with her arm through a rung and her tail in the deep, holding on.

She put her face in the water.

Imani couldn't hear it. Nobody on the ship could, except perhaps Zale, who had gone very still at the binnacle. But she could see it. She could see Ilenne's throat working under the water, and the light on her shoulders and her back, and the colour. The colour went through her as she spoke the way weather goes over a field. It was the warm one first, the brightening, the one Ilenne had shown them on the slab in July as two fingers drawn up the inside of a wrist. Then the pale, the deep pale, all the way down: the bow. Then something Imani didn't have a sign for at all, a slow steady brightness, very even, that didn't change for a long time, while the throat worked.

She put her face up out of the water for breath, which she didn't need, and Imani understood that it was a pause, the way a person pauses in a letter. Then she put it down again.

And then the colour changed.

It went soft. There wasn't a better word. Soft, and slower, and somehow younger, the brightness going in and out at the throat in a long rhythm, over and over, like breathing, like a hand patting a blanket. Imani knew that rhythm. She'd heard it done in Kaldo's lower ward a thousand times through a wall at night: a woman counting a child down. She had done it herself, on the children's ward, at three in the morning, to a girl with a burn who wouldn't sleep. One. Two. Three.

It went on for a little while.

And then, in the middle of it, between one beat and the next, it stopped.

Not finished. Stopped. The way a child stops counting when she's asleep.

Ilenne hung there in the swell with her face in the water, very still, for a long moment. Then she took it out, and pulled herself up the ladder on her arms, and on the third rung, out of the sea, her legs came back, and she climbed the rest on them and came over the rail and stood dripping on the deck. She was so bright that it hurt to look at her, and her face was wet with the sea and with something else, and she held out her hand to Imani for the cloak.

Imani gave it to her.

"Good?" said Zale, from the binnacle, quietly. "Is it said?"

"Said," said Ilenne.

Below them the swell came up the ladder's foot and let it go, and came up again. Out past the ship, a long way off, a few fish turned on the surface all together, silver, and went under, and a little further off a few more did the same, and then further, and further, going west.

"Up," said Imani. "Zale. Up, and home, before your eyes start."

"They've started," said Zale, and took her up.



Ferra got it on the last night of August, at the bay house kitchen table, with the lamp on and the pool door open and the sound of the tide coming in through the grating.

She hadn't asked for it. She'd come round with the book to write up the run, the hours and the height and the miles and Zale's eyes, and had done all that, and Imani had gone up to the long bed and was asleep in it, across the foot, in her boots. And Ilenne had sat across the table from Ferra with her hood down, watching her write, and had said, when Ferra shut the book, "Also."

So Ferra opened it again.

Ilenne told her what she'd said. It came slowly, in her Kaldo and her hands and two words of her own when there was nothing else, and Ferra didn't hurry it. That she'd told her mother she was well, at her six, on her own legs. That she'd told her mother she wasn't coming home in the summer. That she was coming in the spring, and why: because she would not be carried in. She would walk into her mother's hall herself and say what she had to say herself, in her own mouth and in theirs. That the people of the cursed land had kept her alive and would bring her to the door as she asked, in the spring, and that this was her choice, hers, not theirs, and that her mother should wait for her.

"That's what Saban said she'd hear a knife in," said Ferra, very quietly. "Don't wait."

"Not don't wait. Wait." Ilenne shook her head. "Different. Wait, for me. My say." She tapped the table, hard. "Prisoner, they say: I am well, I come, don't be afraid. Prisoner, they don't let say: I won't come now. I come when I want. You wait." She lifted her chin. "My mother knows. Nobody makes me say that."

Ferra wrote it down, word for word, as near as she could.

"And then," said Ilenne. Her voice had changed a little. "Then I count."

"Count?"

Ilenne looked at the book. Then she reached across the table and took Ferra's pencil out of her hand, the way she had in the customs tower, and turned the book round, and on the bottom of the page, under the rest, she drew a line of small marks. Not words. Ferra knew them. They were the marks she'd spent July learning off the reckoning shell's rubbing, her people's numbers, the ones that weren't tens.

Ilenne drew six of them. Then a seventh. Then she stopped, with the pencil a little way off the paper, and didn't draw the eighth.

"When I am small," she said. "Very small. Not sleep. My mother..." She lay her head down on her own folded arm on the table, like a child, and with her free hand she patted the table slowly, beat, beat, beat. "Count. Slow. Every night. Same. And I sleep. In the middle." She sat up. "Always in the middle. I never hear the end."

Ferra looked at the seven marks.

"Only my mother knows the end," said Ilenne. "I don't. Nobody else. Nobody in my mother's house hears it, only me, and I sleep." She pushed the book back across the table. "So I count. Then I stop. In the middle. Like sleep." She looked at Ferra with her eyes very bright. "Now my mother knows it's me. And she knows I am not afraid. You can't sleep when you're afraid."

Ferra sat with that for a long time.

Then she drew a careful line under the seven marks, and wrote beside them, in her own hand, the night count, the first seven, hers. The rest is her mother's. And then, smaller, because it was true, and because Saban would want it there and she'd rather it was in her hand than his: Sent 29 Aug from the wall water, in her voice, by the shoals. Anyone listening for her voice has heard it.

"Saban," said Ilenne, reading it upside down. She'd got the name, and the shape of it.

"Saban."

"Saban says: we don't know if she believes." Ilenne said it with a fair imitation of the reasonable voice. "Until she answers."

"Saban says that about everything."

"Yes," said Ilenne. She looked out of the pool door at the black water moving under the half roof, and the tide coming in through the grating, cold and salt and full of stars, the way it did every night now. "Saban is right."

Ferra blew on the ink.

Upstairs, across the foot of the long ash bed, Imani said something in her sleep that sounded like three, and turned over, and was quiet.
