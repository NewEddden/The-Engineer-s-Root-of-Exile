Chapter 23 - Every Able Hand

The story the town told about him had no mistakes in it.

He came in the spring. He told the low streets their wells were poison. A boy who kept his door was killed on the mill road in the summer. The baron went north and did not come back, and now the prince sat in the baron's keep and did the baron's business. Every fact in it was true, and the thread the town ran through them was that he had come for the barony and taken it, and he could not find one place in the whole story where a correction would go.

So he stopped looking for one, and went to work, and the town kept its distance.

People came up to the keep now. They came with leases and boundaries and debts, with a roof or a grievance, and they stood in the gate yard in a patient line and told him their business and went away again. Nobody came to offer him anything. He needed hands for a flume on the aqueduct, a room for the sick, a school, and he could not get any of it done without people who did not stand in that line.



Ferra's count took nineteen days.

She built it out of everything Kaldo had ever written down about itself, which was not much: the tax roll, the tithe map, Doran's book, the list of the unrecorded dead that Emeric had copied out of the back of Armand's notebook for her, and her own door count, walked one street at a time with a slate and a stub of chalk. She had asked at doors what nobody in the keep had ever asked, and wrote down what she was told, and where she was lied to she wrote that down too.

He came down to Bram's kitchen on the nineteenth evening, because she had sent word that it was done and he was to bring nothing. Bram was still at the forge. The lamp was lit, the roll was open, and her pages were stacked square beside it.

"Three thousand, four hundred and twelve," she said, before he had sat down. "Inside the walls. That's the roll."

"And yours?"

"Two thousand, eight hundred and seventy-six."

Five hundred and thirty-six people who existed in the barony's books and nowhere else. He had planned every week since April against the bigger number. He had sized wells and soap and fuel against it, and none of it had been badly wrong, because none of it had been close enough to the edge for a sixth to matter. The next things would be.

"How many can read?"

"You tell me."

He thought about it honestly: a town of nearly three thousand, a parish priest, a castle, a guild of nobody. "Two hundred."

"Ninety-three." She did not look up. "And some of those can read their own name and nothing after it. Three hundred and some can figure, if you count women who keep a house on nothing, which I do." She turned a page. "Nobody can cut a rune. I asked at every door. That column's empty."

It was a bad number. It was also the first true one anybody had put in front of him about this town, and it had come with the sum showing.

The roll still taxed the dead. Nobody had struck a name out of it in four years, because striking a name meant somebody had to know the name was gone and care enough to say so, and nobody had. So she was doing it now, house by house, with Doran's book open on one side and Emeric's list on the other. A single line through each name, and the source written small beside it: the page in Doran's book, or a ring and a month from the list.

She worked steadily. Every page or so she reached a house with a gap in it, and a line went through, and the source went in beside it, and she turned the page.

He was reading her pages upside down, as he had read her book all summer, and so he saw the street before she reached it.

The lane that ran behind the forge, the house at the end of it. Three names under it in the roll's old brown clerk's hand. Bram's first, with smith after it. Then a woman's name. Then Ferra's.

On the facing page, in Ferra's own count, the same house had two.

He did not know the name. He knew where he had heard it, though: at Doran's table in the spring, among the first of the plague pages, read out by the priest and passed over by Imani without a word, just as Bram's muttered sums stopped. He knew which chair at this table had a bad leg that nobody had fixed. And he knew what he had seen from a window in the keep in the week before the road: the first cart going out by the South Gate, early, before the market was up, and a big man walking at the tail of it with one hand flat on the boards.

Her pen was already moving down the page toward it.

"Let me do this one," he said. "I'll take the column. You've been at it nineteen days."

"No."

She did not stop, or look up, or change her pace. The pen reached the house. She drew a line through the woman's name, one stroke, the same weight as every other line on the page, and wrote the source beside it, a page number in Doran's book and the year, and blotted it, and turned the page.

The next house had four names and four living people in it, and she wrote four in her count and went on.

He did not say anything else about it, then or after. He sat with his hands on the table and read upside down while she struck the dead out of the barony's book one at a time, and when she had finished the roll she squared the pages and said it would do for a start, and he went back up the hill.

He did not ask Bram. There was nothing he could ask that Bram had not already answered, before the market was up, with one hand on the cart.



Rylan walked him down to the east bridge two days later, to look at the old exchange.

The town watched them go by. A woman stopped sweeping. Two men on a bench by the canal ring went quiet and started again when they had passed. Nobody shouted and nobody spat, and nobody nodded either.

"They're not frightened of you," Rylan said.

"Bram said the same."

"Bram's right." Rylan kept walking. "They're waiting. The baron was the only thing in Kaldo that stayed, and now he's gone." He glanced sideways. "They think everyone is temporary. You included."

"So what changes it?"

"Somebody has to be first." Rylan looked at the bridge, not at him. "Somebody they already trust has to stand next to you where everyone can see, and say so. Then the next one isn't first."

They had reached the exchange. Rylan stopped at its door and stood aside to let him in first.

"My lord," he said.

It was the first time. Emeric heard it, and went in, and did not say anything about it, because anything he said would have made Rylan take it back.

He knew who first meant. There was one man in Kaldo whose word could carry the forge district and half the lower ward with it, and Emeric had stood at his back for six hours in the spring to be allowed to borrow it once. He had borrowed it. The town had listened about the water because of it.

He was not going to ask for it a second time. Not out loud, with the town watching. A name that is spent in public stays spent.

For two days he did not go down the hill. He sent what Ferra needed by Rylan, and did his work in the keep, and when Hale mentioned that the forge had asked after the drawbridge chain he sent the measurements down on a slate.



On the Friday, Bram came up to the keep.

He came at the worst possible hour for anyone who wanted to be private, at mid-morning with the market at its fullest and the petition line out past the gate, in his apron, with his sleeves still rolled. He did not come round to the side door. He walked up the middle of the gate yard, past the line, and stopped in front of the table where Emeric sat with Hale, and said it loud enough to reach the gate.

"He earned it."

The yard went quiet in pieces, the near end first.

"I said in the spring I wasn't vouching for him. Not yet." Bram did not look at Emeric. He was looking at the line, and past it, at the gate and the market behind it. "I'm vouching now. He stood at my back six hours and didn't say a word. He went to the priest the right way. He paid for what he broke before anybody asked him to. And this summer he set a wage for the rows and kept himself out of it, because Doran told him to, and he did as he was told." He wiped his hands on the apron, slowly. "That's what I've seen. You've seen the same, most of you, and you're waiting for somebody else to say it."

Somebody near the gate said something Emeric did not catch.

"If he's what half of you say he is," Bram said, without turning, "then I'll wear it. So will mine." He let that sit. "I'd rather wear it than stand here and watch this town die politely."

He turned to Emeric then, for the first time.

"Muster's Tuesday. In the square. I've told them."

"I didn't ask you to."

"I know." Bram looked at him with something close to irritation. "You stopped coming down the hill instead. I can count days."

Then he went back down the middle of the yard the way he had come, and the line let him through, and the market closed behind him.

The first cart had gone out before the market was up. This had been the other way round, as near the middle of the day as a man could make it. Emeric sat at the table with the next petition in front of him and read its first line four times.



On Tuesday the square held more people than Doran had seen in it since the plague. He said so afterward, to nobody in particular, the way he might have entered it in his book.

They came because Bram had told them to, and to see what the prince would say when there was no baron in the chair beside him. Nobody cheered. They filled the square and the steps of the church and the mouths of the lanes, and they waited, and Emeric stood on the church step with Ward one step below him and told them what he wanted.

He kept it short, because Bram had told him to keep it short.

The old exchange, at the bridge foot, would be a school. Any child who wanted to come could come. They would be fed at the door, every morning they came. And any household that lost a child's work by sending that child would be paid for the work, every week, so that nobody had to choose between a loaf and a letter.

Then he told them the count, as the barony's count, without saying whose hand had made it: two thousand eight hundred and seventy-six inside the walls, ninety-three who could read, nobody at all who could cut a rune. He watched the number go through the square. Most of them had not known there were so few of them. Some of them had known it exactly, to the house, and that was worse to watch.

"And who teaches?" somebody called.

"Anyone with a trade." He had expected the question and still had not found a better way to say it. "Every trade in Kaldo teaches some mornings. Whatever you know that a child doesn't, and should."

That went down badly.

Bram, from the front, in a voice like a door being shut: "Fortnightly. And they stand back from the fire."

Then Abloh, from the edge of the square, who had come up from the soap yard with lye still on his sleeves, and said he supposed somebody ought to show them how not to burn their hands, since half of Kaldo had learned the other way. A mason said a morning a month, and no more, and he would want it written down. Imani, from the church wall, with her arms folded: fortnightly, and she would choose which morning, and nobody was to send her a child with a cough and call it a pupil.

It was grudging, every word of it. He took it.

Then came the hours. He did not set them. He asked the square when a child could be spared, and the square told him. Mornings, a woman said. Not early mornings, said another, the cows. Late, then. Three days in seven, said a man at the back, and not four, not with the harvest. Never in the harvest, said everybody. It took twenty minutes and three arguments, and at the end of it the households had set the hours themselves, out loud, in front of each other, and Emeric wrote down what they had settled and read it back to them, and nobody changed it.

The question he had been waiting for came from near the front.

An old man from the west farms asked it. He stood like a man who had walked the same strips of ground every spring for fifty years and saw no reason to stand any differently for a prince, and he had a voice like a gate that wanted oil.

"You'll pay the farms for the hands they haven't got."

"Yes."

"Every week."

"Every week."

"From where?"

"From me."

The old man looked at him as he might have looked at a field somebody had promised would double its yield.

"I'll believe the school when I see it," he said.

Not one name from the west farms went down that afternoon. A good many people watched the old man not give any, and some of them went home without giving theirs either.

At the far side of the square, at the mouth of the lane that ran down toward the north well, Emeric saw Sela. She stood with her arms folded and her back to the wall, and when his eyes found her she was already looking somewhere else. When he looked again the lane mouth was empty.



That night he went down the hill again, because there was nothing left to avoid.

Ferra had the cost before he sat down. Bread at the door from the shop's takings, which the shop could carry. The households' pay from his purse, which it could not carry for long. She had the numbers of the children who might come and the numbers who might not, and both columns came out to the same answer by different roads.

"From your purse," she said. "Not the barony's."

"Yes."

"By the end of the winter it costs you more than your four guards."

"Yes."

She waited with the pen lifted. When he said nothing else, she wrote something small at the foot of the page, a month, and turned the page over so it lay face down on the table.

He did not ask which month. He had a fair idea.

He showed her the other thing instead: three lines in the back of Armand's notebook, written on the walk down. No child under twelve at the kiln, the tannery pits, the salt pans or the barges. No child at any work more than half a day. No child kept from the exchange by anybody who paid for their time.

She read them twice.

"Who checks?"

He did not have an answer, and said so.

"Then it's not a law." She slid the notebook back across the table. "A law nobody can afford to keep is just a list of people you'll fine."

He looked at the three lines. She was right, and the lines were also right, and both things were going to stay true until something changed that he could not change tonight.

He did not cross them out.

"When I can pay for it," he said, and closed the notebook.

Bram came in from the forge as he was leaving, and the two of them met in the doorway, and Bram stood aside to let him past. Neither of them said anything about the Friday. Bram nodded at the door, and Emeric nodded back, and went up the hill in the dark with the lamps of the gate yard ahead of him and the town, for once, not watching.
