Chapter 23 - Every Able Hand

The version of him circulating in Kaldo that autumn was not a lie, which was the difficulty with it.

A prince arrived in the spring. Within a month the healer was going door to door telling people their wells were poison, which nobody had said in thirty years. In the autumn a guard's boy was killed on the mill road for no reason anybody could name. And then the baron who had held this land for thirty years was taken up the north road by that prince and hanged in the capital, and the prince came back and moved into his house.

Every one of those is true. Put them in order and add one line of connective tissue, which is that the prince came here to take the barony and took it, and you have something that no amount of argument gets underneath, because there is nothing in it to correct.

He tried to argue it once, to Hale, over a supper neither of them ate. He heard himself doing it about four sentences in and stopped.

So he did the sensible thing first.

Paid work, posted at the gate and in the market and read out at the parish door by Doran on two consecutive weeks. The quarry, the diggings, the woodcut, the kiln, the yard. Good money, above what anyone in Kaldo remembered being paid for anything, and paid weekly rather than at the end of a season.

Eleven men came.

Two of them were already on his payroll. One was drunk.

Ferra's register, which was not finished and was already the most useful document in the barony, said that there were something over four hundred people in Kaldo who could hold a tool for a day. Eleven of them were willing to be seen taking his coin.

The register came back to him that same week with a correction written across the front of it, and the correction was not about work.

The barony's tax roll said two thousand three hundred. It had said two thousand three hundred for nine years, which should have been the first question anybody asked about it, because no number that describes people stays the same for nine years.

Her headcount said one thousand eight hundred and forty-seven. She had built it out of the tithe rolls, the parish book, Imani's list of the plague dead, and a physical count of occupied dwellings, which she had done herself, on foot, over eleven days, in the rain.

Four hundred and fifty-three people who existed on paper and nowhere else. Some were plague dead nobody had ever struck off, because striking a name off was somebody's job and that somebody had died. Some had left in the war years and never been removed. And some had never been real at all, because a barony's obligations run off its numbers, and Wexler had inflated them in one column and deflated them in another depending on who was going to read it.

Emeric sat with that for most of a night.

Kaldo had been over four thousand before the war. Everyone in this town told you, in the same flat way they told you about the weather, that the plague and the war had taken it down to a little over two. They were wrong, and they had been wrong for a decade, and nobody had ever found out because counting was nobody's job in particular.

Every plan he had made in four months had been made against a number that was wrong by almost a quarter, in the direction that mattered.

He asked her twice whether she was certain. She said she had counted doors, that doors did not move, and that if he wanted a third figure he could go and count them himself.

The garrison roll was the other lie and she found it by the same method in the same fortnight, which was to say that she went and looked. Fifty-eight names drawing pay against a body of men that nobody had ever stood in a yard and counted.

He did nothing about the second one yet. He had forty-odd armed men with nothing to do and he was about to have a great deal for them to do.

His read on the eleven men was that the town was frightened of him.

Rylan corrected it over the course of a walk down to the Left Bridge, gently, and took about a mile to do it.

"They're not frightened." He said it to the water rather than to Emeric. "Frightened people stay in. Half that market was out watching you ride up the hill."

"Then what."

"They're waiting." He walked a bit further. "A man who takes the crown's money has picked a side in a thing, and nobody here is confident the thing is finished. The last man who ran this place ended on a rope. The one before him died of the plague in his own hall. The one before that sent their sons to a war nobody ever explained to them, and about a third of the sons came back."

"So they think I'm temporary."

"They think everyone is temporary." Rylan shrugged, which on him was a considered gesture. "They will not be first. That is the whole of it, my lord. Somebody has to be first."

Emeric worked out who it had to be in under a minute, and then did not go to the forge.

He did not go on the Tuesday. He did not go on the Wednesday. He went as far as the end of the forge district on the Wednesday evening and found something urgent to look at on the way, and was fully aware of what he was doing while he did it.

He had spent five months taking things from that man in increments. An afternoon. A name to a priest. A design corrected. A word dropped in the right ear at the right moment, always small enough that Bram could tell himself it had cost him nothing.

This was not an increment. This was Bram's name, permanently, spent on a prince, in front of everyone he had ever shod a horse for.

So Emeric did the other thing, which was nothing, and he was still doing nothing on the Friday when Bram came to him.

He did it in the yard, in daylight, with the gate open and enough people in earshot that it could never afterward be walked back.

"I said to you in my shop that I was not vouching." He said it at ordinary volume, which in that yard carried perfectly well. "Not yet. I said I would walk you to the priest and no further, and that what this town made of you afterward was yours to earn and not mine to hand you."

He looked at Emeric for a moment.

"He earned it."

That was the speech. Bram was not a man who made them and he did not make one here.

Then he said the part that cost him, and he said it to the yard rather than to Emeric.

"If he turns out to be what half of you think he is, I will have been the man who stood here and said otherwise. In daylight. I will wear that, and so will mine." He picked his cap up off the post. "I have decided I would rather wear that than watch this place die politely for another eleven years."

The muster was eight days later and Bram called it himself.

The forge district came because every man in it owed Bram work, and most of them owed him more than work. The rest came because the forge district came. The farms came because word went west with the Monday carts. By the middle of the morning there were more people standing in the market square of Kaldo than had been in one place in that town since the plague years, and a good number of them had come to look at the crowd rather than at him, which was fine.

The children came too.

Of course they came. Half of them had been working since they could carry a bucket, because a house that lost a father to the war works everyone it has got, and a boy of eleven on a spoil heap is a copper and a half a day, and a copper and a half a day is the difference between a winter and a bad winter.

Emeric turned them away.

He did it badly. He did it from the steps, in a sentence that came out as a prohibition, delivered in the voice of a man reading a rule, and the square turned on him inside five seconds.

It was the most hostile half minute he had had in Kaldo, and it was worse than the well district in the summer, because in the well district they had only thought he was mad. Here they understood him perfectly. He had just walked out onto a set of steps and taken money out of forty households in front of the households.

That is not a principle to a woman running a house on nothing. It is a wage she was counting on, removed by a man who has never in his life needed one.

The one who said it at volume was a cooper.

Emeric had walked past his shop in the spring without being told whose it was and without registering anything about it except that the shutters were up and the hinges had gone orange. He had made barrels for a port that had stopped needing barrels, in a town that had stopped sending anything anywhere, and he had held on for eleven years and then stopped holding on.

His name was Hollis. He had three children. Two of them worked.

He was not being difficult and he was not performing for the crowd, and he asked one question, and it was the right one.

"What does the prince intend to give those households instead. And when."

Emeric knew he was right while the man was still asking it.

So he did not defend the rule. He made an offer against it, and he made it up standing there.

Nobody under working age goes on the Works. That stays. And any household that loses a child's wage by it has that wage made up out of the barony, at the child's rate, weekly, for as long as the rule stands.

He heard Ferra, behind him and to the left, stop writing.

They had not discussed it. There was no line for it anywhere in her sheets and no room for it in the season. She was doing the arithmetic in real time and her face was doing something he had never seen it do, and she could see him inventing it on the steps, which was the part he was going to think about later.

"It is not charity and it is not a gift," he said, "and I would be obliged if nobody described it as either. It is a payment. I am paying you to keep your children out of a quarry."

Then the reason, which he kept short, because the long version would have sounded like a sermon and he had nothing to spend on a sermon.

Children who work do not stop working. They work instead of learning to do anything else, and then they are twenty and they can carry, and that is all they can do, and they put their own children on the heap. A barony that spends its children now does not have anything in twenty years except more of what it has today.

And then he said that he intended to build a school, and that he did not know when, and that he was not going to promise a date.

He said the last part deliberately and out loud, because the one thing he could not afford in that square was a promise he missed.

"I will believe the school when I see it," Hollis said.

"That is fair," Emeric said, "and I would not believe it either."

Hollis was not converted and did not pretend to be, and went back to standing with his arms folded, and took work on the quarry gang the following week and was one of the four best men on it inside a month.

The rest of the terms took twenty minutes and Emeric was completely aware of what he was doing while he did it.

He had heard every one of these lines in another life, out of the mouths of men who had no intention whatsoever of delivering any of them, in rooms with carpet, in a language these people had never heard of.

Pay above the standard rate, set, posted on a board at the yard where anybody could go and look at it, and paid weekly.

The same rate for the same work regardless of who did it. That one got the most argument in the square and the least from the women in it.

A day's rest in seven.

Food on site at noon, because a man fed at noon works the afternoon, and a man who is not does half of it badly and the other half dangerously.

Injury pay. If the Works breaks you, the Works keeps paying you. If it kills you, your household keeps a share.

He did not know yet how he was going to fund that at any scale and he did not say so.

And safety rules, which landed as the strangest thing he said all day, and he had not expected that at all. He had expected the wage to be the strange part. But nobody in that square had ever encountered a rule that existed in order to prevent a thing rather than to punish one after it happened, and several people asked him afterward what the penalty was, and did not really believe the answer.

The thing that convinced them came two weeks later and it was not a speech.

A crew boss on the kiln cut sent a man home at the middle of the morning for working off a bad staging. Not for being idle. Not for being drunk. For standing on something that had been put together wrong by somebody else.

And the man was paid for the day.

The town could not make sense of it. Paid for a day he had not worked, over a thing that had not gone wrong, on the word of a boss who had gained nothing by sending him off. It was discussed in the market for a solid week. Two people came up the hill to ask whether it was true.

It did more for Emeric's standing than the wage did, and he understood why and wrote it down. A wage is a transaction. Anybody can offer one and half the men offering one are lying. That was evidence.

Ferra came at him three days after the muster, privately, with numbers, and she was right.

Above-standard pay. Child wage replacement for forty-odd households. Injury pay with no fund behind it. Crews fed at noon. In a barony whose entire surplus was a soap trade eleven weeks old and one thin harvest.

She could carry it for a season. She could not carry it for two. If the foundry ran late, or the harvest came in poor, he would be choosing between paying his people and feeding them, and both of those were the same people.

Emeric's answer was honest and it was not reassuring.

He knew. He was betting that a barony working at twice the pace paid for itself, and he could not prove it, and if he was wrong the first thing that broke was a promise he had made on a set of steps in public to most of the town.

He asked her to tell him the month before it broke, rather than the week.

She said that she would. She wrote the month she expected it, in her own book, and did not show him.

Then, on the way out, without looking back at him:

"That sentence on the steps is the most expensive thing anyone has ever said in that square." A pause at the door. "I would have said it too."

It was the closest thing to approval he had ever had from her and he thought about it for considerably longer than the remark warranted.

The garrison went on the Works at the same rate as everybody else.

Forty-odd armed men had been drilling in the lower ward for a season with nothing at the end of the drilling, and Emeric put them on the quarry and the woodcut and the kiln cut and paid them the posted wage for it.

Some took it as what it was, which was work with money at the end of it. Some took it as an insult, on the grounds that a soldier is not a laborer, and said so within his hearing, which he permitted.

He set up no formal sorting, and never did. He did not have to. Ward stood in that yard for the better part of a year watching which men carried, which complained, which were good with a crew and which were only good with a grievance, and by the time anybody got around to a roll, most of the work had already been done by a man leaning on a post.

The class ran three evenings a week in the parish hall, which Doran offered before Emeric had finished asking.

Measurement first.

There is no common unit in Randillia below the crown standards, and the crown standards are cut for tax and for stone and are no use whatsoever to a man making a bracket. Every workshop in the kingdom works off its own master's rule. Bram's foot was not Colm's foot. Neither of them was the foot the quarry gang had been using since before the war, which had come off a dead man's stick.

So Emeric defined one.

And the important part of that evening, the part he thought about for weeks afterward, was that he could not justify it. A man at the back asked why that length and not a longer one, and it was a perfectly reasonable question asked in a perfectly reasonable tone, and Emeric had no answer at all, because on Earth the answer had been three thousand years of accident and argument and nobody in this room was going to be helped by hearing about any of it.

What he said instead was that it did not matter which length. It mattered that it was the same length in every hand in this barony. And that once it was chosen it could never be changed, by him or by anybody after him, because the day you change it every rule cut before that day becomes a lie.

Bram cut the master rule in iron. Twelve divisions to the foot, the divisions scribed and then struck deeper so wear would not eat them, and it lived on a bracket in the yard where anybody could walk up to it.

Every rule in Kaldo was copied off it directly. Copies of copies were forbidden, which was the first rule Emeric made that nobody argued with, on account of nobody yet understanding why it mattered.

Then numerals, because half the room could not read a figure.

Ferra took that side of the teaching and was better at it than he was, by a distance neither of them enjoyed discussing. She had no patience and enormous clarity, and she would not let a single person in that hall nod along at something they had not understood. She caught it every time. She caught it in men twice her age who had been nodding along at things for forty years.

Emeric was a worse teacher than either of them, and everybody in the hall could tell, including the eleven year olds who were not allowed to work and were therefore, by the rule he had invented on the steps, available on a Tuesday evening.

The first real snow came in the last week of the month.

The rolls were full. The class ran three nights. The quarry gang had a foreman, chosen by the gang, which was Rylan's idea and a better one than Emeric's. There was a stack of pegs and a hundred and twenty feet of measured rope sitting in the yard waiting on a field that was under frost until the thaw.

Emeric stood in a yard that had held eleven men and a drunk five weeks earlier, and worked out what had actually turned this barony.

It had not been the map. It had not been the wells, or the soap, or the trial, or the wage.

It was one blacksmith standing in daylight and saying a sentence.

He had never been less able to take credit for anything, and he had never had less interest in doing so, and both of those were new.
