Chapter 23 - What the Labour Doesn't Need

Ferra brought him the second half of the number on a hot morning, and this time she sat down before she said it, which he had learned meant she expected an argument and intended to win it sitting.

"Four hundred hands," she said. "Which you have, and which I did not believe you would get, and I have written in the book that I did not believe it, because you will otherwise tell people I was encouraging." She turned the book round on the table. "Cutting is covered. Binding is covered. Carrying is covered, barely, if the weather holds and nobody's ox dies."

"And threshing."

"Three hundred and ten man-days, and not one of them can be moved."

"Say the shape of it."

"That is the shape of it. Cutting spreads." She put her finger on the column. "If a division comes ready early you cut it early and you carry it early and the work smears out over three weeks and the hands you have are enough because they are not all wanted on the same day. Threshing does not smear. It happens after the cutting, on a barn floor, on sheaves you have already got in, and every day a sheaf sits unthreshed is a day something is eating it or wetting it. So the whole three hundred and ten falls inside about eleven days, and the eleven days sit on top of the carrying, and I have put that number in this column in the spring and in the summer and I will put it in again in the autumn and it does not get smaller."

Emeric looked at the two columns next to one another. It was, he thought, the most honestly arithmetic problem anyone had handed him since the wells, and it had the same shape the wells had, which was that nobody had done anything wrong and nobody had counted.

"Nessa's brother won't fix it," he said.

"Nessa's brother is one man with a cart and a wife who is not well, and there are twenty-five more like him, and between them they have closed a wound and left me a shortfall. I said that was an improvement and it is." Ferra shut the book on her finger. "It is still a shortfall, and it is all in one fortnight, and you cannot hire out of Wayford in the fortnight Wayford is cutting its own."

"Then the hands don't exist."

"The hands do not exist in that fortnight," she agreed. "Which is the thing you have been walking round for four days without saying, so say it."

He said it. "Then the work has to stop needing them."

He got the pouch out that afternoon, in the long room, with Bram on one side of the table and Ward on the other, because he had decided some months ago that he would never again take a stone out of that pouch with nobody watching him do it.

There were nine.

They had been uncut when his father gave them to him, in a leather pouch with fifty gold, as a gift that had been meant to be read as a wage. Nine was what was left of a number he had never written down at the start, which he regarded as one of the worse pieces of bookkeeping of his life.

"Ferra," he said. "Open a column."

"For stones."

"For stones. Count it weekly, in front of me, and post the count where the two of you can see it, and when I ask you for one out of turn I want the number said out loud before you hand it over."

Ward said, "You've never asked me for one."

"No. And I want to say the rest of it in front of Bram, because he's the man who could do it and I don't want him ever to have to refuse me." Emeric put his hand flat on the table. "Nothing comes out of the four suits. Not in a bad month, not in a bad week, not if I'm standing in the yard with the thing half built and one stone short and a good reason. Not ever."

"There's four crystals in that armoury doing nothing eleven months of the year," Ward said, mildly, in the voice he used when he was checking a thing rather than arguing it.

"There's four crystals in that armoury doing nothing eleven months of the year and being the reason four men come home in the twelfth." Emeric looked at him. "And a suit a man knows can be taken apart for parts isn't a suit. It's a store I've promised not to open, and a store I've promised not to open gets opened, because I'll be the one deciding and I'll have a reason and the reason will be good. So I'm telling you now, while nothing is on fire, that the answer is no, and I'd like the two of you to hold me to a thing I said when it was easy."

Bram, who had not spoken, picked up one of the nine and turned it over between two fingers that were each about the width of it.

"Five of these," he said. "For what you've been drawing."

"Five."

"Then say four when you're done, and don't say it like it's a lot."

The thresher took eleven days and was, in the end, a drum.

It was a drum with beater bars on it, in a frame, over a slatted table, and a belt ran off it down to a Force Crystal seated in a housing bolted to the floor of Maren's second barn, which she had lent on the condition that she got first use and that Emeric personally carried out anything of hers he moved. A second housing at the head of the table held a Sense Rune, which read what was coming under the drum and reported it to a plate Emeric could see. A third, on the drum's own shaft, was the Kinetic Rune, which put a short hard shove into each beater bar at the moment it came down, so that the bar hit harder than its own weight, and did it four hundred times a minute without a man in the world getting tired.

Ward came out on the fourth day, watched the drum turn for a while, and then said, "That's mine."

"That's yours."

"That's the rune off my bracer. The one that throws a man's fist."

"It's the same rune. It doesn't know the difference between a fist and a beater bar and it never did." Emeric wiped his hands. "You've been walking round for a year with a farm implement strapped to your arm."

"I'll not be telling the garrison that."

"I'll be telling them that. Repeatedly."

The other thing in the barn, which nobody looked at twice and which had taken him longer to design than the drum, was a crank.

It came off the drum shaft through a dog and a pin, and if the belt broke, or the Force Crystal went flat, or every stone in Kaldo went inert on the same afternoon for reasons nobody in this world had ever satisfactorily explained to him, then two men on the crank turned the drum and the drum threshed. Slower. Very much slower, at about a third of what four men with flails could do, so that it was not a solution and had never been meant as one.

Halloway asked him why he'd bothered, since a machine at a third of a flail was worse than a flail.

"Because a machine at a third of a flail on the morning the magic dies is a barn with a floor and a crank and eleven days of light left in it," Emeric said. "And a machine at nothing is a barn with four hundred sheaves in it and a man standing in the doorway working out how long it takes to build a flail."

"You think that'll happen."

"I think I've no idea, which is the whole reason. I don't know what these are." He nodded at the housing. "I know what they do. Nobody in this barony including me knows what they are, and I'm not going to put a barony's winter behind a thing I can't explain, so everything I build has a stupid version underneath it that works by being made of iron."

They ran the first division on a Thursday and it worked, and Emeric had the specific pleasure of watching Maren's two sons and two of Nessa's brother's cousins put through in one afternoon what would have taken eleven men three days, and then Ferra found the crack.

She found it in the count, which was the only place it was findable.

She had, without being asked, put a hand-flailed control through off the same division on the same day, because she said she had no way to know whether a number was good if she had nothing to hold it against, and the flailed sample came out with four in a hundred grains cracked and the drum's came out with nineteen.

"Nineteen," Emeric said.

"Nineteen. Which mills, and which stores badly, and which is worth about a twentieth of the pile off the top if you sell it as feed instead of seed or bread." She had it written already. "And I want it noted that I did not find this. I built a thing to find it with and the thing found it. You have made me quite unbearable on this subject."

The reason took him an hour and a half and he was angry for about half of it.

He had set the Force Crystal's output in June. He had set it properly, off a real sample, in the way he had set everything all year, which was to take a measurement and work backward from it: a bundle of straw out of division three, weighed, resisted, timed, and a figure written down and carved into the housing so that the machine came down on the grain with exactly as much as it needed and no more.

Division three in June had thick straw.

The straw this year, on the divisions that had gone in after the ring closed, on ground that had been watered properly for the first time in forty years, had come up tall and had come up thin at the head, because that was what happened when you gave a plant water and it spent it going upward.

Halloway had told him this twice.

He had told him once at the ford, in passing, walking, in the middle of a conversation about the low gates, and he had said the straw was thin at the head this year and light in the hand and he'd not seen that before. And he had said it again eight days later standing in division seven, differently, with a stalk in his hand, and Emeric had been thinking about a drum.

Emeric went and found him and said it out loud in front of his own sons and two carters.

"You told me the straw was thin. Twice. I heard it twice and filed it as weather both times, and I've cracked nineteen grains in a hundred out of division five because I set a machine off a sample I took in June out of a division that grew a different crop to the one we're cutting." He said it flat, without decoration, because he had learned that decoration made people think he was being gracious rather than accurate. "That's mine and it's stupid and it's costing us. What did you mean by it, the second time. In your own words, and take as long as you want, because I clearly can't be trusted to get it in four."

Halloway took a while.

"I meant it changes," he said eventually. "That's all I meant. Straw's not the same in June as it is in August and it's not the same on five as it is on three and it's not the same after a wet week as it is after a dry one. Every man who's ever flailed knows that in his arms. You swing softer at thin straw. You don't decide to. Your arms do it because you can feel it under the flail."

"And my machine can't feel it."

"Your machine's a very strong man who's been told once what he's hitting and never looks down."

The first correction took him a morning and was wrong, or rather it was right and small, and he knew that while he was doing it, which did not stop him doing it, because division five was on the floor and needed threshing that week.

He re-sampled off division five, reset the housing to division five's number, and cracked four in a hundred instead of nineteen. Which fixed division five, and would need doing again for six, and again for the resting divisions next year, and again after any wet week, and would need a man with a scale and an hour every time, and that man would be him, forever, because nobody else in Kaldo could read the housing.

The second correction took four days and was the actual answer, and it had been sitting in the machine since the first afternoon.

The Sense Rune was already reading what came under the drum. He had put it there to tell him things. It reported to a plate, and he looked at the plate, and then he made a decision, and the decision was the slow part and the stupid part and the part made out of a prince.

So he took the plate off and ran the Sense Rune into the Force Crystal's own housing instead, so that the reading set the output directly, continuously, four hundred times a minute, with nobody looking at anything. And because a single pass could not then be tuned for both the thick stalk and the thin head in the same sheaf, he split it: a first pass at whatever the rune called for on the head, taking the loose grain off cheap, and a second pass behind it, harder, on what was left.

Two stages. It was slower per sheaf than the wrong version had been and it put through more in a day because it stopped stopping.

Cracked grain came out at three in a hundred, which was better than the flail, and Emeric did not say so to anybody for two days on the grounds that it would sound like a boast and he had not earned one.

What he wrote in the list, that night, was shorter than usual.

Halloway told me the straw was thin. Twice. Both times I was inside a drum. The rune that fixed it was in the machine on the first day, doing the one thing I am worst at trusting a machine to do, which is deciding. I had built it to report to me because I wanted to be the one who decided, and nineteen grains in a hundred is what that cost.

The drying shed was seven days, mostly carpentry, and its failure was not a design failure in any way that let him feel clever about it afterward.

It was a shed with a slatted floor and a stone under it that moved warm air up through the slats, and it dried a load of grain in about a day and a half, and grain that came out of it went into a store without going mouldy in the third week, which had been happening in Kaldo for as long as anyone could remember and had been quietly costing the barony a portion of everything it grew.

The stone had no way to stop.

It moved warm air until somebody went in and took the load out, and a man was posted to do that, and the man was one of Ashe's cousins and entirely reliable, and on the fourth day a cart went over on the mill road with a woman and two children under the load of it and he was the nearest man with a horse.

He was gone four hours and it was the correct four hours. Emeric was very clear about that with everyone who wanted to discuss it, including the man himself, who came to the long room the next morning with his hat in his hands and got about six words out.

"You left a shed to go under a cart," Emeric said. "If you'd stayed in the shed I'd have had to think about whether I wanted you in this barony. Nine bushels is a cheap price for finding out I'd built a thing that needs a man in it. Go home."

Nine bushels came out scorched and worth feeding to pigs, and the actual finding was worse than nine bushels, which was that the shed had been designed on the assumption of a person. Emeric had drawn a stone, and a floor, and a roof, and a man. The man was load-bearing and Emeric had not noticed putting him in.

He posted Tarrow's eldest to it, permanently, as an interim, which meant a young man with a good hand and a head for figures now spent his days standing in a warm shed with his fist in a pile of grain deciding whether it was dry, and Emeric wrote that down as a debt rather than a solution.

Then he went to see Bram, who was in the middle of moving a smithy, and asked him for a stone that could read grain and shut a shed.

"A reader on the floor," Bram said. "Same as the one going on my flue."

"Same idea. Different thing to read and it has to throw a catch when it's satisfied."

Bram thought about it with a crate of tongs in his arms.

"A fortnight," he said. "For a man working days. And I'm not going to be working days for about a month, in case you've forgotten what you've had me do."

"A fortnight's fine. It's fine for weeks."

"Aye, well." Bram put the crate on the cart. "You'll get it when you get it."

Maren asked about the work parts on the day they ran division six, which was the right day to ask and she had clearly chosen it.

"Eight parts in twenty, recut against days worked," she said. "My two boys live on that. They've no ground worth the word. So you've built a machine that does three hundred and ten days of work, and I'd like the arithmetic from you rather than off a board, please, in front of them."

Emeric did it standing up with a stick in the dirt, because he had found that a number in dirt was believed and a number on paper was suspected.

"This year it comes out for them," he said. "Eight in twenty is a proportion, not a number of days. If the season's total days go down and the pile goes up, then eight in twenty of a bigger pile split among the same hands is more, for fewer days on the field. Your boys get about a fifth again on what they got last year and they get eleven days of their own summer back."

"And next year."

"Next year it comes out against them, and I want to say that before either of them works out that I knew." He drew the second line. "Eight parts get recut against days worked among four hundred hands next season instead of two hundred and forty. Same eight parts. Nearly twice the hands standing in the queue for them. Their share goes down whatever happens to the pile, unless the pile nearly doubles, which it won't, because we've only got two thousand acres and we're already watering all of it."

Maren's elder son said, "Then the machine takes it off us."

"No. And I'd rather you were angry at the right thing than at me, so follow it." Emeric put the stick down. "The dilution's the twenty-six families. It's Nessa's brother and Nessa's brother's cousins and the eleven letters Imani wrote without asking me, and they're in the queue for the same eight parts, and they'd be in that queue if I'd never drawn a drum. I solved the labour shortage twice in one year. The second solution eats the people the first one was for."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Halloway, who had come over, looked at the two lines in the dirt.

"Then the parts want recutting," he said.

"The parts want recutting and it's not mine to recut. It's twenty parts in the field's own book and it takes a majority of parts and a majority of households, and I've got four parts that don't vote and no business having an opinion in public." Emeric looked at Maren. "I'll say it once in this field and not again. If somebody brings a proposal I'll write the arithmetic for it honestly whichever way it cuts, including against me. Bring it in the winter. Bring it before anybody's hungry, because a thing like that decided in March is decided by whoever's most frightened."

Maren considered him for long enough to be uncomfortable.

"You'd have been easier to like," she said, "if you'd just told me it came out for us."

"It does come out for you. This year."

"Aye," she said. "That's the bit I'd have believed."

The last stone went into a box.

It was a bin, strictly, six feet by four by five, stone-floored, timber-walled, roofed in slate that Emeric had argued about with Bram for two days, standing forty paces up the hill from the granary and nowhere near it. Inside the frame of the lid sat a Stasis working, which held whatever was under it in the state it went in, and it was the only thing Emeric built that year that came out of no measurement whatsoever.

It came off a plate on a wall.

He had read it at eight years old, in the long gallery at the palace, on a brass plate under a case of things nobody had used in two hundred years, waiting for a tutor who was late, and the working had gone in the way things go into an eight-year-old and stayed. He had not thought about it in fourteen years and he had not thought about it in the other life at all, and when it surfaced it surfaced whole, which he did not like and did not mention.

"One bin," he said, when Dunnel asked why one.

"You told me in the spring you wanted eight."

"I wanted eight and I'll want eight for years. Eight bins is the whole barony's harvest sitting where no single fire and no single roof and no single bad week can empty it, and it's the answer to a thing Ward's been telling me about since the rise." Emeric put his hand on the lid frame. "And I don't understand this one yet, and I'm not putting a barony's winter inside a thing I read off a wall when I was eight. So it's one. It holds one thing, and the one thing is the seed."

Dunnel stood and looked at the bin for a while. He had put nothing at all into the venture, argued the cap into being, and got the seed store walled off in language written twice in two sets of words so that no clerk could ever claim to have misread it, and Emeric had watched him do all three and had never once been able to decide whether the man liked him.

"It's outside the venture," Dunnel said. "The seed. On the page. It cannot be put in, valued, or pledged."

"It's outside the venture and it stays outside the venture and it's in a box on a hill I built with a stone out of my father's pouch, which cost the barony nothing and is therefore not the barony's and is therefore not the venture's either. I'll have the chandler write that as well if you want it."

"I want it."

"Then you'll have it. And Dunnel." Emeric waited until the man looked at him. "You'll want to know what happens to that seed if the working stops."

"I was going to ask that next."

"It's grain in a dry stone box with a slate roof forty paces from anything that burns. If every stone in the world went out tonight it'd be seed in a shed, which is what it was last year and the year before, and it would keep the way seed in a shed keeps, which is badly, but it would keep." He shrugged. "That's the whole rule I build to. The clever part makes it better. The stupid part makes sure it doesn't get worse than what you already had."

Dunnel nodded slowly. Then he went and got the first sack himself, off the cart, without being asked, and carried it up the hill and put it through the hatch, and stood there while the rest went in behind him.

Imani turned up at the bin an hour later, having not been invited, and stood watching the sacks go through with her arms folded.

"What's in there with the grain," she said.

"Nothing."

She let that sit for slightly too long.

"You answered that in half a second."

"Because it's nothing. It's a lid that holds a thing as it went in. Whatever goes in comes out the same."

"That's what I said. What's in there with it." Imani watched another sack go through the hatch. "Grain doesn't go into a store alone. It goes in with damp, and it goes in with weevil eggs, and it goes in with whatever was in the field, and in a normal store all of that gets on with its business and you can smell it by the third week and you know. That's not a complaint about your box. That's the only useful thing I know about stores."

Emeric opened his mouth to answer and found that he had four different answers and that all of them were about how the working held state, which was not what she had asked.

"I'll want to think about that," he said.

"Take six months. The seed comes out in the spring and not before, and if I ask you tomorrow you'll tell me something you thought of tonight, which is worth nothing to either of us." She turned to go down the hill. "So ask me in the spring, when the lid comes off, and I'll be standing there. Write down that I said I would."

He wrote it down that night, with the count underneath it, in Ferra's hand and initialled in his.
