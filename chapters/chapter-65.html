Chapter 65 - Forty Minutes

Imani had not moved.

She was where he had left her, at the table by the side door, with both hands flat on Tess's back and her weight on them, and her sleeves rolled to the shoulder and red to the elbow. The linen she had packed into the wound had gone dark through, and one of her women stood beside her with a fresh fold of it ready, waiting for the word. Tess lay face down on the scrubbed boards with her head turned to the side and her eyes shut and her mouth a little open. Her breathing was quick and light. Her face was the color of the boards.

The kit was on its cloth at the end of the table. The needles in their jar. The thread. The spirit. The stoneware bottle and the wire cage. The razor in its strip of leather.

Imani looked up when he came through the door. She saw his face, and the blood down the front of him from the nose, and the linen on his hands, and did not ask about any of it.

"Well?"

"It's clear."

"Clear for what?" She did not take her hands off Tess. "You said when it clears. I've held her. Here it is, clear. What can you do that you couldn't do an hour ago?"

He came round the table so that he was opposite her, across Tess, and put his wrapped hands on the edge of the boards.

"Nothing," he said. "Not with these. I can't hold a needle." He looked at her hands on Tess's back. "You can. And I can show you what's under them."

"Show me how?"

He took his mother's stone out of his pocket, where his hand always was. It was dull grey, with its fine lattice over every face and its edges gone soft from being carried, and it looked like nothing at all, and Imani looked at it the way she looked at anything somebody held out to her as a cure.

"There's a place I go," he said. "With this in my hand. Ward's seen me in it. He says from the outside it looks like a stroke." He made himself go on. "In there I can hold a thing in front of me and turn it over and see all of it at once. I've built the machine at the mill in there, piece by piece, before Bram cut any iron. I can build her back. What I know of how a back is made, and what my hands found in it, all night, on the floor of the powerhouse." He stopped. "And you can come in with me, if you let go. And an hour in there is five minutes out here."

Imani looked at him across the table. Her face did not change at all.

"Five minutes," she said.

"About that."

"And I'd see it. Inside her. Where it's cut."

"As near as the two of us can make it."

She looked down at Tess. Under her hands the linen was going dark again, slowly, from the middle out.

"What do I do?"



She gave her place to the woman with the fresh linen and showed her where to press and how hard, and the woman leaned her weight onto Tess's back with her lips pressed together. Imani wiped her hands on her apron. Then, because she was who she was, she washed them in the spirit to the wrist, and shook them dry in the air.

He sat down on a stool at the end of the table with the stone in his left hand.

"Two fingers on my wrist," he said. "The other one. Where you'd take a pulse."

She put them there, exactly, without having to look for it.

"Now don't count it."

"I always count it."

"Not this time. If you count, you're holding on, and if you're holding on I can't bring you. It finds you. It can't take you unless you let it." He did not know how else to say it, and then he did. "You've done it once already. In your own yard, with the basin. You went under, and you didn't fight it at the end."

"I fought it at the start."

"Everybody does."

She was quiet. Then she turned to the other women, who had stopped what they were doing all down the room and were watching, and told them that she and the prince were about to look as if they had both been struck, that they would look like it for about as long as it took to boil an egg, that nobody was to touch either of them or shout or send for anybody, and that whoever was nearest was to keep the pressure on that girl's back and not let up for anything. Then she sat down on the bench beside his stool, and put her two fingers back on his wrist, and shut her eyes.

He felt for the place behind the stone where the room was, and went.

And he felt her, on his wrist, at the edge of it, and he felt her start to hold on, like a person catching herself dropping off in church, and he felt her stop herself catching. It was like watching somebody step off a wall in the dark on purpose.

She came in.



It was not his room.

It had been his room for two years, and he knew every inch of it, the light that came from nowhere and the quiet and the long bench where he set things down to look at them. Now there was a scrubbed table in the middle of it that had not been there before, and a smell of vinegar and spirit and boiled linen, and along one wall a shelf of jars with their labels in a hand he did not know, and an old chair by a fire that was not lit. It was half hers. He had told Ferra that once, at her father's table, and not known what it would mean until now: with two of them in it, the room was built out of both.

Imani was standing at the table, looking at her hands.

They were clean. No blood, no spirit, no cracks from the lye. She turned them over, and then looked round the room, at the light, at the bench, at the shelf of jars, and her face did something he had never seen it do in two years, and then stopped doing it.

"That's my shelf," she said.

"Yes."

"Those are my labels."

"It's half yours. I can't build your half. You brought it."

She turned, slowly, and saw the chair by the fire that was not lit, with no dent in its cushion, and stopped.

She did not say anything about the chair. Neither did he. He had seen it once before, in a low front room that smelled of vinegar, and had not asked about it then either.

She looked at him for one long breath. Then she put the whole of it away somewhere behind her face, as she put away anything she could not use yet, and turned back to the table.

"Show me the girl," she said.



He built Tess's back on the table between them.

He built it out of everything he had. The anatomy plates in a book he had owned in the other life and never expected to need. What his own hands had felt, pressed on it in the powerhouse and all the way up the hill, under the linen: where the cut began, high on the left side above the hip, and which way it ran, across and inward, toward the spine, and where it stopped. He laid it out the size of life, skin and the fat under the skin and the long straps of muscle along the backbone, and the ribs coming down to their ends, and the backbone itself like a string of knuckles, and he opened the cut.

It was wrong. He knew it was wrong as soon as it was there. It was a picture out of a book, and it had the clean colors of a book.

Imani reached in and corrected it.

She did not ask him whether she could. She put her hand into the cut and moved a muscle he had put in the wrong place, so that it lay where muscles lay in every back she had ever washed. She thickened the fat. She took the clean colors out of everything and put in the real ones, the ones she had seen on her table every week since she was nineteen. The thing between them changed under her hands until it looked less like a page and more like a girl lying on boards, and he understood that it was more true now than he could have made it alone, because he had read about backs and she had held them.

"There," she said. "Now where's it bleeding from?"

He showed her. Deep in the cut, below the muscle, running round the side of the backbone toward the front, a vessel no thicker than a quill, cut clean across, both ends pulled back into the meat, as cut vessels did. That was where the blood was coming from. That was what the packing could slow and never stop.

"You tie it," he said. "Above the cut and below. Two ties on each end, close. With the thread boiled."

"Why two?"

"Because one slips."

She nodded, as if he had told her which way a bandage went.

He showed her the knot, slowly, the thread going round twice and then once, so that it would bite on itself and not loosen. It was nearly a knot her father would have tied on a net, and he saw her see that at once, and see where it was different. She tied it in the room on the cut end of the vessel, and it was wrong, and she tied it again. She tied it twenty times. She tied it forty. There was no blood in the room, and no girl dying under it, and no clock, and she tied it until her fingers did it without her, and then she tied it with her eyes shut, and then she looked up at him.

"What else?"

There was a chip of bone. He had felt it in the night under the linen, a hard edge where there should not have been one, at the inner end of the cut, where the blade had stopped against the backbone and broken off a piece the size of a fingernail. It lay loose in the meat, sharp, beside the knuckle it had come from. He showed it to her, and showed her how to take it out, slowly, without letting it turn.

He did not show her what lay inside the knuckle, behind the place where the blade had stopped. He looked at it himself, in the model, for longer than he meant to, and she saw him looking, and looked where he was looking, and did not ask. Neither of them said anything about it.

Then the closing. Deep first, the muscle, in a row of separate stitches, each one tied off on its own, so that if one went the others held. Then the skin, last, close, with the edges just touching and not crushed. She did it in the room on the model, all the way along the cut, and he showed her a clean closure beside hers to measure it by, and she looked at the two side by side and took hers out and did it again, and the second time there was no difference he could find.

"Once more," she said, and did it once more, faster.

He did not know how long they had been in there. Long enough for his head to have begun to pound, steadily, behind the eyes, and for his nose to fill again, and for the light in the room to have begun, very slightly, to thin at the edges, like the light in a room whose lamp is running out of oil. He had come in near empty. Two of them cost more than one.

Imani saw the light thinning. She saw his face.

"You're paying for this," she said.

"Yes."

She looked at him a breath longer, with something in her face that he had no name for. She had come all the way in. She was standing in a room that was half his head, with nothing between them but a girl's back on a table they had built together, and she knew it, and she was not holding on to anything.

"Then let's go," she said. "I've got it."



He came up to the smell of blood and the sound of a woman saying his name.

It was one of Imani's women, the one at the pressure, with her weight still on Tess's back and her face white. "Three minutes," she was saying. "Three and a little. You both went like the dead, and then you didn't, and I didn't send for anybody."

Beside him, Imani opened her eyes.

She took her fingers off his wrist. She looked at the woman, and then at Tess, and then at the kit on its cloth.

"It was forty," she said, to nobody in particular. "Forty minutes, about." Then she stood up. "Spirit. Hands, everybody near the table. The bottle, and the cage, and you, count her breathing out loud like you counted mine, and if it goes shallow, you take it off her and you tell me. Lamp. More light than that."

Emeric held the lamp. It was the only thing his hands were good for.

The woman at the bottle let the drops fall on the cloth over the cage, a few at a time, over Tess's face, and Tess, who had not moved in an hour, moved, and fought it a little, as Imani had, and then went loose, and her breathing went deep and slow, and the woman counted it.

Imani took the razor out of its leather.

He watched her do it. She opened the cut wider with her father's razor, a little at each end, so that she could see, and her hands did not hesitate at the end of the skin, where the cut went from something she had closed a hundred times to something she had only ever seen in a room that did not exist. She went in. She found the vessel where the room had shown it, deep, running round toward the front, both ends pulled back. She took hold of the first end with the small tongs from the kit, boiled, and tied it, twice round and then once, and tied it again, and then the second end, and then she sat back on her heels and watched the cut, and it filled slowly with blood from everywhere else, and she swabbed it, and it filled more slowly, and then it was only seeping, as any cut seeped, and the packing would do the rest.

The chip of bone came out whole. She held it up to the lamp on the end of the forceps, a white sliver the size of her smallest fingernail, sharp on one side, and looked at it, and set it down on the cloth beside the razor.

Then she closed. Deep first, the separate stitches, each one tied off. Then the skin. It took her the rest of the morning. The woman at the bottle counted all the way through it, and twice said shallow, and twice Imani said take it off her, and waited, and went on. Emeric held the lamp, and his arms ached, and his head pounded, and he did not move.

When the last stitch was tied she cut the thread with the razor and laid a fold of boiled linen over the whole of it and put her hand flat on top, lightly this time.

Tess was breathing. Deeper than before. A little more color had come into her lips, or he thought it had.

Imani straightened up. She looked at the long row of stitches under the linen, and at the bone chip on the cloth, and at her own hands, red again to the wrist. Then she looked at him, over the lamp.

"That was not a thing you can do," she said.

"No."

She held his eyes. She did not ask the question. He could see it in her, plainly, the same one she had asked in the laundry yard, and he could see her look at it and decide that this was not the morning for it, and put it somewhere she would be able to find it again.

"Right," said Imani. She turned to the room, where Declan lay in the bed by the window with his leg bound to the hip, and the townsman from the carts lay on a pallet holding his side, and all down the floor the men from the night lay waiting. She washed her hands in the spirit to the wrist and shook them dry in the air.

"Next one," she said.
