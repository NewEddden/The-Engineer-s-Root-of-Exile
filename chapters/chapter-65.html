Chapter 65 - A Barn on the Border

Paxton's letter came down in the first week of the thaw and it was an inventory.

It did not open with a greeting worth the name. It did not ask for anything. It was written in the hand of a man who had spent forty years listing animals, and what it listed was this:

Ninety-one head driven off the western pastures in the night of the nineteenth, fourteen recovered, the rest gone over the county line and not worth chasing. The grain barn on the Kaldo border burned to its footings on the night of the twenty-first, with the roof timbers pulled down into it afterward, which Paxton noted without comment and which is not a thing a fire does. A wool shipment taken on the western road on the twenty-second, two carts, both drivers beaten and neither killed.

Four days. Three counts. The weights, the head count, the value of the wool at the season's rate, and the barn's capacity in quarters.

The last paragraph was four lines. Half the grain in that barn had been Kaldo's under the standing contract, and Paxton was telling him so that Kaldo could adjust its own books, and he had already written to the coastal magistrate about the wool and did not expect anything to come of it. He signed it and did not add anything.

The four nights had been in the autumn. Paxton had not written at the time because Paxton did not write news. He wrote counts, and a count is not finished until the strays have stopped coming back and the barn's load has been reconstructed off the drovers' own tallies and the coastal magistrate has had his four months to do nothing. So it had taken him the winter, and what came down the western road at the thaw was not a report of a disaster. It was the final figure.

Emeric read it twice and passed it to Ferra because the grain was a contract matter, and thought about it no further for two days, and in those two days he drafted a reply about the grain that he never sent.

Ferra came up on the third morning with the dispatch log and Paxton's letter and put them side by side.

She did not say anything first. She had brought the census to him the same way, seven years ago, and she had learned then that the thing worked better if she let the paper do it.

Her finger on the log. The date the pair had gone out, the item number, the carrier's name, the crown post lodgment receipt reference.

Her finger on the letter. The night of the nineteenth.

Four days.

"Say it," Emeric said.

"The one pair we lost both copies of went out on the fifteenth. Dunnel was killed on the western road on the seventeenth, carrying the second of them. The first of Paxton's four nights is the nineteenth." She squared the two sheets. "That is all I have. I am not telling you what it means."

"You know what it means."

"I know what the dates do. What it means depends on what was in the report, and I have not read the report, because you wrote it yourself and it did not come through my hands."

Emeric sat looking at the two sheets for a while.

Then he went and found out what he had written, which took him the better part of an hour, because he had written fifty-one of the things in a year and they had all been dull and he could not have told anybody what was in any particular one.

It was three pages. It was the ordinary spring report to the crown on the state of the barony and its neighbours, which he had been sending for four years into an empty room.

Most of it was grain, ore tonnage, the crossing's traffic and a paragraph about the school.

On the second page, in the section on the region, there were eleven lines about Milford.

They said that Milford had not been party to the coalition's action against Kaldo in any meaningful sense, that its levy had stood off the field and taken no part, that its holder had since entered into standing commercial agreements with Kaldo in tallow and grain, and that in the writer's judgment Milford should be understood by the crown as aligned with Kaldo and not with Wayford, notwithstanding the record of the campaign.

He had written it himself. He had written it in November, in an afternoon, between two other things, because it was true and because a report to a king should contain true things and because four years of writing into silence had worn down whatever caution he had once had about what he put on a page.

He had never told anybody he had written it. Ward and Ferra and Saban had known the fact for two years. The crown had not. The crown still did not.

And Paxton had asked him, in a yard, with his hand on a saddle, not to spend the only thing he owned that Sethe had not priced.

He sat with it for a long time and then he assembled the rest of it, and he made himself do it out loud, because he had learned that he lied to himself faster in silence.

Nobody but the crown should have known what was in that letter.

The crown did not know, because the letter had not arrived.

Something had acted on the contents of a letter that never reached the man it was addressed to, four days after it was sent, against the exact barony it named, in the exact way a man would act who had just been told that a neighbour he believed was still his had gone over.

And the two copies had died differently. The crown's copy had died the way all fifty-one had died, into the silence at the crossing, leaving nothing. The second-road copy had died in a ditch on a western road with a wool factor, in a robbery that was a robbery, and had never been read by anybody.

One road lost letters into silence. The other lost one into a burned barn.

He had spent the winter believing that the ruined pair was the hole in his year. It was not the hole. It was the only item in fifty-one whose contents could be shown to have moved the world, and it could be shown because it had been read by somebody who had no right to read it and had then done something expensive about it inside four days.

Fifty items proved that letters were disappearing. One item proved that somebody was reading them.

He did not work it out alone and he was clear about that afterward when he told Keller.

Ferra had kept both piles going for four months on an instruction she had been given by a man who had told her plainly that he thought the thing was broken, and she had kept the index in the same form, and she had brought him two sheets and a date and had refused to draw the conclusion, and the conclusion had been sitting in her log since the autumn waiting for somebody to put a letter beside it.

He told her that. She said that the dates were the dates and went back down to the counting room.

Nobody said the other thing for two days, and then Ward said it in the doorway of the long room, without being asked, on his way somewhere else.

"Everything that man's lost, he's lost standing next to you."

Six words more than he usually spent. He did not stay for an answer and Emeric did not have one.

It was the fourth season in a row. The wool terms, quietly, three years ago, through a buyer with a scheduling difficulty. Then a herd, a barn and two carts in four nights. Paxton had written none of it as a grievance. He had written it as an inventory, with the weights, because he was a cattleman, and because the half of the grain that was Kaldo's was a thing Kaldo needed to know about its own books.

The Milford grain was paid in full, at the pre-fire price, on the original contract weights, before anybody in Milford raised it and before Paxton's second letter arrived.

Emeric gave the instruction the same afternoon he read his own report.

Ferra took it and then came back an hour later, which she did not often do.

"You understand the contract doesn't require this," she said. "The grain was in his barn. Risk passed at his barn. If that fire had been lightning we would owe him nothing and he knows it, and he has not asked."

"I know."

"And you understand that whatever you are trying to say to him, you could say it for a tenth of the money. A fifth of that payment says it. The rest is you." She was not being unkind about it. She was doing the thing she was for. "And it goes on separate books, because if the factor sees an unrequired payment of that size against a burned asset she will ask, and she will be right to ask, and I would rather answer that question in a way I have prepared."

"Separate books. Pay all of it."

"It'll read as guilt."

"It is guilt."

Ferra looked at him for a moment and then wrote it down.

Paxton's acknowledgment came back eleven days later. It was two sentences long. It confirmed receipt of the sum and stated the weights it had been calculated on, which did not match Ferra's weights, because Paxton had recalculated them downward using the barn's actual load on the night rather than the contract figure, and had paid the difference back.

He said nothing else at all.

Emeric carried it north himself, again, with Ward, in the second week of spring.

The satchel held the year. The index. Fifty-one lodgment receipts. Fifty-one second-road confirmations with fifty of them attested and one missing. Bram's die and the box of dies with dates on it. The dispatch log in Ferra's hand, four years of it. Paxton's letter. His own report, the November one, with the eleven lines about Milford on the second page.

Keller read for three hours.

He read it the way he read everything, which was without any expression whatsoever, and twice through the parts he cared about, and he asked eleven questions across the three hours and nine of them were about the lodgment receipts and the clerk who issued them. He wanted to know the clerk's name. He wanted to know whether the clerk had ever been spoken to by anybody from Kaldo about anything. He wanted to know who had chosen the fold sequence and whether it had been changed at any point in the year, and when Emeric said no, not once, he made a note, which he had not done for anything else.

At the end of the third hour he squared the stack.

"I am opening a matter," he said.

Emeric had prepared for the other answer and found he had not prepared for this one.

Keller reached across the table for a bound register, opened it at a marked page, and wrote four lines in it. A number. A date. A description, in the neutral language such things are written in, of an inquiry into the interception of crown post between Kaldo and the capital. He blotted it, turned the register round so Emeric could see that it said what he had said it said, and turned it back.

Then he went to the door and said a name, and a young man in a clerk's coat came in, and Keller told him he had a matter and gave him the number, and the young man wrote the number on the outside of an empty folder and went away with it.

That was all of it. It took under four minutes and involved a register, a pen and a folder, and it was the thing Emeric had spent two years and a man's life getting to.

"Now," Keller said, sitting back down. "Understand what you have and what you do not, because you are going to go home and tell people, and I would rather you told them the true version."

"Go on."

"A matter is not a charge. It is an inquiry with a number on it. It permits my office to compel production of records and to examine witnesses, and it means that if you are found dead on a road this year somebody in this building has a reason to look at it, which is not nothing and is considerably less than you want."

"And what I don't have."

"A hand." Keller said it flatly. "You have a pattern of interception. You have a coincidence of dates, and it is the best coincidence I have been shown in eleven years, and I have opened a matter on it, and it is still a coincidence until somebody puts a name to it. And you have a barn."

"The barn is the proof."

"The barn is proof that something happened. It is not proof of who. There is no name on a single page in that satchel. Not one. Not a clerk's name, not a carrier's, not a baron's. Your case describes a crime perfectly and identifies nobody, and I cannot arrest a pattern." He tapped the stack once. "And I will tell you the other half, which is worse. The instrument I have for pursuing this is my office writing letters down a road that eats letters. I am aware of the difficulty. I am going to do it anyway, because it is what I am permitted to do, and you should not expect it to produce anything."

Emeric sat with that.

"Then what produces something?"

Keller did not answer the question he had been asked. He answered a different one, and he did it in the tone of a man stating a fact about the weather, and Emeric understood inside three words that this was deliberate and that it was the entire reason the last twenty minutes had gone the way they had.

"A barn is burned by people," Keller said. "Documents do not burn barns. Men ride out at night, and they are paid, and there are more of them than anybody needs, and they drink, and they talk, and every one of them has something a document does not have, which is the ability to decide that he would rather testify than hang. That is not available from paper. It has never once been available from paper."

He squared the stack again.

"I am not instructing you to do anything. I have no instruction to give you and if I had one I would not give it to you. I am telling you what convicts men in this kingdom, which is other men, and I am telling it to a holder who I happen to know keeps a merchant with correspondents in every barony between here and the sea."

He looked up.

"Do not tell me what you do with that. Do not write to me about it. If you send me anything that is not formally lodged I will return it unread, and I will record that I did, and I will have meant it."

Saban's road started moving inside the month.

It moved slowly and it moved sideways. Three carters on the western circuit who owed him money. A woman who kept an inn eleven miles from where Dunnel had died and who had a reason to dislike a particular company of men. A factor in Wayford who bought nothing and sold nothing and was paid a small retainer to notice things. Nobody was asked a direct question. Nobody was told what was being looked for. Saban's entire method was to buy proximity and wait, and he had said at the outset that it would take a year or more and might take nothing at all.

Nothing came of it for a long time.

And Sethe kept buying.

The reports Saban brought back that spring had stopped being a shape and had become a list. Four companies now, not three, and one of them a name that had fought in the north for the crown and had come home unemployed. A figure that had gone from a retainer to something closer to a wage. Two mages engaged on terms, with lineage, out of a house that did not usually deal south. A grain purchase in the autumn that was too large for Wayford's own table and had gone into store rather than to market.

And a season. Not a year. Saban would not put it stronger than a season and would not name it out loud, and when Emeric pressed him he said that a man who buys grain in the autumn and stores it is a man who expects to be feeding somebody in the summer.

So the barony went into the spring with two clocks running.

One of them was a young man in a clerk's coat in a building six hundred kilometres north, with a number written on an empty folder, who would write letters down a road that ate letters, and who would be at it for years, and who did not know Kaldo existed except as four lines in a register.

The other was a man with four companies on a wage and grain in store.

Ward put the garrison back on a war footing in the third week of spring without being told to, and reported afterward that he had done it, and Emeric did not countermand it.

Nobody in that barony would have bet on which clock was faster, and nobody was asked to.
