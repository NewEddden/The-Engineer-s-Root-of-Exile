Chapter 258 - Dead Zone, Stolen

The oil engine fired for the first time at a quarter past seven on the Monday morning, the second of August, and the first thing it did was walk Bram's tea off the end of the bench.

It stood in the test bay against the boiler house wall, where Bram put anything he expected to shake: one upright cylinder in a block of grey iron as high as a man's chest, a flywheel taller than the apprentice who had greased it, a pipe out through a hole knocked in the brick, and on the block's flank, small and bright and out of keeping with everything round it, the pump. Emeric had written its name on the sheet, the fuel injection pump, and Bram's men said all four words every time, as though leaving one out might loosen something. A brass body the size of a fist, with a plunger fitted to its barrel so close that a hair laid across it would have stopped it like a log under a cart. The cellar had made the masters in one night's run in July. The shop had made everything else. Bram's best hand had lapped four plungers against the master before one pleased him; the first injector they cut had dribbled like a tap with a bad washer, and the second had sprayed a mist so fine that it hung in the lamplight over the bench and did not fall.

The oil was the column's middle cut, thin and pale yellow, that would not light if you held a match to it in a dish. Ferra had made Bram hold a match to it in a dish, twice, in front of her, before she would let a barrel of it into the shop.

Two of the shop's biggest men swung the flywheel, with a little lever lifted on the cylinder's head that let the air out as fast as it was squeezed, so that the wheel went round light; and when it was going as fast as two men could drive it, Bram knocked the lever down with the heel of his hand.

The air in the cylinder had nowhere to go. The piston squeezed it into a space no bigger than a cup, until it was hotter than anything in the shop but the forge; and at the top of the stroke the needle opened, and the mist went into that heat and lit by itself, with no spark and nobody's hand near it.

The bang went through the floor into everybody's boots. The flywheel jerked forward like a kicked dog, and the second bang came, and the third, and then they were coming faster than anybody could count, a hard flat hammering like a sledge on a plate; and the pipe through the wall coughed black, then grey, then nothing, and the cup went over the bench's end and broke on the flags.

Nobody picked it up.

"It doesn't like tea," said Emeric, over it.

Bram did not laugh. He stood with his head on one side, listening the way he listened to a bar ring under his hammer; and the apprentices watched his face, because his face decided things in the long shop and the engine was only noise.

"Loud," said Bram.

"Very."

"Ugly."

"I drew it ugly. Pretty's later."

Bram watched the oil glass on the pump's side, the level going down a hair at a time. Then he went round the back of the bench and held his hand near the iron without touching it.

"That's not for a mill," he said. "A mill's got a river." He took his hand away. "That's for something that has to carry its own dinner."

Emeric did not answer, and Bram did not seem to want him to.

Ferra came in at half past seven with the column's figures under her arm and stood beside him with her hands over her ears until she had got used to it, which took her less time than anybody else in the bay. Then she read the oil glass, and the clock on the boiler house wall, and wrote two figures.

"What's it for?" she said.

"Later."

She gave him the look she gave a bill that had come without an invoice. Then she turned to a clean page near the back of the book, ruled a line down it, and wrote at its head in her square capitals, LATER; and under that, Oil engine, and the date; and drew a box round the date.

"You've a column for that now," said Emeric.

"I've had one in my head for nine years. It takes up less room on paper."

Bram shut the oil off at eight, and the long shop's ordinary noises came back up round the silence one at a time, like birds after a gun. Ferra waited until he had gone down the shop to shout about the cup, and then said, without looking up from the page, "It's got a name."

"On my side."

"Then say it."

"Diesel," he said, as low as the bay let him. "After the man who built the first one."

She wrote it beside the line, small, in brackets, and considered it.

"It sounds like a cough," said Ferra, and shut the book.



The other engine ran that night, at the far end of the same shop, and it hardly made a sound.

It had the bench at the end of the long shop where Bram had made the first canister a year before, with the window shuttered and the door bolted. A bulb burned over it in a wire cage on the works' current, because Bram wanted a light in that corner no stone could put out. Under it, on oak baulks bolted through the floor, stood a riveted iron box the height of a man's chest, lined with firebrick; out of its lid through a packed collar came an upright shaft, and on the shaft a flyball governor, two brass balls on hinged arms like the ones that had danced on the first hill engine years ago and made Bram say toy; and from the shaft's head a bevel gear and a rod out sideways to the drum of the wire-draw bench.

That was all anybody could see. Bram had made sure of it.

Inside, as Emeric had drawn it on four sheets in June, a fortnight after Bram had told him at the counting room table to find the Band something to do: two iron weights the size of anvils, matched to the ounce, each in its own greased sleeve, and in the top of each, in a cup with its neck open to the box's air, a gravity stone. Under them a cast ring lay flat on rollers, three swells cut round its face like the waves a child draws. A roller under each weight rode the waves, the weights set opposite, so that as one came down a swell the other was pushed up one; the falling weight pushed the ring round, and the ring turned the shaft. The hill's beam pump, Bram had said, reading it, with a ring where the beam ought to be.

A weight going back up cost what it had paid coming down, which was where most men's everlasting engines ended, as Bram had pointed out with some satisfaction. So the stones made each weight heavy on the way down and light on the way up; and the telling was done by one more stone on the shaft under the lid, a plain one. The deciding stone, Bram called it, though it decided nothing. It had one job cut into it from inside: to know where the shaft was in its turn, and say heavy to the falling weight and light to the rising one, and nothing to either if it was not sure. The governor's rod came down to it through a second collar, so that when the shaft raced the falling weight was told to be a little less heavy.

Bram had cut it himself. He had gone into the room on the Thursday night with a plain stone from the reject drawer, sat there about four days of the room's slow time, and come out at two in the morning grey to the lips with his band's gauge down to a sliver. He had written runs into the forge's index since the spring. He had never written a stone that a machine would carry out into the world under its own lid, where he could not go and look at it.

"It'll do," was all he had said; and he had gone home and slept till noon, which nobody in the shop had ever known Bram do on a weekday.

"And when it's going," Bram had said in June, his finger on the first sheet, "they drink. They're empty in a quarter of an hour, and it's two iron lumps in a box."

"In a room full of air, they would be. This box is full of the Band. A stone with its neck open in the Band fills as fast as it spends; the Hod's proved it every night for a year." Emeric had put his finger on the box drawn round them. "So the box is the Band. A room full of it, on the ground, with a door in it."

"It's a box," Bram had said.

"It's a very small room."

He had written band engine at the head of the first sheet. The door was an airlock: two little iron doors on the box's end, one outside the other, with a bar Bram had forged so that neither could open while the other stood open. Bram called it the lock. On the far side, facing the wall, a square of soft lead sheet on copper rivets over a pit of sand in the floor. A stone that let go, Bram had said, let go as heat, and he wanted it to go somewhere he wasn't. So Emeric had drawn the blowout panel and named it, and Bram had written SAND under the name, larger.

Tobe brought the charge at ten, up from the cellar's niche in its iron sleeve: one vial out of a canister's eight, a finger of pale blue light. He set it in the lock's cup, shut the outer door, dropped the bar, opened the inner, and pulled the little lever that broke the vial's neck against a brass rose; then shut that door, and lifted the bar, and stepped back.

Emeric could feel none of it. Iron stopped a mage's sense as a wall stops a voice, which was why the canisters rode in iron. He knew what was in the box the way Bram did, from the drawing, and from Tobe's face.

Ferra was on a stool at the bench's end with the works book on her knee. Ned stood in the doorway with his arms folded, because Bram had said it was his ship's leavings and a man ought to see where his leavings went. Behind Ned's shoulder, half in the passage, was Wes.

Bram took the brake off the shaft.

Nothing happened, long enough for Ferra to look up. Then, inside the box, low and dull, like a full sack set down on a floor in the next house, a knock.

The shaft turned a little, and stopped. Another knock, and a third caught it before it could stop; and then they came steady, a long beat and a short, like a heart heard through a pillow, and the governor's balls lifted away from the shaft as it came up to speed and hung there spinning.

Bram put his hand on the clutch of the wire-draw bench and looked at Emeric, which he did not usually do, and Emeric nodded, which Bram did not usually need.

The rod was Grayrock copper as thick as a finger, sharpened by hand and pushed through the draw plate's first hole into the dogs of the chain. Bram let the clutch in. The copper came through the plate with a thin squeal and a smell of hot tallow, longer and thinner and bright as a new coin; the balls dropped a hair as the load came on, and the knock went a little longer, and caught itself up.

Nobody said anything for some while. Ned came in from the door at last and went round the box slowly with his hands behind his back, the way he had walked round hull two in the hay. Then he put one palm flat on its side, as a man does with a stove in a strange kitchen.

It was warm.

"Like a stove," said Ned.

"It burns what you bring down," said Bram.

Ned took his hand away and looked at it. "Then I'll want to know what I'm bringing down, and what for, before I'm asked to bring more of it."

"Wire," said Bram. "Tonight."

"And tomorrow?"

Bram looked at Emeric. Emeric looked at Ferra, who was writing.

She had turned to the page she had headed SURPLUS in June with nothing under it, and wrote the first line: the date; band engine, the pair; one vial; wire. Then the figure from the fourth sheet for how long a vial ought to last at this load, about a working week of the shop's hours; and drew a box round it.

"If the sheet's right," she said.

"If the sheet's right."

"I'll tell you next Monday whether it was," said Ferra, and wrote that down too.

He had reckoned its output on the same sheet in figures nobody at the bench would have known what to do with: a few kilowatts at its best. To Bram he had said five horses, and Bram had said a horse ate hay a lad could fetch in a bucket, and he would believe in the five horses when they had pulled something.

They had pulled four coils of wire by midnight.

Wes had not come in. He stood in the passage with his hands in his pockets and his lips moving, and Emeric, listening under the squeal of the copper, heard what he was doing. He was counting the knocks.

"He's said yes," said Ned, beside him, low. "To Tobe, at the lock, for the winter. I asked him, like you said. He took his week to be sore at himself, and then he said yes." He watched the boy in the passage. "He'll be counting that box in his sleep by Christmas."



The gangs reached the gorge on the Tuesday, paid off the Wayford line on the Monday and taken on again the next morning at the same rate, with the line's light track and spoil wagons and the two steam cranes behind them on the work train. The ganger stood on the shelf above the falls a long time without saying anything, and then said it was a deal of water.

Emeric went out on the first train with Ward, because he had told Ferra he would not look at the dam until August, and it was August.

The cut was a notch in the gorge's lip where the river narrowed between two shoulders of grey rock before it went over, with Linus's faded chalk still on the two knobs. Above it, in the slack water, the gangs were building the cofferdam: timber cribs, great square boxes of logs pinned at the corners, floated out on lines and sunk one beside the next and filled with broken rock as each grounded, so that a wall of boxes was walking out from the south bank a crib at a time.

On the north shoulder Fennick was blasting the river a road round the cut, so that when the cribs closed the water would turn into it and the bed of the cut would lie open to the sky. Every charge went into the powder book first, by weight, as the licence said: weighed on Bram's steel scale on a flat rock, written in Fennick's cramped hand with his three-fingered hand holding the page down against the wind, initialled; and only then down the rock with the fuse in his teeth.

"Two and a half pound," he called up, round the fuse, which made it mostly vowels. "In the book."

"I believe you."

Fennick took the fuse out of his mouth. "Don't believe me. Read it. The inspector doesn't believe me, and I'd as soon nobody got into the habit."

On the shelf where the footing would go, Paskin's students stood in a row with their hands at their sides, and the turf and thin soil peeled back off the rock in front of them in one long dark roll, like a rug taken up off a floor, and slid over the edge into the spoil wagons below. The girl from the junction footing was at the row's end. She did not look round when Emeric came along the shelf. She did look round, a little later, when he did not offer to help.

The ganger came and stood beside him and watched the cribs go down.

"A barge," said the ganger, nodding at the newest crib as it took its rock and settled, "would have been quicker. Sink a barge. Fill it."

"We've only the one barge."

"Aye. And she flies." The ganger did not smile. Emeric was nearly sure he did not smile. "She'll want a winter, this."

"Two."

"Two, then." He went off down the shelf to shout at a crane.

Emeric watched the boxes walk out into the river and thought about the last time he had thought about a cofferdam, on a bench in the dark with a woman asleep on each shoulder; and kept his face perfectly straight, which Ward noticed, and did not ask about.



The lift hull came home that evening, a day late, with the wrong man at the casting seat.

Emeric felt her a mile out over the north road, and three people aboard her like coals. Old Edrin's note was there; but it was in the cabin, not at the seat, low and flat and turned in on itself, the way a note goes when a man has sat a long time with his arms round his knees.

He was at the field below the north gate before she was down, with Ward a step behind him and nobody sent for.

The hand at the casting seat was a yard boy of Fletcher's who had been taught to look as though he were pouring and had plainly been doing his best at it for three days. He looked, as Saban said afterward, like a lad with a bellyache told to hold it till the next inn. He bumped her onto the grass and took his hands off the plate and sat looking at them.

Old Edrin came up out of the cabin hatch by himself.

He did not perform anything. He climbed the ladder like an old man, slowly, with both hands, and stood on the deck in the evening light with his white head bare, and looked at Emeric across the grass; and then he held up his left arm and pushed the sleeve back, so that nobody would have to ask.

His wrist was bare. There was a pale ring round it where the sun had not reached for fifteen months, and nothing else.

"I'll come down," said Old Edrin, "if somebody will hold the ladder. I'd as soon not be carried. I've been carried enough for show."

Ward held the ladder.

The note came out of the yard boy's shirt, sealed with a thumb in the wax; and Saban, who had come down from the town at a pace that was not quite a run, read it standing, twice, and gave it to Emeric without a word.

It was in the tier master's clerk's thin careful hand, the young man with ink on his fingers. It said where: the lane behind the tier master's house, which ran down from the lowest terrace to the cookshops. It said when: a little after nine on the Friday night, the thirtieth. It said that every lamp in that lane had gone out together at that hour, and had come back one at a time toward four in the morning, and that the watch had put it down to a bad batch of stones. It said that he had asked all that night and the next morning, everywhere he knew to ask, and that nobody had seen anybody, and that he would go on asking.

That was all.

"A lane," said Saban. "And an hour." He took the note back and folded it along its folds. "He's a good man. If he says nobody saw anybody, then nobody did, and that's worth knowing." He put it inside his coat. "It's not worth much."



Imani had the old man on a bed in the ward's end room by seven, with the screen round it, and found what there was to find: a bruise going yellow on his left temple where he had met the cobbles, a graze on the heel of one hand, and a pulse she did not like the speed of and said so. Then she sat back on her stool and let him talk, because she judged the pulse would come down faster if he did.

"I went for a pie," said Old Edrin.

He said it to the ceiling, lying very straight with his hands on the blanket, like a man laid out for a viewing who had not been told it was over.

"Eel. There's a cookshop at the foot of that lane that's done eel pie since I was a lad on the tiers. I've had one every night we've moored." He shut his eyes. "Down at nine when the casters had done looking at me, slow, the way a man walks who's poured all day. The tier master's boy used to wait at the top with a lamp." He was quiet. "Every night, at nine. I'd a name for it."

Saban, at the foot of the bed, closed his eyes too, for about as long as a breath takes.

"Halfway down there's a turn by a wall. I was at the turn, and it came up the lane at me." His hands moved on the blanket. "The wind that moves nothing. It didn't stir my coat. It went through me, and everything I've got went out at once, like a lamp; I never knew till it went how much of my standing up I'd been doing with it." He opened his eyes. "My legs went. I sat down on the cobbles like a dropped sack. And I remember a hand under my elbow."

Nobody said anything.

"A kind hand," said Old Edrin. "And a voice. Steady, grandfather. A young man's, I think. Not a capital voice. I couldn't tell you from where." He turned his head on the pillow then, and looked at Emeric. "And then nothing. I don't know how long. And I came to sitting against the wall like a drunk, with the lamps out the whole length of the lane, and my sleeve down." He held up the wrist. "Buttoned. He'd buttoned my cuff for me after. So I'd not take cold."

He let the arm down.

"The pie was in my lap. I ate it." He said it the way a man confesses something. "I sat in the dark against that wall and ate it, because I didn't know what else to do. Then the clerk saw me to the hull. And I told the lads I'd not have it wired down the line from Wayford as we came over, for every soul in your gate tower to read in a clerk's hand before I could say it myself." He looked at the ceiling. "That was vanity. It's cost you a day."

"It cost a day," said Ward, from the screen's end, "and the lads let you decide it, which they oughtn't. That mistake's theirs."

Old Edrin was quiet a long time.

"In your study," he said at last, to Emeric, "the night Armand brought me up, Captain Ward told me I'd be one more door. And I told him I didn't mind being a door." He moved his bare wrist on the blanket. "I find I mind."

"You were a door," said Ward. He stood with his arms folded and his face exactly as it always was. "Nobody came through it."

"They took..."

"They took a bead off your wrist. They had you sitting in the dark for as long as they liked, and they buttoned your cuff and left you your supper." Ward did not move. "Whoever it was wanted the bead and not the man. That's the only good news in it, and I'll not dress it up for you." He looked at Emeric, briefly, and back at the bed. "This time."

"That's enough," said Imani, and stood up, and they went.



"The bead," said Armand.

He said it at the long table at ten, with his hands folded on his stick and his eyes half shut, which was how Armand looked when he was angriest. He had put that band on Edrin's wrist himself.

"An ordinary stone," said Emeric. "Cut from inside. A count for the battery, a gauge to show it, and a call through the bond if it's taken off him. No room in it. Nothing that decides anything."

"I strung it," said Armand. "Blue beads and the grey one." He opened his eyes. "Cut from inside. Which wants a delver."

"It does."

"So whoever has it, and can read a cut, holds a stone a delver cut, this year or last, for an old caster nobody has looked at twice since he was twenty." Armand's voice did not rise. "In my tower no man living could have read it. I'm not sure there's one in the realm. I'm not sure there isn't, and neither is whoever went to the trouble, or he'd not have gone to it."

Emeric had been round it a dozen times since the field, and it came out the same every time. The clasp opened to Edrin's thumb and nobody else's; in a dead field its stone slept, and a sleeping clasp was only a spring. The wire could not be cut by anything outside the forge, so the clasp was the only way off. The call through the bond, which should have rung in him like a bell the moment the band left its wearer, could not ring in a dead field; and the bead itself, which he could have followed afterward the way a man follows a bell in fog, had gone dark where it lay. The alarm, the clasp and the beacon: a dead field stopped all three. Whoever came up that lane had known it, and had made one first.

"It's never called," he said. "Not when they took it, and not since."

"Then it's in iron," said Ward.

"Or a long way off," said Saban, "and I'd like to know which."

"The blue beads are a battery to anybody who puts them on," said Emeric. "A thief who couldn't read a cut would think he'd stolen a good purse." He put his hand flat on the table. "Almost nobody knows a stone can be cut from inside. The high church, if anybody does. Or somebody none of us has ever heard of."

"Neither of which," said Ferra, writing, "steals purses."

"It's the wood," said Saban.

Everybody looked at him. He had sat through the bead with his hat on his knee, turning it.

"The same quiet," said Saban. "In the wood the lamps went out and a ship came down, and nobody saw anybody, and the only man left was the one too heavy to carry. In the lane the lamps went out and a man sat down, and they left him because he wasn't what they'd come for. They paid a lamp watchman for an hour over the wood. Somebody's paid for an old man's supper hour: nine o'clock, every night of every mooring for a year, and the turn where a man on the cobbles can't be seen from the tier." He turned the hat. "A thief takes a purse. That's somebody who buys hours, and finds men to stand in them, and pays them short so they don't ask who's paying. The same purse." He set the hat down. "Who holds it, I still don't know."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"I said it to Edrin in your study, before the investiture," said Ward. "That a man who knows how she flies can be taken, and held, and have what he knows taken out of him by anybody who knows how. I'll say it at this table now, where it's written." He did not look at Ferra. "That's what this was. Not the taking. The asking whether it could be done. It can." He looked at Emeric. "And they know which town to look in."

Bram had said nothing all evening. When the table had gone quiet he lifted his chin off his folded arms.

"The pair," he said. "What does it do in one of those?"

Emeric looked at him.

"Not the bead. Mine. A box full of stones that pay for the work." Bram lifted one hand off the table and set it down. "Somebody walks down my lane at nine o'clock one night and breaks a stone outside my shop. What does it do?"

"I don't know."

"Then I'd like to know." Bram put his chin back on his arms. "Before somebody shows me."

"Once," said Armand, with his eyes shut. "And in daylight, where we can see what it does to everything round it."

"And a small stone," said Ferra, without looking up. "We paid a litre and a half for a copper's worth of stone once already this year."

Emeric wrote it in his notebook, the three of them, in the order they had come. Once. In daylight. A small stone. Then he looked at the page for some time, and at the table, and said nothing else; and Imani, at the far end, watched him say nothing.



On the stair, at midnight, Ferra stopped him with two fingers on his sleeve.

"The ship goes up tomorrow night."

"For her tank. And Tess wants her figures at height again."

"I'm coming."

He looked at her. Two steps above them Imani had stopped as well, and was looking at the wall.

"You've been up," he said.

"I've been up to count the Band," said Ferra, "and I've been up a mile with a list. I've never been up for nothing." She took her fingers off his sleeve. "I'd like to know what it's like with nothing to count."

Imani went on down the stair past the two of them and said, to the wall, going by, "That was settled at four o'clock. Do keep up."



The K Discovery came out of her shed a little after nine on the Wednesday with her lamps dark.

Imani came to the slip and did not come aboard. She stood at its head in her ward apron with her arms folded against the river's cold, and when Fletcher at the gangway's foot raised his eyebrows at her, she shook her head.

"Not tonight," she said.

Emeric came down the slip to her. She laid her hand flat on his chest for the length of a breath, over the place she counted, without counting it.

"My turn's coming," said Imani; and took her hand back, and went up the slip toward the ward without looking round.

He flew her from the top cockpit, and Ferra had the second seat.

She had brought the book; she brought it the way other people brought a coat. She sat with it shut on her knee, both hands flat on its cover, and did not open it from the slip to the Band.

At a mile the town was a handful of coals tipped out along a river. At two it had drawn together into one bright shape with the river's dark thread through it and the wall round it in a ring of small sharp lights; the works on the far bank was a second brightness, with the column showing one red eye where somebody had left the furnace door open. And out from the town in three directions, thin and very straight, ran lines of light.

The railways. Every halt and station had its lamp, and from two miles up the lamps strung out along each line like beads on a wire: north up the river to Grayrock, where the wire ran into the small square cluster of the pithead; west across the flats into Milford's loose scatter; and north again by the long curve under the brickfield, the Wayford line, going away and away, fainter, until it went down under the edge of the world. Four baronies, on three wires.

Ferra leaned to the glass and said nothing for a long time; and at about three miles, without taking her eyes off it, she began to count under her breath.

He listened. It was the Wayford line. She was counting the lamps from the town outward, one bead at a time, going north.

"Forty-one," said Ferra at last, "and then I can't tell."

"What's the figure for?"

"Nothing." She sat back. "Nothing at all. I've never counted a thing for nothing in my life." She looked at the book under her hands and did not open it. "It's very pleasant. I can see why other people do it."

At four miles the probe lit.

It hung in its little wire cage under the bow, where the top cockpit could see it by leaning; and they both leaned, without saying so, so that it hung in the glass between their two heads. A grey lamp stone nobody had charged, going white: slowly, like the inside of a shell, and then not slowly, a small hard star under the ship with the black sky over it and the lit country under it. The Band came through the iron as the slow seep Imani had set it to be, a warmth on his face and the backs of his hands.

He brought her to rest, the levers at their notch, and put the dial lamp out with his thumb; and the cockpit was dark but for the probe's light coming up white through the glass along the underside of Ferra's jaw.

"You've said about ten words since the field," said Ferra.

"I've said a great many words since the field. I said them at the table for two hours."

"To the table." She did not look away from the probe. "Not to me."

He looked down past it at the town, and the beads going out from it.

"They didn't want Edrin," he said.

"Ward said."

"They wanted to know whether they could." He took his hands off the levers, because there was nothing for them to do. "And they could. That's what I keep coming back to. Not the bead; the bead's a battery and a gauge. It's that they could. A lane, an hour, a cheap stone broken at the right turn, and a man who's flown for fifty years sits down on the cobbles like a sack and somebody buttons his cuff." He was quiet. "If it had been me in that lane."

"You'd have felt them a street off."

"Not in a dead field. In a dead field I'm a man walking in the dark. You saw me come out of the wood." He looked at the probe. "And they'd not have taken my bead and buttoned my cuff. Not me. They'd have put me in a cart."

Ferra said nothing. She was listening the way she read: all of it, in order, nothing skipped.

"Bram in a cart, with somebody who can do what I can and is as rude about it as I could be, and by morning they've the cellar and every master in it. You, and they've the books. Every figure, every name we pay and what for. Imani, and they've the blood and the register. Armand, the Band. Saban, every man he's got on every road." He stopped. "Ward."

"And you."

"And me." He said it to the glass. "Me, and it's all of it. The ships and what holds them up. The stones and how to tell them what to do. The room. Where the seed is, and what it is. Everything down there, every lamp on those lines, theirs by morning, and they'd not have had to fight anybody for it. Only to have one of us in the dark for a night."

He breathed out.

"Edrin knows the pump hours," he said. "I know everything."

The probe burned between them. Far down, one of the beads on the Wayford line went out and came on again, a train passing in front of it.

Ferra undid her strap.

She set the book down on the cockpit floor by her boot, square to the boards, as she had set it by the saloon door the night of the quilts; then she leaned across the gap into the probe's light, so that he could see nothing of the lit country, only the white of it on her face from below, the freckles across her nose, her eyes very steady a hand's width off; and she put two fingers under his jaw, as she did, and turned his face from the glass to her, and kissed him.

She did not try to make it a kiss that mended anything. It was slow, and dry at first, and then not; her mouth was cold from the cockpit's air and warm under that, and her hand went round into his hair at the back, where the locs were tied, and took hold quite hard, the way she held a ship's rail in a gust. He kissed her back. The levers were somewhere by his left knee and he did not care where. Under them the ship hung four miles over everything they had made, with the probe blazing under her bow like a lamp left lit in a shut house; and through his sense the Band lay round them thick as warm water, and her note in it, very close, quick, not calm at all.

She let him go a little way. Not far. Her forehead stayed against his.

"I'm not writing that down," said Ferra.

"Which?"

"What you said. Any of it." Her breath was not steady and she let it not be. "I'll remember it. I don't want it in a book somebody could read in a cart."

He laughed. He had not expected to; it came out of him once, against her mouth, and she kissed him again, shorter, as though to stop it.

"You've carried that since the field," she said.

"Since the wood."

"Since the wood." She thought about it. "Four weeks, and you've said it once, four miles up, where nobody else could hear." She sat back into her own seat, but kept her hand on his chest, flat, over his heart, over the place Imani counted. "I've not decided yet whether that's a fault or a habit. I'll tell you which."

The speaking tube by his elbow cleared its throat.

"Four miles, Your Grace," said Fletcher's voice, small and brassy and perfectly level, from the bow. "And the probe's lit. If anybody up there's looking at it."

"We're looking at it," said Ferra, before he could; and the tube was silent for some time, and then shut, with a small brass click.

They stayed in the Band an hour while the tank filled, and Ward at the saloon key sent the works its words at ten and had the works' answer inside a minute. Ferra did not open the book. She kept her hand where it was the whole hour; and once she leaned to the glass and counted the Wayford line again and got forty-one again, and was pleased about it out of all proportion.



The ship came down onto the slip a little before two, and he walked Ferra up the hill to the keep and kissed her at her door in the passage; and then he went back down the hill.

He had felt Imani from the slip: in the low house across the lane from the ward, awake, a quick bright note sitting very still, which for Imani meant she was sitting up on purpose. The door was on the latch. She was in the kitchen at the back in her wrapper, with her hair down and a cup in front of her gone cold, and she did not get up when he came in.

"Sit," said Imani.

He sat on the stool by the table. She got up then, and put a cup in front of him and filled it from the pot on the stove, and while he was lifting it with his right hand she took his left wrist off the table, and turned it over, and laid two fingers on the inside.

"She kissed you," said Imani, counting.

"She did."

"Up there. With the probe."

"How do you..."

"Seventy-four. Because I know her." She let the wrist go. "Now tell me what you told her. All of it. The same. I'll not have the short version because it's two in the morning and you've said it once already."

So he told her, and it came out shorter anyway, because it was the second time and he knew where it went: the lane and the hour, the cart, the six of them one by one and what each would carry with them into the dark, and himself last.

"Edrin knows the pump hours," he said. "I know everything."

Imani heard all of it standing at the stove with her arms folded. She did not interrupt. When he had done she said nothing for a while; then she came round the table and stood behind his stool, and put her arms round him from behind, across his chest, and set her chin down on the top of his head, and stayed there.

"Taken," she said, into his hair. "I know what taken does to a body. I'll not have it done to any of mine." Her arms tightened. "So that's two of us."

He put his hand up over hers.

"You've the face," she said, after a while.

"Which face?"

"The one from the wood. In the hay, when you stood over that man for a quarter of an hour deciding not to." He felt her chin move on his head. "Only it's the other way round. You're deciding to, now. Something. You've not told yourself what yet."

"I haven't decided anything."

"Then when you have," said Imani, "you'll bring it to me first. Before the table. Before Ferra's book. Here." She did not let go. "I'd like to see it before it's got its good coat on."

He drank his tea. She did not let go until he had.



Ivo's bath came to the ward on the Thursday morning on a dray, packed in straw, with Ivo walking beside it with one hand on the crate, as a man walks beside a horse he does not trust in a narrow street. Glass, cast in one piece, long and low, walls a finger thick: the biggest single thing his shop had ever made. The three that had cracked in the annealing oven, he said, were behind his shop if anybody wanted to see what a summer looked like.

The frame had been ready a fortnight: brass, set all round with small stones close as the teeth of a comb, which Emeric had cut a few a night in the room over the summer. Isa Brann had wound its wire in the scanner shop at the end of bay three, every turn, and laid the loom down the frame's arm to the print head by the window, and said nothing about any of it to anybody. The mana scanner, it said on his sheet. The ward had called it the tank since the first week, and the tank it was going to stay.

By one there was a bench in the passage outside the old linen room with nine of the garrison on it in their shirts, volunteers to a man, because Imani had sent word to the barracks that anybody who would lie in a bath a quarter of an hour and be looked at would get a hot dinner after with meat in it.

The first two came out as fog: a sergeant who laughed the whole time at being in a bath in front of Bettany, and a levy man who shivered from the moment his back touched the water. Bettany turned the crank that walked the frame down the bath, and the print head ticked and scratched across the paper, and what came off it was a body-shaped grey with nothing in it you could have put a finger on.

"Slower," said Imani.

The fifth had a spine in it like a string of pale beads. And then Noll Finch.

He got into the bath the way he did everything in Ward's yard: carefully, having asked Bettany to say the third thing again. He put his head back in the sling and his arms at his sides and lay still, the way he stood at the rail when Rylan read the sheet. Bettany had to walk the frame at half the pace for him, because whatever the bread and the yard had put in him since the spring pushed back harder than a commoner's trickle; so it took eleven minutes, and in eleven minutes Noll Finch moved nothing but his eyes.

Imani took the scan off the print head and carried it to the window.

A ribcage. Every rib pale and clean, curving from the spine like the frames of a hull on the stocks; the collarbones; and inside the ribs the dark of the two lungs and the heart's shadow between them, a little to the left, like a thumbprint. Emeric had seen a great many pictures like it in another life, on lit glass on a wall, and his mind gave this one its old name before he could stop it. He said nothing, and looked.

Imani looked at it a long time.

"That's a clean one," she said.

She wrote the date on its corner, and N. FINCH, and eleven minutes, and pinned it to the board on the wall by itself at the height of her eyes. Noll was sitting up in the bath with the water running off his shoulders, looking at it the way a man looks at a notice that might have his name on it.

"Is there something wrong with me, mistress?"

"Not a thing," said Imani. "That's what it's for. Go and get your dinner."

He went and got his dinner. Emeric saw him a little later in the linen room's doorway with the bowl in both hands, eating, and looking at his own ribs on the wall.



Saban had been going down to the east tower every day for four weeks.

He went at noon with a jug of small beer from the quay and half a loaf, and sat on a stool in the old strongroom two doors along from Marrow's with the man from the wood, and talked. About timber, mostly: what a beech plank fetched at Wayford against an oak one, and why; about a carter he had known in the Ridings who lost a whole load of barrel hoops in a ford. He asked no questions. He had asked the man's name on the first day and been given nothing, and had not asked again.

"It's how you buy anything off a man who doesn't want to sell," Saban had said at the table, when Ferra asked how it was going. "You go every day. You don't haggle. You talk about something else. One day he says, what'll you give me for it, and you've won, and he thinks he has."

"And if he never says it?"

"Then I've had a month of somebody to talk to about timber," said Saban, "which is more than I get at this table."

On the Friday Emeric went down with him, because Saban asked, and stood in the passage outside the grille with his back against the wall where the man could not see him.

He could feel him: the thin flat note of a man whose fire had gone out in a wood and come back a commoner's trickle; and behind it, as always, everything, behind a door he had been through twice in his life and both times wrongly. He kept his sense round the man and no further, the way a man keeps his hands in his pockets in a shop full of glass.

Saban did not talk about timber. He talked about a lane.

He told it plainly, as he had heard it in the ward: an eel pie every night at nine for a year; a turn by a wall; the wind that moves nothing; a kind hand and a young voice; and the old man left against the wall in the dark with his cuff buttoned and his supper in his lap.

"They left him," said Saban. "Same as you. The thing they wanted wasn't him, so they left him where he sat." He poured the man's beer. "You, they dragged a yard. I keep thinking about that yard. Somebody had hold of you, and thought about how far it was to the cart, and let go."

The man on the cot said nothing for so long that Emeric thought he had gone to sleep.

"It was a voice," he said.

Saban did not move. He did not look up from the jug.

"Not a face. A cart, with the cover down, in a yard in the north, at night. I stood at the tail of it and it talked to me through the cloth." His own voice was hoarse with a month of not using it. "It knew my name. It knew my mother's name, and the street. It said there'd be a stone to break and a man to help me break it, and a cart after, and I'd be fetched." He was quiet. "I wasn't fetched."

"No," said Saban.

"It paid me at the tail of the cart. A hand came out through the cloth and put it in mine." He turned over on the cot, toward the wall. "Silver. Clipped, every piece. I thought at the time, they pay short, the northern lot. Everybody knows they pay short."

He said nothing else. Saban drank his own beer to the bottom, and got up, and left the jug and the bread on the stool, and came out; and the turnkey locked the door behind him.

In the passage Saban stood a while with his hat in his hands.

"There's men in my trade would call that soft," he said. "You could have had that in July, in the hay, in an hour. And the yard, and which north, and every face he's ever looked at."

"He hasn't said yes."

"No." Saban put his hat on. "He said a voice. I'll take a voice. I'll go on talking to him about timber, and in a month he'll give me the yard, and in two the street." He looked at the grille, and then at Emeric. "It's slower. I've found I'd sooner it was slower. You might write that down somewhere. I'd not have said it ten years ago."



They did it once, on the Saturday, at noon, at the end of the spoil siding below the works.

The shop went home at noon on a Saturday. By a quarter past the yard was empty but for the gulls, and Bram's crane had the pair under a sheet on a flat truck, knocking all the way, a little quicker with nothing to draw. The vial in it was four days and a half old. Wat took the truck down the siding at a walk to where the rails ran out at the river's turn, two hundred yards past the last thing in the works with a stone in it, and her driver uncoupled and took her back; and two of Ward's men closed the bank at either end.

Bram had chosen the place. Armand had chosen the hour, noon, so that whatever the dead field did they would see it done. Ferra had chosen the stone from the reject drawer: a cloudy lamp stone the size of a hazelnut with a crack across it, that would not hold a charge two days together. She had written down what it cost, which was nothing, and what the last one had cost, which was a litre and a half, and underlined the second figure.

They stood back at forty paces in a row: Ferra with her watch open in her palm, Bram, Armand on his stick. Ward did not stand back. He walked down to the truck with Emeric and stood at his left shoulder.

"I watched you walk into one in the wood," said Ward, "like a man in a cellar with the candle out. Once was enough."

Bram had taken the sheet off. The box sat on the truck in the full noon light, black and blunt and ordinary, knocking, the balls spinning on their arms; a man walking the bank would have taken it for a pump nobody had got round to connecting to anything.

Emeric held the stone in his palm and pushed. It took what it could and stopped, as stones stop; and he pushed past the stop, as he had in the cellar in March, past where it hummed and went hot, past where it shook against his skin like a trapped bee, until it was white and singing and the crack across it was a line of light. Then he bent and set it on the end of a sleeper a yard from the box's side, and took two steps back to where Ward stood.

It went with a crack like a dropped plate.

The wind came out of it across the bank and through the two of them and on, and moved nothing. Not the grass. Not a hair on Ward's head. The world went out of Emeric with it, the gulls and the river's slow cold life and Ward's note at his shoulder and the three at forty paces, until there was nothing in his sense anywhere and he was standing on a railway bank in the August sun like any man, with his ears and his eyes and nothing else.

And in the middle of the nothing, a yard from a stone in pieces on a sleeper, the box went on knocking.

He heard it with his ears, which was all he had. Long and short, the same unhurried beat it had kept since Monday night, steady as a clock in a shut-up house; and on the shaft in the sun the balls stayed out and did not come in a hair.

Bram came down the bank. Nobody told him to stop. He walked into the dead field at his ordinary pace and stood by his box and laid his hand flat on its side, as Ned had, and listened with his head on one side, as he listened to a bar ring.

"Knocking," said Bram.

Armand stood at forty paces with his eyes shut and his face lifted, feeling for the hole's edge from outside, as Emeric had from the K Discovery over Lisle's wood.

"It isn't dead," said Armand, quite loudly, across the forty paces. "It's shut."

Bram turned his head.

"Your box. It's iron all round, and full of its own Band, and the field's outside it." Armand opened his eyes. "A dead field empties what it can reach. It can't reach in there any more than a flood gets into a stoppered bottle. The stones in that box don't know the bank's dead. Nobody's told them."

Ferra wrote. He could not hear her pencil at forty paces; he could see it going.

He walked out of the field after a quarter of an hour, because Ward made him, and the world came back at its edge all at once, and far up the hill, very small, Imani's note at the ward.

The bank healed from its edges in, slowly, and it was dusk before the last of it came back. The box knocked through all of it. Bram did not take his hand off it for the first hour, and then only to eat the bread Ferra brought down.

Emeric opened his notebook at Tuesday's page, where it said Once. In daylight. A small stone. Under it he wrote Armand's word: Shut.

"Proven," said Ferra, at his elbow, reading it upside down.

He was doing a sum. He had begun it while the box was knocking in the dead field: how many boxes the size of a room it would take to carry the treatment works' pumps through a dry week, and the ward's lamps, and the gate tower's sounder, so that the town never went dark again for want of a river or a stone. He had got as far as the vials.

"Not trusted," said Bram, from the truck.

They both looked at him. He had his hand on the box still, and did not look round.

"It knocked through that," said Bram. "Good. I built it." He patted the iron once, as a man pats a horse he has decided not to sell. "It'll draw wire and turn lathes and light what the shop wants lit at night. It'll not carry the pumps. Nor her ward. Nor the telegraph. Not if it knocks through a dead field every Saturday from now to my funeral." He looked at Emeric. "Once. Daylight. A small stone. You've not got a big stone at midnight in the rain with Tobe in bed, and you'll not find out about that one with the pumps hung off it."

Emeric stopped doing the sum.

He wrote it under Shut, in Bram's order, the pumps and the ward and the telegraph, and turned the notebook round and held it out; and Bram read it, and nodded, and went back to listening to his box.

Ferra had turned to the back of her book, to SURPLUS, and Monday's line, and the box round a working week.

"Four days and a half," she said, "and the knock's not gone long." She did not tick the box. "I said Monday."

"You did."

"Then I'll tell you on Monday," said Ferra, and shut the book.



Old Edrin had his new band that evening, at Armand's hand, in the counting room where he had signed the oath fifteen months before.

Emeric had cut its grey bead in the room on the Thursday night, a plain stone like the first, because he could find no better way to cut it. Anything a stone did, a dead field stopped; and the only thing he could think of that kept a band on an old man's wrist in a dark lane was another man walking beside him. Armand closed the clasp on the wrist himself, and the clasp knew its wearer.

Old Edrin turned his wrist over and looked at it.

"Second jewellery I've owned," he said.

"There's a rule comes with it," said Saban, from the door.

The old man looked round.

"You don't walk a lane alone. Not here, not at the capital, not across a posting-house yard to the privy. Not for a pie." Saban came in and stood at the table's end. "Anywhere off the hull, one of the lads goes with you, and if the lads are asleep you wake one, and if he grumbles he can grumble to me."

"I've walked lanes on my own since before you were breeched."

"You have," said Saban, "and you've had a good long run at it. That's what makes a habit worth buying." He did not smile. "Nine o'clock every night for a year, and somebody knew it, and I didn't. I priced a man cheap once this summer. I'll not do it twice."

Old Edrin looked at him a long time with his horse's face perfectly still, and then at Ward, by the window.

"And you agree with the grocer, Captain."

"He's not a grocer," said Ferra, at her table, without looking up.

"I agree with him," said Ward.

"Then I'll take a lad to the pie shop and buy him one too," said Old Edrin, "and he'll get fat, and it'll be on your books." He stood up, slowly, with a hand on the table, and then took the hand off and did the rest of it without. "When does she go north again?"

"When I've somewhere to send her."

"Then I'll be at the seat," said Old Edrin. "Not one of Fletcher's lads with a bellyache." He looked at his wrist once more, and pulled the cuff down over it, and buttoned it himself.



That night Emeric sat at his table with the shutter open on the warm dark and the notebook open in front of him.

He let his sense go out over the town, as he did every night. The ward on the hill and the night nurse walking it. The long shop across the river, where the pair stood on its baulks behind the bolted door, knocking, and he could not feel it and knew it was knocking all the same. Ferra across the passage, gone down into sleep all at once. Imani's note down the hill, faint and steady. And under the east tower, two doors apart, two men awake: Marrow, who had been paid short all his life and said so, and one who had been promised he would be fetched.

He turned past the page to a clean one.

He sat with the pencil in his hand a long time. Then he drew.

A stone first, small, in the middle of the page. Round it, a little apart, six marks, close. And beyond those, out to the page's edges, a great many more, as many as he had patience for, each no bigger than a grain; and from the stone in the middle, to every one of them, a line.

He looked at it until the lamp needed trimming. Then he closed the notebook on it and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left, and did not show it to anybody.
