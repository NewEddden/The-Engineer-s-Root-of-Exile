Chapter 386 - Long Trials

Sela came to the slip on the Saturday morning before it was light, in the brown shawl she'd worn to the works gate the year they built the engines, and stood at the foot of the K Tam's gangway with her hands under it.

Nobody had sent for her. Cade's yard didn't send for people at half past five in the morning; it barely sent for its own men, who came in by the side gate in ones and twos with their bread in their pockets and their breath going up in the lamplight, and looked at the schoolmistress standing by the new hull, and took their caps off, and went round her. She didn't seem to see them. She was looking up.

The K Tam rode at the yard's mast forty feet over the slip she'd been built on, light and quiet, with her six veins ticking over at the bottom of their dials to hold her there. She was her sister's pattern exactly, laid out from the same drawings on the same floor: oiled hardwood over the riveted frame, the long louvres down her flanks, the four pegs folded up under her like a heron's legs, the mast stepped and the canvas furled. Cade had wanted to paint her grey like the escorts. Fletcher had said that over his dead body, and since he'd built half of her, they'd painted her the deep green of the yard's old lighters, with a white line at the deck edge. Her name was on her bow in white letters a foot high. The lamp at the masthead caught the letters as she swung, and let them go, and caught them again.

Emeric came down the yard road with Ferra a little before six and saw her there and slowed.

"Did you know she'd come?" he said.

"No," said Ferra. "I should have."

There was a Tam already. It was years older than this one and pulled coal up the Grayrock line on six wheels, and its name had been on it in chalk on the morning it first went out, because Sela had stood at the works gate one Saturday until somebody fetched Bram and asked him, very politely, whether the crews would let her name one. They'd let her. They'd painted it on properly the week after, and it was still there under the soot. Emeric had ridden that engine's footplate to Grayrock on its first run, and had thought about the boy on the castle door the whole way, and had not said so to anyone.

This one Ferra had put on the hull list in October, between hull seven and hull eight, with a question mark. Sela had crossed the question mark out herself, at the school, with Ferra's pen, and handed it back without a word.

She heard them on the cobbles and didn't turn round.

Emeric stopped a few yards off. He'd meant to say something. He'd had something ready since October, in case, and standing on the slip in the cold with the green hull swinging over them he found that every word of it was for himself, and he didn't say it.

Above them a hatch opened in the K Tam's flank and Nell Haddow put her head out. She'd skippered the coal lighters for twenty years and the K Tam for a month, and she had a voice that carried across a harbour in a gale and didn't know how to be quieter than that.

"Ma'am," she called down. "You'll want to come up? Have a look at her? There's tea."

Sela shook her head.

She looked at the name a while longer. Then she said, to the hull, or to the yard, or to nobody, in the voice she used for a child who'd done something she hadn't expected him to be able to do:

"I got him off the docks."

That was all. She pulled the shawl up round her shoulders and turned and went back up the yard road toward the gate, past Cade's men, who took their caps off again, and past Emeric and Ferra, and nodded to them both, and went out. The school opened at eight. She'd be at the door of it at a quarter to, the way she had been every morning since the school had a door.

Nell Haddow looked after her from the hatch for a moment. Then she looked down at Emeric.

"Was that all right?" she said. "The tea?"

"That was all right," said Emeric.



They went out at seven, three of them: the K Endeavor with the Challenger slung under her keel in her cradles, and Zale's ship, and the K Tam on her first long run behind her sister. Escort one and escort three stayed on the coal quay, where Ruan's men had been on their quarters since Thursday bolting the close-in mounts to their rings, one pair each, with the painted lane on the port side aft and check fire stencilled on every hatch in a hand that was Ruan's; they'd be done by the week's end, and nobody needed the king for it, which Ruan had told him twice.

They went low, the way the convoy would go: a few hundred feet over the grey water, south and then west past the last of the inner islands, with the wind on the starboard bow and the K Endeavor's flywheels humming in her hold so that the slung boat under her hung straight as a plumb in every gust. The Challenger looked smaller from above than she did in her shed. She looked like what she was: a black seed with a glass eye, under a hull five times her length, being carried somewhere she hadn't been.

Emeric rode in the K Endeavor until the afternoon, because he was going down in the Challenger and wanted to look at her from the cradles first. Ilenne rode in Zale's ship, because she always did now. At two, forty miles past South Sentinel, in a hundred and ninety feet of water by Noll's lead, with the swell long and easy and nothing on the sea in any direction but themselves, the K Endeavor came down to thirty feet and stopped, and Fletcher's men went down the cradle ladders, and the Challenger was let into the sea.

It took a quarter of an hour and nobody said anything for all of it. The cradles went down on their wires a foot at a time. The sea came up her flanks and over her deck. Then she was floating, low and black, with the cradles slack under her, and Ned Tallis came up out of her fore hatch and looked at the sky the way a farmer looks at it before he cuts hay and said, "Right."

The long run was a day and a night. That had been Fletcher's number since the summer, written on the slate in the Challenger's shed in chalk, rubbed off by somebody every week and written up again: 24 hrs under. Nobody out. Nobody sick. She'd done six hours. She'd done ten, in January, off the Sentinel's seaward shelf, with Ned and Noll and two of Fletcher's men aboard and Bram swearing at the air box every forty minutes. She'd never done a night. She'd never done a night with eight people breathing in her and the batteries the only light, and the cold coming in through the steel, and nobody to come and get them if the lift latch stuck but the K Endeavor at thirty feet over their heads with her wires down.

They were eight. Ned at the helm. Noll at his stones. Fletcher, because it was his boat. Bram, because it was his air box, and because Tess wasn't there to stop him. Two of Fletcher's men, Davy Rusk and a young one called Ossie, who'd built the bunks. Finn, who was going to be in the water beside her in the morning in the new suit and wanted to know what she sounded like from inside first. And Emeric.

He went down the fore hatch last and Ned shut it over him and turned the wheel, and the sound of the sea outside went from a sound to a pressure, and that was that.



The first hours were busy and nobody minded the cold.

Ned took her down by stages, the way Imani's book said a diver went down, though she didn't need it: twenty feet, a stop, forty, a stop, Noll calling the stones at each and Fletcher going round the seams with a lamp and his fingertips. At a hundred and sixty feet Ned levelled her off and Noll said, "Now," and Bram, at the motor bed, reached down and shut the gate on the jet.

The boat changed.

It wasn't that she went quiet. She'd never been quiet, and in her shed with her hatches open she'd sounded like a smithy. It was that one sound after another went out of her, the way lamps go out down a street. The jet's hum went first. Then the pump's knock. Then the long low drone that lived somewhere in the frame, that Emeric had stopped hearing in January and only noticed now because it had gone. Noll had spent the autumn on that drone. He'd laid gravity dust in every motor bed and pump bed in her, under the rubber, the same dust that held a stone still in a press, and keyed it to hold, so that the beds held still while the machines on them ran; and he'd done it so carefully, bed by bed, a quarter of an ounce at a time, that Bram had said he'd seen men lay a baby down with less fuss.

What was left was the propeller, turning slowly on the batteries, a soft even churn that came up through the deck into the soles of their feet. And the drip somewhere aft that Fletcher had been chasing for a month. And eight men breathing.

"That's it," said Noll, softly, as though she might hear him. "That's quiet running. That's all of it."

Bram looked at the motor bed. He put his hand flat on the casing. Then he looked at Noll for a long moment, in the battery light, with his big hands hanging between his knees.

"Not bad," he said.

Noll went a deep red from the collar up and turned back to his stones very quickly.

Outside, somewhere, Ilenne was in the water.

She'd gone over the side of Zale's ship when the Challenger went down, in her shift, on her legs, because the sea here was still Kaldo's sea, thinner than anything she'd been born to, and wouldn't give her the tail; she swam the way she'd swum round Zale in the harbour, legs together, driven from the hips, and she breathed it all the same. She'd said she would swim beside them. She'd said she would listen. She'd said it the way she said most things about the boat, with her face held very still and her colour doing whatever it wanted, and Emeric understood that she had been waiting since the summer before last to hear whether they could make a thing that didn't shout.

At five she tapped on the nose.

They all heard it. Three taps on the glass, light and quick, the call sign she'd chosen off the divers' tapper code in January because she said the long ones were rude. Ned looked round. Fletcher, who was nearest, went forward to the nose drum on his knees and put his face to the glass, and Emeric went after him.

She was there, a yard off the nose in the green dark, lit by the boat's one forward lamp: her hair going up like smoke, her eyes very bright, her legs moving slowly under her, together, to hold her station. The pearl of her skin had gone almost silver in the cold water. She looked at them through ten feet of stacked glass and two feet of sea. Then she put up one hand, and held her thumb and finger apart, a little way. The width of a coin.

"What's that?" said Ossie, behind them.

"It's how loud we are," said Fletcher.

"Is that good?"

Fletcher looked at the gap between her thumb and finger. He'd built the boat in two years out of nothing, in a shed on a rock, with a man from the land telling him how, and the first thing the first person who'd ever lived under the sea had said about her had been loud, and she'd said it laughing.

"It's a coin," he said. His voice wasn't quite steady. "It used to be her whole arm."



The night was long. That was the point of it.

They ate at eight, off Fletcher's tea and a tin of Maren's hard biscuit and a pot of the bean stew, heated on the little galley plate aft, which was the size of a coffin, as Fletcher had said in June, and about as cheerful. Bram read the air box every forty minutes and wrote it on the slate. Noll read his stones. Ned kept the boat level by touch, hour after hour, on the trim, with his eyes half shut and one hand on the wheel, the way a man rides a quiet horse.

At ten Ned brought her down onto the bottom at a hundred and ninety feet, on Noll's lead and the sounder, as gently as setting a cup on a saucer, and shut the propeller down, and then there was nothing at all.

Emeric lay in his bunk in the dark.

It was the third on the starboard side, top row, with Abloh's grey curtain at its open edge. He'd lain in it before, in the shed, for five minutes, to see that he fitted. He hadn't lain in it at a hundred and ninety feet with the sea's whole weight on the hull a hand's breadth from his face and the steel ticking as it settled and cooled. Below him and to either side the others had gone quiet one by one: Ossie first, who could sleep anywhere, then Rusk, then Finn, breathing slow and even with his arm over his face. Fletcher was awake. Emeric could hear him not sleeping, across the narrow aisle, the way you can hear a man listen.

Twelve bunks. In seven weeks there'd be twelve people in them, or eleven, and an empty one at the end of the port row with its curtain drawn, that she'd left for coming back. He thought about who might lie in it. A face nobody in Kaldo had seen yet. A cousin, a child, a girl with a message, a soldier sent by a queen to see what the land was like and say. He found he didn't mind not knowing. He found he liked it, a little, the way you like a letter on the mat you haven't opened.

Somewhere aft, in the dark, the drip dripped.

"I'll have that," said Fletcher, quietly, across the aisle, to nobody. "If it's the last thing I do."

Emeric laughed under his breath, and Fletcher heard him, and laughed too, and Bram, from the bunk below Fletcher's, said, "Some of us are trying to sleep at the bottom of the sea," and turned over, and was snoring in a minute.

He didn't sleep much. He didn't expect to. At two he got down and went aft on his stocking feet and read the air box for Bram, and wrote it on the slate in Bram's hand, near enough, so Bram wouldn't know. At four the cold had got into the steel properly, and he lit his own warmth under his skin, a little, the way the knights did, and thought about Finn in the morning at sixty feet for an hour and a half, and the warm layer, and Imani's cap. At half past five he heard Ned get down, and the soft click of the trim, and then the propeller, turning very slowly, coming up through the hull into his back.

At a quarter to noon on the Sunday they came up.

Ned brought her up by stages, the way he'd taken her down, and at thirty feet Noll released the lift on the forward stones and she rose the last of it on her own, and the sea's pressure went off the hull like a hand lifted, and the light came green, then white, through the nose. Fletcher opened the fore hatch. Air came down it, cold and wet and enormous, smelling of salt and of the sky, and every one of them breathed it in, and Ossie said, "Oh, that's lovely," in a voice like a child's, and nobody laughed at him.

Twenty-one hours and forty minutes. Fletcher wrote it on the slate, under 24 hrs under, and drew a line under it, and then, after a moment, wrote close enough, and then rubbed that out, and wrote tomorrow, 24.

Ilenne was on the K Tam's ladder when they came out on deck, on her legs, dripping, with her hood up against the wind. She'd slept in the sea beside them, she said, through Zale; she'd come up twice in the night to tell Zale's watch they were still alive, which the watch had appreciated. She looked at Fletcher as he climbed out of the hatch. Then she put up her hand again, and held up her thumb and finger, a coin's width apart, and then she closed them a little more.

"Night," she said. "Quieter. Night, very quiet. Like rock." She tilted her head. "You sleep. The boat sleeps. I hear only the... " She tapped twice on the rail with one finger, slow. "Drip."

Fletcher looked at her for a long moment. Then he went back down the hatch without a word, and they heard him going aft, and the sound of him getting down on his knees in the bilge with his lamp.



They tested Mark II in the afternoon, beside her.

There were four suits. They came up out of the K Endeavor's hold in their felt bags with Abloh's labels on the bags and Imani's seals on the bags' cords, and Finn and Tamo and Mira and Bren put them on on the tender's deck, with the Challenger floating fifty yards off like a whale asleep and Ilenne in the water between, waiting. They looked like the sixth: the close black skin, the plate cut down to what a swimmer could carry, the round helm, the stone lit at the breastbone. The difference was in the rubber, if you knew where to look. Down the outsides of the arms and the legs and along the back there were long panels where the black had a faint grain to it, like shark's skin, and in the right light, if you turned them, a faint blue.

Water-stone dust, laid in the rubber the way Bram laid it in the Challenger's steel. On a word from the stone at the breastbone, the sea gave way along the skin instead of dragging at it. With the word off, it was a smooth black suit and nothing else.

"Show me," said Ilenne, from the water.

Finn showed her. He went in off the tender's side and swam the Challenger's length and back with the dust dead, twice, on the pump-jet at half, while Imani timed him from the tender with her watch held up in front of her face like a mirror. Then he tapped twice on his chest plate, which was the word, and swam it again.

You could see it from the deck. Not much. A smoothness in the water round him, as if the sea had decided to be a little more polite to this man than to other men; a wake that came off him cleaner and stayed smaller. He came back to the tender's side and hung there on the ladder and took his mouthpiece out and said, "Was that faster? That was faster," the way he'd said it in January.

"A fifth," said Imani. "Near enough. A fifth faster, on the same jet."

Finn put his face back in the water and swam it again, to be sure, and came back grinning so wide the mouthpiece wouldn't go in.

Ilenne swam over to him. She swam round him, once, slowly, close, the way she'd swum round Zale in the harbour in August, and looked at the panels on his arms with her head on one side. Then she put her hand flat on one and drew it down his forearm, against the grain, and the sea round her fingers went smooth and she took her hand away and looked at it.

"My trick," she said. "In a coat."

"Is that bad?" said Finn.

"No. Good." She considered it. "Clumsy. Good." She looked up at Emeric on the tender's deck. "You make my trick in a coat, Emeric. For people with no..." She moved her hand in the water, a small sideways thing, and the water round her did something subtle that he felt in his sense more than saw. "With no ask."

"That's what I make," said Emeric. "Things for people with no ask."

She thought about that for a while, floating, with her chin just above the water. Then she went faintly warm at the throat, which meant she'd decided she liked it, and swam off toward the Challenger to look at the dust in her plating again, which she'd seen fifty times.

The warm layer was the long dive.

Finn did it, because Finn had shivered at a hundred feet in the sixth suit after forty minutes and been furious about it ever since. Sixty feet by stages beside the Challenger's flank, on a line, with Mira on the line's other end at the surface and Tamo as his buddy, an hour and a half, the mesh of little fire stones in the wool under his rubber switched on at the breastbone for the first time in open water, capped at the warmth of a body and no more, Imani's cap, which she'd sat over Tess's bench for a week to make sure couldn't burn a man if a stone went wrong. Imani timed it. She timed his breathing on the taps, every ten minutes, and wrote it down, and her face didn't change for an hour and a half.

He came up blue at the lips, the way everybody did, and grinning.

"Warm," he said, before anybody could ask. "Not warm. Not cold. Like a bath somebody's let go off." He pulled his helm off and shook the water out of his hair. "I could've stayed."

1 Mar, Imani wrote on her board. Mark II, slip skin: a fifth on the same jet, all four divers. Warm layer, F., 60 ft, 90 min: no shiver. Pulse steady throughout. He says he could have stayed. He would say that. Approved for long work at the door.



The boots were the evening, and they were Bren's fault.

Emeric had brought two pairs in a box in the K Endeavor's hold, and had meant to try them himself in the lee of the island in the last of the light, quietly, with nobody watching but Zale. That had been the plan. The plan had lasted as long as it took Bren to see the box.

They were heavy, plain, knee-high boots in black leather with thick soles, and inside each sole, if you took the insole out, were four things in a line from toe to heel that were worth more than the boots and the boy wearing them together: a fixed water stone at the ball of the foot that felt the water under the sole and firmed it, a little, for an instant; Tess's pressure switch from the mag boots at the heel; a thumbnail chip of shard stone, the stasis stone, one facet set to anchor, cut from an offcut of the Church's captured hulls; and a gravity stone at the arch. Step down, and the heel switch closed, and the water firmed and the chip pinned it for the length of a breath, and the gravity stone cancelled what was left of the sinking and pushed you off along the line of your foot. Step down again. The pin only gripped mana in water. It couldn't grip thin air, which was what kept the things from being a pair of flying boots, and what Tess had made him promise, when she'd cut the chips, to say out loud to everybody who ever put them on.

"They only work on water," said Emeric. "They don't make you light. If you stop, you sink. If you miss a step, you sink. If you go too slow, you sink. If you..."

"Sink," said Bren, already with one boot on. "Got it."

"Bren."

"Majesty."

Emeric looked at him. Bren looked back with the expression of a man who'd been dropped out of an airship into a battle before he was twenty, and had never since been frightened of anything with a heel switch in it.

"Float collar," said Emeric.

Bren put the float collar on.

The lee was flat calm, a long sheet of grey water between the K Endeavor's tender and the island's black rocks, with the sun going down red behind the island and the gulls going home. Bren stood on the tender's gunwale in the boots with the collar round his neck and his arms out, and everybody who wasn't on watch on all three ships had come to the rails to look, and Zale had brought Ilenne over on the K Tam's boat and was sitting in the stern of it with her, twenty yards out, the way you'd sit at a horse race.

Bren stepped off.

He went in to the knee. Then the chip took, and the water under his boot went hard as packed sand for an instant, and the gravity stone pushed, and he came up out of it with a shout and took a second step, and a third, and was running.

It was the most ridiculous thing Emeric had ever seen anybody do on purpose. Bren went across the lee in great slapping strides with his arms going and his knees up round his chest, spraying like a dog shaking itself, every footfall a small flat crack and a splash, leaning forward so far he looked about to fall on his face, which he did, eighty yards out, when he looked round to see if they were watching. He went in all at once, like a dropped sack. The collar brought him up. He came up spluttering and laughing and shouted, "Eighty yards! Did you see? Eighty!"

"You stopped," called Emeric.

"I looked round!"

"That's stopping."

Mira did it next, and got a hundred and forty, and fell in on purpose, she said, to see what it was like. Then Finn, who got all the way across the lee to the island's rocks and stood on a rock with his arms up. Then Emeric, because there was nothing else for it, and got a hundred yards, and went in because he was thinking about the grip.

That was the thing. He felt it, running. Every step, the chip under his heel caught the mana in the water and held it for an instant, and the instant was short; but it wasn't as short as it had been in the bay at home, where they'd tried them off the coal quay steps on the Thursday. The water here was a little thicker than the bay, and the grip was a little firmer, a little longer. He felt the difference in his heel the way you feel a stair that's higher than the others. Kaldo's bay was the thinnest water there was. This was a little thicker. Past the wall, the water would be thick enough to hold Ilenne's tail, and the chip would bite hard, and a foot would stick to the sea like a boot in mud while the rest of you went on.

He came up out of the lee with the collar round his ears and said so, at once, to Mira on the tender, before he'd got his breath, because he'd made a rule about saying things before they happened and he wasn't going to break it for being wet.

"Past the wall it'll grip harder. Twice this, maybe more. They'll want retuning there. Somebody's going to get thrown the first morning if they don't."

"Somebody'll be Bren," said Mira.

"I heard that," said Bren, from the water.

In the K Tam's boat, Ilenne had watched all of it without a word. She'd watched Bren's eighty yards, and Mira's hundred and forty, and Finn on his rock, and the king of Randillia going into the sea like a sack of meal; and her colour had done a number of things that Emeric didn't have lines for and Imani, on the tender beside him, very obviously did. When Zale rowed her over to the tender's side she put her hand on the gunwale and looked up at him.

"Why?" she said.

"To cross water," said Emeric. "Fast. Without a boat. If somebody's fallen in, or there's a reef, or a ship's in trouble and the fliers aren't up."

Ilenne looked at the lee. At the long trail of splashes going across it where Finn had run. At Bren, hauling himself up the tender's ladder with his boots pouring.

"You can swim," she said.

"We can."

"In the water. The water is there. For swim." She looked at Emeric for a long, patient moment, with the expression he'd seen her give the island's cook when the island's cook had tried to make her eat a boiled egg. "This," she said, "is the most stupid way. To cross water. I ever see."

"Yes," said Emeric. "I know."

"Good," said Ilenne, satisfied, and let Zale row her back to his ship.



On the Monday morning the K Endeavor went home with the Challenger in her cradles and the divers in her hold and Fletcher in her bilge, where he had been since the noon before, and from where, at the moment of parting, he sent up a message by Ossie that he'd found the drip and it was a gland, and that he'd have it by the time they were back, and that the king wasn't to touch it.

Zale's ship and the K Tam went on west.

It was the K Tam's long run: that was what the trial was for, on Ferra's sheet. Two days out, low, the way the convoy would go, and the wall water, and two days back, and her master to say at the end of it whether she'd do. It was also, though it wasn't on the sheet, the run Ilenne had asked for. She'd asked in January, at the long table, in her careful Kaldo, without looking at anybody: whether when the green ship went out to the wall she might go with it. To listen. The way she'd gone to send.

So they went, and Emeric went with them, because the king was going to have to learn to leave Kaldo for four days at a time sooner or later and Ferra said he might as well start with four.

The sea went from grey-green to blue under them on the Monday afternoon, and stayed blue. Nell Haddow flew the K Tam a quarter of a mile off her sister's quarter the whole of the two days and never once lost her station, and at the Tuesday's noon sighting, by the K Tam's chronometer and the sun, she was four miles off Zale's reckoning and said so on the speaking tube with some satisfaction, and Zale said four miles was nothing, and Nell said it was four miles more than nothing, and they were both still saying it at dusk. On the Tuesday afternoon, because the sheet said sea trial and Nell wanted it ticked, the K Tam came down onto the swell as lightly as her sister and put her veins to sleep, and went two hours on the water under her diesel and then under canvas alone, a green hull heeled to a blue sea with a white bone in her teeth, and then folded her sails and lifted off again without a word of complaint from anybody but the cook. Imani watched Zale's eyes from noon on the Tuesday. Ferra wrote everything. Ilenne stood at the rail, as she had in August, and brightened.

They came to the wall water at the Tuesday's sunset and stood off until morning, because Ilenne said the morning was when the sea talked; and they came down on the Wednesday at first light to thirty feet over the long heavy swell, the two of them side by side, and Hesk put the ladder over Zale's lee side, and Ilenne took off her cloak and gave it to Ferra to hold.

She didn't hesitate this time. She went down the ladder hand under hand and into the sea and her legs went in the first yard, and she let go of the ladder altogether and went under.



She was down for a long time.

Ferra stood at the rail with the cloak over her arm and her book under the cloak and counted, because she couldn't not. Emeric stood beside her with his sense open as far as it would go, which in this water wasn't far; the sea here was loud with mana, full of it, the way a room is full of noise, and Ilenne's own brightness went down into it and was lost in it within thirty feet. Imani stood at Zale's shoulder at the binnacle, watching his eyes. Zale watched the water.

"Twenty minutes," said Ferra.

Nobody answered.

"She doesn't need to breathe," said Imani, after a while, to nobody in particular. "Down there. You know she doesn't. She could stay a week."

"I know," said Ferra.

"I'm only saying."

"I know what you're saying." Ferra's hand had gone white on the rail. "Twenty-six."

At thirty-one minutes the sea under the ladder brightened from below, the way a window brightens when somebody carries a lamp up a stair inside the house, and Ilenne came up through it and took hold of the ladder's last rung and hung there with her face in the air.

She didn't come up at once. She hung on the rung with her head back against her arm and her eyes shut, and the swell came up round her shoulders and let her go and came up again, and her colour went through her the way it had gone through her in August, only backwards: not out from her into the sea but into her from it, as though she were being written on. The warm one first, the brightening, so strong it went all the way down into her tail in the water. Then the pale, the deep bow, and she bowed her head in the sea and held it bowed. Then something that wasn't either, that went in and out at her throat in a long slow rhythm, over and over.

Imani took a breath beside the binnacle and didn't let it out.

It went on. In and out, in and out, at the throat, like a hand patting a blanket; past the place where it had stopped in August, and on, and on, and Ilenne's lips were moving now in the air, without sound, counting, and her face was wet, and it went on, and then it came to an end.

Not stopped. Ended. The way a thing ends that has been counted all the way through.

Ilenne opened her eyes.

She came up the ladder on her arms, and on the third rung her legs came back, and she climbed the rest on them and came over the rail and stood on the deck dripping, and she didn't take the cloak. She took the book. She took it out from under the cloak on Ferra's arm and opened it, standing there in her wet shift with the sea running off her onto the deck, and found the page from August with the seven marks on it and the line under them, and took the pencil out of the book's spine, and knelt down on the boards, and put the book on her knee.

She drew the eighth mark.

Then the ninth. Then the rest, one after another, not fast, saying each under her breath in her own tongue as she drew it, in a voice Emeric had never heard her use, small and low and somehow very young; and the marks went across the page past the end of Ferra's line and on, and down to the next line, and on, until they came to a mark Ferra knew, because it was in her table from the reckoning shell as the place where the counting turned over, the first of the big marks, the one her people's numbers came back round to the way a clock comes back round to twelve. Ilenne drew that one last. She drew it carefully. Then she put the pencil down on the page.

"There," she said. In Kaldo. "All."

Ferra knelt down beside her on the wet boards and looked at the page for a long time.

"She heard you," she said.

"She heard me." Ilenne's colour was doing something so bright and so soft at once that it hurt to look at, and she didn't try to hide it, not from any of them. "Me. Not a... " She looked for the word, and found it, and it was one of Ferra's, from the summer. "Not a prisoner. A prisoner can't make her count. A prisoner, she can't sleep. I count, I stop, like sleep. She counts the rest." She touched the last mark with one wet finger. "Like when I am small. She says, I hear you, you are free, you are not afraid. In that." She sat back on her heels. "Nobody else can say it. Only her."

Zale had come away from the binnacle. He was standing a yard off with his hands at his sides, not touching her, looking at her with an expression Emeric had seen on him once before, at the top of the slip in February when he'd come out of the harbour blue with cold and laughing.

"What else?" he said quietly. "There was more. I saw it go on. After."

Ilenne nodded. She got up off her knees, slowly, and took the cloak from Ferra at last, and put it round herself, and stood with it held closed at the throat.

"My mother comes," she said. "To the door. Herself. Not the captain. Not her old men. Her." She looked round at all of them. "You come in. The people of the dry land. With me. In her house. Her word." She said the last two words with great care, so they'd have them right, and then said them again in her own tongue, and made a sign Emeric hadn't seen, the flat hand lowered like the bow and then turned palm up, and Ferra wrote both down.

"When?" said Ferra.

"When I come. She doesn't say a day." Ilenne lifted her chin. "She says: in your spring. She waits."

Ferra wrote that down too. Her pencil had stopped shaking. The door's terms, 4 Mar: the queen meets us at the door herself. We go in with her daughter, by her word (sign: the bow, turned up). No date. She waits on the spring.

"Is that all of it?" said Emeric.

Ilenne opened her mouth to answer.

And her colour went.

It went from the throat down, all at once, the way ink goes into water: a hard dark slate, the warning colour, the one that had come up on her in patches in the wash at South Sentinel the night the guards came, the one Imani had a sign for and had never once wanted to use. It went down under the cloak to wherever it went. Her face didn't change at all. She stood there in it with her hand holding the cloak closed and her eyes on Emeric's, perfectly still, and slate to the collarbone.

Zale saw it. Emeric saw him see it; saw his hand come up off his side an inch, toward her, and stop.

"Ilenne," said Zale.

"Also a name," said Ilenne. Her voice was quite level. "At the end. My mother says one name. So I know." She held Zale's eyes for a moment, and then Ferra's, and then she looked away, west, at the sea. "It's mine."

Nobody said anything.

"The house," said Ferra at last, very gently. "The one you ran from."

Ilenne didn't answer. She didn't need to. The slate was going, slowly, back up out of her, the way it had come, and the pearl coming in behind it, and by the time it had gone up past her throat and out she'd taken a breath and let it go and turned round from the rail to face them, and her face was her own again.

"Mine," she said again, more quietly. "I say it when I want. Not now."

Ferra looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked down at the book on her arm.

There were two lines in it with nothing written after them. She'd ruled them both in July, in the word room, on the slab, at Ilenne's own asking: one for suvarre, the word Ilenne had given the cut on her side and wouldn't put into Kaldo, and one for the word she'd answered the captain with in the wash at South Sentinel the night the guards came, that Zale had heard and said he'd rather not guess at. Two blanks. Ferra had never once, since July, tried to fill them.

She turned to the end of the morning's page and ruled a third.

She wrote the date at the start of it, 4 Mar, the wall water, her mother's, and after that she wrote nothing at all, and closed the book.



Linnet Shaw sent it home that night on the long set, in Ferra's cipher, from the K Tam's radio cabin with the two hulls stood off on the swell and the stars out over them as thick as frost. The night count, finished. The door's terms. A name in the warning colour, not given. Ferra wrote the message in eleven lines and then cut it to seven, because Saban hated long ones, and then put one back.

The answer came before midnight, down the Well's long mast and out to them over a thousand miles of dark water, faint and patient through the noise. Linnet took it down on the pad and gave it to Ferra, and Ferra unciphered it at the cabin's little table by the lamp with Emeric reading it upside down over her shoulder.

It was Saban's. It was four lines, which for Saban was a speech.

S.: Good. The count is the proof; tell her so from me. The side has a name now and we haven't. Better than blind. Mind it.

And, under that, after a gap in the cipher where the operator at the Well had waited for more and more had come:

Kaldo quiet. Nothing here that won't keep till Friday.

Ferra looked at that for a while.

"He's never once said that," she said. "Not in all the years I've known him. That nothing would keep."

"He said it would."

"That's what I mean," said Ferra, and folded the sheet in half, and put it in the book beside the third blank line, and went up on deck to find Ilenne and tell her what Saban had said about the count.
