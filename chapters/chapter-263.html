Chapter 263 - Bread Basket

The K Discovery came down over the lake at a little after eight on the Thursday morning, the twenty-eighth, out of the high cold into a low grey sky, and settled among the reeds by the secret dock with no more noise than a barge coming alongside; and there was nobody on the bank to see her but two of Fletcher's yard hands with a line each, and a heron, which got up and flapped off down the lake without hurrying.

Rowena's four ships were only a few hours out from the Isle by then, with the freed men below their decks and weeks of sea between them and Emberfell. The K Discovery had come south high and straight in the dark in a little under four hours.

Edric had slept most of it, though not in a bunk. He had lain on Emeric's for about the length of a breath, and then got off it and sat on the deck with his back against its frame. When Imani asked him why, he said that in the sheds a man who lay flat got kicked in the belly in the dark, and a man who sat got kicked in the knees, and knees mended. Then he put his chin on his knees and slept like that from the Isle to the lake.

Imani had come up off Rowena's deck in the harness on the ship's line with her mouth shut tight the whole way, and left her medics aboard the flagship with the blood boxes and four pages of orders. There was one man going south a great deal faster than the rest.

They kept him in the shed until dark. Ferra came down at ten with a crock of Maren's broth and a loaf, and stood in the cabin door and looked at him, and he looked at the book under her arm before he looked at her face. She set the crock and the loaf down in front of him and went out without a word, and when Emeric found her on the slip a quarter of an hour later she was writing in the book, fast, and did not show him what.

Edric ate the broth very fast and the bread very slowly, a pinch at a time, and when he had eaten two thirds of the loaf he put the rest inside his shirt. Nobody said anything about that.

At dusk Bram brought his cart round to the shed with sacking over the back, the cart that had carried the cellar's masters through the town all spring until nobody looked at it any more, and they took Edric up the back lanes under the sacking with Arthur's hand flat on his brother's back the whole way. Nobody in the lower ward was to know. That had been settled on the ship, and not by Emeric. Not until the queen's ships had their story ashore, Ward had said. Not until he could stand on his own legs, Imani had said. And Edric, when they asked him, had looked at his hands a while and said only: "Not on a cart."



Imani had him on the end bed by seven with the door shut and the lamp turned up, and went over him from the crown of the head down, saying what she found to the nurse with the pencil and not to him; and Emeric stood against the wall by the door, where she had put him, and listened.

Lice, seen to at the back door with a comb and a bucket. Four teeth gone and the rest loose in bleeding gums. Every rib. The skin over his shins, which she pressed with her thumb and watched the dent stay in after the thumb had gone. A weight off the ward's scale, which she wrote down beside the name of a boy of fourteen from her register, so that she would have something to hold it against.

The hands she took longer over. The palms had gone past callus into something like the sole of a boot, split along every crease and healed in ridges. Across every finger and up the wrists ran fine white lines, hundreds of them, crossing each other like the scratches on an old table. The two smallest fingers of the right hand would not straighten past a curl however she pressed them.

"Shard cuts," she said, to the nurse. "Fine, and they don't close clean. These two are the cords in the palm, healed tight." She looked at him. "Did they hurt?"

"Everything hurt," said Edric. "You stopped asking which."

He watched her write it. Then he said, "You're Sabel's girl."

"I am."

He did not ask after Sabel.

She put the cup of her stethoscope on his back and told him to breathe, and on the third breath he coughed, a long dry cough that bent him over his knees and went on until the nurse looked at Imani; and Imani kept the cup where it was and listened.

"Dust," she said at last.

"Everybody coughs," said Edric, when he could. "You cut, and it comes up off the face like flour."

"The tops of both lungs. Fine. Like..." She did not finish. She wrote it herself, in the margin, and Emeric saw her draw the small careful mark beside it that in her register meant she did not know what it would do.

It was the leg she came back to.

She had seen him get up off the cabin floor, and Emeric had not: the left leg taken last and set down carefully, the way a man sets down something he has decided not to mention. She found no wound on it, no swelling she could see; an old white pucker of scar on the outer side of the thigh, the size of a seed. But the thigh above it was warmer than the right; and when she pressed with her fingertips, slowly, deep, a hand's breadth above the knee, he did not make a sound, and every muscle in his face shut.

"There," she said.

"It's nothing."

"It's hot, and it's deep, and I can't find the bottom of it. My hands say something's there and not what." She took her hand away. "Tomorrow, first thing. The tank. Nothing to eat after midnight."

Edric looked at the third of a loaf on the chair by the bed, where he had laid it when the nurse took his shirt.

"Not that," said Imani. "I'll keep it for you."

He watched her put it in the press, and turn the key, and put the key in the pocket of her apron. Then he lay back.



The tank had its own room off the ward's passage, the old linen store: whitewashed, a towel warming on a flat stone at the bath's foot, and on the wall a clock, and under the clock, in Imani's hand, 20 MIN. NO MORE. The ward called it the tank. Emeric's name for it was the mana scanner, which was written on the drawing and nowhere else.

The bath was Ivo's: one long piece of glass, longer than a tall man, poured in the big oven in June, the first having cracked in the cooling with a sound Ivo still described to people who had not asked. It was full to within a hand of the rim with water at the warmth of blood, with Imani's canvas sling across the head end and a padded bar for the knees. Riding on brass rails along its top edges, with its feet down in the water, was the frame: a flat brass oblong set all round its inner edge with sensor stones as small as barleycorns and as close as the teeth of a comb.

He had cut those stones himself, in the evenings through July, and after that the tank had not needed him. It needed somebody who could watch a clock and a man at the same time, which was Bettany. Isa Brann had wound the frame's wire. Tess had given it a motor and a long screw that drew the frame along its rails and turned, by a chain, a second screw over the print table, so that the head over the paper could never get ahead of the frame or fall behind it.

Edric looked at it from the door for a long time.

"It's warm water and a frame," said Imani. "It doesn't touch you. You lie still while it goes over, and it draws you. Eight minutes, front and side. Noll Finch had to go twice because he laughed."

"Draws me."

"The inside of you. Your bones, on paper." She held the door.

Edric looked at her, and then at Emeric by the wall. Then he took the ward's gown off himself and got into the water the way a man gets into a river he doesn't trust, and lay back into the sling with every part of him tight.

"It's warm," he said, as though nobody had told him.

Arthur was at the head of the bath before anybody asked him, on his knees on the boards, with his hand on his brother's shoulder above the water.

Bettany set the frame at the foot of the bath, looked at the clock, and put her hand on the motor's switch.

"Still now," she said. "Like you were asleep."

"I don't sleep still."

"Like you were dead, then," said Bettany comfortably, and threw the switch.

The motor hummed. The frame went up the bath under the water as slowly as the hand of a clock, a flat bright line passing under his heels and his knees; and at the wall the print head ran along its screw over a long sheet of Kaldo paper and left a ghost behind it, a grey shape the length of a man coming up out of the white an inch at a time, feet first.

Edric did not move. Arthur's knuckles were white on his brother's shoulder, and Edric's were white on the edge of the sling, and neither of them moved at all.

Four minutes for the front. Then Bettany and Arthur turned him onto his side with a bolster at his back, and the frame came up again, and a second ghost came up out of a second sheet, a man lying on his side; and at the end of it Bettany looked at the clock and said "Eight," and wrote it down.

Imani had the towel off the warm stone and round him before he was out of the water.



She did not look at the scans until he was back in his bed under two blankets. Then she stood in front of the print table with her arms folded, and Emeric came and stood beside her.

His sense leaned toward the end room before he had asked it to. It would have been easy. Edric's mana was thin, as a commoner's was, and starved thinner, and it would let the plain sense straight through him the way Wren's had in April.

"Don't," said Imani, without turning her head.

He looked at her.

"I can see your face doing it." She tipped her head at the paper. "Not you. This. If it's wrong, I want to find out from the paper, with him open on the table and you keeping your mouth shut. Not from you first and the paper after, to agree with you."

He let his sense go loose again, and looked at the paper.

He had seen sixty of these since August and they still went through him: a man on paper with his outside gone. The bones pale; the flesh in greys; the lungs, where the air was, nearly black. Emeric had a word for these from the other life and did not use it out loud. In the ward they were scans, because Imani had said so.

Edric's lungs were not black. They were grey in their upper halves, both of them, a fine grainy grey like ash sifted onto a slate, thinning downward, with here and there a paler speck in it, a small hard grain. Imani had read every chest in her drawer. She had not seen one like this.

"That's the dust," she said.

"That's the dust." He followed it down with his finger, not touching the paper. "It goes in with every breath and it doesn't come out. The lung walls each grain off with a little knot, and the knots grow into each other. There's nothing to cut. It stays."

"What does it do?"

"Out of the dust, kept warm, fed, he'll cough less. It won't go. In some men it goes on growing for years after. In some it stops." He did not soften it. "I don't know which he is."

She wrote that down in the register, including the last part.

Then she put her finger on the paper. Not on the chest; low down, on the left thigh. He understood that she had seen it before he came in, and had been waiting to see whether he would.

The thighbone ran down the sheet pale and straight. A third of the way up from the knee, against its outer edge, there was a fleck brighter than the bone: a short sharp stroke no longer than the top joint of a little finger, as white as the paper could go. And round the fleck, swelling out past the line of the bone into the grey of the muscle, was a smudge of a different grey, rounder and softer, like a sooty thumbprint.

"I don't know what that is," said Imani. "Either of them."

"The pale is bone." He drew along the thighbone in the air above the paper. "You know that. The bright thing against it is harder than bone to the frame. Harder than anything that grows in a body. That's stone."

"The shard."

"A sliver of it. The scar on his leg is where it went in." He moved his finger out to the soft smudge. "And that grey is the pocket. Softer than the muscle and wetter, so the frame reads it as its own grey, and it's pushing the muscle out of its road. See the edge bow." The edge of the thigh bellied out there by the width of a finger, the way a sack bellies over a stone in it. "That's pus. It's been sitting round the splinter a long time, walling itself off, and losing."

"And how deep?"

He had wanted her to ask that. He moved to the second sheet.

"The front tells you where. Up and down, side to side. It can't tell you how far in, because everything's laid flat on top of everything else." On the side view the thighbone ran down the paper again, and the bright fleck lay against it again; only now it was not at the bone's edge. It was in front of the bone, toward the front of the thigh, with the grey round it. "The side tells you how deep. Front, then side, and you've got it in the round." He looked at her. "Your drawing. On the back of the register."

"I drew a man on his side," said Imani. "I didn't know what I'd see."

She put her hand flat on the side view and spread her fingers on the paper, the way she measured a wound, and measured. On the front view the fleck lay seven of her fingers above the pale gap of the knee. On the side it lay three fingers deep, and the grey round it the width of her palm. Then she took her ink down the passage to the end room, and when he followed her in she had measured it again on the leg itself, from the knee up, and was setting a small black dot on the skin a little in front of the old white scar.

"It comes out," she said. "This morning."

She said it to Edric, and then she looked up.

"You'll cut," she said. "I'll start the ether and hand for you. Light, because of those lungs; as short as you can." She put the stopper back in the ink. "And then I'll know what that paper's worth."

Emeric sat down on the stool by the bed, so that his face was lower than Edric's, which Imani had made him learn years ago.

"There's a splinter of shard in there, against the bone, and gone bad round it. Left, the bad gets into the bone, and then it's the leg, and maybe more than the leg. Out, you're sore a fortnight and you walk without that."

"Without what?"

"The way you put that leg down last."

Edric looked at his leg on the blanket with the black dot on it.

"Summer before last," he said. "A face came off the cut, a big one, and a bit of it went in my leg like a nail. I dug it out that night." He looked at the dot. "Most of it. I thought most of it."

"You dug out the end," said Imani. "The rest went further in when you did."

Edric was quiet. Then he said, "Cut it, then."



Imani gave him the ether at ten, in the cutting room, with the two scans pinned up on the whitewash at the height of her eyes, four tacks each, so that the man on the wall lay there twice over above the man on the table.

She gave it slowly, a few drops at a time on the folded linen, her fingers on the pulse at his jaw, her voice low in his ear; and he counted in a whisper to six, and coughed, and everybody in the room stopped; and then he went on, to eleven, and his hands opened on the sheet. She gave the bottle to Bettany and showed her the count she wanted on her fingers, and went to the basin.

Emeric was there already.

He had not stood at it since April. One night, one girl, Rylan's daughter; and before that only the knowing that had come with the trial, which was not the same as having done a thing. He scrubbed to the elbow for the count of three hundred, and Imani scrubbed beside him, and neither of them spoke; and across the room Bettany counted Edric's breaths in a low even voice, like a woman counting stitches.

The gloves went on wet and cold out of the bowl. The nurses had washed the leg three times with carbolic from the dot outward, and under the lamp the dot was still there, a little blurred, with the old white seed of the scar behind it.

He took a scalpel off the plate.

"Along the leg," he said, "not across it. The meat in there runs up and down. Cut with it and it closes; cut across it and you've cut the rope." He laid the edge on the skin a little above the dot and drew it down, slowly, the length of his hand, toward the front of the thigh's outer side.

A starved man has no yellow layer under the skin to speak of. The knife came down at once on a sheet of tough white, shining, wrapped round the whole thigh like a stocking.

"That's the stocking. It holds everything in. You cut it the same way, along." It parted with a small sound, like canvas giving, and the red meat of the thigh came up through the slit.

Imani's retractors took the edges. He put the closed points of the forceps against the muscle along its grain and opened them, as he had in April, and the fibres parted along their own line, and he went down between them a little at a time.

He was slow. He knew it, and Imani knew it, and at the head of the table Bettany's count went on going up.

"Faster," said Imani, very quietly.

"I can't."

"Then I'll hold, you cut, and nobody talks." She shifted her grip on the retractors and leaned in, and the wound opened under her hands wider than he would have dared to open it, and stayed open, steady as a vice.

Three fingers down, the muscle changed under the forceps. It went firm, and hot through the gloves: a round swelling the size of a hen's egg, pushing up against the parted fibres from underneath.

"There's the grey," he said. He looked up at the wall, at the side view, at the smudge round the white fleck, and back down. "Three fingers. The side said three." He did not look at Imani. "When it opens it'll come up. Swab."

He touched the top of the swelling with the point of the scalpel, the least touch he could make, and it opened, and it came up.

Grey-yellow and thick, welling, with a smell the whole room knew and nobody remarked on. The swabs went in, and came out, and went in. He washed the pocket out with boiled salt water from the jug, twice, until what came back was pink and then nearly clear. Then he put his finger into it.

The pocket went down to the bone. Its surface under where the pus had lain had gone rough in a patch the size of a copper coin, like a stream stone that has had moss on it: rough, and still sound. A month more and it would not have been. He felt along it for the splinter at the bone's outer edge, where the front view had put it.

Nothing.

He felt again. Smooth bone at the side, under the roughness. Nothing that pricked.

He looked at the wall. The front: against the outer edge. The side: not at the edge. In front of it.

He moved his fingertip forward round the curve of the bone toward the front of the thigh, a finger's breadth, slowly; and there it was, lying against the bone, a hard sharp edge that caught on the rubber of his glove like a fishbone.

"There," he said. "In front. Where the side said." He took the long forceps out of Imani's hand, which was already holding them out to him. "The pale on the wall is the bone, and that's the bone under my finger. The grey was the pocket, and you've just had it in the basin. And the side said in front, and it's in front." He slid the forceps down along his finger, closed them on the thing, felt them hold, and drew it out a hair at a time against the pull of the meat that had closed round it.

It came up into the lamplight: a sliver of grey stone the length of the top joint of his little finger, thin as a fingernail paring, sharp at one end and broken square at the other, where a man with a nail had snapped it off two summers ago in the dark. He laid it in the dish.

Then he looked at Imani, because he wanted to see it.

She was not looking at the sliver. She was looking at the wall, at the white fleck in front of the pale bar; and then down into the open leg, at the empty pocket and his finger still in it; and up at the paper again. Her lips came apart and stayed apart. Her hands on the retractors slackened, and the wound began to close over his finger.

"Imani," he said.

She took a fresh grip without looking down, and held.

He washed the pocket once more, and felt the bone all round again and found nothing else, and took his finger out of the leg. She was already reaching for the needle holder.

"Not shut," he said.

She stopped.

"Not this one. Shut pus in and it comes back worse, and next time it's in the bone." He looked at a glove on the basin's edge that had torn at the thumb going on. "It needs a door. A strip of that, boiled, from the bottom of the pocket out at the bottom of the cut, so it can run. It heals from the inside out."

"It'll scar like a hoof print."

"It'll scar either way."

She cut the torn thumb off the glove and slit it into a strip and stood over it in the boiling pan herself, counting under her breath; and when she laid it in the wound, down to the bottom of the pocket and out at the cut's lower end, she laid it exactly where he would have, and closed over it loosely with a few wide stitches, without looking at what her hands were doing.

"Forty-one minutes," said Bettany, at the end, and took the cone away.



The nurses had taken Edric through to the end room, and Bettany with him. The basin was the long stone trough under the high window, and they washed at it side by side with their hands in the same water, because there was only the one jug of hot and neither of them was going to wait for a second.

Her hands were small beside his in the water and very quick, and his were slow. They had not shaken in the leg.

Imani turned his right hand over under the water with hers and looked at the palm, as she would have looked at a patient's.

"You stopped twice in the muscle," she said. "Before you opened the forceps. Both times. As if you were reading it off something."

"I was."

"And you looked at the wall every time you'd a choice to make. Every single time." She let his hand go. "You're out of practice."

He was.

"It agreed," she said, to the water.

He waited.

"The paper and the leg." She took the brush and began on her nails, hard, though they were clean. "Three fingers. In front. I'd measured it on him with my own hand before you ever picked up the knife." The brush stopped. "I've been lancing since I was nineteen, and every time I've gone in blind and felt about for it. And I'll go in blind again tomorrow on somebody, for something that thing can't see." The brush started again. "But not for that."

She lifted her right arm out of the water to rinse it, and stopped, with the water running off her elbow, and looked at her sleeve.

She had pushed it up past the elbow to scrub, as she always did: the sleeve of the faded blue dress she wore in the ward, and up the lane, and home. It had slipped while she held the retractors, a finger's width at the cuff, down over the top of the glove. There was a brown mark along it the size of a thumbnail.

She looked at it for a long time.

"I walked to the ward in this," she said. "Past the bakery and the school door. It'll go home with me tonight past the same, and come back here tomorrow." She looked at his shirt, which he had worn up from the yard. "So will that. I boil the steel. I boil every cloth that touches them. I scrub to the elbow for three hundred. And then I walk in off the lane in whatever I've got on and hold a man open with a sleeve that's been round the town."

She was quiet a while.

"Nobody cuts in their own clothes in my ward again," said Imani. "Not me. Not you. Not a nurse. Clothes that live here and never go out of that door, and go in the copper every night." She frowned at the sleeve. "No cuffs. Nothing at the neck to hang down. Short in the arm, so there's nothing to push up and nothing to come down. Loose, so it doesn't pull when you lean over a bed." She looked at him. "What did they call them? Where you were."

She was the only one besides Ferra who could ask him that.

"Scrubs."

She thought about it.

"Because you scrub in them."

"Because you scrub before you put them on."

"That's worse," said Imani, and dried her hands. "I'll have it anyway. It's short."



Abloh came up from the sail loft on the Monday with a bolt of Milford linen under his arm, undyed, the colour of oatmeal, and stood in the ward's passage while Imani told him what she wanted, and wrote it on his slate in chalk, and did not like any of it.

"Two pockets," he said. "On the hip. Deep. No flap, no button."

"No button. A button doesn't boil."

"A pocket with no flap's a pocket that empties itself every time you bend over a bed." He chalked a pocket on the slate, and a flap over it, firmly. "I've sewn pockets on everything that ever went up a mast."

"I'll be putting my hand in it with a wet glove on," said Imani, "or not at all. I'm not lifting flaps."

"Then you'll lose your scissors."

"Then a nurse will pick them up off the floor and put them in the pan, and they'll be boiled, which is where I want them."

Abloh rubbed the flap out with the side of his fist.

"And the trousers on a string, like a sailor's. And all of it in a copper every night." He wrote that too. "They'll shrink a hand the first boil. I'll cut them a hand big." He looked at the bolt under his arm. "Oatmeal. You'll look like a row of sacks."

"Clean sacks," said Imani.

He cut twelve sets that week and complained about the pockets the whole time, to Ferra, and to Maren, and once, at length, to Emeric on the works road, who had not asked. Imani wore the first set on the Thursday, with flat leather slippers that lived inside the ward's door and never went out of it, and the nurses wore theirs by the Saturday, and the ward's passage looked, Bettany said, like a mill at the change of shift. Emeric told Imani they suited her. She said that if he said one more word about them she would boil him too.

She had Abloh cut her a second set besides, for the ship: sleeves to the wrist and close, so nothing caught on a hatch rim, and pockets with buttoned flaps, because things fell out of open pockets four miles up. Abloh cut those without complaint and was visibly happy for a day. She kept the two sets in different presses at opposite ends of her house and would not let anybody fold one in with the other.



Edric walked on the fourth day with a stick and Arthur, the length of the ward and back. The drain came out on the sixth, and the cut closed from the bottom up, as Emeric had said it would, angry red and then pink. He ate everything put in front of him. He stopped putting bread in his shirt on the eighth day, and nobody said anything about that either.

Arthur was in the ward every hour he was not in the yard, and went home every night to his mother's table and did not say a word. He was no good at it. Sela, Imani heard from Nessa, thought he was courting, and had asked Nessa, low, which of Ward's girls it was. Arthur, told this in the ward's passage, went into the end room and shut the door.

Edric asked about Tam on the Tuesday.

Emeric had come in at noon to look at the cut, because Imani had told him to look at his own work, and Edric was sitting up with his hands in a bowl of warm oil on his knee, where Imani had them twice a day now.

"Arthur says Tam was on your door," said Edric.

Emeric put the dressing down.

"He was."

"And they killed him for it."

"For what he heard through it. And for going to the man who'd paid him, to say he was done." They were the words he had used to Sela. "On the mill road, below the bridge. From behind."

Edric looked at his hands in the oil.

"Is he hanged? The one that did it."

"No. He's in the strongroom under the keep." Emeric did not look away. "Your mother asked that he live a long time."

Edric was quiet a long time. Then he made a sound in his chest that turned into a cough; and when it stopped he was very nearly smiling.

"That's her," he said.

He moved his hands in the oil.

"He was eleven when they took me. He used to come down to the docks after me of a morning, and I'd send him home, and he'd go home round the other lane and come out ahead of me at the bottom." He looked at the window. "Every morning. Like I'd not see him."

"I saw your ship," said Edric, after a while, to the window. "I'll not say."

"I'd be grateful."

"Arthur said you'd not ask me to." He turned his head and looked at Emeric's eyes, the crown's, gold in the window light, very nearly as his mother had looked at them across a counter ten years ago. "I'm not doing it for you. I'm doing it for him. And for the ones still on those ships." He looked back at the window. "Highness."

"Grace," said Arthur from the door, where nobody had heard him come. "Now."

"I'll call him what I like," said Edric. "He came for us in the dark with the crown's eyes in his head. I'm thinking about it."



"You want to be there," said Ferra.

It was the Saturday night, in the counting room. He had been talking for some time about the queen's letter, and what the lower ward would say if Edric were seen in it before it had heard about any ships; and she had let him, with her chin on her hand.

"At the well, when she sees him," she said. "You've been working out where you'll stand."

He had. He had not known it until she said it, and then he could see the whole thing laid out like one of Linus's maps: the lane, the school door, the corner by the bakery, and himself on the corner.

"It's hers," said Ferra. "And his. You carried him up off that island. You don't get to carry him down the lane as well."

She went back to the book and turned a page, and did not read that one either.



The bells rang in the capital on Saturday the sixth, and Kaldo had it on the Monday.

Saban brought it to the long table himself, off the lift hull from the capital mooring. Rowena's letter had come ashore on the north coast with one of her officers to ride it to the palace door; and on the Saturday at noon the capital had rung for her, every bell in the city, the great bells first and the parishes after, for the whole of an hour.

"For a foreign queen," said Saban. "Two hundred and forty of the king's own soldiers out of a camp on the Isle, written lost eighteen years, and she takes them out in the dark with four ships and sends the king their names." He put his hat on the table. "The capital's broadsheets had it by the evening, with her name an inch high. The Queen of Emberfell, in the field again." He looked at Emeric. "She'll be very pleased."

"And one buried at sea," said Ferra. "You said."

"It's in her letter. At the foot."

"Then it's two hundred and thirty-nine." Ferra wrote the figure under Rowena's name and drew a line under it, and under the line, smaller, wrote one more.

"And Corbin?" said Ward.

"Didn't ring." Saban turned his hat round on the table. "The chapel bell in his own house was the only one in the city that didn't. He had it tied, so that everybody would notice, and everybody did. On the Sunday he asked to see the queen's list, and asked one thing about it: how many were the south coast's levy. Told about half, he asked how many of them were going south when they were landed. Not why. How many."

Nobody said anything.

"He's sure you were in it," said Saban, "and he can't say how. He's been sure of things about Kaldo for ten years and never once been able to say how. He'll count days, if he's told Sela's eldest is walking about Kaldo while the queen's ships are still three weeks off Emberfell. He'll have a date."

"Let him have a date."

Saban put his hat back on. "The town won't count. It'll know a queen's ships brought men off the Isle, because on Thursday the broadsheet will tell it so." He looked at Imani. "Can he walk to the well by Thursday?"

"He can walk to the well now," said Imani. "He won't, till he's off the stick. He's like that."

"Then Thursday," said Saban, "if he's off the stick." He went to the door, and stopped in it. "I like his mother," he said, which was not a thing Saban said about anybody, and went out.

On the Wednesday Edric gave Imani his stick back.

He walked the length of the ward without it, and back, and Imani watched his left leg the whole way and at the end of it nodded once and put the stick behind the press. The limp was gone. He had walked with it, she said afterward, for two years at the least, and nobody in the sheds had noticed, because everybody in the sheds limped.

"Tomorrow," said Emeric. "When the school lets out. Arthur will walk you down."

"You'll come," said Edric.

"Arthur's enough."

"You'll come as far as the well." Edric looked at him from the bed with his mother's look exactly, the one that left a man nowhere to stand. "She should see who."



The broadsheet had been up on the bakery wall since seven with the queen's ships across the top of it in the biggest type the press owned, and the lower ward was still talking about it at the well at noon when Emeric came down the lane from the ward with Arthur on one side of Edric and himself on the other.

Edric had on a coat of Arthur's with the sleeves turned back twice, and boots Arthur had blacked the night before; and he walked the lane like a man checking a road against what he had been told of it. The lamps on their posts. New brick where the old timber had been. At the corner he stopped and looked at the bakery a long time. It was the one thing in the lane that had not moved.

Nobody in the lane knew him. A woman at the well looked at him, and then at Arthur, and then at him again, and her bucket stopped on its way up.

Across the square round the well the children were coming out of the school's door the way water comes out of a sluice, every one of them shouting about nothing; and behind them, last, as she always was, Sela came out onto the step and pulled the door to, and came down into the lane with the empty bread basket on her hip.

She was halfway across the lane when she saw them.

He had seen that face close like a hand at the sight of him in this lane, and set like mortar behind her counter, and he had never once seen it do nothing; and it did nothing now, for the space of a step. She took the step. She looked at Arthur, and at the man beside Arthur, and her hand came away from the basket.

The basket fell. It hit the cobbles on its edge and rolled a little way on its rim, as a hoop rolls, and tipped over on its side in the middle of the lane, and lay there.

She did not look at it.

"Edric," said Sela.

Emeric let go of Edric's arm, which he had not known he was holding, and stepped back, and back again. Nobody saw him do it.

Edric did not go to her. He stood in the middle of the lane with his ruined hands open at his sides, as though he were waiting to be told whether he was allowed to; and Sela crossed the rest of the lane to him, not quickly, and stopped in front of him, and put up both her hands and took his face between them.

She looked at it.

She did not cry. Not then. She looked at his face between her hands the way she looked at a slate, every line, and he let her; and the children stopped shouting one by one down the lane, and the woman at the well stood with her bucket on the rim and did not lift it.

Then Sela took his right hand from his side and turned it over, palm up, and looked at that.

"They'll mend," said Edric. His voice came out rough, and he cleared it. "Mostly. Sabel's girl says."

Arthur bent down and picked up the bread basket, and stood holding it against his chest in both arms.

Emeric went back up the lane.



He went out through the wall to the west hill, and up the old path through the terraces past the ruin, because nobody would come to him there with a sheet of paper; and sat down on the bank above the ruin in the thin November sun, with the town below him and the marsh going out flat and brown to the sea.

He had expected to think. Instead there was the lane, over and over. The basket on its rim. The hand turned over.

His sense was open, as it was always open, and the hillside was full of small warm notes, sheep, a dog, a boy with the sheep; and one of them had been coming up the path for a quarter of an hour, slowly, stopping, and he had not once asked it who it was.

"Ward said you'd be up here," said Sela.

He got up. She was at the top of the path in her shawl, out of breath, her good shoes grey with the dust of the terraces. She had left the lane, and her son in it, and come up the whole hill alone.

She did not come closer. She stood where she was and looked at him, the way she had looked at Edric's face between her hands: all of it.

He did not know what was on his face. He saw it arrive on hers.

"Oh," said Sela. "Oh, you daft..."

She came across the grass and put her arms round him.

She was a small woman. He had not known it in ten years. She stood very straight and people moved out of her road, and he had never once stood near enough to her to know that the top of her head came to his collar. She held on, hard, with her face in his coat, and began to cry.

Not for him. He knew that at once, and it did not matter. She cried the way a dam goes, from the bottom, all of it at once after long holding: for the one who had come up the lane in a borrowed coat with his hands open, and for the one who had walked down the mill road on a summer night with a name in his mouth and not come back up it. He could hear both in it. She said Tam's name, once, into his coat, and then Edric's, and then nothing that was a word.

He had to bite down hard, on the inside of his cheek, until he tasted it. He put his arms round her because there was nowhere else to put them, and kept his own where it was, behind his teeth. The boy with the sheep had stopped to stare. A dog barked twice a long way off, and stopped.

It went on a long time. Then it slowed, as rain stops, a few last drops and then the quiet; and Sela stood back, and wiped her face with the end of her shawl, both cheeks, hard, the way she wiped a slate.

She looked at his coat, which was wet through on one side, and did not apologise for it.

"He says you came in the dark," she said. Her voice was rough and perfectly level. "He'll not say more than that. He says it's not his to say." She looked at him. "So I'll not ask you either."

"Thank you."

"Don't." She put her shawl straight. "Supper. Sunday. Not tonight; tonight's ours." She turned to go down, and turned back. "Bring the two of them. And something Maren's made, for I'll not have it said I fed the duke out of the crock."

She went down the path the way she had come up it, slowly, stopping, and did not look back.

He sat down again on the bank.

Something had gone off him on that hill that he had carried since the mill road, since a morning at her counter and a bar of soap set down square with the others; he could feel the place where it had been, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. And Tam was still dead. A boy of nineteen with a bad shave, who had let him in at the gate and gone red at his eyes, was still dead on the mill road below the bridge, and nothing that happened on any hill would make that otherwise. Both of those were true. He did not try to make them into one thing.



Ferra came up the path at about three with a satchel over her shoulder, and sat down on the bank beside him without saying anything, and took out the quarter's book, and the Milford grain book, and the wool book, and opened the grain book on her knee and began to add a column.

She did not ask. He had known she would not. After a while she leaned, so that her shoulder was against his, and went on adding.

Imani came at four with a basket.

She came up the last of the path out of breath and annoyed with herself about it, and looked at his face, and at his wet coat, and sat down on his other side and opened the basket. Bread. Cheese. Cold mutton in a cloth, two of Maren's apple pasties still warm, a stone bottle of cider and three cups.

"Eat," she said.

"I'm not..."

"You've eaten nothing since six. Bettany saw you go out of the ward with nothing in your hand." She put a pasty into his. "Eat that, and then you can not be hungry."

He had not known he was hungry. He ate the second pasty as well, while Imani cut the mutton on the cloth across her knee and Ferra added her column, and nobody said anything.

Ferra looked across him at Imani's knees.

"You're in your scrubs," she said.

Imani looked down. Oatmeal linen to the ankle under her coat, with the small scissors showing at the top of one of Abloh's pockets. She had come straight out of the ward's door in them and up a hill. She looked at them for some time.

"They go in the copper the minute I'm down," she said, "and if either of you tells Bettany, I'll know which."

He laughed. It surprised all three of them, and it hurt a little under the ribs, where Sela's arms had been; and Imani reached across without a word and took his wrist, and held it, and did not tell him the number.

They sat until dark.

The sun went down behind the hill's shoulder, and its shadow went out across the town, and the lamps came on under it a street at a time, the canal ring first, white, under its arc lamps, and then the avenue, and then the lanes. Ferra counted them under her breath. He heard her get to forty and lose her place and start again.

Down on the river, on the old stone piers that had stood empty since before the plague, the steel bridge was done. Bram's men had taken the last of its scaffold off the week of the Isle, and tonight its lamps were lit for the first time, a row along each side of the deck, two lines of light on the dark water from bank to bank with the trusses thin and black between them.

"Fourteen a side," said Ferra.

"Fifteen," said Imani. "There's one out at the far end."

Ferra looked, and wrote something small in the margin, and did not show either of them.

When it was fully dark they went down the hill path together slowly, Ferra's hand in his and Imani's arm through his other arm, with no lantern, because he could feel every stone on the path and told them where to put their feet.



Arthur was in the yard at six as he was every morning, and said nothing until Ward had gone in. Then he came across the cobbles to where Emeric was putting the ash blades back in their rack, and stood there, red from the collar up.

"She took it off," he said. "Last night. At the table."

Emeric did not need to ask what.

"She sat with her hand on it all through supper," said Arthur. "And after, when Edric had gone to sleep in his chair, she just..." He made a small movement at his own throat with two fingers. "Undid it. And sat with it in her hand a long while, looking at him. And then she put it in the drawer with the spoons."

"With the spoons."

"With the spoons." Arthur looked at the blades. "I don't know why the spoons."

Neither did Emeric, and he did not ask. He racked the narrow blade beside the broad one, and Arthur went back to the class.



Supper at Sela's was on the Sunday, in the kitchen behind the shop with the stove going and the slates stacked on the dresser, and Maren's pie in the middle of the table, because Maren had heard where it was going and would not let anybody carry anything else.

Nobody spoke of the Isle. They talked about the school, and the price of coal, and Arthur's coat, which Sela said she would take in for Edric, and Edric said she would not, because Arthur would want it back when he had fattened. Edric ate two helpings of the pie and then sat back and looked across the table at Ferra.

"You're the one with the book," he said.

Ferra put her cup down.

"Arthur says you write down who's dead. In a column. So nobody forgets them."

"Served," said Ferra.

"There were men in that camp from down here. The south. Some came out with me. Some didn't." He put his hands flat on the table on either side of his plate, and Emeric saw Sela see him do it, because it was what she did herself. "I'd give you their names. If you've the page."

Ferra had the page.

He gave them to her for an hour, with the plates cleared round him and the stove ticking, from memory: the south coast's men in the camp, shed by shed, bunk by bunk, and their villages; and who had come out with him that night and who had not, and when not, and how, as far as he knew it, in the same flat voice from first to last. Ferra wrote, in two columns, one each side of a ruled line. Sela sat at the end of the table with her hands folded and listened to every name; and when he stopped, because he had come to the end of what he knew, she got up and put the kettle over.

The left-hand column had sixty-three names in it. The right-hand had twenty-nine.

Ferra sent both to the capital on the Monday with a fair copy of the queen's list, which Saban's man had got from somewhere, and a letter of her own to Keller. It ran to a page and a half and said, in the end, one thing: that the crown's rolls carried the king's soldiers taken on the Isle eighteen years since as lost; that a queen's letter, and the camp's own roll book in the queen's keeping, said they had been held; that a man who is held is a man on the roll; and that a man on the roll is owed his pay.



Varek's letter came by the next post, on Tuesday the sixteenth: a soldier's fold, plain grey wax with a thumbprint in it, in the square careful hand. Four lines.

The capital rang for the queen's ships, and I had the fort's bell rung with it.

The other shore's full of talk: black knights with no crest, up out of the sea without a sound, and something very high over the camp that nobody saw clear. Demons, they're saying. Ships don't do that.

I don't know what you're keeping, and I'll not ask it in a letter.

Her son's name is on the queen's list. You didn't promise her. Good.

He read it twice at his table by the lamp. Then he took out of the back of the drawer the toll ticket with a name and a year and a port on it in the same square hand, ten years old and soft as cloth at the folds, and put the letter with it, and shut the drawer.



Tam ran light from the coal sidings to the works on the Wednesday morning with nothing behind her but the guard's van, because Bram had asked the driver to, and the driver had asked who for, and been told, and gone to oil round without a word.

Edric rode in the van's open end with Arthur beside him and Emeric on the step. The engine was close enough in front of them to feel: the heat coming back off her, the beat coming up through the floorboards, the six big wheels rising and falling together on their rods like a man walking under a load. On her cab side, painted properly now where his mother had chalked it in July with her good shoes off the ground, was her name.

Edric looked at it the whole way in. When the engine stopped under the long shed's roof, with a sigh from her cylinders, he got down off the van without the hand Arthur held out to him, and walked along the ballast to the cab, and put his ruined hand flat on the iron beside the name.

The iron was hot. He kept his hand there anyway, a breath longer than a man comfortably could; then took it away and looked at his palm, and wiped it on Arthur's coat.

"Big," he said.

"Biggest we've built," said Bram, who had come out of the shop door with his arms folded. He looked at Edric's hands, which nobody had told him about and which he had plainly seen at once. "You were at the cutting. On the Isle."

"Eighteen years."

"Then you know stone better than any man in my shop." Bram unfolded his arms. "Come and see what we do with it."

Edric looked at Arthur, and Arthur looked at the ground, and Edric went after Bram into the shop with his hands at his sides. Emeric stayed on the ballast by the engine's warm flank until they had gone in under the doors, and then a while longer.



Keller's answer came back up the line on Thursday the twenty-fifth, on one sheet, in his clerk's hand, with his own initial at the foot.

Evidence sufficient. The men are restored to the roll from the day each was struck from it. Entered.

K.

"About eighteen years' pay," said Ferra at the long table, with the sheet flat under her hand. "Each. From the day they were written lost to the day they were landed." She did not say the whole figure aloud. It was on the page under her hand, and it would have bought a barony. "The crown owes it to them."

"It won't pay it," said Saban.

"Not this year. Maybe not next." Ferra did not look up. "It owes it. It's written in the crown's own roll in the crown's own clerk's hand, and a debt that's written is a debt whether anybody's paid it or not. Every one of those men can walk into a court with it for the rest of his life." She turned the page to the right-hand column. "And so can these men's widows."

"And Kesrath?" said Ward.

"Nothing," said Saban. "Not a protest. Not a ship moved. To say anything it has to say it had them. So the wardens who ran tell their demons to the fishermen, and the fishermen tell them across the strait, and Kesrath's court looks at the wall."



The lighthouse was lit on the last Saturday of November, at dusk, and the town came out to watch.

It stood at the mouth of the river's north-east arm, where the arm came out of the marsh into the sea, a dozen miles from the wall: a round white tower of the town's own brick on the point above Fletcher's old village, with a stair up the inside and a room of glass at the top.

Ivo had ground what was in the glass room, from the spring to October, in a shed he let nobody into, off masters the big room had made: the ring profiles, each true to a hair, and glass blanks poured where nothing could sink or rise in the melt. A first-order lens, Emeric had written on the drawing, and Ivo had called it that since without asking why. It was taller than a man. Rings of glass round a round centre, like the rings in a sawn trunk; and above and below them rings of prisms, each cut to catch the light that would have gone up into the roof or down into the floor and bend it level and send it out with the rest. Eight panels, so that eight beams went out at once like the spokes of a wheel; and the whole of it rode on rollers and was turned by a falling weight through a clock's escapement, so that the spokes swept round over the sea and the marsh, and any boat within twenty miles would see the flash come, and go, and come.

The lamp burned oil off the column downriver. Armand had said a light stone would be brighter and never want trimming; Emeric had said that a light ships steered by could not go out because somebody had broken a stone up the coast. It had both in the end, a light stone caged beside the burner, and the oil the keeper would light every night of his life.

Most of the town went up on the wall to see it, from the water gate round to the north tower, with children on shoulders and lanterns blown out so as to see better; and a crowd at the end of the new bridge, which had the best of the view, straight down the long reach of the arm.

He stood on the wall over the water gate with Ferra and Imani, and Ward behind them. Ivo was out at the point with the keeper, an old lighterman who had been given the post because, Ferra said, he had asked first and meant it.

The sun went down behind the west hill. Along the wall the talk went quiet in pieces. Out over the marsh the sky went from yellow to green to a deep plain blue, and the line of the sea went dark first, and then the marsh, until there was nothing out there but dark, with no edge to it.

A light came on in it.

Small: a pinpoint, low down where the arm's mouth was, steady and yellow. A sound went along the wall that was not a cheer; the sound a crowd makes when it has been looking for something a long time and has found where it is.

Then the light began to turn.

Out over the marsh, in the thin haze that lay on it at dusk, a pale shaft stood out from the pinpoint, faint and very straight, and wheeled, slowly, like the hand of a great clock laid flat on the dark. As it came round to face them the pinpoint swelled into a star, white and hard and sudden, bright enough that people on the wall put up their hands; and then it was gone past them over the land, and the pinpoint was small and yellow again; and the next spoke came round out of the sea.

Along the wall people began to count. The children first, out loud, from one flash to the next. Then everybody. Ferra's lips moved. Imani had her fingers on his wrist and was counting something else.

"Ten," said Ferra.

"Ten," said a boy further along the wall, triumphant, half a breath behind her.

Ten seconds, flash to flash. Every boat coming in off the sea in the dark would know from that which light it was, and so where Kaldo stood. He had written the figure under the lens on Ivo's drawing in the spring; it was a different thing on the wall in the dark, with a town counting it round him.

Further along, where the north well's lane came up onto the wall by the old stair, he saw them in the next flash. Sela in her good shawl between her two sons, with her arm through Edric's; and Arthur on her other side with his hands in the pockets of his own coat, because his brother had one of his own now. Sela was not counting. She stood looking out at the light with her chin up and her collar plain at her throat. Edric was looking at it with his mouth a little open, like a boy.

The light came round again. He watched it pass over the three of them, and on over the town, and away.

"Ten," said Ferra, and wrote it down.
