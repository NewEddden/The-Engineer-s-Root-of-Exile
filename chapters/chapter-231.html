Chapter 231 - Ore Lighter

Fletcher built the racks in iron.

He built them because he had been asked for an ore lighter and given a drawing of racks, and an ore lighter's racks were iron: four tiers of them, on either side of the hatch, down the length of her hold, angle iron riveted in a lattice with a lip on every shelf so that nothing would slide in a swell, the whole of it painted with red lead against the wet. They were very good racks. Bram came down to the slip to look at them and said they were very good racks, and that was the first time in Emeric's memory that Bram had said anything Fletcher had built was very good without a qualification after it, and Fletcher took it as a man takes a coin he suspects is false.

Emeric had known they would be iron. He had not said anything. He had drawn the racks with no material written on them, on purpose, and handed the drawing to Fletcher, and gone away.

On the last Tuesday of July, with the racks in and the deck plated and her six lift stones seated in their cases under the deck and the six reserve beside them, they tried her stones in the shed for the first time, with the doors shut: Emeric at the helm post with his hand on the lift rail, and Fletcher on the staging, and Orrin, brought from the works for an hour because he had the faculty and could say what a stone was doing without being a duke, standing in the hold among the racks with a charged fire stone in his hand.

"Put it on the shelf," said Emeric.

Orrin put the stone on the middle shelf of the second tier, inside the iron lattice. He took his hand away. He stood back, and shut his eyes, and let his sense go out, as a hedge mage does who has been asked to feel for something and does not like being watched doing it; and his face changed.

"It's gone," he said.

"Gone?"

"Not gone. I put it there." Orrin opened his eyes and looked at the stone on the shelf, a hand's breadth from his own hand, quite plainly there, faintly warm and shining. "But I can't feel it. A pace off. It's as if I'd put it behind a wall." He reached into the lattice and took it out and held it, and his face cleared. "There. Now it's there." He put it back. His face changed again. "And gone."

Fletcher had come down off the staging. He stood at the foot of the hatch and looked at Orrin, and at the stone in the iron, and at his own red-leaded lattice going away down the hold in four tiers, very good racks; and his face did nothing at all for a long breath.

He did not ask anything. He did not look at Emeric. He went back up the staging and down the other side and out of the shed by the small door, and Emeric heard him walk away along the lake path, slowly, toward his own house.

In the morning the iron racks were on the slip beside her, unbolted, in a heap, every shelf of them; and Fletcher was in the hold on his knees with a two-foot rule and a stick of chalk, marking the frames for new bearers, and the wood was coming down from the yard on Cade's barrow: oak, seasoned, the good oak from the old tithe barn that Fletcher had been keeping for three years for something that deserved it.

"Iron's for a hull," he said, when Emeric came to the hatch. He did not look up. "Whatever you're putting on these shelves wants air round it. And you knew."

"I knew."

"You could have said."

"You'd have built them in oak and never known why."

Fletcher marked a bearer. He drew the chalk along it slowly, the whole length, and sat back on his heels and looked at the line.

"I know why now," he said. "And I know a thing about iron I didn't know yesterday, and I've built in it for thirty years." He took up the rule. "I'd sooner have known it from you. But I'll know it better this way." He measured. "Get out of my light."

Emeric got out of his light. At the hatch he looked back, and saw that Fletcher had stopped measuring, and was sitting on his heels in the hold with the rule across his knee, looking aft along it, toward the house.

The house had gone on in the middle of July, and it was oak as well. On the first drawing, in June, it had been iron, like the rest of her. On the second, which Emeric had sent down to the slip in the week after the long table, it was oak, and in the margin beside the word Emeric had written two more: for warmth. Twelve feet by ten, low, its roof curved like a wagon's tilt and its sides bellied like a barrel's staves, because a curved wall carried the push of the air inside where a flat one bent; double, oak outside and oak in with felt and pitch between; its windows small and thick in bronze grids, the same small panes as the ship's bow, which took the cold better than a big pane did and cost one pane and not a wall if they cracked; and on its roof, over the helm post, a cupola of the same glass, so that the man at the lever could see all round him. A pump. The ship's brass crown on the roof to draw from, and a gauge. A mask on a hook at every seat, with a breath candle in its cup. Fletcher had read the margin, and built the house in oak, and had not asked.

"That's the same," he said now, from the hold. "The house."

"The same."

"Not for warmth."

"Warmth as well."

Fletcher grunted, and went back to his bearer.

The rest came down to her in the last days of July, piece by piece, from the people who had made it all once already for the ship and knew how. Tess came with the steady's younger brother in a box, a brass wheel half the size of the ship's in its two rings, and a rack of the black jars to spin it, and sat cross-legged on the house floor for two evenings with a lamp, wiring its contacts to the trim keys of eight small stones Emeric had cut for her bow and stern and either side, and swearing at them gently and continuously, as at a cat. Bram came with a copper tank and three fire stones to sit under it, and the pipe to run under the house floor and come back, and two of his riveted strips, brass and iron, to shut the damper when the floor was warm enough; and a cold stone with its finned plate for the duct in her side, for the August days she would lie in the reeds with her house shut and the sun on her iron. Air stones went into her channels, one at every outlet, as the ship's did, so that she could hold herself against a wind and go where she was pointed, which nobody had ever asked of an ore lighter. The latch went in amidships, its bellows and its catch, and its drogue in a canister on the deck above her middle. The spares went into a locker of their own, sealed, glass-lined. And a set of sweeps and a lugsail went flat along her rail under a canvas, so that if every stone in her died at once she would still be a boat, and could be rowed home by men who had rowed all their lives.

Abloh brought the chutes.

Five of them, silk, packed tight in canvas, each on a harness of webbing with a buckle at the chest and two at the thighs; and he fitted every one himself, on the slip, on the man who was to wear it, with his mouth full of pins. Ned had never in his life had a garment fitted to him. He stood on the slip with his arms out while Abloh went round him, tugging, letting out, taking up, and wore the expression of a horse being shod by a man it has not met.

"What's it for?" he said.

"If she burns," said Abloh, round the pins. "Or breaks. You go off the side and pull that." He flicked a wooden toggle on its cord at Ned's chest. "When you're clear of her. Not before."

"And then?"

"Then you come down slower than you would have." Abloh took a pin out of his mouth and put it in the webbing. "Arms down."

Ned put his arms down, and looked at the toggle on his chest, and said nothing at all.

Emeric fitted the corner himself.

He did it on the last Friday of July, after the shift, alone, with the house door open to the lake and a lamp on the floor. The after corner on the starboard side, where Bram's pipe ran nearest under the boards and the floor would be warmest: a seat built into the wall, with arms, and a padded back, and a strap to go across a lap. A shelf at the elbow the width of a clock. A hook for a mask. A brass rail at the right hand, for a hand to hold that did not want to be seen holding anything. And beside it, at the height of a seated man's eye, one of the small thick panes in its bronze grid, so that whoever sat there could look out at the dark and the cloud and the stars without getting up.

Fletcher found him at it at ten. He had come down with his own lamp to look at the day's rivets, as he did every night, and he stood in the house door and looked at the seat.

"That's a chair," he said.

"It's a seat."

"It's got arms." Fletcher came in. He looked at the shelf, and the hook, and the brass rail, and the window; and Emeric watched him work it out, as he had worked out the oak, without being told. He had walked home along the lake path past the ship's slip every night that week, and on most of them somebody had been standing on it, in a fur coat, on a stick, in the dark. Fletcher bent his knees and put his eye to the window, at the height of a man in the chair, and stayed there, crouched, with one hand on the seat's arm.

"Lower," he said.

"What?"

"Two fingers. He stoops." Fletcher straightened, slowly, on the arm. "You've cut it for yourself sitting. He'd be looking at the frame." He took out his rule. "I'll do it Monday. You'll make a mess of the bronze."



The lift rail was finished in the first week of August.

Ned had seen its sleeve in July and known it at once: a sleeved iron rail that ran from the helm post in the house at her stern down through the house floor into her, under the deck, to the six lift stones in their cases amidships and the six reserve beside them; and through it, when the helm's lever was over, the bank of stones that would ride in her racks fed the lift stones what they spent. It was the same rail as hull two's, and the K Discovery's, in a cheaper sleeve. And it had one thing they did not, because she was a lighter and would be flown by lightermen, and lightermen did not have a delver aboard to tell them what a stone was doing: at the helm post, on the rail, a hand's breadth above the house floor, a bare place a hand's width long, where the sleeve was cut away and the stone of the rail showed through, worn smooth, so that a man with no faculty at all could put his two hands on it and push.

"If the bank fails," said Emeric, to Ned, on the slip, with Tobe and Cade standing behind him, "the lift stones go to the reserve. If the reserve fails, they go to whatever's left in the rail. If that fails, they go to you." He put his hand on the bare place. "Your hands. Anybody's. You push into it the way the class pushed into the rail in the yard. It holds her. Not long. A farmhand can push into a rail for a count of forty before he's done. A man who's carried stones for years can do more."

Ned looked at the bare place on the rail. Then at Emeric.

"If the bank fails," he said, "at the top of wherever we're going. Which nobody's said."

"Then you'll want something better than your hands."

So the flasks went in last: four of them, upright, in a rack of their own at the helm post's foot, under the house floor, down in the hold where the rail came through, with a trap in the floor above them for a man to go down by. The rail could reach them there, and if one of Bram's bottles ever let go at a seam it would let go into iron and not into a room full of men. Iron bottles the length of a man's forearm with a valve on each and a pipe from each valve to the rail. They were empty. Bram had made them; they were the strongest thing he had ever made that was not a boiler, and he had tested every one with water under the hand pump to twice what Emeric asked of them, and one had wept at a seam and gone back to the forge, and he had made it again. They had no lining in them yet, and nothing to put in them, and Emeric did not say what would go in them, because he did not yet know whether it would work.

"Four bottles," said Ned, looking at them. "Of what?"

"Of nothing, yet."

Ned looked at the four empty bottles at the foot of his helm post for a long while.

"She's a hod," he said at last.

"A what?"

"A hod. A brick hod. What the labourer carries up the ladder on his shoulder." Ned put his hand on her iron flank, as he had in July. "Legs and a house and a bow like a flute, and it's still up and down she's for. Up the ladder full and down empty, or the other way, whichever it is you've not told me." He took his hand away. "Ore lighter. She's no more an ore lighter than I'm a bishop. She's a hod."

And that was her name. Nobody gave it to her; Ned said it, and Tobe said it at the works that evening, and Cade wrote it in chalk on the slip's board under her slip number, and Fletcher, who had built three hulls in his life with names chosen by other people, looked at the chalk the next morning and did not rub it out.



The bottles went up the hill to Ivo on the Monday, in a handcart, with Bram walking beside it.

Ivo's glass house stood on the slope above the works where the old charcoal burners' huts had been, three cones of brick with the smoke going straight up out of them on a still day, and Ivo in the middle of them in a leather apron, one eye milky and the other very sharp, with his forearms burned in white seams from wrist to elbow from forty-odd years of gathers; a narrow quiet man who said very little to anybody about anything that was not glass, and a great deal about glass, to anybody who would stand still. Emeric had gone up the day before with a drawing.

"Inside," Ivo had said, looking at it. "Not a bottle of glass. Glass inside an iron bottle. Fused to it."

"All over. Every inch of the inside. No gap, no pinhole, nowhere for anything to get from the inside to the iron."

"Anything," said Ivo.

"Anything."

Ivo had looked at him for a while. He was one of the few men in Kaldo who had worked out a long time ago that glass was the only thing the Holder ever asked for that he would not say why, and he had never once asked.

"Enamel," he said. "Like a pot. Like the pans the coast sells, the blue ones." He turned the drawing over and drew on the back with a stick of charcoal from his apron: a slip, ground glass and clay and water, poured in and swirled and poured out, the bottle turned over and over until the inside was coated like the inside of a cream jug; dried; fired in the kiln until the slip melted and ran and took hold of the iron. "Twice. Three times. Every coat finds the holes in the last." He put the charcoal back. "Iron and glass don't love each other. Iron grows when it's hot more than glass does, and shrinks more when it's cold. Fire it right and they'll hold in a kitchen. I don't know about anywhere else." He looked at Emeric. "Where else?"

"Somewhere cold."

"How cold?"

"Colder than you've ever been."

Ivo had not asked how a man knew a cold he had never been in. He had nodded, and said "Monday," and on Monday the bottles came up the hill in the handcart and went into his slip one at a time and into his kiln one at a time, three coats each, two days; and came out on the Wednesday in a row on his bench, still warm, and he held each one up to the light at the mouth and looked down into it with his eye to the neck, turning it slowly, and every one was blue-white inside, glassy, smooth as the inside of a shell.

"They'll hold in a kitchen," he said. He set the last one down. "I'd want to know what they did in your cold before I put my name to it."

"So would I," said Emeric.

"Then you'll tell me." Ivo turned back to his furnace. "When it breaks. Bring me the pieces."



Ward wrote the rule into the crew book on the Thursday, at the long table, in front of the crew.

There were three of them, and all three had sworn the ships' oath long since, the oath every man who had ever handled the ships' stones had sworn, which said what it said and was kept in Ferra's book; they signed under it again that afternoon in the long room, with the table watching, on the first page of the new book, because it was a new book: Ned Tallis, forty-six, lighterman, at the helm; Tobe, from the foundry, who had held the K Discovery over Wayford through the whole of the Staple summer with his hands on her rail and had never once spoken of it outside the shed; and Cade, Fletcher's second, who had stood helm watch on the ship herself and was the only man in Kaldo besides Fletcher who could take a hull apart in the dark and put it back. Three. And the book: the crew book, new, bound in black, with the Hod's name on the cover in Ferra's capitals, and on its first page nothing but the oath and their three names.

Ward opened it at the second page. He wrote at the top of it, in his square hand, standing, while they watched.

NOBODY GOES UP TO THE BAND ALONE.

He underlined it once. He turned the book round so that it faced them.

"That is the rule," said Ward. "There are others. Ferra will write them. That one is mine." He looked at the three of them. "Not alone. Not for an hour. Not for a quarter of an hour because the other man is ill. Not because the Holder says so. If there are not two of you, she stays on the slip."

"Three of us," said Ned.

"Three is two and one more," said Ward. "Two is the least."

Ned looked at the page, and at Ward, and then, slowly, as a man who has worked rivers all his life and knows what a buddy is for, he nodded.

"And this," said Ned.

He had brought his harness to the table over his arm, as a man brings a coat he has been lent and has doubts about, and he put it down on the table in front of him, chute and buckles and toggle and all.

"Your sailmaker says off the side and pull," he said. "He doesn't say when."

The table looked at Emeric.

"Below a mile," said Emeric. "If she burns, or breaks up, or her stones die and she's coming down and nothing you do stops it, and you're below a mile, you go."

"And above a mile?"

"Above a mile you stay with her," said Ward.

Ned waited. He had worked rivers since he was twelve, and he knew the difference between a rule and the reason for it, and he wanted the reason.

"Silk comes down slowly," said Emeric. "That's what it's for. From four miles a man under a chute hangs in the air for most of half an hour. The air up there won't keep him breathing, and the cold will have his hands useless inside ten minutes, and the wind four miles up runs off the west like a mill race; you've felt it on hull two at a mile, and it's twice that higher. He'd come down twenty miles east, in the dark, in the sea as likely as not, with his hands dead, if he came down breathing." He put his hand flat on the table beside the harness. "So above a mile you stay with her. Whatever she has left, you ride her down on it, as far as she'll take you. Then you go."

Ned looked at the harness for a while. Then he pushed it across the table to Ferra, as if it were a paper that wanted her mark.

"Write it in, Chancellor," he said. "Under his. Below a mile, go. Above it, stay."

Ferra wrote it in, second, under Ward's. Then she wrote the rest of them, in her own small hand; and there were nineteen; and the last was a figure for every one of the four flasks at the helm post's foot, with the space after it left blank.

"For when you know what goes in them," she said to Emeric, and closed the book, and gave it to Ned.



She was launched on the seventh of August, at night, from the far end of the lake.

Not the slip below the shed; Fletcher would not have her come down the shed slip in front of the ship, he said, a thing that big going into the water where a thing that precious lay, and nobody argued with him about it. She was taken down the lake on rollers on the Wednesday, on the shore track, behind six of the works' horses, a forty-foot iron box with a low oak house on her stern, on a cradle of oak, past the reed beds to the far end where the old slipway was, unused for thirty years, and set up on it with her bow to the water.

And the works came to watch.

Emeric had not expected that. He had expected Fletcher's men and the crew and perhaps Bram, at night, at the far end of the lake, for an ore lighter; but the works had heard that Fletcher was launching something, and a launch was a launch, and there had not been one at the old slipway in thirty years; and by nine there were three hundred people along the shore by the reeds with lanterns and children and bread and beer, the night shift's wives and the day shift itself, and the steamfitters and the kiln crews and half the plant floor and most of Ward's class, sitting along the shore in the dusk as if it were Lantern Night.

Arthur was at the top of the slipway with the crew, where he had not been asked to be. He had written his name against the Band crew in letters that went through the paper, and been told by Ward that the Band crew was three men and a book and that the book did not have his name in it yet; and he had come anyway, in his coat with all its buttons, and stood a pace behind Cade with his hands behind his back as he had seen Ward stand, and when Ned looked round at him Ned did not tell him to go.

"Not on the first runs," Ned said, to the hull, not to Arthur. "Not till she's proved. I'll not take a boy up on a boat I don't know."

"I'm not a boy," said Arthur.

"You're a boy to me," said Ned. "I've a nephew your age in that yard who can nearly go up a wall, and he's a boy to me as well." He did not look round. "When she's proved. You'll go with Cade. Cade'll teach you the racks." He thought. "And if you stand behind him like that the whole time he'll throw you off the deck, so stand somewhere else."

Arthur stood somewhere else. He did not go home.

"An ore lighter," said Saban, beside him at the top of the slipway, looking at the crowd. "Launched by night. From the far end of the lake. With the Duke of Kaldo standing beside it." He turned his hat. "I'd have charged."

"Nobody's going to see anything but a lighter go into the water."

"No," said Saban. "But they'll talk about why the duke came to watch one."

That was true, and there was nothing to be done about it. Emeric went down to the boat shed at the slipway's head, the old fishermen's shed with its roof half gone, where Fletcher had his tools and the launch crew's lanterns, to find the crew book, which Ned had left on the bench there and wanted.

The shed was dark. The lanterns had all gone down to the slipway. There was light from the shore through the holes in the roof and through the door, enough to see the bench, and the crew book on it, black, and a shape beside the bench that was Ferra, with her own book under her arm, who had come for the same thing.

"I've got it," she said.

"So I see."

She did not move. He did not either. There were three hundred people a hundred yards off along the shore in the lantern light, and Fletcher's voice at the slipway telling somebody about a wedge, and Ward somewhere on the slipway in the dark, and the lake, and the night; and the shed was very dark, and smelled of tar and old nets and the reeds.

"Work first," said Ferra.

"It's done. She's on the ways. Fletcher's got her."

"Fun later."

"It's later."

"It is not later. It's nine. The second bell's at..." She stopped, because he had put his hand flat on the bench on either side of her, one each side, so that she was standing between his arms with her back to the bench and the crew book against her chest and both their books between them; and she looked up at him in the dark with her face pale and her eyes very bright, and said, quite quietly, in an entirely different voice, "Oh, I hate you."

"You don't."

"I don't," agreed Ferra, and pulled his head down by the hair.

The books went onto the bench. Both of them. She did not look where they went, which was a thing Emeric had never once seen Ferra do with a book. She kissed him as she had kissed him in the cabin of the lift hull and on the slip in the winter and in the starlight a mile over the sea, all at once, with both hands in his hair and her whole weight against him; and he lifted her, without meaning to, as he always did, and set her on the edge of the bench among the nets, and she said "Ow, a hook," and moved, and did not stop kissing him. Her collar was in his way. It was a working collar, starched, with a little brass stud at the throat, and he got the stud undone with his thumb, which he had not known he could do, and put his mouth to her throat where the collar had been, and felt her breath go out of her all at once and her heels come round the backs of his knees.

"Ferra?"

It was outside the shed. Close. Twenty paces, on the shore path, and coming.

"Ferra! Where's she got to with that book? Ned's wanting it." Bram's voice. Bram's ordinary voice, raised over the crowd, the voice he used across the works yard to a man who had walked off with his good chisel. "Has anybody seen my girl?"

Ferra stopped breathing.

She did not move. Neither did he. They stood in the dark of the shed with her on the edge of the bench and her heels round the backs of his knees and his mouth at her throat and her two hands in his hair, absolutely still, like a pair of thieves in a larder when the door opens; and outside on the path somebody said something to Bram, and Bram said, "In the shed? What's she doing in the shed, the lanterns are all down here," and his boots came on along the path.

Ferra moved.

She was off the bench and on her feet and out from between his arms before he had finished lifting his head. She had the crew book in one hand and her own book in the other and she had put her hair right with the back of her wrist, all of it, in one movement, he did not see how; and she drew one long breath through her nose, and let it out, and her face, in the light from the door, was the face of the Chancellor of Kaldo coming out of the counting room at noon: composed, pale, a little impatient.

She went out of the shed door.

"Here," she said, on the path, in her rent-roll voice. "I've got it. It was on the bench under the nets. Somebody had put the tide tables on top of it." He heard her walking away with her father along the path toward the slipway. "Ned should keep his own book. If he can't keep his own book he can't keep the flasks."

"He'll keep it," said Bram's voice, going away. "Ned's never lost a thing in his life but a bishop."

Emeric stayed in the shed.

He stayed in it a good while longer than a man stays in a boat shed to look for nothing, in the dark, among the nets, with his hand on the bench where she had been, and his breath not doing what it was told; and he could not have gone out sooner if the whole works had been on fire, and he knew it, and stood there and let it pass, and thought very hard about rivets.

When he came out at last and went down the path to the slipway, Ferra was standing at the top of it with her father and Ned, with the crew book in Ned's hand now and her own under her arm, talking about the flasks. She did not look round. Her face was perfectly composed. Her collar was undone at the throat, the stud open, the starched edge standing out crooked on one side, a thing that had never before happened to Ferra's collar in public; and her father was talking to Ned about wedges and looking at the hull and not at her, and the whole shore of three hundred people was looking at the hull and not at her; and nobody had seen.

Imani came up the slipway from the shore behind them with her bag. She was coming to stand with the crew, because there was a launch and launches broke things.

She passed behind Ferra on her way. As she passed, without stopping, without a word, without turning her head or looking at Ferra's face or anybody's, she put up one hand and did the stud of Ferra's collar back up, and straightened the starched edge with two fingers, the way she would have straightened a bandage on her way down a ward; and went on, and stood beside Ned with her bag at her feet, and looked at the hull.

Ferra did not move at all. Her ears went scarlet.

Emeric looked at the lake.



Fletcher knocked the last wedge out at a quarter to ten.

She went down the old slipway slowly, then less slowly, on her greased ways, with a noise like a long breath let out, iron on oak, and the crowd along the shore went quiet as she went, the way a crowd goes quiet at a launch; and she went into the lake bow first with a great flat smack and a wave that went out across the black water in the lantern light and came up the shore and wet three hundred people's feet; and she came up, and rolled once, and twice, and settled, and sat on the lake, square and black and flat, forty feet of iron box with a low oak house on her stern and a canvas hood laced down over something on its roof, riding high and level and empty, exactly like an ore lighter with ideas.

The shore cheered. It cheered a good deal longer than an ore lighter warranted, because it was a launch and there was beer, and somebody's children ran into the lake up to their knees and had to be fetched out, and Kit Bastow began the miller's wife.

Fletcher stood at the foot of the slipway with his hands behind his back and watched her ride, and did not cheer, and did not say anything at all. He had built a ship with legs and been told it was a market boat. He had built a second with a hold for casks and been told it was for salt. He had built a forty-foot iron ore lighter with three legs and a bored bow and an oak house with fire stones under its floor and a chair in its corner, and a sleeved rail from her helm into her middle and a bare place on the rail for a man's two hands, and four iron bottles at the foot of her helm post with nothing in them, and oak racks because iron made a fire stone disappear from a hedge mage a pace off; and he stood there and watched her float, and said nothing about any of it, then or ever.

Ned took her across the lake on the sweeps, slowly, with Tobe and Cade, to the reed bed at the far end, and moored her there in the dark among the reeds where nobody could see her from anywhere, and the crowd went home along the shore with its lanterns, singing.



He came down the keep stair at a quarter past the second bell, by the counting room clock, exactly.

Her door was not locked; it never was, now, after the second bell. She was sitting up in bed in the dark with the shutter open and her knees drawn up under the blanket and her arms round them, and she did not say anything when he came in, and did not move when he sat down on the end of the bed.

"She did my collar," said Ferra.

"I saw."

"In front of my father."

"Behind your father. He was looking at the hull."

"She didn't look at me." Ferra put her forehead down on her knees. "That's worse. If she'd looked at me I could have looked back. She did it like a bandage. Like I was a bed on her round." Her voice came muffled through the blanket. "Three hundred people. My father. Ned Tallis. And my collar open like a girl coming out of a hayloft, and Imani doing it up on her way past as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world."

"It was the most ordinary thing in the world. To her."

"That's what's worse." She lifted her head. Her face in the starlight from the shutter was flushed to the hair, and her mouth was not straight at all; it was trying very hard not to do something and failing. "You stayed in that shed a very long time."

"I was looking for nothing."

"Everybody saw you come out."

"Everybody was looking at the hull."

"Saban wasn't." She put her face back on her knees, and her shoulders began, very slightly, to shake. "Saban saw you come out. He looked at you, and he looked at me, and he looked at my collar, and he took out his pencil and wrote something on his cuff." She was laughing. She was laughing into the blanket, silently, helplessly, with her whole body. "He wrote something on his cuff."

"He writes everything on his cuff."

"Not that," said Ferra, and lifted her head, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and looked at him across the dark bed; and then reached out and took hold of the front of his shirt. "I've an hour. Then you're to be on the lake by two and so am I, in my good coat, looking as if I've slept." She pulled. "Don't undo my collar this time. I haven't got one on."



She went up at two.

Not far. Not to the Band; that was for the next week, when the flasks had something in them and Ward had read every one of Ferra's nineteen lines to the crew twice. Only up. Off the black lake among the reeds, with the lamps dark and the town asleep across the water and the moon down; Ned at the helm post under the cupola, with the canvas off it now and his hand on the lever, and Tobe and Cade in the house behind him, and Emeric at the trap in the floor with his sense out through the whole of her, every stone, and Ward on the reed bank in the dark with his arms folded, and Ferra beside him in her good coat with the crew book, looking as if she had slept.

"Lever," said Emeric.

Ned put the lever over a notch, as he had put hull two's over a thousand times.

The six lift stones woke under the deck. Emeric felt them wake: six small bright points in their cases under his feet, opening, drinking from the rail, from the bank of charged stones in the oak racks, a little, as much as they were told and no more. The lake water slid off her flat bottom. She came up out of it, dripping, a foot, two, level as a table, with the reeds standing up round her, and hung there.

Ned did not say anything. He stood at the helm post with his hand on the lever and looked down through the nearest pane at the black water a yard below her, going away, as the second notch took her up; and up; until she was over the reeds, and over the lake, and the reed bed was a dark smear below and the lake a dull sheet with the stars in it; and still he did not say anything, and his face in the starlight did not do anything.

At three hundred feet he took the lever back a notch, and she stopped, and hung, level, in the dark over the lake.

"Hm," said Ned.

That was all he said. He stood at his helm post with his hand on his lever three hundred feet over the lake he had worked boats on since he was twelve, and looked out through the cupola at the dark country all round, with the stars standing on the glass an inch above his bald head, and said "Hm," once, the way he said it to a boat that had done what he expected; and then, after a long while, more quietly, to himself, or to the boat, or to nobody:

"A hod."

He brought her down onto the water by the reeds at half past two as gently as he had set hull two down in the wet meadow below Wayford; and Tobe threw the line to Ward on the bank, and Ward made it fast, and nobody said anything at all, and they went home.



Armand asked to go on her on the Saturday.

He asked at the long table, at the end of the day's business, as a man asks for the salt; and the table went quiet.

He had been up ten times on the ship since the beginning of July, on Tuesdays and Saturdays, an hour and a quarter at the Band's foot each time by Imani's clock and never a minute more, and Ferra's column at the back of her book was a page and a half long. He came up the keep stair without stopping. He had not put his hand inside his coat at the table since the first flight, or not that Emeric had seen. And he had begun, in the last two weeks, to come down to the shed on the nights the ship did not go, and stand on the slip in his fur coat in the dark, looking at her, for an hour, and go back up.

"The ship's for the ship's business," he said. "You've said so. You can't go on taking her out twice a week for an old man's heart." He turned his stick on the floor. "The lighter will go up twice a week for whatever she's for. And there's a chair in her house." He did not look at Emeric. "With arms, and a strap, and a rail for the hand, and a window cut two fingers lower than it was cut the first time, for a man who stoops. I've stood on that slip most nights for a fortnight, Ward. I watched Fletcher lower it, with a lamp, swearing at the bronze." He looked at Ward. "That's three and one more. Your rule's kept."

"She is a new boat," said Ward. "Nothing aboard her has been four miles up. Not her seal. Not her pump. Not her heat."

"Nor had the ship's, once. And I have a fur coat."

"It was Wexler's," said Ferra, not looking up.

"It is a very good coat," said Armand. "He was a thief but he bought good coats."

Imani had not said anything. She was looking at Armand's hands, on the head of his stick. They were still. They had been still since Tuesday's flight. By tomorrow or the day after, if the summer held to its pattern, the right one would start, on the edge of a sheet, and he would put it in his lap; and then the ship would go up again, and it would stop.

"His mask on its hook by that chair, with the candle in it," she said. "A chute on him like the rest of them, every time, whether he could use it or not." She did not look at Armand when she said that. "He's not to go down into the hold. Not once, not for a minute, not to look. And me." She looked at Ward. "Every time he goes. Me."

"That is four and one more," said Ward.

"Then it's five," said Imani. "Ned can count."

Ward looked at her for a long breath, and then at Armand, and then, last, at Emeric; and Emeric found that everybody at the table was looking at him, and that it was his to say, and that he did not want it to be.

He looked at his teacher's hands on the stick.

"When the flasks are proved," he said. "Not before. Not on the first run. When we know she comes down."

"When the flasks are proved," said Armand, and inclined his head, as if accepting a reasonable term in a contract he had already decided to sign; and picked up his stick, and got up, and went to the door, quite steadily, and down the stair without stopping.

Ferra wrote something. Then she turned her book round on the table, without a word, and pushed it toward Emeric, and he looked at it: the back page, the column, A. ALOFT, a page and a half of dates and hours in her small square hand; and under the last line she had ruled another, and left it empty.
