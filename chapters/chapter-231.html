Chapter 231 - Salt Again

Zale came in on the sulphur boat, as he had gone out on one, and he came in the way weather comes in.

Emeric did not know he was coming. Nobody did; there had been one letter in two years, and it had said when I have seen it I will come, which was a promise with no date in it, the only kind Zale made. The first Emeric knew of it was Hobb, at the door of the long room in the palace's east range in the middle of a morning, out of breath from the stair, saying that there was a man at the quay on the lower water asking for the prince by his first name and laughing at the harbourmaster, and that Fletcher had sent up to say it was the brother.

He went down himself. He did not think about it until he was on the stair, and then he thought that a year ago he would have sent somebody, and did not know what that meant about him, a year ago or now.

Zale was sitting on a bollard at the end of the quay with his sea bag between his feet, watching the yard. He had the brown of a man who had been out in weather for a long time, and he was thinner, and there was grey over one ear that had not been there. He was watching the walls of Fletcher's yard, the new high ones round the dock, with an interest that he was taking some trouble to make look idle. When Emeric came down the quay he stood up, and looked at him, and laughed, the easy laugh, the one that came off.

"You've got fat," he said.

"I've got a stair. Three hundred steps of it. I'm thinner than I was."

"Then you were fat before." Zale put out his hand, and then did not wait for Emeric to take it, and put his arms round him instead, briefly, hard, the way he had done at the gate, as if he were getting it over with. He smelled of salt and tar and sulphur and a great many days in the same shirt. When he let go he picked up his bag. "I stopped at Emberfell. Mother says you're to eat. She said it the way she says the weather, so I don't know if it's an order."

"What else did she say?"

"That you'd tell me what you wanted me to know." Zale looked at him sideways. "She said that the way she says the weather too."

He had brought stones. That was the first thing out of the bag, on the long-room table that afternoon, with Ferra and Ward and Bram there because Emeric had asked them to be, and Saban there because Saban had heard: a pouch of the pale glassy splinters off the island, a bigger pouch than the first, knotted with a sailor's knot that Zale undid with his teeth.

"Last of them," he said. "On that coast, anyway. There's nobody selling this summer. I went into every harbour tavern from the cape to the Kesrath shore where I bought these the first time, and the men who used to sell them under the table weren't under it. Some of them were there, drinking. They just weren't selling. One of them told me the buyers had stopped coming." He tipped the splinters out onto the table. "The ones who used to come up from the south, from over the water, with the good silver. Not a word from them since spring. So the sellers sat on what they had, and then the Kesrath boats stopped bringing more, and I bought the last of it off a man who wanted it gone."

Saban had picked up one of the splinters and was holding it to the window. "Stopped when?"

"Spring. Early summer. He wasn't a man who kept a calendar."

Saban looked at Emeric across the table and said nothing, which for Saban was a paragraph. Emeric knew what he meant. A market did not go quiet at both ends at once by accident. Somebody at the top of it had told it to.

Emeric filed it. There was nothing to do with it yet.

"And the other thing," he said.

Zale sat back. He had taken the chair nearest the door without anybody noticing him take it, and Ward had noticed, and had moved his own chair a foot, so that the two of them sat on either side of the door like a pair of posts. Zale had noticed that too.

"His name was Ansel Vey," Zale said. "The copier. The man who wrote the book."

Nobody said anything. Ferra's pen had stopped.

"Merrow Sound," Zale said. "It's a long way. It's past where our charts stop and past where theirs stop, on the far side of the western sea, a town at the head of a long inlet where it rains the way I told you in the letter, sideways and from underneath. They keep a school there, of sorts. A house of men who copy old things. He was one of theirs. There's a man there still who was a boy there when Vey went. He remembered him because Vey used to give him the ends of candles." He smiled a little. "I'd have remembered him for that too. Vey found the signs first in a ruin up the coast from Merrow, on a headland, in the fog. The same ones that are in the book. The same cuts, the same tree. He copied them for years. Then he got it into his head that they came from somewhere, and that somewhere was east, and he asked for a boat east, and they gave him one, and he never came back." Zale spread his hands. "That's what they have. A name, and a room he used to sleep in, and a boy who's an old man now who got candle ends."

"Ansel Vey," Ferra said, and wrote it, and underlined it, which she almost never did.

"Of Merrow Sound." Zale watched her write it. "I thought you'd want it in the book. It's the thing I came home with. It's the only thing I found out there that anybody else hadn't found first."

He told the rest of it at supper, and some of it after, on the gallery with Emeric, with the dome dark behind its screens below them and a jug between them that Zale drank most of.

He had gone to the island. He had not said so at supper. He said it on the gallery, with the lamps out, the way he had said the things he did not want to say in the small room at Kaldo two years ago, looking at the dark instead of at his brother.

"You told me to be careful there," he said. "Because it was somebody's. I was careful." He drank. "I found a Kesrath boatman in a harbour on the other shore who ran supplies out to it, every tenth day. Barrels. Timber. Men. He'd take a passenger if the passenger paid, and didn't ask questions, and was sick over the right side. I paid. I went out with a work crew, forty of them, in the dark of the moon, and sat on the island for ten days in a stone hut with a sailcloth roof while they worked the ridge at night and slept by day, and on the second night I walked up to where they were working, as if I were one of them, and a man with a lantern stopped me at the foot of the benches and asked me who I was, in their speech. I told him in mine. He told me in his that if I came up the benches again I'd go into the hole with the others." Zale was quiet for a moment. "He didn't say which others. I didn't ask him. I went back to the hut and sat in it for eight days and on the tenth I went back out on the dawn boat."

Emeric did not move. He was holding his cup on his knee and he kept holding it.

"Why?" he said. "You'd been told."

"Because he went there." Zale turned his head. "Vey. It's in the book, the last page. The channel, the shards, the way down. I read that page a hundred times on a hundred boats. I wanted to stand where he stood. That's all. I don't know what I thought I'd find." He laughed, not the easy one. "I found forty Kesrathi and a man with a lantern."

"And the book."

"What about it?"

"It's the last page," Emeric said. "You said so. The way down, and he'd follow it in the morning. If this is the last page. And then he went down, and he didn't come up." He set the cup on the gallery rail. "So how is it on your window seat and not down the hole with him?"

Zale looked at him for a long time. Then he laughed again, properly this time, and shook his head.

"Two years," he said, "I've been carrying that book about, and you're the first person who's asked me that. I never asked it myself till the island." He put his elbows on his knees. "The boatman. The one who took me out. On the ninth night in the hut, when the crew was up the ridge and he'd come ashore early for the next day's loading, he got drunk with me, because I had a bottle and he didn't, and he told me a story his father used to tell. About a foreigner who came out on his father's boat, a long time ago, before the Kesrath men were working there regular. A thin man with a cough, who couldn't speak anything anybody could understand and drew pictures instead. He went up to the ridge alone. On the last morning he came down to the boat before dawn and gave the boatman's father a book, and money, a lot of money, and made him understand by pictures that he was to take the book back to the coast. Then he went back up the ridge. And the boat waited for him till the tide, and he didn't come, and they left." Zale shrugged. "The father took the money. He didn't know what to do with the book, so he sold it. He sold it to a man who sold things. And it went up and down that coast from hand to hand for years and years, I should think, a book nobody could read, and a fool of a prince bought it in a harbour tavern for the price of a supper because it had a drawing of a tree in it he liked."

"He sent it home," said Emeric slowly.

"He tried to. He wrote the last page the night before, up there, and came down in the morning and put it in the only hand going anywhere, and went back up." Zale looked out at the dark. "He knew he mightn't come back. It says so. If this is the last page. So he sent the book away from where he was going, and took something smaller down with him. That's what I'd have done." He picked up the jug and found it empty and put it down. "I sat on that island for eight more days after the boatman told me that, in that hut, looking up at the ridge. I couldn't get up it. Nobody could. They had lanterns on the benches all night." He was quiet. "And on the tenth day I went back out on the dawn boat and stood at the back of it and looked at the island till I couldn't see it."

Emeric said, "When?"

"When what?"

"The dawn boat. When was it?"

Zale thought. "Winter. Early. The long nights. I'd been sick for a week on the way out, so I'd have to count." He counted, on his fingers, the way a sailor counts tides. "The second week of the winter, near enough. The tenth day of their row. They cut notches on a board on the hut door. Ten, and a boat." He looked at his brother. "Why?"

"I like to know when things were."

"You always did," said Zale. "You used to ask Father what time it was when he'd just told you a story. As if it mattered what time the dragon came."

He went down to the counting room after Zale had gone to bed.

It was in the east range now, beside the table room, and Ferra's books were on their shelves in the order she kept them, which was an order nobody else had ever been able to follow and nobody had ever needed to, because she was always there. She was not there now. He did not wake her. He took the lamp and found the book he wanted himself, the flight log from the second winter, bound in grey, and opened it at the island.

Her hand. The small square figures down the page. Ashendeep. Second week of winter, tenth day on the Kesrath board. Up the line an hour before dawn, by the ship's glass. And further down, the entry he had asked her to make and then forgotten, because he had told himself to: A boat out of the anchorage under the east fort, pulling east by a little south, for the Kesrath shore. Supply. On the tenth day, as the board said. One man standing at the stern, facing the sky.

He sat with it for a long time.

Facing the sky. Not the island; not the ridge he had sat under for ten days and could not climb. The sky. To the west, where the dark was still thick. Where a long black shape had gone up off the rock for a minute against a greying sky, and climbed, and been gone.

He had been a hundred feet up a line with the sense out, and he had looked down at a man at a stern rail and thought: a rower resting. An overseer counting stars. Nobody. And had not thought about him again.

He closed the book and put it back on the shelf where it had been, exactly where it had been, and sat in the dark with the lamp turned low. He could not say it. That was the whole of it. There was no way to tell Zale that the thing at the stern of the dawn boat had been looking at his brother, without telling him what his brother had been hanging from. And he was not ready to tell Zale that. Not tonight. Not on a gallery with an empty jug.

You'd tell me what you wanted me to know. He heard it in his mother's voice, which he had not heard say it.

He went up to bed and did not sleep much.

In the morning he took Zale down to the fitting shop.

It was a long low room at the back of the works, off Bram's shop, with a good north light and a stove at one end and the four frames along the wall. The suits hung on them. Bram was there, and Ferra, who had come down because she came down to everything, and two of the rubber shed's men who pretended to be sweeping.

Zale stood in the doorway for a moment and looked at the suits. Then he came in and walked along them, slowly, the way he had walked along the ship's rail the first time Emeric had ever seen him on a deck, when they were boys and their father had taken them out on the king's barge and Zale had been at the rail the whole day and Emeric had been sick. He stopped at the fourth suit and looked at it longest.

"That's mine," he said. It was not a question.

"It's the spare," said Ferra. "And it's yours."

Zale looked at her, and then at Emeric, and his face did something complicated and then decided on a grin. "You made me a suit. Before I came home. You didn't know I was coming home."

"You said you would."

"I say a lot of things." He touched the suit's chest plates, the overlapping scales. "What is it?"

"It's for going under water. A long way under, for a long time, and coming back." Emeric took the helmet off the bench and held it out. "I'm going to go down where Ansel Vey went. Further than he went. With the right things, which he didn't have. I've been getting ready for it all summer." He watched Zale take the helmet in both hands and look through the green glass at the room. "It's your book. I thought you'd want to come."

Zale did not answer that. He was looking through the glass.

They put him in it. Bram did most of it, with his mouth full of pins, and it was a little broad across the chest and a little long in the arm, as the prince had said it would be, and easier to take in than let out; and Bram took it in there on the frame with a knife and a pot of rubber cement, and Zale stood with his arms out like a scarecrow and bore it and talked the whole time, about Merrow and the rain and the boy with the candle ends and a woman in a harbour on the Kesrath shore who had tried to sell him her daughter or possibly a goat, he had never been sure which. The rubber men stopped pretending to sweep.

When it was done he stood in it in the middle of the shop, in the plates and the frame and the rubber, without the helmet, and looked down at himself. Then he reached back over his shoulder and found the catch there, under the steel of the frame, and the folded thing it held, and frowned. He reached round to the small of his back and found the pack there, and the ring on its cord at his hip.

"Emeric," he said.

"Yes."

"Why does a suit for going under water have a cape on it." He turned round, looking over his shoulder, like a dog after its tail. "And a pack with a ring on a string, which I know what that is, I've seen a man jump off a cliff at Merrow with one of those, it was a sheet and a lot of rope and he broke both his legs."

Bram, at the bench, put his pins down very carefully and did not look up.

"I'll show you tonight," Emeric said.

He showed him at the lake, after dark, at the works jetty, with nobody there but Ward and Ferra and Bram at the rope.

It was a still night, early in the autumn, with no moon yet and the stars thick over the water, and the lamps of the island out on the water where the old town was going to bed, and the lamps of New Kaldo beyond. The lake lay flat and black. Ward had brought the rope, a good one, a hemp line tarred in island pitch, and he tied one end of it round the bollard at the jetty's end himself and checked it twice, and brought the other end to Zale and clipped it to the ring on the front of the suit's belt, and checked that twice.

"Rated for his whole weight," Ward said, to nobody. "Because the stones are what fail."

Zale looked at him, and at the rope, and at his brother.

"Stones," he said.

"Stand still," said Emeric. "Stand with your feet a little apart. Put your hands out a bit, for balance. Not far." He put his own hand on the steel of the frame at Zale's back, between the shoulder blades, where the two upper nodes sat under the plates in their iron. "I'm going to do it. Not you. You've got the levers on the gauntlet, and you'll learn them, but not tonight. Tonight you just stand there."

"Do what?"

Emeric told the nodes. Not much. The first notch, the one Ward called Light, the one that took some of a man's weight and not all of it.

Zale made a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound a man makes when he steps onto a stair in the dark and the stair is not where his foot expected it. His hands went out wide of their own accord. His knees bent. He stood on the jetty's planks and was lighter than he had any right to be, a man in plate and steel and lead who weighed, now, about what a boy did.

"What," he said.

"Stand still."

Emeric gave the nodes a little more. And a little more. Until the weight that was left on Zale's boots was not enough to keep them on the planks, and they came off, a finger's width, and then a hand's, and then he was standing on nothing, a foot above the jetty, turning a little on the rope as the rope took up and held him, his arms out, his mouth open, his face in the starlight a boy's face, the one that had gone out of the stable yard.

He did not shout. Emeric had thought he might. He did not say anything at all. He hung there on the end of Ward's rope over the jetty with the black lake under him and the stars over him, and looked down at his own feet, and at the planks under them, and at the gap between. He moved one foot, carefully, as a man moves on ice. He turned his head and looked at the rope, going taut from his belt to the bollard, and at Ward's hand on it, and at Bram behind Ward with the slack coiled over his arm. And then he looked at his brother, standing on the jetty below him with a hand up on the frame at his back.

"Up," Zale said. "Take me up."

Emeric let the rope out a little, and the nodes a little more, and Zale went up. Ten feet. Twenty. Out over the water at the end of the line, in the dark, a black shape against the stars, turning slowly, until Ward said, "That's the rope," and Emeric held him there.

He was up there a long time. Emeric did not hurry him. Nobody said anything on the jetty. After a while Zale put his arms out straight from his sides, like a boy playing at birds, and held them there, and stayed like that.

Then Emeric brought him down, slowly, the way he had once brought himself down, the first time, a long time ago, with nobody holding any rope at all; and Zale's boots found the planks, and the weight came back into them, all of it, and his knees gave and he sat down hard on the jetty with his back against the bollard and his legs out in front of him, and put his head back against the wood, and looked at the sky.

Ferra had come to stand beside Emeric. She did not write anything. Her book was shut under her arm.

"Men fly," Zale said, to the sky.

"Some."

"You fly."

"Yes."

"How long?"

Emeric thought about lying and did not. "Years."

Zale turned his head against the bollard and looked at him. There was a great deal in the look, and Emeric could read some of it and not the rest. There was the question after that one, and the one after that. Where to. In what. How high. Who else. The cape and the pack with the ring on it, and what a man would need them for if all he ever did was hang on a rope off a jetty. Zale was not a stupid man. He had been past the edge of the map. He could count.

He did not ask any of them. He looked at his brother for a long moment, and Emeric saw him see the door, and decide, for now, not to go through it. Zale always knew where the door was.

"Years," Zale said. He laughed, very quietly, at the sky. "I laughed when they told me about the exile. Did I tell you that? In a tavern. I thought, that's Emeric, he always did what he was told."

"You told me."

"I'm not laughing now." He got up, slowly, with a hand on the bollard, stiff in the new suit, and stood, and felt the weight of it on him, and looked out at the dark lake. "The cave. Vey's way down. You're going."

"I'm going."

"Then I'm going." He turned. "Don't tell me why I shouldn't. I've heard you think it since the fitting shop. It's dangerous, I've never done it, I've been home a day, I don't know what the half of this is." He knocked his knuckles on his own chest plate. "It's my book, Emeric. I carried it round the world. It's why I stayed gone, if you want the truth, not Corbin, not the sea. Somebody went off the edge of the map after something and didn't come back, and I wanted to know what it was." He looked at the rope still clipped to his belt. "I don't generally want to go anywhere I can't see the way out of. You know that about me."

"I know."

"So I'll learn where the way out is." Zale unclipped the rope and handed the end of it to Ward, who took it. "Teach me."

Emeric looked at Ferra. She looked back at him, and after a moment she opened her book, there on the jetty in the starlight, and wrote something on the page she had headed Dive a month ago, in the list of names that had had three names on it and a blank. He did not need to see it. He knew what she had written, because the blank had always been for him.

"Every night," he said to his brother. "In the lake. Till you're sick of it."

"I'm never sick of water," said Zale. "Ask anybody."

He was sick of it inside a week. He never said so.
