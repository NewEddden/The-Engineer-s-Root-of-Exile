Chapter 270 - Coal

Fennick blew up his shed on the sixth of June, at twenty past four in the afternoon, and lost nothing in it but the shed and his eyebrows.

The town heard it. The town could not help hearing it: a flat hard crack from the quarry road a mile west of the walls, that rattled the station's windows and put every pigeon off the castle roof at once and set the dogs going from the port to the north gate; and then, a long breath later, a column of grey smoke going straight up out of the trees beyond the brickfield, very tall and very thin, like a pencil stood on end. Every head in Kaldo turned toward it. In the castle ward, Emeric was halfway across the cobbles with a drawing under his arm, and he stopped, and looked at the smoke, and knew at once whose it was, and started running.

Ward got there first. Ward always got there first.

The shed had stood at the end of the quarry track on its own, a long way from anything else, because Fennick had asked for it there, and Emeric had said yes, and Ward had walked out and looked at the place and said it was not far enough, and had it moved another two hundred yards into the scrub. It was gone now. Where it had been there was a ring of black earth thirty feet across, with bits of board lying round it in the grass and a tin roof sheet hanging in an oak tree twenty yards off, slowly turning; and in the middle of the ring, sitting on the ground with his legs out in front of him like a child at the seaside, was Fennick.

His face was black. His hair, which had been grey and plentiful, stood up off his head in a stiff brush, white at the tips. His eyebrows were gone. He was looking at his hands, which he held up in front of him, turning them over, one and then the other, slowly, as though he had been given them that morning and was not yet sure how they worked.

Ward was standing over him. Ward did not say anything.

"It's the cold," said Fennick, to his hands. He did not look up. His voice came out very loud, the way a man's voice does when his ears are ringing. "It wants to be kept cold. I knew it wants to be kept cold. I'd the ice on it. I'd the ice on it all morning. Then the ice ran out and I thought, I'll do the last bit, it's only a little bit, the last of the wash, and it's not so warm today." He turned his right hand over. "It's warmer than you'd think, a shed. In June. With the door shut."

"Are you hurt?" said Emeric, coming up, out of breath.

"I don't think so." Fennick looked up at him. The whites of his eyes were very white in the black. "I was outside. I'd gone outside for a breath of air and to get away from the smell, it gives you a head, and I was standing at the door with my back to it, and it went." He looked round at the ring of black earth as though seeing it for the first time. "I think the door saved me. It went past me. The door went past me." He pointed, vaguely, at the oak tree. "That's my door."

It was. It was in the oak, above the roof sheet, wedged in a fork, quite high.

Imani came up the track ten minutes later in the ward's cart with her bag, and went over Fennick from his scalp to his boots on the black earth while he sat there and told her about the cold; and found nothing wrong with him but the eyebrows and the hearing, which she said would come back in a day or two, and a long cut on the back of his left hand from a bit of the shed's window that he had not noticed. She stitched it there, sitting on the ground. Fennick watched her do it with interest.

"Nitroglycerin," he said, while she tied off the third stitch. He said it the way Emeric had said it to him in March, carefully, every part of it; and then he said it again, as if he liked the sound. "It's the best thing I've ever made. It's the best thing anybody's ever made. Do you know how much of it was in there?" He held up his thumb and forefinger, a little apart. "That much. In a dish. That's all."

"I know," said Emeric.

"For the station's rock. The footings. The bedrock's like iron down there, Bram's men have been at it with bars and powder for a fortnight and they've gone in a foot." Fennick looked at the ring. "That much'd have gone in ten."

Ward had not said anything since they arrived. He said something now.

"Your key."

Fennick looked up at him.

Fennick had a key. It was an iron key on a leather loop, and it opened the powder store at the quarry and the shed's door, which was in a tree, and the iron box under the shed's floor where he kept what he was working on, which was nowhere; and Ward had given it to him himself, when Fennick had first come to Kaldo and been made the barony's man for anything that went bang, and had said, giving it, that it was Ward's key and that Fennick would have it as long as Ward said.

Fennick took it off his neck and gave it to him.

"A month," said Ward. He put it in his pocket. "Then we'll see."



The coal ground was two miles downstream, on the north bank, in a field of thistles.

Emeric had been walking it for a fortnight, with Bram and a surveyor's chain and Ottoline's best level on its legs; and on the evening after the shed went up, with the smell of it still faint on the west wind, he stood in the middle of the field with the drawings unrolled on a board on the surveyor's tripod and told Bram what was going to be there, and Bram listened, and argued, and was right about the cooling water.

A power station. Emeric had written it across the top of the first sheet, in the winter, and then crossed out the word power, because Bram had said everything Kaldo did was power, the river was power and the wind was power and the band was power, and what this one was, was coal. So the sheet said COAL STATION. A boiler house, long and high and brick, along the bank, with a row of fireboxes in it fed from a coal bunker on its back wall, and the bunker fed in its turn from a siding off the Grayrock line that would come across the river on a new bridge and run along the field's top edge, so that the Grayrock coal came down the valley in its own trains and went straight off the wagons into the bunker and out of the bunker into the fires and never touched a man's shovel between the pit and the flame. The boilers: four of them, of the kind Bram's shop had built for the express, rolled steel plate and riveted, tested with water at half again what they would ever be asked to bear before a fire was lit under them. And then, beside the boiler house, the turbine hall.

That was the thing.

"Not an engine," Emeric had said, in the winter, to the six round Ferra's table, with the first drawing of it in front of them. "Not a piston. There's no piston in it. The steam comes in at one end, hot and hard, and goes through a row of blades on a shaft, and the blades are set at an angle, like the sails of a mill, so that the steam going through them turns them. Then another row. Then another, each one a little bigger than the last, because the steam's grown as it went and has more room to push in. And the shaft turns, very fast, faster than any engine we've ever built. And on the end of the shaft, the alternator, turning with it." He had drawn it for them: the long casing, the shaft, the blades in their rings. "Steam turbine."

"Fast," Bram had said. "How fast?"

"Fifty times a second. Near enough."

Bram had put his pipe down.

"A thing that size," he had said, "turning fifty times a second, has only to be a hair out of true, and it'll walk itself off its bed and through the wall."

"Yes," Emeric had said. "That's the whole of the work."

The blades were the other half of the work. They had to be shaped as exactly as anything Kaldo had made: every one of them in a row the same as the next to a hair, curved just so, and set in their ring to a hair, or the ring would be out of true and the shaft would shake. The Mana Forge could shape them. It had shaped the masters already, in the winter, one blade of each row, in the rebuilt Forge's long bed, in a night; Emeric had written the run, and watched it go, and taken the masters out in the morning, and they were perfect.

They were not going into the turbine.

"Gauges," Bram had said, standing over them on the cellar's bench in the morning, before Emeric could say it. "We cut every blade in the shop. Off the masters. On the mill. And measure it against the master with the gauges." He had picked one up. "If the Forge goes down, or you go down, we can still cut a blade."

That had been the order. The Forge made the masters, once. Bram's shop made the blades, all of them, every one, on the milling machine with the copying arm that Dov had built in March, and checked each one against its master on the gauge before it went in the ring; and taught three of Bram's apprentices how, in case. The masters went in a box in the cellar under Ferra's key, with the Forge's run beside them on a sheet, so that anyone who could read it could make the masters again if the box was ever lost.

"And it's only coal," said Bram now, in the thistle field, in the evening light, looking at the drawings on the tripod. "All of it. No stones."

"No stones in the loop."

"Not one."

"Not one," said Emeric. "If a dead zone came down on this field tomorrow, the fires would go on burning and the steam would go on going through the blades and the hospital would go on being lit, and nobody inside the walls would know it had happened."

Bram looked at the field for a long while: the thistles, and the river going by below it brown and quiet, and the line of white pegs that marked where the boiler house would stand, and the darker line beyond, where the turbine hall would.

"How long?" he said.

"Footings this summer. Walls before the frost. The boilers and the turbine in the winter. First fire in the spring."

"That's not what I asked."

Emeric looked at him.

"How long does it run?" said Bram. "Before you pull it down."

Emeric had not said anything about pulling it down. He had not said it to anybody. He had not written it on any drawing. But Bram had been watching him draw for ten years, and Bram had seen the drawings of the falls dam, and the river dam behind it, and the wind row at Milford, and the second pot room, and had heard him say to Ferra in the hall one evening in May, not knowing Bram was on the stair, that coal was a loan you took out against your grandchildren's air.

"Ten years," said Emeric. "If we're quick."

Bram nodded slowly.

"Then build it to last fifty," he said, "because you'll not be quick." He started rolling up the drawings. "And when you pull it down, you'll want the brick."

Ferra wrote it down that night. She did not ask him about it; Bram told her, at supper, between the meat and the pudding, as if it were nothing; and she put her fork down and took her pencil out of her book's spine and opened the book to a page near the back, a page Emeric could see from across the table had nothing on it but a date at its head, and wrote under the date, small, the coal station, and a second date, and closed the book.

He did not ask her what the second date was. He thought he knew. He thought she had given him eleven years, not ten, because she knew him too.



The band engine went into its box on the coal ground on the twenty-third of June, before there was anything else on the coal ground but pegs and thistles and a gang digging footings, and that was on purpose.

Ferra asked him why, at the table. She asked him the way she asked about money: not whether, but why this and why now and why there.

"There's a coal station going up that'll make more power than all of Kaldo can use for five years," she said. "Without a stone in it. You've said so a dozen times. And the first thing you put on its ground is a stone engine." She put her finger on the line in her book. "Before the boilers. Before the bridge. Before anything."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was a fair question. He had known she would ask it, and he had thought about how to answer it, and in the end he answered it the way he would have answered Bram.

"Because of the hospital," he said.

The band engine was not new. The first of it, the pair, had been built last August in Bram's shop, out of two iron weights and a ring and a box, and it had run ever since in the old powerhouse yard behind the dynamo house, in its riveted iron box with its airlock and its blowout panel over the sand pit, doing nothing very much. Two weights, matched to the ounce, going up and down in their greased sleeves with a gravity stone in each one's top; a wave cut in a ring underneath, so that the weights falling and rising turned it; and the deciding stone in the corner that told each stone when to make its weight heavy and when light. It was not free power. Nothing was. It burned the band: the box was filled with the stuff the ships brought down from the sky, and the stones drank it as they spent, and the box had to be filled again, every few days, from the bottles off the condensing plant. Ned Tallis, who had run it since the winter, said it was like a stove, and that he had never met a stove that ate so much.

But it did not need coal, and it did not need the river, and it did not need the wind. It needed the band, and the band came down from the sky every night in the Hod's tanks whatever the weather on the ground; and as long as there was band in the box, the weights went up and down.

And now there was a set of eight.

Bram had built it in May. Eight weights in two rows of four, each pair opposite on its own wave, all of them turning one long shaft through a gearbox; the box twice the size of the old one and riveted from the plate the express's boilers were rolled from, with two airlocks and two blowout panels and a sand pit under each; and Ferra's column for it in the works book already, with the band it would burn by the week. It sat on its own footing on the coal ground at the end of the line of pegs where the turbine hall would stand, with a shaft coming out of the box's side through its packed collar; and on the end of the shaft, a clutch, a heavy iron thing of plates and springs and a lever as long as a man's arm; and beyond the clutch, nothing yet. A bed of oak beams, empty, waiting.

"When the turbine's built," said Emeric, "its alternator goes on that bed. On the far side of the clutch. Turned by the turbine, all day, every day. The engine sits on the near side with the clutch out, doing nothing, burning nothing, its weights at rest." He drew it in the air for her. "And there's a wire from the alternator up the valley to the hospital, and to the school, and to the water towers' pumps. Those three first. Before anything else in the town gets a wire from this station, those three get theirs."

"And if the coal stops."

"Then the turbine slows, and Ned feels it in the needle on the board, and he throws the clutch in." He put his hand down. "And the engine takes the alternator. And the hospital stays lit."

"For how long?"

"For as long as there's band in the box. A week, on what we keep there. Longer if the Hod flies."

Ferra looked at her book for a long time.

"And if a dead zone comes down," she said, "the engine stops. And the coal doesn't."

"And the coal doesn't."

"So one carries the other." She did not look up. "Whichever one's standing."

"That's the rule."

She wrote. He could see it from where he sat, the short line under the engine's figures in the works book, in her square hand: What must not stop goes on what cannot stop.

"That's not the rule," said Emeric. "That's better."

"It's yours," said Ferra. "You said it about the ward's lamps, years ago. To Bram. I wrote it down then." She turned back through the books in her head, and he watched her find it. "I've only moved it."

Ned Tallis painted it on the band engine's box the next week, on the iron, in white, in letters a hand high, along the side facing the river, where the gang digging the turbine hall's footings would see it every time they straightened their backs; and under it, smaller, because he was Ned, IT'S A STOVE.



Ward gave Fennick his key back on the sixth of July, a month to the day, at the hill.

He did not tell anybody he was going to. He walked Fennick out of the town at seven in the morning by the north gate, the two of them, Fennick with his eyebrows grown back in a thin grey fuzz that made him look permanently surprised; and up the old track onto the hill to the north, past the brickfield, to the mouth of the mine.

Emeric had reopened that mine in his first summer in Kaldo, with Bram and a dozen men and a pump made of a barrel and a lever. It had given them iron that broke when you heated it and lead and the zinc that went into Tess's first cells, and then it had flooded in the November, the lower working, as it had flooded every November for a hundred years before them; and they had pumped it out in the spring and taken what they needed and let it flood again, and gone on to Grayrock's ore, and in time stopped going down to the lower working at all. The upper workings were a store now for the brickfield. The lower was a black hole in the hill full of water.

It was not full of water now. Ward had had it pumped dry in June by one of the express's little steam pumps on a cart, and shored, and lit.

Emeric found out on the seventh, when Fennick came to the castle with his key on its loop round his neck and his face pink under the fuzz, and asked to see him, and stood in Ferra's room with his hat in his hands and could not get it out for a long while.

"He's given me the hill," he said at last.

"The hill?"

"The lower working. All of it. For a test ground." Fennick turned his hat round. "He walked me down it yesterday. Lamps on wire all the way down from the hamlet's line, and oil lamps hung beside every one of them. He'd had it all done. In June. While I hadn't the key." He looked at the floor. "He said it's what the shed should have been. A long way under a lot of rock. Where a bang's only a mine's noise, and the rock takes the shock, and the smoke goes up the old shaft like a mine's smoke, and anybody watching from the road sees carts going in and out of a mine, which is what a mine looks like."

Emeric looked at Ferra. Ferra was writing, and did not look up.

"And he said," said Fennick, "that if anything goes bad down there, if anybody ever makes a dead zone in it by mistake, it's in solid rock, and it'll fill in again in minutes, and never come up to the top." He put his hat on, and took it off again. "He'd thought of all of it. I'd not thought of any of it. He's a knight. What's a knight know about rock?"

"He's known about rock since before you were born," said Emeric. "He's spent his whole life being thrown into it."

He went up to see it on the eighth, with Ward.

Ward walked him down the long sloping drift in the lamplight without saying much, past the old wooden props he remembered and the new iron ones he did not, down to where the drift opened into the lower working. It was bigger than he remembered. In the first summer he had seen it by lantern, half full of black water, a cave with a floor of mud. Now it was a long dry hall cut in the dark grey rock, as long as the castle ward and half as wide, with the rock of the walls and roof glistening a little in the lamps where the water still seeped; lamps on their wire every ten yards down both walls, and an oil lamp on its hook beside each; and at the far end, where the working had been cut into the face and abandoned a hundred years ago, a wall of bare rock, raw, flat, with a chalk line drawn across it at the height of a man's head, and a number chalked under it in Fennick's hand.

Behind them, set back in a side cut, there was a heavy timber screen with a slit in it at eye height, and a bench behind the screen, and a pail of sand.

"A proving gallery," said Emeric.

Ward looked at him.

"That's what it's called," said Emeric. "Where they test a thing. A gun, or a charge, or a stone. Before it goes anywhere it matters." He did not say where he had learned the words. "Somewhere a long way under, where nothing can see and nothing can hurt."

"I didn't know it had a name," said Ward. "I only knew what it was for." He looked at the face at the end. "Fennick's first test is in three days. The station's footing charge. A dish of it in a hole in that face, and a fuse, and the screen." He looked at Emeric. "I'll be behind the screen. I'll want you not to be."

"I'd like to try something first."

Ward waited.

Emeric walked down the long dry hall to the face. He put his hand flat on the rock beside the chalk line. It was cold and wet and dense and quiet, the deep dense quiet of a great deal of stone, so much stone that his sense, going into it, slowed down and stopped like a cart in mud.

The face was dead. Ward had had a dead zone put on it a week before, a small one, forced, by a stone overloaded in a hole in the rock with the men behind the screen; Imani had insisted on that before anyone worked down there, to know how long it took to fill, and it had filled in eleven minutes, the rock pushing the mana back in round the hole from every side the way water fills a footprint in wet sand. The face had been chalked for it since: the dead face, Fennick called it, his test face, the one nothing magic would ever be asked to hold. The station's footings were going to be blasted out of rock just like this. Emeric wanted to know how this rock broke before Fennick put a dish of anything into it.

There was a block lying at the face's foot, where the working had been abandoned: a slab of the grey rock as big as a cart's bed, that had come off the roof sometime in the last hundred years and lain there under the water ever since.

He put his hand on it.

He lifted it. Not far. A body's height, slowly, with a cast he had done ten thousand times on lesser things, the gravity running down his arm into the stone the way it had run into Bram's hammer heads and the express's wheels and the Hod's keel; and the block came up off the floor, dripping, and hung there in the lamplight, turning a little.

Ward had come down the hall behind him. He stopped ten yards off.

Emeric moved it out over the dead face's foot, a little way, and held it there.

Then he made it heavy.

Not let it fall. Made it heavy. The same cast, turned round: the stone told that its weight was not its weight but four times its weight, six, ten, the way the heavy trebuchet's counterweight was told, so that it came down out of the air not as a stone falls but as if the hill itself had leaned on it.

It hit the floor at the face's foot with a sound he felt in his teeth.

The floor broke. Not cracked: broke. It went in under the block like a pie crust under a thumb, a star of splits running out from it across the gallery floor and up the face itself, three, five, a dozen, and the chalk line across the face jumped and went crooked as the rock behind it shifted. Dust came out of the splits in long grey puffs. Somewhere back in the dark of the working, small stones fell, a patter, like the first of a rain.

Then quiet.

The block lay where it had landed, sunk to a third of its depth in a crater of broken grey rock.

Emeric stood and looked at it. His arm hummed, to the shoulder.

Ward had not moved.

"How heavy?" he said.

"Ten times." Emeric looked at the splits running up the face. "Twelve, at the end."

"For how long?"

"From there to the floor. Less than a breath."

"And it cost you?"

He thought about it. He had done heavier casts. He had held a hull's keel off its blocks in the slip at the port for a quarter of an hour once, with Bram swearing under it, and lain on his back on the slip afterward for the rest of the afternoon. This had been one breath.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing I'd notice."

Ward walked forward, slowly, to the edge of the crater, and looked down into it at the block, and at the splits running away from it across the floor, and up at the face.

"That's not a test of the rock," he said.

"It's a test of the rock."

"It's a test of the rock," said Ward, "and it's a test of something else," and he looked at Emeric across the crater in the lamplight, and Emeric saw him put it away in the place where he put things he would come back to, and turn, and walk back up the gallery toward the screen.

Emeric stayed.

He looked at the block a long time. And he did not see the block, after a while. He saw a field.

He had been fifteen. It was the summer before the summer he stopped going to his father's councils, a long hot summer of dust, and his father had come to his rooms one morning at six, himself, without sending, which he had never done before and never did again, and said put your boots on; and they had ridden out of the capital with a dozen of the guard, south, two hours, to the crown's proving field. He remembered the field. A long flat valley floor with the grass burnt brown, and earthworks thrown up along it, and at the end of it, by a river, the hulk of an old warship hauled up on rollers, her masts gone, her side to the field.

There had been a man there of perhaps twenty, from the tower. He had been introduced. Halric Venn. A gravity mage, the only one in his year; thin and serious, with ink on his fingers, who had looked at the prince of fifteen standing beside the king as if he were a sum he could not make come out. He had stood at the breech of a long iron tube on a carriage, as long as three men lying down, with a gravity stone set in its back end in an iron cup; and he had put his hand on the stone, and two of the crown's men had put an iron ball in the tube's mouth, and Venn had woken the stone, and nothing had happened at all that Emeric could hear.

And then the hulk's side had caved in.

It had gone in like a kicked barrel, three hundred yards off, a great round hole and the planks round it bent inward and splintered, and the whole hulk had rocked on its rollers; and only then, a long while after, had there been any noise, a groaning of timber coming back across the field. There had been no bang. A gravity bombard did not bang. It pushed.

The crown had others. He had seen them that day too, one after another, while his father stood beside him with his hands behind his back and said almost nothing: the heavy trebuchet, its counterweight a box of stone with a gravity stone set in its top, so that a frame half the size of an old trebuchet threw a rock twice as far; and the burst shot. They had not fired the burst shot. It was not done. The officer who showed it to them had told them, in a low voice, what it was: a bombard's stone forced past its limit, so that it threw one ball with everything it held and then blew, and left a dead zone round the battery that killed every stone in it, and every knight's strength, and every mage's glyph, the crew's own included. Forbidden, the officer had said. Except at the last.

And at the end of the line of carriages, by itself, an empty frame. A bombard's carriage, iron, new, with its trunnions set not level, as every other carriage's were, but upright: pointing at the sky.

He had asked what it was for.

The officer had looked at the king. The king had looked at the frame, and then at his son.

"Nothing yet," the king had said.

Emeric stood in the proving gallery under the hill, in the lamplight, with the block sunk in its crater at his feet, and thought about an empty frame pointing at the sky, and a thin young man with ink on his fingers. He thought about the Hod, and the K Discovery, and the K Endeavor, and the public lift hull with its paying passengers on its plain benches, flying the Milford route in broad daylight where anybody with a spyglass could see it.

Then he went back up the gallery to where Ward was waiting by the screen, and they walked up the long drift together into the July morning, and neither of them said anything until they were out in the sun.



The first Proving class came to the base on the eleventh of July, and Ward hated it, and said so.

He said so at the table on the tenth, the night before, the last time anybody could have stopped it; and Emeric had known he would, and had let him, because Ward had a right to it. Ward had stood on the floor.

"You're feeding them for the stair," said Ward. "That's all it is. You're fattening them for it."

"We're sending them up the stair with more in them than they'd have had," said Imani.

"They shouldn't be going up the stair."

"No," said Imani. "They shouldn't. And they are." She did not raise her voice. "Every one of those boys is going north at first snow whether we feed him or not. His father's sending him, or his captain's sending him, or he's sending himself, because there's no crest without the floor and he wants a crest. I can't stop that. Harrold can't stop that. You can't. We have the number on the wall downstairs that says what happens to four and a quarter of every ten of them." She put her hand flat on the table. "If I can make it three, I'll make it three. If I can make it two, I'll make it two. I'll not stand in the ward with my hands in my lap and let it be four because I don't like the stair."

Ward looked at her across the table.

"It's harm," he said.

"It's harm reduction," said Imani. "That's a different thing. You do it when you can't stop the harm. You do it because you can't."

Ward looked at her a long time. Then he looked down the table at Emeric, as if Emeric might say something that would make it different; and Emeric did not, because there was nothing.

"I'll train them," said Ward. "I'll train them as hard as I've trained anybody. Harder. I'll send them up that stair as ready as a man can be sent." He stood up. "I'll not like it."

"No," said Imani. "Nor will I."

He went out. Nobody at the table said anything for a while.



They came on the eleventh, by the Milford road, in a long dusty column of carts and horses, and they were not what anybody had expected.

There were sixty-one of them. Saban had brought nineteen: lords' sons, from baronies up and down the kingdom, whose fathers wanted a crest for them and had heard, from Saban's walkers in the right taverns and the right chancery corridors, that a year at the Duke of Kaldo's base sent a boy north with a better chance. Nobody had said what kind of chance, or why. The fathers had paid. Ferra had set what they paid, on a sliding scale that went from a sum that made a duke's steward put his hand to his chest down to nothing at all, according to the father's means as Ferra worked them out from the crown's tax rolls; and two of the nineteen had come for nothing, and Ferra had made sure that nobody at the base knew which two, not even the two.

They came from all over. A baron's younger son from Dunholt's hills, whose father held of the duke there and whose hall, the boy said, smelled of the iron works. A cousin of a Tarrowmede baron, who called his duke the king once and went red. Two from the keeps of the Narrow March, sons of barons who held their few miles of coast of the marquis and had grown up looking at the Isle. A Fairhythe boy whose house had refused an earldom three times and wanted him crested anyway, in case. A Cinderhale baron's second son in a coat that had been his grandfather's, who was one of the two, though nobody knew it. Ferra had explained the market to Emeric in the spring, over the fee scale, in two sentences. "A house of earl's rank or above has to name a crested knight to carry its banner. The law will take a household knight, the way it takes Ward for us; custom wants a son, and every baron who means to be an earl one day wants one ready."

The crown's men were thirty-four. They came in a column of their own behind the lords' sons, in the crown's grey, with a sergeant at their head and their orders in a satchel under his arm in the king's own seal: soldiers who had served their years and been put forward for the floor by their captains, and then, to their great surprise, been posted. South. To a duke's garrison. For training. They did not know why. Their sergeant did not know why. They had been told only that they would go north to the floor at first snow as before, and that until then they were the Duke of Kaldo's, and that the king would be obliged if they did as they were told.

And there were eight others, who had come on their own, or been sent by people who were not lords: a miller's son from Milford whose father had sold a field; a girl from Grayrock who wanted to be a knight more than she wanted anything and had walked; a smith's boy from Wayford. Imani had seen every one of them in the tank. She had seen every one of the sixty-one in the tank, over the last fortnight, in the ward, front and side, and written them down, and Ottoline had filed them.

And Joss. Who was not going north. Who was on the list first, in Imani's hand, for the arm.

They came into the base's yard under the clock that was still set wrong, and stood in their lines in the dust while Rylan read their names; and then Ward came out of the officers' room and stood on the step and looked at them.

He looked at them for a long time.

He looked at the lords' sons in their good boots, and the crown's men in their grey, and the miller's son and the girl from Grayrock and the smith's boy at the end, and Joss, at the very end, in his Kaldo coat with his left hand curled against his belt. He looked at every one of them. Emeric, at the gate with Imani, watched him do it and knew what he was doing. He was counting them. He was counting them the way Ferra had counted Armand's books: out of every ten.

Then he told them what they were going to do.

He told them in plain words, the words he used to the class at six, and he did not soften any of it. They would eat. They would eat more than they had ever eaten, of food grown in Kaldo's fields and Milford's, food that was more than food, and they would eat it under a healer, every meal, and the healer would say how much and when, and anybody who ate a mouthful more than the healer said would be on the Milford road home by noon and would not come back. They would work it off. They would work it off as fast as they took it in, every day, all day, in the yard and on the track and on the ropes and in the hall; and they would empty themselves into stones at night until they were empty, and then sleep, and then do it again. They would be sick. All of them, some days. They would bleed from the nose. The healer would see to it. They would go north at first snow with more in them than any man had ever had going up that stair, and Ward did not know what it would do for them on the floor, and nor did anybody, and he would not tell them otherwise.

"I don't know how many of you will stand up," he said. "I know how many would have. I'll not tell you that number. It's on a wall. If you want it, you can ask me at first snow."

The yard was very quiet.

"Hall," said Ward, and turned, and went down the step.



The gravity hall stood past the track and the ropes on its own ground, as it had stood in April with no roof; and it had a roof now, a long low one of slate, and a door at each end, and no windows at all. Bram had finished it on the fourth. He had said June and it was July, and he had said so himself, to Rylan, before Rylan could; and then he had walked Rylan round the inside of it with a lamp for an hour, and Rylan had come out and not said anything about the date again.

Inside, it was one long room, lit by lamps on wire down both walls. The floor was planked, oak, on a bed of sand. Under the sand, set in the clay in a grid a yard apart, all the way down the room and all the way across, each one bolted into an iron cup in a block of cement, were gravity stones.

They pushed down. That was all. Anchored, each in its cup, each told the same plain thing by the deciding stone in the corner: down, and this much. So that anything standing on the planks above them, a man or a bench or a dropped boot, weighed more than it did outside the door. Not much more. A half again, today, for a first day. Rylan had the deciding stone's dial, and the dial went up to three times, and Rylan had said that nobody would see the far end of the dial this year if he had any say in it, and he did.

And in the wall by each door, low, an iron collar with a threaded mouth, like the glass room's; and a rack of cartridges beside it.

That was Imani's. The hall was not sealed like the dome; it could not be, with sixty men going in and out of it all day. But it was close, and its doors shut, and with a cartridge let in through the collar every hour, the air inside it was a little thicker than the air outside. Not much. Not a tenth of the dome's. Enough that a man working himself to the bone in it under a half again of his own weight took in, through his skin, as he worked, a little more than he would have outside; enough that his body, torn a little by every hour of it, mended a little faster than it had any right to, and was a little more each night than it had been in the morning.

Slow induction. In a room. On purpose.

"And if the stones fail," said Ferra, at the door, with her book, because she had come out from the town on the cart to see it and would not be kept out of anything she had paid for.

"Then everybody weighs what they weigh," said Rylan, "and they fall over, because they've been braced for more, and some of them laugh."

"And if a dead zone comes."

"Then the stones stop, and they weigh what they weigh. And the air goes thin, all at once, and the dose stops, and they don't mend any faster than you or me." He shrugged. "Nobody's held up in the air by anything in there. Nothing comes down on anybody. There's nothing to fail but the help."

Ferra wrote that down.

"What else goes on in here?"

"Everything they'd do outside, heavier." Rylan counted it off on his fingers. "The runs. The sack. Blades at the post. And the square, afternoons, two by two." He looked down at the planks. "That's why it's oak and not flags. A man thrown at half again his weight lands at half again his weight. I'd sooner they found that out on wood."

Ferra looked at the floor for a while, and then wrote that down too.

The class went in at eleven. Sixty-one, in their shirts, in a line, through the east door; and Emeric stood at the door and watched them cross the threshold, and felt each one of them do it, with his sense: the small sudden lurch in every note as the weight came onto them, the way a man's note lurches on a ship's deck when it rises to a swell. One of the lords' sons sat down on the planks at once, hard, and looked round, red to the hair. The girl from Grayrock staggered and caught herself and did not sit down. The crown's men in their grey went in at the end of the line, and their sergeant, who had been a soldier twenty years, put his foot on the planks and stopped dead in the doorway with it there, and said a word the girl from Grayrock should not have heard, and went on.

Joss went in last.

He went in slowly. He stood on the threshold for a breath with his left hand against his belt, shaking, the way it always shook; and then he stepped onto the planks, and the weight came onto him, and Emeric felt his note lurch with it, as all the others had.

The hand went on shaking. It did not stop. Nobody had said it would. But Joss stood on the planks under a half again of his own weight, in the long room with sixty strangers, and did not sit down, and did not stagger; and he looked down at his hand for a moment, and then up the room at Ward, who was standing at the far end by the west door with his arms folded, watching them all.

Ward looked back at him. Then he looked away, at the rest of them, and raised his voice.

"Run," he said.

They ran.

Emeric stood at the door and watched them go down the long room and back, and down and back, sixty-one of them under the lamps on the oak planks in their shirts, heavy, staggering, breathing like bellows; and Imani stood beside him with her watch in her hand and her pencil in the other, writing every one of them down as they passed.

At the far end Ward did not run. He stood by the west door with his arms folded and watched them come at him, and turn, and go away again, and come back; and his face did not change, all morning, at all.



Ferra came up from the winter garden at a quarter past nine that night with the lantern in one hand and the gauge book in the other, and put the book down open on the long table in the hall between Emeric's plate and Bram's, and sat down, and did not say anything.

Bram looked at the page. Then he put his fork down.

It was the same line it had been every night since April. The day's cartridge in at eight, at the line, as it had gone in every day for a year; and at nine, where the needle ought to have come back down to the line because the tree had drunk the cartridge, the needle had not come down. It sat above. It had sat above on the first night after the pod, a hair; and on the tenth night, a little more; and Ferra had ruled a second column on the page in May and called it not drunk, and the second column had been climbing ever since, in her small upright figures, a little every week, like a child's height on a door frame.

"I bled it into the collar at six," said Bram. "Same as Tuesday. Same as Saturday."

"I know," said Ferra. "I wrote it down."

"It's not the gauge," said Bram, to Emeric, because Emeric had been going to say it. "I took the gauge off in June and set a new one on, and they read the same. It's not the collar. I soaped every seam on it with Tess holding the lamp, and nothing bubbled. It's not the seals on the glass." He picked his fork up again, and looked at it, and put it down. "Nothing's getting in. The tree's not taking it. That's all it is."

"Then don't put the cartridge in," said Imani, from the far end.

"Then what's in there drops off the line on the first warm night and the beds go thin, and the tree with them." Bram shook his head. "I'll not starve it to find out if it's hungry."

Emeric said nothing. He had been in at the dome every evening for a fortnight with his sense open, sitting on Bram's crate by the collar, and he knew what Bram didn't, which was no help at all: that the room was not the only thing getting denser. The tree was. Its note had been a slow heavy thing since the seed went into the ground, a drinking note, pulling at the room the way a dry field pulls at rain; and since the pod it had stopped pulling and begun to sit, full, and fuller, every week a little more, like a cask somebody kept topping up and never tapped. It was making something. It was making it and keeping it.

And there were the flowers. They had come out along the lowest tier in May, small, pale, cupped, a hundred of them on the long thin vines where the pod had hung, and he had stood under them with Ferra's lantern and thought, there, it's going again; and they had stayed open. All of June. All of July. Nothing had come of a single one.

He had a list of reasons, and he had tried them. The light was wrong under the glass: Bram had taken the screens off for a week, and nothing. The roots had found the ring's iron and didn't like it: Tess had sounded the ring with a stone on a wire and the roots were nowhere near it. The tree was sick. He had nothing to try for that. He had not said it aloud.

"It's not ill," he said now, because Ferra was looking at him. "Whatever it is, it isn't that. It's too strong." He turned her book round with one finger and looked at the second column. "It's waiting."

"For what?" said Ferra.

"I don't know."

She took the book back, and turned it the right way up, and wrote the date in the margin beside the column, and under it, in pencil: waiting (he says).
