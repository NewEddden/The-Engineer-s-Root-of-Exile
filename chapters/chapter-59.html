Chapter 59 - Paid Mages

The salt boatman came up the river on the nineteenth of May, a week before he was due, with no salt in his boat.

Rylan brought the news to the keep at a run and delivered it in the gate yard with his hands on his knees. "There's a boat at the wharf," he said. "With people in it. Not salt. People." He got his breath back. "They want paying."

Emeric had given the boatman the letter at the end of April, with a purse for his trouble, and asked him to let it be known in the coast towns that a prince up the river would pay anybody who could do magic and would come for coin. Ferra had entered the purse in her book without a line under it, which was how she entered money she did not expect to see again.

By the time he got down to the wharf there was a crowd on the new deck, and in the middle of it, sitting on a coil of the boatman's rope, were the mages.

There were a few of them. They had the look of people who had been wet for four days and expected to be wet for four more: oilskins, sea boots, bags tied shut with tarred cord. The youngest, a long thin man, was lying flat on the deck with his arm over his eyes, and had been, the boatman said, since the boat came in off the sea, because a river moved under a boat differently from the sea and nobody had thought to warn him. The oldest was a woman with grey in her plait and a seaman's squint. She was the one who stood up when Emeric came along the deck, and she did the talking.

They were all water. On the coast, she said, a hedge mage who was not water did not eat. Water kept a hull dry and a hold sweet and a deck clear in a blow, and any boat would carry a man who could do that for a share of the catch, and no boat would carry a man who could light a pipe from across a room. She said it without apology. They had heard in the coast towns that there was a war coming down the river and a prince at the bottom of it who was buying, and they had come to sell.

Her terms were a silver a day each, and their keep, and a dry room, and their passage home on the boatman's boat whatever happened, paid now into the boatman's hand so that nobody would have to collect it from a dead prince. Half the silver before they unpacked.

"And for that?" Emeric said.

"For that we put out what you want put out. We sink what you want sunk, if it's small." She looked at him, at his eyes, with plain professional interest. "We don't fight knights. Nobody pays enough for that. You're the fire one."

"I'm the fire one."

"Then you know what we cost."

He did. He paid the boatman on the wharf for the passage home, and walked them up over the east bridge to the exchange himself, and the long thin one came last, with his hand on the bridge parapet the whole way across.



Ferra counted their silver out onto the trestle at the exchange door, coin by coin, while the woman with the grey plait watched every one of them land, and wrote them into her book on a fresh page. Then she went home and did the sum. That evening she showed it to him at her table.

She had the whole barony on two facing pages now, coming in and going out. Coming in was short. The Grayrock barges had not come and were not going to. The spirit went west in the carter's casks and Milford paid for it, and some soap went with it. Saban had paid for the arch bar in March and would not be back for more until the autumn. That was the whole of the left-hand page, in four lines. Going out was everything else. The garrison. The households' pay and the school's bread. The dock quarter's forty, kept on at a wharf where nothing came. Bram's charcoal. And now the coast.

"A silver a day each," Ferra said. "And their keep. Every day they sit on your wharf costs about what the whole garrison eats." She ran the end of her pen down the right-hand page without touching it. "If nobody comes, and nothing changes, the barony pays its way to the end of September. After that I start selling grain we need."

"And with them?"

"With them, the middle of August. If he comes this month." She looked up. "If he comes in July, it's them and nothing else."

"Paxton said before midsummer."

"Paxton said Renner meant to." She turned the book round so that it faced him. "The carter walked in this morning to say he can't come for his casks. He says not to look for him this side of the summer. The steward's taken his wagon." She waited until Emeric looked up. "And every other wagon in Milford."

He took that to Ward on the wall after dark. Ward heard it out with his eyes on the river, and said one word.

"Wagons."

"Is that soon?"

"A levy walks. Wagons carry what it eats." Ward was looking north, toward the bend, where the needle's man would be sitting in the doorway of the hut with his bread. "Nobody takes a carter's wagon to stand it in a yard."



Ward had the mages on the wharf at first light and asked to see what they could do. The old woman showed him.

She went to the end of the deck and lifted her hands, and a ring of light came up in the air in front of them, the size of a plate, with the mark for water in the middle of it and a few strokes round it. The ring closed. Below the wharf the river humped up, brown and shining, a mass of it the size of a water butt, and rose out of the current with the water pouring off its edges, and hung, and went sideways over the deck and came down on the ore yard wall with a slap that soaked Osric to the waist.

Osric said something about it that made every one of the mages laugh, the long thin one loudest.

"Again," said Ward.

She did it again. Ward watched the ring of light over her hands, and then the far bank, and then the bridge, and never once the water.

"I could read a letter by that," he said. "So could any man over there with a bow."

"So we work by day," the woman said. "On a boat."

"You will not be working by day."

She shrugged. "Then put us behind something."

He did. By noon they all had places, and every place was behind stone: the parapet of the east bridge, the river door of the powerhouse, the ore yard wall, the foot of the river tower. Emeric asked what each place was for, and Ward said fire, and then said boats, and then said fire again.

"How long?" Ward asked her, at the end. "Hard. The way you would do it with a street burning."

"A quarter of an hour. Less, for him." She meant the long thin one, who was sitting on the ore yard wall in the sun, looking at the river with deep distrust. "Then you carry us."

"And then buckets," Ward said, to Emeric and not to her, and went back up the bank.



Armand had stopped climbing the keep stair in April. Rylan and Declan had carried his bed and his books down to a room off the hall that had been a store, with a window onto the gate yard and no stair anywhere near it, and Armand had called it his demotion and asked for his chair to be set where he could see who came in. He slept in the chair now, mostly, sitting up, because lying flat made him feel he was drowning. Imani came up from the ward twice a week and looked at his ankles, which had gone thick and shiny, and pressed them with her thumb and watched how long the dent stayed, and told him nothing he cared to repeat.

He heard about the coast with his eyes half shut and his hands folded on the rug over his knees. When Emeric was done he asked what they were, and Emeric told him.

"Water," Armand said. "On a river. Sensible." He stopped to breathe. "What does Renner know about you?"

"What Paxton's letter says he knows."

"Which is."

"A fire trick. A great many lamps."

"And what does a man with knights do," Armand said, "about a fire trick and a great many lamps?"

Emeric did not answer at once. He knew. He had known since September, when a young man from the tower stood in the dyehouse and explained the thing to Armand as he had been taught it, and went out holding his nose, and he had not let himself put it into words since.

"He breaks a stone," he said.

"He breaks a stone. A good one, as big as he can buy, over the first thing he means to take." Armand opened his eyes. "He will think it puts out your lamps. It won't, and I would give a good deal to see his face when he finds that out. But it will put out your coast men, all of them, and you will have paid them for nothing. And it will put out you."

"One stone," Emeric said. "One place. It can't be everywhere."

"Nobody knows how far it goes. Nobody has ever done it on purpose." Armand's breath was getting short. He waited for it. "And it doesn't need to be everywhere. It only needs to be where you are."

Out in the gate yard a cart came in, and somebody shouted for Hale, and Hale's voice answered from the hall, correcting the shouter about something. Armand listened to it with his head back against the chair.

"Tell Ward," he said.

"Ward knows."

"Then tell yourself," said Armand, and shut his eyes, and Emeric stayed by the door until his breathing was even again and he was asleep.



He made the ether at the dyehouse on the twenty-second, in the yard, with Imani.

She had asked to see it made, because she would be the one using it, and she would not use a thing she had not watched being made. She stood well back with her arms folded while he poured the oil of vitriol off Tess's shelf into a jar of the strong spirit, the kind that went to her ward for hands, a thread at a time, stirring. The jar went hot. She put her palm against its side, took it away, and looked at her palm.

The potter had made him a second retort, the twin of the one the zinc came out of. It sat in a tray of sand over a low charcoal fire at the top of the yard, and from its neck a clay pipe ran the whole length of the yard, down through a trough of well water, to a crock sunk to its shoulders in a tub of cold water by the gate. The dyehouse hearth was out. He had put it out himself, and said so to Tess, and told her not to light it until he said.

"What comes over," he told Imani, "boils in your hand. Its breath is heavier than air. It pours along the ground, like water, and if it finds a flame it burns all the way back to wherever it came from. The crock. The man holding the crock." He looked at the fire at the top of the yard, and the wind, which was coming down the yard toward the gate. "So the fire stays up there, and we stay down here, and nobody in the lane lights a pipe."

"You've thought about the lane."

"I've thought about the lane."

The fire had to be hot enough and no hotter. Too cool and nothing came over. Too hot and something else came over instead, sweeter and no use to anybody. He kept it with charcoal and a rake, a little at a time, through the whole of the afternoon, and did not once put a hand out to it any other way. He had thought about that and decided against it, because he could feel Tess watching from the dyehouse door, and because the whole point of what was dripping into the crock was that it would work on a night when he could not.

By evening there was a pint of it, perhaps. He washed it in cold water in a stoppered jar, shaking it, and let it stand, and it lay on top of the water clear as glass, and he drew it off into a small stoneware bottle and pushed the stopper in hard.

Imani took the bottle and turned it over in her hands. She drew the stopper an inch and smelled it and stepped back fast, with her hand at her face.

"That goes straight up behind the eyes," she said.

"That's where it's going."

She put the stopper back. "Tomorrow," she said. "My yard. And I'm putting my hearth out, which they'll take worse than the war."



She put it out at dawn. Her women stood round the cold kettles in the laundry yard and did not know what to do with their hands.

The dog went first. It was a yellow, low-slung thing with a torn ear that lived on what the laundry women dropped, and it came when it was called because it always had, and it let itself be held. Emeric had a little cage of bent wire the size of a cupped hand, with a cloth stretched over it, and he held it over the dog's nose and let the stuff fall onto the cloth from the bottle, a few drops at a time. The dog fought it, and sneezed, and paddled its legs, and one of the women made a sound. Then it went loose all at once, and its breathing went slow and deep.

Imani took the dog's hind foot and pinched the pad of it, hard, between her nails. Nothing. She lifted its eyelid with her thumb and looked, and let it down. Emeric took the cage away, and the dog lay on the stones and breathed.

It came back slowly, and stood up on legs that did not seem to be its own, and was sick on the step, and went off round the corner of the yard toward the kitchen door with its tail down and its dignity gone.

"They're always sick after," Emeric said.

Imani looked at him.

"Always," she said.

"It stands to reason."

She let that go by, and he saw her let it go, and saw her keep it. Then she sent one of the women in for a pallet and lay down on it, in the middle of the yard, and held her wrist up to him.

"No," he said.

"The dog can't tell me what it's like. I'm not putting a man under something I haven't been under." She waited, wrist up. "You'll count my pulse. One of my women counts my breaths out loud. If they go shallow, you take it away. If my lips go blue, you take it away and pull my jaw forward. You told me all of it yesterday." She looked at him from the pallet. "Or was that for a dog?"

He knelt down beside her and took her wrist.

She fought it, as the dog had. Everybody did. She coughed and turned her face away and he followed it with the cage, and her other hand came up and took hold of his sleeve, hard, and held on. The woman counted. Then Imani said something into the cloth, slurred, quite clearly not to him, and her hand let go of his sleeve, and her breathing went slow and deep and even, and her pulse under his fingers was steady and a little fast and then not fast at all.

He pinched the back of her hand. Nothing. He pinched it again, harder, and looked at her face, and it did not change. He took the cage away and held her wrist and listened to the woman count, until Imani's eyes moved under their lids, and she made a face, and turned her head, and one of the women got the basin under her just in time.



When she could sit up she sent the women in. She sat on the edge of the pallet with the basin on her knees and her hair half down, grey in the face, and breathed through her nose.

"How long?" she said.

"Long enough to pinch you twice."

"Did I feel it?"

"No."

She nodded. "Then it works." She was quiet. "What did I say?"

He had told her the day before that people talked, going under, and she had remembered it. He thought about saying nothing, and saw her watching him think it.

"You told your father the needle was on the shelf."

She looked at the basin.

"It is on the shelf," she said. "He mended nets. On the piers, when there were still boats to mend for. That's his chair by my fire, that you looked at the first time you came to my door and didn't ask about." She turned the basin round once in her hands. "He went the first winter of the plague. The razor's his too. I'll bring it down for the kit. It's the best edge in Kaldo, and he'd sooner it was used."

He did not say anything. There was nothing he could say to it that would have been the right size.

"That's mine," Imani said. "Now you." She lifted her head and looked at him, straight, the way she looked at a wound. "You held that cloth like a man who's held it a hundred times. You knew where I'd fight it and when I'd stop. You knew the dog would be sick. Always, you said." She waited. "Nobody in this kingdom has ever put anybody to sleep with a bottle. You told me that yourself. So I'm asking. What are you?"

The yard was very quiet. Through the side door he could hear one of the women telling another that the hearth was going back on whatever anybody said.

He opened his mouth, and there was nothing in it that was true and that he could give her, and he shut it again.

Imani watched him do that. She did not look angry. She looked like a woman who had taken a pulse and got a number she did not like, and taken it again, and got the same number.

"All right," she said. She stood up, carefully, with the basin. "I'll ask again."
