Chapter 59 - Service

The road up to Grayrock had been bad when Renner owned it and nobody had touched it since, and by the second spring of the ore contract it had become the most expensive object in the barony.

Emeric priced it because Ferra made him. She came to him in the thaw with two years of carriage lines and asked him what he thought it cost to bring a ton of stone one day downriver, and he guessed, and he was wrong by more than half, and she let him sit in it for a moment before she told him.

Four times. Not four times what it cost on a good road somewhere else, which would have been an argument about somewhere else. Four times what it cost Kaldo, that same season, to move the same weight the same distance along the lower water road, which the barony had ditched and crowned in its second year and which now took carts in March.

"Where does it go," Emeric said.

"Axles. Wheels. Horses. Time." Ferra read from the sheet without any emphasis at all, which was how she made things land. "Two carts a season broken past mending. Eleven wheels. We are paying a carter in Grayrock to keep four spare beasts standing idle against the days the ruts take one, and the reason we pay him is that it is cheaper than the alternative, and I have checked that it is cheaper and it is."

"What does mending the road cost?"

She had that too. Of course she had that too. Stone, which Kaldo now had at a fixed price and could quarry rubble from the same workings for nearly nothing. Labour, which in the weeks between planting and haying was cheaper than it would be at any other time of the year. Drainage, which was the part that actually mattered, because the road did not fail from traffic, it failed from water standing in it every spring and freezing.

The figure at the bottom was paid back in under a year. Not eighteen months. Not on current terms, with four things that had to stay true. Under a year, out of carriage alone, before a single additional ton moved.

Emeric put the sheet down and said the obvious thing.

"It's a capital project."

"It is."

"It's new construction on a scale we have not attempted since the loan."

"It is," Ferra said, "unless it is maintenance of an existing road, in which case it is a thing the barony is obliged to do and has been doing badly for forty years."

He looked up at her.

"Tell me that again," he said. "Slowly."

She did not build the argument in a day and she did not build it to persuade him. She built it to survive being read by somebody who was looking for a reason to refuse it, which was a different kind of building, and she had learned it from the woman it was aimed at.

The tithe map came out of the chest first. It was older than the loan by a long way, older than Wexler, drawn up for the church's purposes by a man who had cared about which fields owed what and had put the roads in only because he needed them to say where the fields were. The Grayrock track was on it. It was on it as a road, named, running from the northern quarter up to the hill, in the same ink as the Duvrenne road and the lower water road, and no reasonable person looking at that sheet could argue that what Kaldo proposed to build was a road that had not previously existed.

The obligation came second, and it came out of the barony's own charter, which Emeric had read once in his first winter and had not looked at since, and which said in a dependent clause of a sentence about tolls that the holder of Kaldo kept the ways of Kaldo in repair. It was not a grand provision. It was the sort of thing charters say because everybody's charter says it. It was nonetheless an obligation, in writing, predating the loan by four hundred years, and a lending house that prevented a borrower from meeting a prior obligation was a lending house with a problem of its own.

The third thing was the one that did the work, and it was the dullest. Ferra pulled three years of the barony's own accounts and found the maintenance line, which existed, because Wexler had run it and had spent almost nothing out of it and had spent something. Gravel. A culvert. Two days of hired labour on the Left Bridge approach. Small, regular, unglamorous expenditure, every year, under a heading, in a book, from before the loan and continuing after it with Vantic's own initials against two of the entries.

Maintenance was not a category Kaldo was proposing to invent. It was a category the house had already funded, twice, without comment.

"The question I want her to answer," Ferra said, "is not whether we may build a road. If you ask her that she says no, and she will be right, and then the no exists and you can't unask it." She squared the three documents. "The question is whether resurfacing a road that appears on a map predating the loan, which the barony is charged by its charter to maintain, out of a heading the house has funded before, is maintenance or construction. That's a ruling. She likes rulings."

"And if she rules construction?"

"Then we've lost a road and we haven't lost anything else, because we won't have asked for an exception." Ferra looked at him steadily. "You are not to be in the room while she reads it."

"She'll think I'm avoiding her."

"She will think nothing of the sort. She will think the paper is the paper."

Vantic read it for two hours.

She read it in the small room with the door open, which was how she always worked, and she went through the three documents twice and then went and fetched the loan file herself rather than sending for it, and read the covenant against the charter clause with both sheets flat on the table and a finger on each.

Then she wrote for considerably longer than she had read.

The ruling, when she handed it across, was four lines. Resurfacing and drainage of the Grayrock way, being a road of record predating the instrument, falls within the borrower's standing obligation of repair and is admitted against the maintenance heading. Cost not to exceed the schedule attached. No widening. No new cut.

The note in the file behind it was two full pages.

It set out the tithe map and its date and where it had been kept. It set out the charter clause in full, and the three prior entries under the maintenance heading with their years and amounts, and the two she had herself initialled. It recorded that she had considered whether the scale of the proposed work removed it from the character of maintenance, and had concluded that scale followed from forty years of deferral rather than from a change of category, and that the deferral was itself a failure of the obligation. It recorded that the borrower had not sought a variation of the covenant and had not been granted one.

Emeric read it standing at the end of the table, and Vantic let him, and when he got to the end he understood what he was holding.

"This isn't for us," he said.

"No."

"It's so that when somebody in the capital pulls this file in nine years and asks why the factor let a covenanted borrower build a road, there's an answer on the page and it's yours."

"The house does not employ me to be persuaded," Vantic said. "It employs me to be right in writing. If I allow a thing and cannot say why, I have not allowed a thing. I have made a mistake that has not been found yet."

She said it without heat and without any suggestion that she was explaining herself, and then went back to the ledger, and that was the end of it.

Emeric went out into the corridor and stood there for a moment, and what he was thinking was not about the road.

He had spent a winter and most of a spring working out how to move this woman and had tried, in an unserious back-of-the-mind way, about four approaches. He had thought about arguing the barony's case. He had thought about the obvious fact that a thriving Kaldo serviced its debt and a strangled one did not, which was true and which he had assumed would count for something. He had thought, once, briefly and with some shame, about being charming.

None of it would have worked. None of it had ever been going to work. The lever was not persuasion and was not need and was certainly not warmth. The lever was handing her something so thoroughly documented that allowing it became the defensible act and refusing it became the one she would have to justify.

Ferra had known that for a year and had not said so, because she had assumed he could see it.

"Thank you," he said, from the doorway.

Vantic did not look up. "I have allowed maintenance of an existing road," she said.

They built it between planting and haying with a hundred and forty men, and it was the least interesting thing Kaldo had done in three years and the most immediately profitable.

Ditch both sides, deep, running to daylight every forty yards, because the road had never failed from weight. Crown it. Rubble base out of the Grayrock spoil, which the hill was glad to be rid of and which came down empty on carts that had gone up loaded with grain. Bind it. Set the bad corner above the ford with cut stone because rubble would not hold on that fall. No widening, because the ruling said no widening, and Emeric held to that so precisely that he had the line staked before the first spade went in and walked it himself.

It took thirty-one days. Nobody sang about it. Farris complained about the timing of it every single day and then sent his own carts up to help move rubble on the two afternoons it rained too hard to work the ground.

The first full month of carriage on the new surface cost a fraction over a third of what the same tonnage had cost the year before. Ferra brought him the figure with her face doing the thing it did instead of smiling. Two carts had broken in the whole season, both on the hill above the ford, both loaded over the rate by a driver who had been told twice. The four idle horses went back to work.

Emeric read the number three times and then took the sheet down to the yard and read it to the carters, which was not a thing he normally did, and they were pleased in the flat way of men who had known it already from their own backs.

The first payment out of real income went north in the summer.

It was not a large sum by the standards of the debt. It was the ordinary service on the ordinary schedule, the same figure that had gone up twice before, and the difference was entirely in where it came from.

The first two had been paid out of what the loan itself had advanced and out of the last of the harvest money, which was a thing Ferra had described in the book with a precision bordering on cruelty, so that anybody reading it would understand that Kaldo had been paying a debt with the debt.

This one came out of soap, which was going up the hill to Grayrock and west to Milford and, since the spring, down the long water to the coast in Fletcher's boats and out from there to three ports Emeric had never seen. It came out of bar and plate and fittings off a foundry running on high grade ore at a price that had not moved. It came out of the etched work, which was small in volume and embarrassing in margin. Saban moved all of it and took his cut and the cut was in the numbers too.

Ferra did not announce it. She came into the small room at the end of the day with one sheet, put it in front of him, and stood there.

The column that had run one way since the war ran the other way. Not by much. By enough that it was not an accident of the season, and she had done the work to show that it was not an accident of the season, on the back of the sheet, in her own hand, unasked.

Emeric looked at it for a long moment.

"How long have you had this?"

"Since the ninth."

"It's the twelfth."

"I wanted three days of it holding," Ferra said. "It held."

He did not say anything that either of them would have had to remember afterward. He signed the payment authority and she took it away and it went north with the quarter's letters.

The other thing he noticed that summer he mentioned to nobody at all.

For a year and a half, Vantic had cleared her throat before she spoke to him. Not a cough. A small, formal, entirely deliberate noise, made before every question and every ruling and every correction, which he had taken at first for a habit and had worked out inside a month was a signal, and which meant approximately: I am about to speak to you as the house and not as a guest in your keep.

Somewhere between the road and the payment she stopped doing it.

She did not become warm. She did not use his name. She corrected him twice that month, once in front of Ferra, on a point about how the etching stock was carried in the inventory, and she was right both times. She simply began speaking to him without the noise in front of it.

Emeric noticed it on a Tuesday and decided it was the single most encouraging thing that had happened to him all year, and never told a living soul, because saying it out loud would have made it into something, and if it became something she might stop.

He asked about the furnace in writing, properly, at the end of the summer, because by then he believed he understood how she worked and he was right about that and it did not help.

The request was six pages and Ferra wrote four of them. A second furnace at the foundry, and the finishing shop to go with it, on ground the barony already held, using the same ore under the same contract. Stated cost. Stated build time. Stated output. The order book that already existed and could not be filled, with names on it, three of them Milford and two of them coastal. Payback in nineteen months at the conservative figure and eleven at the likely one, and Ferra had put the conservative figure first.

It was, he thought, the best-documented thing anybody in that barony had ever asked anybody for.

Vantic said no in a day and a half.

She did not say it unkindly. She turned the request round on the table and put her finger on the clause, and the clause said what the clause had said for two years, which was that the borrower would establish no new plant, works, furnace or manufactory during the term, and she read the sentence out once and then let him read it himself.

"This isn't new industry," Emeric said. "It's more of an industry that exists."

"It is a furnace that does not exist, on ground where there is no furnace," Vantic said. "I would like to be able to tell you otherwise. There is no reading of that clause available to me under which a second furnace is the first furnace."

"You have discretion."

"I have discretion over security, over schedule, and over the character of expenditure. I have no discretion over the negative covenants. They are the reason the rate is what it is." She squared the papers. "And if I had it I would not use it, because I am not competent to judge whether your furnace pays and neither, on this evidence, is anybody in this barony. You have an order book and a projection. The house has watched three hundred order books and a great many projections."

"Then send it to the house."

"I will send it to the house," Vantic said, "and the house will refuse it, and I will tell you the day the answer comes."

She sent it. She showed him the covering letter before it went, which she did not have to do and which he understood as the nearest thing to an argument on his behalf that she would ever make: it was four lines, entirely neutral, and the fourth line noted the conservative payback figure without recommending anything.

The answer came back in twenty-six days.

It was one paragraph and it was polite and it was no, and it gave a reason, which Emeric had not expected, and the reason was better than his argument. A covenant relaxed once has been shown to be relaxable. The house did not lend against furnaces. It lent against the certainty that a borrower under strain could not spend his way further into strain, and that certainty was worth more to it than any single furnace was worth to anybody.

He read it twice and could not find the flaw in it, which was the worst part.

Twenty-six days, he thought afterward, and then put the thought down and picked it up again that evening. Twenty-six days, capital and back, question asked and question answered, from a woman writing out of the same keep, up the same road, in the same satchels.

He had written north eleven times in two years and had never had a line back.

He turned that over for the length of one cup of wine and arrived where he always arrived, which was that a lending house in the capital gets its letters answered because a lending house is somebody, and a third son in a southern barony is not somebody, and the two facts sat together without any friction at all. It was a complete explanation. It accounted for everything he had observed. He had never once tested it and it did not occur to him that evening to test it either.

What changed in him after the furnace was refused was not dramatic and took a while to show.

The freeze had been an inconvenience for two years. A thing to be worked around, argued at the edges of, occasionally beaten by a piece of good paper. After that letter it stopped being an inconvenience and became a date.

He went back to the notebook, to the pages he had begun in the first week after the loan, where every project the covenant had killed sat in a list with the clause number beside it. There were thirty-one lines on it now. And he ruled a second column down the right-hand side of every page and began filling it in: what each of those things would need on the day the freeze lifted. Ground. Stone. Men. Which of them had to be built before which others. Which could go up in a season and which wanted two. What Armand would have to measure first.

It took him most of a month of evenings and it was the happiest he had been in a year, and Ward, who came in at midnight twice and found him at it, said nothing about the fact that a man was building a barony on paper that he was forbidden to build in the ground.

Ferra's projection to clear the debt at plausible surplus was seven years. The covenant bound while any part of the principal stood. So the date was seven years away, or nine, or eleven, depending on four things that had never once gone wrong except that four of them had, and he could not calculate it, and he began planning against it anyway.

It was a new kind of patience for him and he was rather pleased with himself for having it, and it was a worse kind than the one it replaced, because patience with a date attached is only patience until the date moves.

Imani asked about the hospital in the last week of the summer, and this year she asked with numbers.

She had started keeping them properly the way everybody in that barony now kept things, because the barony paid a man his wage while he was hurt and therefore counted the days he was hurt, and because she had discovered that a register answered questions she had been guessing at for twenty years. She had two years of it. She brought it in a satchel.

Forty-one beds needed against eleven she had. What the ward in Doran's church could not do, itemised: no clean room for cutting, no way to keep a fever case away from a crush injury, no water at the height she needed it, and a floor she could not get properly clean because it was a church floor and it was consecrated and she was not going to be the woman who tore it up. What it cost the barony in days not worked, which was a figure she had not had last year and now did, and which was larger than the building.

She had costed the building too. She had gone to Colm about the tile herself.

Emeric read all of it and she watched him read it, and he took longer over it than he needed to.

Then he showed her the clause.

He did not summarise it and he did not apologise for it. He put the covenant page in front of her and let her read the sentence, and she read it slowly, because she read everything slowly and always had, and when she had finished she straightened up.

She did not say anything about it. She did not say that it was unjust, which it was not, or that he should fight it, which he had, or that people would die in the meantime, which they would, and which both of them knew, and which she had the figures for in the satchel.

She put the satchel back together and asked whether she could have the register copied so that next year's would run on from it without a break.

He said yes.

She went back to the ward in the church.

At the end of that summer Kaldo had a road that had paid for itself, a ledger that ran the right way for the first time since the war, a debt serviced out of its own earnings, and a factor who no longer cleared her throat.

It also had thirty-one lines in a notebook, with a second column beside them now, getting longer every season while the reasons to build every one of them got longer faster.

Emeric was solvent. He had made the barony work inside the cage, which was the thing he had promised himself he would do, and he had done it in less time than Ferra had thought possible.

And the price of it was that he was not permitted to do the one thing he was actually for.
