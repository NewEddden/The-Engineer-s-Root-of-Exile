Chapter 153 - Chain and Level

He walked four places before he chose one, and he chose against the one he wanted.

The one he wanted was the high ground north of the lake, on the Milford side, where the land rose in a long gentle swell above the water and you could stand at the top of it and see the island and the whole of the lake and the Whispering Woods going away to the east like a dark sea. It was beautiful. It had good drainage. A city on it would have been seen from every road into the barony, and Emeric stood on the top of it on a raw morning in the last week of winter with Ward and Saban and the wind in his face, and wanted it so much that he made himself walk down again and count what it did not have.

It had no stone. The nearest face was the quarry the island had used for as long as there had been an island town, and it was on the other side of the lake, and every block would have to go round by the bridge. It had no port. It had no flat for a rail yard that was not somebody's best field. It was as far from the river's head as it could be and still be in the barony, so an aqueduct would have to cross the lake or go round it.

"It's the best view in the south," said Saban.

"It is."

"You're not going to build on it."

"No."

Saban looked at him sideways. "I've never known you turn down a thing because you liked it too much."

"I've never had one I liked this much," said Emeric, and went down the hill.

The place he chose was the fourth.

It was south, below the island, a little over a morning's walk down the river road. The river left the lake as the two branches round the island and came together again below it, and ran south through the farmland in a long easy valley, and where the valley opened out towards the coast there was a flat. It was a wide, dull, slightly tilted flat of rough pasture and thorn and a few old sheepfolds, above the lower water and the quay where the sulphur came in and Fletcher's yard with its slips, and it sloped very gently down towards them.

Behind it, to the west, there was a ridge. Not a high one. A long spine of grey limestone that the sheep had grazed bare, with a face on its eastern side where somebody's grandfather had cut stone for a barn and stopped. Emeric walked along the foot of that face with his hand on it, and felt the rock under his palm with the sense, and it was good rock, bedded evenly and deep, and it went on and on under the turf in both directions.

Above the flat, to the north, the river ran in its valley, and a mile or so up the valley it came down over a shelf of the same limestone in a long white run, and stood higher there than any part of the flat.

He stood on the flat with his back to the sea and looked at it for a long time.

It had stone, its own, a quarter mile from where the wall would stand. It had the river, above it, high enough that water taken off it at the shelf would come down to the city by its own weight. It had the quay below it and the coast beyond, which was the lane, and the sulphur, and one day more than the sulphur. It had flat for a rail yard outside the wall on the east side that was nobody's best field, because nobody's best field was ever on sour thorn pasture above a salt marsh.

And it looked at the sea. He had not chosen it for that, and he noticed that he was glad of it anyway.

"It's ugly," said Ward.

"It's flat."

"I said it was ugly." Ward looked back up the valley, north, towards the island, which was out of sight behind the fold of the land. "It's the right one."

The reason the stone mattered more than anything else was a thing he told the table plainly, because he wanted it on the record before anybody made a promise to anybody else.

"The city's going to eat stone," he said. "More stone than anything in the south has ever eaten. If the stone comes from anywhere but its own ridge, the city waits on whatever brings it. If it comes up the Grayrock line, it waits on the line, and the line's years short of anywhere, and every time a rail's late the wall stops." He touched the survey sheet Ferra had not yet seen. "So it doesn't come up the line. It comes out of that ridge and down a spur on its own rails, a quarter mile, and nothing that happens at Grayrock or at the works or on the road ever stops it."

Bram looked at him across the table.

"So the rail works," said Bram slowly, "isn't for the city."

"The rail works is for everything else. The city feeds itself."

Bram thought about it, and nodded, and wrote something on the back of a tally.

The surveyors came in the thaw.

Ferra found them. She did not say how, and Emeric did not ask, because it was her work and she did it better than he could. There were three of them. Two came up from the coast together, an uncle and a nephew named Tarrant, who had laid out fields and drainage for the salt-marsh farms along the shore for thirty years and had never been asked to lay out anything larger than a parish. The third came from Milford, a lean silent man named Weekes, who had surveyed Paxton's grazing for his tithe maps and brought a letter from Paxton, which Ferra read and put in the book without comment.

They brought their own instruments. That was all Emeric had asked for. A chain of a hundred links on a wooden handle, and a level, which was a brass tube with a bubble in it on three legs, and two staffs painted in bands, and a box of pegs, and a great many notebooks. He had not had to invent anything. He had not wanted to. The chain and the level and the staff were old tools, older than Kaldo, and he found that he liked them more than he had expected to, because every one of them was a thing that a man could check with another man's copy and find out who was wrong.

They were paid by the week, from the barony's purse: four Silver the week for the elder Tarrant and for Weekes, and two Silver and six Copper for the nephew, which was more than any of them had been paid in their lives and less than the smallest thing the city would cost. Ferra put it on the line she had opened for the drawing, and the line had a name now, and the name was South.

The first thing Emeric gave them was the master foot.

Bram's master foot, the iron rod in the case in the keep, with the mark struck at each end, against which every rod in four baronies had been made. He had a surveyor's rod struck from it for each of them, and a chain made to it link by link by Jorrin, and he stood at the foot of the ridge on the first day with the three of them and told them that every measurement they took would be taken in that foot, and no other, and that he would check any chain against the rod any morning he liked.

The elder Tarrant looked at the rod for a long time.

"The coast foot's a thumb longer," he said.

"The coast foot's wrong."

Tarrant considered that. "It's the coast's," he said.

"Then it's wrong for the coast. For this, it's this."

Tarrant looked at Weekes, and Weekes looked at the rod and said nothing, which Emeric learned over the following weeks was how Weekes agreed with things.

They cut the datum on the second day.

It was a block of the ridge's own grey stone, squared by one of Bram's masons and set in the ground at the foot of the ridge where the flat began, on a bed of rubble dug down to the rock. On its top face the mason cut a mark: a short line, and a cross through it, and beside it a number. Every height on the city would be measured from that mark, up or down. Every street's fall, every drain's fall, every wall's course.

Emeric set the level on it himself, the first time. He put his eye to the tube and waited for the bubble to settle, and looked down the flat at the nephew holding the staff two hundred feet off, and read the band.

The bubble moved.

He waited. It settled again. He read the band again. It was not the same band.

He did not say anything, and he did not have to. The elder Tarrant, beside him, said, "It's the frost coming out of the ground under the legs. Wait till noon."

He waited till noon.

For a week the level argued with the chain.

It was the most satisfying week he had spent in years and he did not tell anybody that either.

The chain said the flat fell so much from the ridge to the sea. The level said it fell less. The chain was measured along the ground, over every hump and hollow of the pasture, and the ground was never quite flat, so every chain's length was a little more than the true distance and every fall worked out from it came out a little wrong. The level did not care about the ground. It looked along a line of sight that did not bend. Tarrant knew this. Weekes knew this. The nephew knew it in the way a young man knows a thing his uncle has told him, and every evening he worked the day's chain against the day's level and got two different answers, and brought them to his uncle, and his uncle said, "The level," and the nephew said, "But the chain," and it went round again.

By the end of the week the nephew had stopped saying but the chain. He had begun holding the chain level off the ground on the slopes, in short lengths, with a plumb bob at the end, the old way, the way his uncle had been doing it all along without mentioning it.

The level won. It always did. Emeric had known it would, and he had let the week happen anyway, because a man who has learned why the level wins does not need to be told it again, and a man who has been told it will forget.

The drawing was made in the delve.

He had meant to make it on paper, and he did make it on paper, in the end, in ink, sheet by sheet. But the paper came second. The first drawing was made in the room above Kaldo, with all seven of them in it.

It was the room from the first seating: the long table in the air, and below it Kaldo in relief, the island and the lake and the branches and the bridge and the road, all of it built out of what had been put into it over the years by the six of them and by him. It had been wrong in places since the first day, and they had fixed it, and it had gone wrong again as the town changed, because the room showed what had been put into it and nothing else, and it did not know about anything that had happened since. The tannery lane still stood in it, whole, nine houses sharing their end walls, and nobody had yet had the heart to take them down.

He extended it south.

He did it slowly, the way he had done the first room, putting in what he knew and letting the parts he did not know stay vague. The river road going south out of the valley. The farms along it, where he had walked. The flat, from the surveyors' sheets, which he had read every evening until he could see them, the heights and the falls and the chain lines. And as he put the sheets in, the flat came up out of the vagueness below the table, true to within a hand, grey-green, tilted towards the sea, with the ridge behind it and the quay below it and the river above.

The others watched it come.

"Where's the Holt farm?" said Saban.

Emeric looked at him.

"On the river road, going down." Saban pointed, over the side of the table, at a stretch of road below the island where the room had only fields. "There's a farm on the left. Holt's. Long barn with a stone end, and a pond. My father bought wool off Holt."

"I haven't walked that stretch."

"I have." Saban put it in. He did it with the ease of a man who had been doing it for three years, and it came up out of the fields: a long barn with a stone end, a yard, a pond, a farmhouse with smoke from its chimney, in the late light of some evening Saban was remembering.

Ward looked at it.

"That burned," said Ward.

Saban turned.

"The year before you came back," said Ward. "The barn went in a harvest fire, and the house with it. Holt went to his daughter's in Milford. There's nothing there but the stone end. Sheep go in it." He did not say it unkindly. He never did.

Saban looked at the farm he had built for a while, with its smoke going up out of the chimney. Then he laughed, not loudly.

"Well," he said. "It was there when I bought his wool."

Bram laughed too, and Imani, and even Armand, who had been very quiet since the fire and whose hands had still not stopped. Ferra did not laugh, but her mouth moved.

Emeric laughed with them, and noticed as he did it, the way he always noticed it and always put it down again, that there was nothing in the room to tell a burned farm from a standing one. It had taken Saban's farm as easily as it had taken the surveyors' sheets. It would have taken a whole city of Saban's memories, and shown it to them true and solid and smoking at the chimney, and nobody in the room could have told which parts were wrong except by knowing.

He took the farm out himself, and left the stone end, because Ward had seen it.

Then they drew the city.

They drew it the way he had said they would: all of it, before a spade. He put in the circle first, the wall's line, laid out on the surveyors' flat so that it sat on the rock where the rock was nearest and swung wide round the soft ground at the marsh edge. Then the rings inside it. Then the spokes. Then everything else.

It took them eleven nights, at an hour a night, which was all the room could bear with seven in it before he felt it in his teeth and Armand put his hand on the table and gave it what he had. Armand gave less this time than he had in the first seating. Emeric noticed and said nothing.

At the centre there was a building.

He had drawn it on the sheet in the case as a square with a round thing inside. In the room it became what he meant: a great square building of grey stone round a courtyard, and at its heart, over the long room at the centre, a dome. It held everything the island's keep held and a great deal the keep had never held, because the keep had been built by a baron for a baron. The offices of the barony. The long room, for the table. The records, all of them, Ferra's books and the census and the survey and the deeds, in rooms of stone with no timber in them. The courts. The treasury. The printing.

"That's a palace," said Saban.

"It's offices."

"It has a dome on it," said Saban. "Nobody's ever put a dome on an office. It's a palace. That's what they'll call it."

Emeric started to answer and looked round the table, and saw Bram's face, and Imani's, and Ward's, and knew that Saban was right and that he had lost the argument before he had made it.

"It's offices," he said anyway.

"Palace," said Bram, to nobody, and wrote it down.

From the palace the streets went out in rings. Between the second ring and the third, where he had drawn a line of blue in the pencil on the sheet, he put in water.

"That's a moat," said Ward.

"It's not a moat. It's inside the wall."

"It's a ring of water. It's a moat."

"It's the water's road," said Emeric. He had them all watching now. "The aqueduct comes in there, at the top, from the river above the shelf. It comes into the ring. The ring goes all the way round the city, and it's always full, and it's always moving, very slowly, because the ground falls and it falls with it, and it goes out at the bottom to the beds." He touched the ring in the air above the table. "Everything in the city drinks off it. There's a tower in the middle of every quarter, like the one on the market well, that lifts off the ring and gives to the mains. If the city burns, it's the fire's water, standing, everywhere, in the middle of every street, and no bridge between. If it doesn't burn, people will think it's pretty."

Ward looked at the ring for a long time.

"How deep," he said.

"As deep as a man."

"You could put a boat on it."

"You could."

Ward did not say anything else. He did not agree. But he did not call it a moat again.

Doran came out to the flat on a cold bright morning at the end of the thaw.

Bram had asked him to. It was done, in the south, when a new building went up: the priest blessed the first stone, or the corner, or the place where the door would be. Nobody knew what you blessed when it was not a building but a mark on a block at the foot of a ridge from which every height in a city would be measured, and Bram had gone to Doran and asked him to bless the datum, and Doran had said he would come and see.

He came in the cart with Bram, wrapped in two coats against the wind off the sea. He was past sixty now and walked with a stick on rough ground, and he walked across the flat on it to the datum with the three surveyors standing back in a row with their hats off, and looked at the mark on the stone for a while, and put his hand flat on it.

He said the blessing. It was short. It was the one for foundations, and he said it in the old language and then in the plain one, and made the sign over the stone.

Then he asked to see the plan.

Emeric had the first ink sheet in the cart, weighted in a board against the wind. He brought it and held it open for him. Doran leaned on his stick and looked at it, the circle and the rings and the palace in the middle with its dome, and the blue ring of the water's road, and the spokes. He looked for a long time, with his head a little on one side.

"Where's the church?" he said. He said it mildly. He said most things mildly.

Emeric showed him.

It was on the plan. It was on the second ring, on the street between two spokes on the north side, a good long plot facing south across the ring street, with room round it for a yard and a burial ground and a house for the priest. It was a big plot. He had made sure of that. It was on a ring street, where people would pass it every day of their lives.

It was not at the centre.

Doran looked at it. He looked at where it was, and then, briefly, at the middle of the sheet, where the palace stood with its dome over the long room. Then he looked back at his plot.

"Thank you," he said.

He did not say anything else. He did not ask about the dome. He handed the board back to Emeric with both hands, the way he handed back a book at the church door, and turned on his stick and went back across the flat to the cart, and Bram helped him up into it.

Emeric stood holding the sheet.

"He didn't like that," said Ward, beside him.

"No."

"He didn't say he didn't like it."

"No," said Emeric. "He didn't."

Nobody would have called it a city that spring.

It was a quarry. The grandfather's face in the ridge was cleared of turf and scrub and opened wider, and Bram's masons came down from the island with their gear, and the first blocks came out of it grey and sharp on the fifth day. It was two kilns, square and ugly, at the foot of the ridge beside the quarry, burning the ridge's own limestone for mortar. It was a sawmill on the river where the valley narrowed, on a race cut off the water above the shelf, with a wheel and a single saw frame that Jorrin built, and men up in the Whispering Woods felling the oak and the pine while the sap was down, because timber cut in the thaw splits.

It was a spur of track, a quarter mile, from the quarry face to the edge of the flat, on light rail from the old Grayrock stock that Bram had been going to melt down, with a winch at the top and the loaded cars let down by their own weight and hauled back empty by a horse.

And it was trenches. That was the part that looked like nothing at all. Long straight trenches, cut in the rough pasture along the chain lines and the surveyors' pegs, following the fall of the ground to the sea, where the drains and the mains would go. They were dug and left open, with the spoil heaped along their edges, and men walking past on the river road looked at them and wondered what the prince was digging for, and some of them said he was looking for gold, and some said it was a new kind of field.

In the second week the mortar would not set.

It set on the island. It had set on the island for longer than anybody could remember. But the footings of the city would stand in wet ground on the marsh side, and Emeric had told the kiln-master from the beginning that he wanted a mortar that would set under water, and the kiln-master had said that there was no such thing, and then said that there was, and that his father had known a man on the coast who made it, and it needed a different stone. A greyer one, with clay in it. And a hotter kiln than the island's lime had ever had.

They tried the ridge's grey stone, and it did not set under water. They blamed the mason who had mixed it. Then they blamed the sand. Then Weekes, who said nothing about anything, walked along the ridge one morning for a mile and came back with a lump of dull blue-grey stone from a band at the north end of the face that nobody had quarried because it was too soft to build with, and put it on the kiln-master's bench, and went back to his level.

It was the right stone. The kiln was still not hot enough. That was a problem for later, and Emeric wrote it down with the others.

He put the first stake in himself, at the datum, on the last morning of the thaw.

It was not the first stake. The surveyors had put in hundreds. But it was the first stake on the wall's line, where the circle crossed the chain line from the datum to the sea, and he had asked them to leave it for him, and they had.

It was a plain oak stake, squared, a little over a yard long, with its top painted white. He knelt on the wet turf and set it in the hole the nephew had cut and held it while the nephew tamped the earth round it, and then he stood up and put his hand on the top of it.

The wind was off the sea. It smelled of salt and of the kilns burning at the foot of the ridge. The trenches went away from him down the flat in their long straight lines, full of thaw water, catching the light. Out on the lower water a lighter was coming in to the quay under a brown sail.

Behind him, up the valley, over the fold of the land, he could see the island's smoke in the air. All the chimneys of Kaldo, three families to a hearth, going up grey into the morning. And under it, fainter, if he looked, the smudge where the tannery lane still smoked when the wind stirred the ash.

He stood with his hand on the stake and looked at the smoke for a while. Then he took his hand off it and went to see about the kiln.
