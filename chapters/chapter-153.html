Chapter 153 - Four Columns

Ferra ruled the sheet on the eighteenth of March, at the long table, before anybody else had come in, and it took her the whole of the first bell.

Four columns. She drew them with the long rule and the steel pen from the top of the sheet to the bottom, and at the head of each she wrote a name in her small square hand, and underlined it. Kaldo. Grayrock. Milford. Wayford. Then she ruled the lines across, one for every head the barony's own book had carried for five years, and a good many it had never needed until this winter: rails, and wire, and the fifth.

Emeric came in at the second bell with Hale and the minutes and stood at the end of the table and looked at it.

It was the first time. The baronies had been his since the court's rulings, and they had sent their books, and the books had been read; but they had come in one at a time, by post and then by wire, a column at a time on different days, the way rain comes in through different holes in a roof. Nobody had ever laid all four side by side on one sheet for one quarter and read across.

"Sit down," said Ferra. "You're in my light."



The stewards did not come. They did not sit at the table and never had, which had been settled years ago and argued about every time since; they reported to it. They reported now down the wire, all three, on the morning of the eighteenth, one after another in the order Ferra had set, so that the clerks in the gate tower were writing from first light and the slips came down the tower stair to the long room in a boy's hand every quarter of an hour.

Paxton's came first. It was an inventory again, as it always was, and it was right again, as it always was. Wheat and barley and beans to the bushel. The herds by the head. The sluice on the lower field mended at last, on the second of February, by the Thursday after the Thursday he had promised. Ferra read it down the Milford column with her pen moving beside it, line by line, and made no mark.

Wayford's came second, from Aster, in Aster's careful clerk's words. The square swept. The vats running. The crossing house's bags up a fifth on last spring. The weighhouse at the junction above the vats open and weighing, its fees as usual. The road mended at the two bad places north of the ford. Nothing else. It was the third quarter in a row that Wayford's report had said nothing else, and Ferra put it down the Wayford column in four lines and looked at the four lines, and said that it was a quiet barony, and went on.

Grayrock's came third, from Bryda, and it was not quiet.

It was long, for one thing. Bryda paid the wire's rate by the word and Grayrock's book carried it, and she did not waste words, so when a report from Grayrock ran to three slips it was because she had something to say. The mine had raised more ore in the winter quarter than in any quarter since it was sunk. The gravity drive was through the first body and into the second. The line to Kaldo had carried four hundred wagons since it opened in January, by horse. And at the landing, at the end of the slip, a sentence Emeric read twice.

Twelve ore wagons stood at the landing since the ninth, loaded, waiting on bolts. The wagons were Kaldo's, built at the works, and their bolts were Kaldo's, cut on Bram's taps. The plates they bolted to and the nuts on the axle boxes were Grayrock's, cut by Grayrock's smith on his own taps, the ones his father had left him. Kaldo's bolts did not go into Grayrock's nuts. Grayrock's did not go into Kaldo's. When a box worked loose on the line, as boxes did, the wagon stood until a bolt came down from Kaldo that fitted Kaldo's half, and a nut was cut at Grayrock that fitted the bolt. Twelve wagons. Nine days. The steward of Grayrock sent her respects and would like to know which of the two smiths was wrong.

"Both," said Emeric.

Ferra looked up.

"Neither." He put the slip down on the Grayrock column. "That's the trouble. Bram cuts a good thread. Grayrock's smith cuts a good thread. They're both good, and they're different, because nobody ever told either of them what a thread was supposed to be." He looked at the twelve wagons, in Bryda's spare words, standing at a landing he could see in his head. "Every smith in the kingdom cuts his own. That's fine when the bolt and the nut come out of the same shop and never leave it. It stops being fine when they do."

"Then tell them," said Ferra.

"Tell them what?"

"What a thread's supposed to be." She wrote something in the Grayrock column. "Nine days of twelve wagons standing is a hundred and eight wagon-days. Vantic will have it in Grayrock's book as a cost by now. I'd like it not to be in the next one."



The forge did it on the twentieth, at night, in the cellar.

It was the kind of thing the forge did best and the kind Emeric had built it for, before the bands, before the cells, before any of the things with a stone in them that the town would have stared at if it had known. A thing with no magic in it at all. One thread. One shape, the same in every size, cut on a master as true as the forge could make it, so that every tap and die in four baronies could be cut from the master and checked against it, and every bolt from every shop would go into every nut from every other.

He had chosen the shape in the room the night before, from a page he remembered: a man called Whitworth, on Earth, who had walked round the workshops of his own country asking every smith for his screws and found that no two of them agreed. The sides of the thread at a fixed angle to each other. The tops of the ridges rounded and the bottoms of the grooves rounded, because a sharp groove is where a bolt cracks. So many ridges to the inch for each size, written in a table: a few coarse ones for a big bolt, many fine ones for a small, and every size on the table and no size off it. It had taken Whitworth years to get his country to agree to it. Emeric meant to have it in a week.

Tess sat at the dial with her key in the case and her data book on the bench. She had been sworn to the cellar for a year now, and she still went quiet every time the stone door shut behind them, the way some people go quiet in a church.

He told the forge what he wanted. Eight master taps, one for each size on the table. Eight master dies to match. A set of gauges for each size: a ring that a good bolt would go into and a ring it would not, a plug that would go into a good nut and a plug that would not. And, because they would need them the morning after, master profiles for the lathe's thread tools and the drill points, so that every shop that cut a thread ground the tool that cut it to the same edge.

The forge worked. Tess watched the dial. It took four hours.

In the morning he took them down the hill to the tally room in their box and laid them on the felt beside the gauge block, the plain square of steel that had been Kaldo's first measure of anything, and Bram came in from the works with his apron on, and looked at them.

He picked up the smallest tap. He turned it in the window light, and looked along its threads with one eye shut, the way he looked along a rail, and put it down and picked up the biggest.

"This is what Grayrock's smith gets," said Bram.

"Grayrock's smith gets a set of taps cut from these. So does every shop. Yours first."

Bram turned the big tap over in his thick fingers. "And mine are wrong."

"Yours are yours. Nobody's are wrong. From today they're all going to be the same, that's all."

"Mine are wrong," said Bram, "by about the width of a hair at the top of every thread, because my father's master tap was cut by his father on a lathe with a worn screw." He put it down. "I've known that for thirty years. I didn't know what to do about it." He picked up the ring gauge, the one marked not, and screwed a bolt from his own apron pocket into it, and it went in a turn and a half, and stopped. He looked at it a while. "Kaldo thread," he said.

"It's a standard screw thread. That's the name for it."

"It's a Kaldo thread," said Bram, "and Grayrock can like it or lump it," and he took the box of masters back to the works under his arm, and by the end of the week every tap in Kaldo had been cut again from them, and the first set had gone up the line to Grayrock in a wagon with a letter from Ferra that ran to three lines.

The masters went back into the tally room. They lived there from then on, in a case of their own beside the gauge block, under a glass lid, and every quarter every shop in four baronies sent the tally room a bolt and a nut and a drilled plate off its own bench, and a clerk put them to the gauges, and wrote down which went and which did not. Nobody asked a smith to like it. They only asked him to fit.



Vantic came back on the quarter day.

She came down the line from Grayrock in the first wagon of the morning string, which the horse company had fitted with two benches and a canvas hood for the steward's clerks and anybody else who did not mind ore dust, and she sat on one of the benches in her grey with the House's book on her knees and her gloves on, upright, while the wagon rolled down the river road behind two horses at a walk. She had been at Grayrock a fortnight, reading the mine's book under the commission. She got down at the Kaldo end of the line in the yard below the works as if she were getting out of a carriage at a door, and walked up the hill to the keep with the book under her arm, and went up to her tower.

She came down to the long room at four, when Ferra sent for her, and read the four columns.

She read them standing, without touching the sheet, with her hands folded in front of her, the way she had read everything in Kaldo for three years. It took her most of an hour. Ferra sat across the table from her and did not speak and did not watch her, which was the one courtesy Ferra had never failed to pay anyone who was reading a book of hers.

"The Grayrock column," said Vantic at last. "Line forty." She did not point. "Twelve wagons, nine days. It's in Grayrock's book as a cost. I wrote it there on the tenth." She turned her head a little. "Bryda's smith received a set of taps from Kaldo on the twenty-second. Bryda wrote to me about it. She said she didn't care for being told how to cut a bolt, and that the first twelve fitted." She looked at the column again. "I should like to see the wagon-days next quarter."

"You will," said Ferra.

"Then that's all." Vantic looked at the sheet a breath longer. "Four columns," she said. "On one sheet." She said it without expression, as she said everything; and then, with a very little expression, so that Emeric was not sure afterward he had heard it: "I've read a great many baronies' books. I don't believe I've ever seen four of them agree."

She went back up to her tower. Ferra looked at the door for some while after it had shut.

"She's staying," Ferra said. "For the engine. She asked Bram on the stair when it would run." She picked up her pen. "She's never once asked when anything would run."



The orders were on the table too, that week, in a different hand.

Saban brought them in from the coast boat: three letters from three printers in three coast towns, and one from a physician in the largest of them, all asking for the same two things. The school's primers, the reading book and the figuring book, in lots of fifty. And the plates. Imani's anatomy plates, twelve of them, printed on the press at the works from Tess's pear-wood blocks that had been cut from Imani's own drawings: the bones, the great vessels, the gut, the hand opened layer by layer, every one of them as true as she could make it and labelled in her square plain words. The physician on the coast had seen a set at a bookseller's and wanted his own, and two more for his students, and would pay what Kaldo asked.

"They know us on the coast for the printing," said Saban. He laid the letters on the table. "Not for the lamps. Not for the boats. The printing. They think the reason a Kaldo carter can read a bill of lading is that Kaldo prints well and cheap and has a school." He turned his hat on his knee. "Let them."

"It's true," said Ferra.

"It's true," Saban agreed, "and it's cheaper than the truth, and it'll keep them busy a good long while." He looked at Imani, who had come in late from the hospital and was reading the physician's letter over Ferra's shoulder with her lips pressed together. "Your name's on the plates now, on the coast. He calls you the Kaldo anatomist."

"I'm not an anatomist," said Imani. "I'm a healer who's had to look." She read the letter to the end. "He's spelled the hand wrong," she said, and put it down, and went back to the hospital; but Emeric saw her at the window of the print shop the next afternoon, with the plates spread on the bench and a pencil, going over the hand.



Armand read the barony at the end of the month, as he had said he would at the first reading three years ago, and every year since: everybody who would sit for it, three readings on three days, in his room off the hall, with Linnet to bring them in and the book on the board across his knees.

It took him nine days this year. Kaldo had grown. He read the still crew, who had carried stones bare-handed for four years, and wrote them down. He read a hundred townsfolk matched to the still crew, man for man and woman for woman, who had never carried anything heavier than a bucket, and wrote them down. He read the six, with their bands. He read the four knights.

He read the knights last of the band-wearers, on the last morning, Ward and Osric and Declan and Rylan one after another on the stool under the window, and Emeric, sitting by the door waiting his turn, watched his face while he read them.

It did not move. Armand's face did not move for anybody. But he read Osric three times, as he read everybody, and then he read him a fourth time, which he did not do for anybody, and wrote, and blotted it, and turned back a page in the book to last September's line, and looked at it, and turned forward again, and said nothing at all. Osric got up off the stool and went out whistling. Declan went in, and was read, and went out, and Armand turned back a page again.

When Rylan had gone Linnet looked at the door, and then at Emeric, and went out, and shut it behind her.

Emeric sat on the stool. He held out his wrist.

Armand did not take it at once. He reached down beside his chair into the basket where Linnet kept the drained stones for him to practise his reading on, and moved them aside, and brought up from under them a small book bound in brown calf, very worn, the size of his hand.

"This came south in my trunk," he said. "Under the shirts. I didn't give it to you with the other one, at the banquet. The other one was my work. This was my job." He opened it on the board. It was full of columns, ruled by hand, in a hand Emeric knew: Armand's, but younger, larger, before it had begun to shrink. Names, and dates, and three numbers to each name. "Every child I ever read for the tower, in twenty years of tutoring. The tower reads the royal children at ten. I read you."

He turned the book round on the board. His thin finger lay under a line.

Emeric. Ten years old. A date in the autumn, the year after his mother. Three numbers, small and neat.

Armand turned back twenty pages without looking for the place and laid his finger under another line. Amara. Ten years old. A date thirty-odd years before. Three numbers.

They were very close.

"She was my apprentice," said Armand. "Later. I read her at ten, too, the year she came to the tower. And when I read you, twenty-odd years after, I went home and turned back to her line, the way I just did, because I wanted to see. And it was the same. As near as makes no matter." He closed the calf book on his finger. "The tower track. That's what we call it. A child who reads like that at ten goes to the tower if he's let. You weren't let. Your father had other plans for you, and so did you."

"I don't remember being read."

"You were ten. You were a very quiet boy by then. You sat on a stool like this one and gave me your wrist and looked at the floor the whole time." Armand's mouth did something. "You'd stopped casting at eight. I knew that. Everybody in the palace knew that. The prince who'd stopped. I thought it was grief. Then I thought it was the drink, later, when there was drink. It didn't matter to the reading. The tower teaches that capacity is set at birth, and read once, and there's an end of it. So you were on the tower track, and then you were a drunk on the tower track, and that was all a reader needed to know."

He put the calf book down on the board, open, and took Emeric's wrist at last, two dry fingers on the inside above the band, and shut his eyes. He did not read. He held it.

"I read you at the gate," he said, with his eyes shut. "Here. The day I came. You gave me your hand off the cart, and I read you, because I've read every hand I've shaken for forty years and I can't stop. And you were a third of that." He opened his eyes and looked at the calf book. "A third. Of a boy of ten. A grown man of twenty-three with a third of what he'd had at ten. A strong commoner. A hedge mage, on a good morning."

"You didn't say."

"I didn't write it down." Armand let go of his wrist. "Which is worse, and you know it. I stood on that stair and told myself the boy's reading had been wrong. A warm afternoon. A tutor who wanted the queen's son to read like the queen. Because the tower says capacity can't change, and so one of the two readings had to be wrong, and the boy's was the one I'd made twenty years ago and could blame on a younger man." He looked at Emeric. "It's what I did with Mott. It's what I'd have gone on doing with Mott, if you hadn't made me read everybody."

He opened the big book, this year's, and turned back to last September's line, the one Emeric had read upside down. Then forward to this morning's page, where there was a space at the bottom, empty, ruled.

"September, you were here." His finger lay under last September's three numbers. "Circle range. A good Circle mage. I wrote it down this time, and said nothing, and told myself it was the band." He took Emeric's wrist again. He read it, three times, slowly, a breath apart, with his eyes shut; and opened them, and wrote three numbers on the empty line, and blotted them, and turned the big book round on the board so Emeric could read it the right way up, and laid the calf book open beside it, with his finger under the line that said ten years old.

The new numbers were higher than the boy's.

Not by much. By enough. Higher than the boy's, and higher than the girl's on the page twenty leaves back.

"The band didn't do that," said Armand. "The band's done something for Osric. A little for each of the knights, and I've written it down and I'll go on writing it down. It's done nothing like that, for anybody, in six months."

Emeric looked at the two books side by side.

He knew what it was. He had known on the quay, with his hand at his side, waiting for the ache that did not come; he had known it in the bones of the thing before he had a word for it. He found the word now, sitting on Armand's stool, and it was a word from the other life, from the medicine the trial had put into him whole: that muscle which has wasted in a splint comes back far faster than muscle which has to be built from nothing. That the body remembers its size.

"It wastes," he said.

Armand waited.

"Capacity. It's like muscle. You said so yourself, when we read Mott. Damage it slowly and it grows back bigger; tear it and it scars." He put his finger on the calf book, on the boy. "Then leave it alone and it wastes. Fourteen years, I didn't use it. Not a candle. I sat in rooms and drank and made a show of not being worth killing, and it wasted, the way a leg wastes in a splint. That's the gate. That's your third." He moved his finger to last September. "And then I started using it again. I didn't know it was coming back. I didn't know it was anything to come back. I slept with my mother's stone in my hand for five years. I've handled stones every day of the week since the still. I've been in the room more hours than I can count." He moved his finger to this morning. "And it came back. Faster than Mott's grew, because Mott had to build it from nothing and I only had to build it back. The body remembers the size it was." He took his finger off the page. "I wasn't small. I was rusted."

Armand looked at him without saying anything at all.

"Two," he said at last.

"Two?"

"Two men. Mott, and you." He laid his hand flat on the calf book. "Two isn't a result either. You know that. If I stood up in the tower with two, they'd send me broth by the bucket instead of the bowl." He picked up his pen. He dipped it. Under Emeric's three numbers on this morning's line, slowly, with the pen held as if it were heavier than it was, he drew a line, from one side of the column to the other, and then, after a breath, a second under it.

He did not say why. He did not need to. Armand had never underlined anything in that book in three years.

"There's a thing it explains," he said, when he had blotted it, "that you haven't thought of, and I've been thinking of it since September." He put the pen in its stand. "The tower keeps its mages casting. Every day. The gallery, the ten men at every hour with the ten behind them; the rotations on the walls; the drills in the yard that no grown mage would do if he weren't made to. We tell the young ones it's discipline. It's always been discipline. Four hundred years of it." He looked at the window. "And every one of us who ever stopped, faded. We called that age. Or drink, or grief, or a weak heart." He was quiet. "I stopped, when I took the tutoring post. It was a warm room and a quiet one, and nobody asked me to cast in it."

"What could you do," said Emeric, "before?"

Armand smiled. It was not a smile Emeric had seen on him before, and it took twenty years off his face and then gave them back.

"At forty," he said, "on the gallery, in the great storm the year before the war, the man behind me went down in a faint, and there was nobody behind him. I held the north-east corner of the gallery up alone. In the wind. For an hour and a quarter, until they got a man up the stair. Sixty men standing on it." He looked at his own hands on the board, which were thin, and spotted, and very still. "I couldn't lift that pen now by anything but my fingers."

Emeric did not say anything.

"So," said Armand, briskly, in the voice of the lecture hall, as though he were closing a book at the end of an hour. "You're done with rationing. I've watched you for three years casting like a man counting coppers at a market, a little here, a little there, never more than you can spare, and paying for every one of them in bed. You can stop." He picked up the big book and closed it. "Go away. Linnet will want her chair."



He went to the stable yard that night after the house was abed.

It was the yard where he had cracked the slate. Two years and a half ago, nearly; he could still see the place, the newer slate a shade darker than the rest along the stable roof's edge where Hale had had it mended. The same wall, twice his height, with its flat coping of old stones. The same water butt in the corner. The same lamp over the stable door, which he put out.

Ward was in the arch. Emeric did not see him and did not need to; Ward's note was in the arch, slow and even, a wall, the way it was in every arch Emeric had ever gone through at night for five years.

He did it Rylan's way first. He looked at the top of the wall and saw himself on it, whole, finished, there, and let the mana go down into his legs without telling it anything but that, and jumped.

He went up the face of the wall like a thrown stone. Too fast, as before. The coping went past his feet. He was above it, a body's length above it, in the dark, with the keep yard spread out under him and the lamps of the wall walk going round the town beyond.

And this time he had seen where it stopped.

He let the gravity go into himself, down, the other way from the jump: not a push, a cancelling. His own weight, taken off him, the way he had taken it off a coin on a table the first spring, and off a hand's width of his own body behind the shutters the night he hovered and his nerve went before his mana did. It was nothing. It was the cheapest thing he could do. And at the top of the jump, at the place where Rylan's jump turned and came down, his did not. It stopped.

He hung there.

A body's length over the top of the stable wall, in the dark, in the cold, with nothing under his boots but the air of the yard, and nothing holding him but the absence of his own weight. He could feel the small violet light of it at his heels, a flicker, the glyph of it no bigger than a coin, closing and opening again with every breath he held it. He could have held it till morning.

He looked down at the wall's coping, and chose a stone on it, the third from the corner, with a patch of moss in its middle; and let his weight back a little at a time, so that he came down out of the air slowly, the way a leaf comes down on a still day, and put his boots on the third stone from the corner, one on each side of the moss.

He stood there on the top of the wall.

Knights could jump. Every knight in Randillia could jump, some of them over a cart and some of them over a house, and all of them came down where the jump put them, on whatever was there, because there was nothing else they could do; once they had left the ground, the ground decided. He had stood on the wall walk at Kaldo and watched Rylan go off the river stair onto the barge and land where it chose to have him. No knight he had ever heard of had ever hung at the top and looked down and picked a stone.

Because no knight could cast. And no mage could jump.

He came down off the wall the ordinary way, by the water butt, and went to the far end of the yard to try the other thing.



The other thing did not work.

He had thought about it in bed for a week. If he could take his weight off at the top of a jump, he could take it off at the top of a stride; and a man running is only a man making very small jumps, one after another, and coming down after each one. Take the weight off and the stride goes farther. Every stride goes farther. A man who weighed nothing between his footfalls would cover the ground like a hare.

He took the weight off and ran.

His first foot came down on the cobbles and went out from under him sideways as if he had stepped on ice. His second foot never found anything. He went the length of the yard in three long helpless slithering bounds, with his arms out and his boots scrabbling at the stones, and the water butt came up at him out of the dark, and he went into it with his shoulder, and it rocked, and slopped, and a sheet of cold water came over the rim of it and went down his neck.

He stood in the corner of the yard, wet, with his weight back on and his shoulder against the butt, and breathed.

Ward came out of the arch. He did not hurry. He came across the dark yard and stood a few paces off and looked at Emeric, and at the water butt, and at the long scrape marks on the cobbles where Emeric's boots had gone looking for something to hold.

"You took your weight off your feet," said Ward.

"Yes."

"A man pushes off the ground with his weight." Ward did not say it as a lesson. He said it as he would have said that it was raining. "No weight, no push. You might as well run on the river."

Emeric stood in the wet and looked at the scrape marks.

Of course. It was on the first page of every mechanics book Marcus had ever opened. A foot pushes along the ground only as hard as the ground pushes back up on it, and the ground pushes back up on it only as hard as the man is heavy. Take the weight off a wheel and it spins. Take the weight off a boot and it slides. He had taken his weight off his feet at exactly the moment his feet were the only things that could have used it.

"Heavy when it's down," he said, slowly, working it out aloud, the way he worked out a column. "Light when it's up. The foot's heavy when it pushes. The man's light in the air between." He looked at the length of the yard. "Not all at once. Each stride. On and off. On and off."

"Can you do that?"

"I don't know."

He tried it. It was worse. He got the timing backward on the second stride, heavy in the air and light on the ground, and came down so hard on his heel that he felt it in his teeth, and then light on the next, so that his boot slid, and he went down on one knee on the cobbles, and stayed there.

Ward looked at him.

"Heavy, light," said Ward. "Down, up." He was quiet. "It's a dance. You never could dance." He held out his hand and pulled Emeric up off the cobbles. "Again tomorrow," he said, and went back to the arch.
