Chapter 304 - The River Road

They lit the table at a quarter past one on the second of July, because Tess said that if they did not light it today she would light it tomorrow without him, and he believed her.

It stood in the middle of the Works Office's map room on the second floor, with the blinds down on the river side against the afternoon. A long table of dark oak, chest high, fitted in the Forge's gunmetal and brass, with a case of glass on top a forearm deep and a sleeved brass post at each corner. Down each long side ran twenty-four brass pulls, one above another, like the drawers of an apothecary's chest. They were drawers. Each was a shallow tray, and in each tray, laid edge to edge and offset from the trays above and below the way a mason lays brick, sat three hundred plates of clear stone a few hairs thick. Under the lowest, in a tray of its own, lay a plaster model of Kaldo painted in plain colours, with pins in it and small paper flags. That much any carter could have read.

The plates were lamp stones, sliced in the Forge out of five hundred that Saban had bought a few at a time from five different coasts, and every one of them a delver had cut from the inside into a grid finer than a flour sieve. Told to, any cell in that grain would light. Told otherwise, it would drink what light fell on it and go darker than the room. The light sand table, Emeric had called it in the spring, because a sand table was what an army stood round to argue over ground, and nobody had asked him which army. In his own head it was a volumetric display, of the stacked kind, a thing he had read about once on a long train in another life and never seen.

Of the seven thousand and some plates, Tess had cut a little over four thousand in the winter, in the delve, at night. He had cut most of the rest beside her. The last six hundred he had cut alone, in April and May, while she slept under the tree.

She looked at the head chair now as if it owed her money.

She had come down from the hospital in a borrowed wheelchair, a plain ash-and-cane chair off Imani's ward with WARD 3 stencilled on its back, because her own was still in pieces on the Motion Shop's bench and Merrin had said it would be Thursday, which meant the Thursday after. Osric had pushed her the whole way. Osric was standing behind it now with his hands on its handles and the look of a man who has been told twice already not to help.

"It pulls left," said Tess.

"I know," said Osric. "I've been pushing it right since the hospital."

"You could have said."

"You'd have got out and walked."

She gave him a look over her shoulder that Emeric could not see the whole of and did not need to. Then she put both hands on the head chair's arms, the new hands with nothing on them, and lifted herself out of the ward's chair and across into the high-backed one in a single swing, the way a man vaults a stile, and pulled her feet across after her with one hand and set them side by side on the chair's step, where they stayed. Ferra, at the table's far end with her stone book open beside the case, did not look up, which was how Ferra let a thing be done without watching it.

On the chair's right arm was a recess, and in the recess a small brass cradle, and over the cradle a lid of glass on a hinge. Tess lifted the lid with one finger. The control stone lay in its brass in the shape of a long small egg, grey, unlit, one of the year's stones, the one the stone book had been holding a line for since the spring.

"Go on," said Emeric.

"I am going on. I'm looking at it first." She looked at it. "It's smaller in the chair." She put two fingers on it, lightly, the way she put two fingers on a valve's glass to feel whether it was warm, and went in; and he went in after her, because he had cut the last six hundred alone and wanted to be there when the stone found them. The model stood in her room as they had laid it in over the spring, out of Ferra's surveys and their own two heads, orderly as everything of hers was. Tess took hold of the bottom tray, the ground tray, and told it.

When he came out the room was lit.

It was flat. That was the first thing, and he had known it would be, and it still surprised him. One slice of light, a hand above the plaster, lying in the glass like a map drawn in fire on a sheet of water: the ring of the curtain wall, pale; the streets inside it, thread on thread, the wide ones brighter; the market square, a pale lozenge; the keep a small square on its ward. And between the streets, where the canals ran, the light did not merely stop. It went dark. The inner canal ring lay in the middle of the picture as a black ring blacker than the shadow under the table, and the river along the east wall was a black ribbon with the bridges laid across it in light, and the moat was a black ring round the whole. Past the fields, at the case's edges, there was a soft grey haze, which was where the stone had been told it did not know, and was saying so.

Osric bent and looked along it from the side, with his eye at the glass.

"You can see the joins," he said. "From here. Like a wall."

"From there," said Tess. "From the top you can't. That's the brickwork." She had not taken her fingers off the stone. "Don't look at it from the side. It's not for the side."

"Where's the hospital?"

She moved her fingers a hair, and the light moved under the glass. It did not move the way a lamp moves; it was redrawn, all at once, so that Kaldo slid toward the case's head and grew as it came, and the streets drew themselves finer instead of fatter, and there was the hospital's roof line in its square, and the works beyond it by the river, and over the works a small cross of light that she had not had to put there.

"That's your room," said Osric.

"It's where the fire buckets are. The Works Office marks the fire buckets." She looked at the cross for a while, and moved her fingers, and the picture went back.

Ferra had come round the table's end. She read the streets the way she read a column, from the top, and then put one finger on the glass over the north ward, where the tram ran out along the canal and turned back at the new pump house.

"The loop's not there," she said.

Emeric looked. The tram line ran up the north street in a fine bright thread and stopped short of the pump house, square, as if somebody had cut it with a knife.

"That's April's survey," said Tess.

"The loop went in on the ninth of May."

"Then it's in your book and not in the stone. The stone only knows what we told it. We told it April." She looked down the glass with her eyes half shut. "It doesn't look old, does it. It looks exactly as sure as the rest."

"It's a good survey," said Ferra.

"It was a good survey. In April." Tess took her fingers off the stone, and the light stayed; it would stay, now, as long as the plates had charge in them. "I'll write the loop in on Thursday. I'll sit at it." She did not look at Emeric. "You're busy tonight."

Ferra went back to the end of the table and wrote her line. He read it upside down from where he stood, in her small exact hand, under the last entry on the page: 2 July. One. To the table, Works Office map room, in the chair's cradle. Plates cut T. and E. Lit by T., a quarter past one. F.

"Tomorrow," said Tess, to the glass, "the second tray. The streets stand up when there's two. You'll see the keep come up off the ward." She pulled the glass lid down over the stone with one finger, and it clicked. "Come and see it."

"Tomorrow," said Emeric.

"I said come and see it. I didn't say tomorrow. Tomorrow you'll be asleep." She held her arms up, without turning round, and Osric came and put the ward's chair at the right angle, and she swung back into it. "Push it right," she said. "All the way to the hospital. And don't tell Imani I lifted myself, she'll want to see it again."



The K Discovery went up from the secret dock at a quarter to five, on her stones, with her propeller turning slow and her irises shut, into a high pale afternoon haze over the bay that would hide a ship at four miles up as well as a cloud would.

Imani was not on the quay. She had said she would not be. She had said it at noon in the ward's passage with her sleeves rolled up and a tray in her hands, with Bettany waiting behind her for the tray, and then she had given Bettany the tray and put two fingers under his jaw in front of the whole passage and counted, out loud, to herself; and said seventy-two, and that he was a liar, and that she would be in the line room from four. And she had gone back into the ward without looking round.

Fletcher had the bow helm and said nothing for the first hour, which was Fletcher's way of being pleased with a ship. Linus Vance had the chart table with the river sheets pinned flat by Ferra's brass weights. Ferra had the ship's set in its bay behind him, and her book, and the log.

The suits were in their crate on the saloon floor: the five black ones from Grier's, and two more that Abloh had cut in June on Tess's word, for the river, with no plate and no ports and no helm, only the wool and the mesh in it and a close hood, so that two men lying under a landing stage with Church men standing on the planks over their heads would be what the other five were, a cat in the dark. The rebreathers lay beside them with their covers blacked and their hoses laid down the backs.

Emeric lay down on the saloon floor beside the crate with his head on his folded coat, because Imani had said he would sleep on the ship, and he had decided somewhere over the coast that he would rather do what she said than hear about it.

He slept, which surprised him. He woke once, somewhere over the middle of the country with the light going yellow through the forward iris's slot, and lay with his eyes shut and heard the set in its bay give its two short purrs and Ferra pick up the handset.

"Discovery," said Ferra. "Reading you."

The voice in the handset was too small to hear from the floor. He did not need to hear it. He heard Ferra listen.

"Ninety minutes. On time. The westerly's on the beam and Fletcher's taking it personally." A pause. "He's asleep." Another. "On the floor. With his coat under his head. I'm not going to wake him to tell you that." A longer pause, and something in it that might have been the edge of a laugh going past on Ferra's side, quickly, like a bird past a window. "No. I'll tell him. Line room out."

She put the handset back on its hook. He kept his eyes shut.

"She says she knows you're not asleep," said Ferra, to the log. "She says your breathing's wrong for it."

"She can't hear my breathing from Kaldo."

"She says she doesn't need to." The pen moved. "Go back to sleep. Properly. You've an hour."

He did, properly, until Ward put a boot against his shin at a quarter to eight.



They suited by the lamp's slot in the order they had suited for Grier, without talking. Under-suit, which went on like a second skin and pulled at the hair on his forearms. Shin plates. Boots, laced, the sole stones grey and dead. Gloves. The helm over his locs, which Imani had bound flat at the back at noon with a strip of cloth and no comment. The hood over the helm. The world went into its blanket.

He took the right glove off again and pushed it through his belt.

Ward watched him do it. They had had this out three times in June, and Ward had lost it every time, because there was no way round it: the mesh stood between a man's hands and the world like a wall, and tonight Emeric had two things to put into a river that nobody else could. So one hand would be bare, in the water, under the reeds, for as long as the work took and not a breath longer.

"A hand," Ward had said, on the race bank, "is a hand's worth of you. To anybody feeling for it."

"Under a yard of water. In a river full of fish."

"A big fish," said Ward, and had not said anything else that night.

He said nothing now either. He looked at the bare hand once, and then at Emeric's face inside the hood, and put his own helm on and drew his own hood, and his slow note went dim through the iron as if somebody had cupped a hand round a lamp.

Across the saloon, Declan and Rylan were getting into the river suits. Declan did it as he did everything, from the top down, every lace pulled and then checked by eye; then the rebreather, the round bag on the chest under its blacked cover, the bottle along the left ribs, the brass can on the right. He unscrewed the can's lid, looked into the basket at the grains, sniffed them, screwed the lid down, and held the can a while in his bare palm before he put his gloves on.

"Warm?" said Rylan.

"Not yet. It's not working yet."

"I know it's not working yet. I'm asking so you'll say it."

"Cold means up," said Declan.

"Cold means up." Rylan pulled his hood on and looked out of it at the three in the black suits on the bench across from him, Arthur and Mira and Bren in a row with their helms on their knees. "Ferryman," he told them. "Tonight. You'll be able to say you knew me before."

"We knew you before," said Mira.

"You'll be able to say it with feeling."

Bren laughed, and stopped, because it had come out louder than he meant, and looked at his gloves. Arthur did not laugh. He was looking at Linus's sheet thirteen where Linus had laid it on the bench between them, with his finger on the landing stage, as if he could learn it by pressing.

Saban sat in the corner with the field radio on its frame between his knees: the box the size of a bread crock with FIELD scratched on its lid in Ivo's nail-hand, the cane aerial folded down beside it in two lengths, the handset on its coiled cord. He had his hat on, and a wool cap in his pocket for when it mattered; until it mattered, he said, a man should be allowed his hat.

"The three words," said Ferra, from the bay.

"Ship, king, come now," said Saban. "In yours."

"In mine."

"I'll not say the second unless he's on the ferry with us, and I'll not say the third unless the first two are true." He touched the handset. "And I'll not say anything at all for the pleasure of it. I know."

The six with the rifles sat along the after bench with the long guns upright between their knees, six of the old three hundred, every lever set to air and wired there with a twist of brass by Ferra's own hand in the armoury that afternoon, so that nobody could say afterward that a Kaldo gun had been fired on powder on the king's road. They were Ward's best six from the range. None of them had flown before tonight, and they sat very straight, the way men sit in a church they do not belong to.



Fletcher brought her down out of the haze over the hills at a quarter to nine, in the last of the light, with cloud lying along the ridges to the north like wool caught on a fence. Linus said it was the right cloud, which was a thing Emeric had not known cloud could be.

The field was where the sheet had it: two miles up from the crossing on the near bank, a long rough pasture behind a wood of oak and alder, with the river beyond the wood and the reeds beyond that. Nothing in the field but a few sheep, which went to the far hedge and stood looking. No farm nearer than a mile. No light anywhere but the last of the sky, green over the west and going.

She came down with her stones dimmed almost to nothing and stopped a man's height above the grass with her forward leg half down, and the trim keys clicking, and the pasture lifting and flattening under her keel the way it had under her in the hay at Grier's. At nine by Linus's watch the hatch came down.

Ferra was at the hatch's head. She had come aft from the bay and stood at the coaming with her book shut under one arm and her free hand at her collar, at the top button of the grey, where the cord went under the cloth. She did not touch the button. She stood with her hand beside it.

Saban went down first with his box, pushed from below by Ward. Then the six. Then Declan and Rylan with the rebreathers in their arms like babies. Then the three. Then Emeric, at the hatch's head, last but one.

"Ramp," said Ferra.

"Ramp," he said.

She took her hand away from her collar and went forward to the bay, and he went down into the grass, and Ward came down behind him; and the hatch went up, and the K Discovery rose off the pasture without a sound that would have carried past the hedge, and turned her blunt black nose north toward the cloud on the hills, and the dark took her before she reached it.

Emeric watched the place where she had gone until Ward said his name, once, in the flat voice, and he turned round.



They went down through the wood in the order Ward had given them at the long table, and along the reeds.

The reeds were what Linus's margin said: a man's height, and more, so thick that a man walking in them had to go by the sound of the man in front, and the three in the black suits made no sound, so that it was like following nobody. The river went past on their right, wide and slow, the brown gone black now, smelling of mud and weed and summer water. A heron got up somewhere ahead with a sound like a sheet being torn, and everybody stopped, and Arthur's voice came back very quiet through his hood: "Bird."

Under the mesh Emeric's sense came back to him as it had at Grier's, close and dull, like voices through a wool blanket: the others faint warm shapes beside him, Ward's slow wall the clearest; past them a dim cool haze that was the water, and small sparks that were fish. His right hand was another matter. He kept it bare at his side as he walked, and the night came into it the way it comes into a hand out of a carriage window, the cool of the river, the faint hum of the reeds full of their own small lives. It was like seeing through a keyhole.

A quarter mile above the crossing the reeds thinned at a bend where the bank had come down in some flood and left a shelf of mud going out under the water, and there Declan and Rylan stopped.

They did it the way they had done it every night for a month off the wheel house step. Masks up, bags on, bottles opened a half turn and listened to, hoses down the backs. Declan checked Rylan's and Rylan checked Declan's, in silence, with their hands, by feel. Then Declan held his can in his bare palm again before he pulled his glove on, and Rylan saw him do it and did not say anything this time.

Emeric crouched at the edge of the mud beside them with his face close to their hoods, and said it the way Tess had told him to say it. Exactly where.

"Downstream row. The two farthest out, under the end of the stage, where it's seven feet. River side of each pile, not the bank side. Three knobs each, a yard apart, the lowest a hand off the mud." He put his bare hand on Declan's forearm and then on Rylan's, so they would know it was him. "Ten minutes to the hour. They'll go an hour after that. Maybe less, if the river's quicker than the race."

"It's slower," said Declan.

"Then maybe more."

"Maybe," said Declan, which from Declan was a long speech. He put the mouthpiece in. Rylan put his in, and grinned round it, which looked terrible, and raised one hand; and they went down the mud shelf into the black water without a sound and went under, and there was nothing. No bubbles. Emeric stood at the edge with his bare hand out over the river and felt them go down, two small dulled warmths, and settle on the bottom, and begin to move with the current, slowly, downriver, the way they had come down the race a hundred times. Then the river took them out of the keyhole, and he could not feel them at all.

He looked at Ward. Ward looked at his watch.

"Twenty-two minutes to the stage, in the race," said Ward. "Say twenty-five."

They went on.



The crossing came up out of the dark as a smell first, horse and tar and woodsmoke, and then as a light.

The ferry house stood on the near bank where the road came down to the water, a low house of grey stone with a turf roof and a lantern hung under the eave of its door, and a lit window, and a dog somewhere behind it on a chain that had heard nothing yet. The road came down past it out of a cutting in the bank to a hard of trodden gravel, and off the end of the hard ran the landing stage: a jetty of heavy planks on two rows of piles, going out over the water perhaps thirty feet, black with weed below the line, with a post at its end and a lantern on the post. Tied to the stage by bow and stern lay the ferry.

She was a big square flat of a boat, tarred black, with low rails and a ramp at each end, broad enough for a farm cart and its horse or six horses standing. From a tall post above the stage a rope ran out over her deck and away into the dark toward the far bank, where another lantern hung on another post, two hundred yards off; it ran through a block on a frame on her deck, so that she went over on it and could go nowhere else. Two long poles lay along her rail. A lantern on a short mast in her middle, unlit.

A man sat on the stage's end with his legs hanging down, smoking. Another in the ferry house's doorway, older. A boy of ten or so on the step under the eave lantern, with his knees up, doing nothing, the way a boy does nothing when he has been told he must stay up for something.

"Waiting," said Ward's voice beside him, through two hoods.

"They've been told," said Saban, very low, from behind them. "Forty-two horses and a carriage, by eleven. The ferryman will have had his money in advance. He'll not have been told what for." A small pause. "He'll not have asked."

They went to their places as they had gone to them in the race with barrels and a door on a rope. The six and Saban up the bank to the right, upriver, where the reeds stood thick on a low rise above the cutting fifty yards from the hard, with a field hedge behind them and the road below: the six lay down along the rise's lip in the reeds with the long guns out in front of them, and Saban sat down behind them against the hedge with his box between his knees and unfolded the cane, one length, not two, and put the handset in his lap and took his hat off at last and put the wool cap on. The three in the black suits went round the ferry house in the dark, twenty yards from the boy on the step, and he did not look up. They had the batons in their thigh loops and knew where the sentries would be put when the escort came down, because anybody who knew horses and rivers would put them there: one up the bank, one down. Arthur would have the upriver one, Mira the down. Bren would be wherever Arthur had told him to be.

Ward and Emeric went into the reeds at the hard's upstream edge, between the cutting and the stage, twenty paces from the stage's shore end. They lay down. The mud was cold through the wool and smelled of everything the river had ever carried.

At eight minutes to ten Emeric went along the reeds on his belly to the stage.

The reeds came right to its shore end, where the first pair of piles stood in a foot of water. He lay flat in them with his helm a yard from the planks and the smoking man's boots hanging over the far end of them, thirty feet off, and put his bare right arm into the river to the shoulder, and laid his palm flat on the nearest pile of the downstream row, under the water, on the weed.

It was not the race. In the race he had put his hand on the wall where the knob would be, and it had cost him nothing. Here the knobs wanted to be thirty feet off and seven feet down, on two piles he could not reach without going into the water, and he was not going into the water; he had told Ferra so. So he did it the long way, Armand's way: he let the pull go out of his palm along the pile's face, along the wet timber and the skin of water on it, out through the next pile's foot and the next, as you feel down a rope in the dark, until it was where he wanted it. Then he pulled.

A glyph came. It had to. It drew itself in front of his palm under the water, a ring no bigger than a coin, and he shut it down as small and dim as Armand had ever taught him and it lit the weed on the pile for an instant, a dull red smudge a yard under brown water in a bed of reeds, and went out. Nobody on the stage looked down.

The heat came back along the timber to him slowly, because it had a long way to come, and he let it go out of his forearm into the river, which took it downriver and did not notice. Thirty feet off and seven down, on the river side of the farthest pile, the water on the timber went thick, and white, and hard. A yard up, again; a yard up again; then the next pile. Six small cold fists frozen into weed and wood with their backs to the current, and he could not see any of them and did not need to.

When he drew his arm out his palm ached faintly in its middle, like a hand that has carried a bucket too far. He went back along the reeds on his belly to Ward.

"Ten to," he said.

Ward wrote nothing down, because there was nothing to write on, and nodded once inside his hood.

At eleven minutes past ten, with his bare hand flat in the shallows among the reed roots, Emeric felt them come. Two small dulled warmths at the end of the keyhole, low, close to the bottom, coming down the river very slowly with the current out of the dark above the stage. They went under the stage's end. They stopped. He felt one of them settle, and then the other, at the foot of the farthest two piles on the river side, and hold.

He let his breath out.

"They're on," he said.



The escort came down the road at twenty-four minutes past ten.

He heard it before he saw it, and the dog behind the ferry house heard it before he did and began to bark: a long way up the road, iron on stone, a great many hooves at a walk, and harness, and a man's voice saying something short, and the wheels. Then the lanterns came over the rise above the cutting, on poles, one and then another and then more, swaying, with the horses under them coming out of the dark into their light by twos, big horses, heavy, the kind that can stand a long day and had stood one; and the men on them in the household's dark coats with the long swords at their saddles.

The ferryman put his pipe in his pocket and came out onto the hard. His man on the stage got up. The boy on the step took the eave lantern down off its hook and went with it down to the stage, carefully, holding it out in front of him with both hands like a thing that might spill.

They came down the cutting and onto the hard and filled it. Emeric counted, because Ward would ask, and because it was a thing to do with his eyes that was not looking at the carriage. Sixteen riders, two by two. Then a wagon, a long covered one, two horses, with a lantern on its tilt and a driver on the box; and round it, riding close, three men who were not the household's: two in long pale coats to the boot, riding very upright, with their hands on their thighs, and one in plain black, a narrow man on a bay, who rode with his face turned to the river the whole way down the cutting, the way a man turns his head to a sound he has not yet heard. The temple guard, and the third. Then the carriage.

It was a closed carriage, black, plain, on high wheels, a pair of greys in the shafts and the coachman on the box with a household sergeant up beside him, and its panels had been painted over in black so that whatever arms had been on the doors were gone. The blinds were down. Four riders round it. Behind it more of the household, twenty-odd, two by two, filling the cutting.

And between the carriage and the twenty, on the road, with a space before him and a space behind him that nobody had told anybody to leave, one more rider.

Emeric looked at him once.

A man on a grey horse, in a dark riding cloak with the hood drawn, sitting easily, the reins in his left hand and his right hand closed, resting on his thigh. The horse walked as if it had been told to. The riders ahead of him did not look round, and the riders behind kept their distance from him exactly, as men keep their distance from the edge of a quay. The lantern light went over him and did not seem to find much.

Under the mesh, Emeric's sense found less. A man's note, dulled through the iron as everything was; quiet; ordinary. Smaller than the temple guard's, either of them. Nothing at all to look at twice.

He did not look at it twice. He put his eyes on the carriage and kept them there, and beside him in the mud he felt Ward's note go very steady and very still, the way a held breath is still, and stay like that.

The carriage was where his father was.

He knew it without the sense. He knew it through the sense too, faintly, through the blanket, from twenty yards: inside the box of black painted wood with the blinds down, one note, sitting very upright, steady and tired, a note he would have known in a crowd of ten thousand. He did not reach for it. He let it be there. It was enough that it was there.



The household's captain was a big man with a beard gone grey at the chin, and he did it the way Saban had said he would, because it was the way anybody who knew horses would do it.

He put his men on the hard and along the road above it. He sent two along the bank on foot with lanterns, one up and one down, to watch the water. Emeric did not feel them taken. Under the mesh the three were nothing and the two sentries hardly more; but he saw the upriver lantern stop at the edge of the light, and after a while lie down on its side in the reeds, still lit, quite still, as if a man had set it down to rest his arm. That was what the baton looked like from outside: a man sitting down. Downriver, the other lantern went out. Nobody on the hard looked at either.

Then the captain went down onto the stage with the ferryman, and the ferry's ramp came down onto the planks with a hollow bang, and six of the household, a lieutenant among them, led their horses onto her by the bridle, with the horses' feet loud on the tarred deck and one of them not liking it; and the ferryman and his man took the poles, and she went out on her rope.

Emeric put his bare hand into the river and felt her go. Not the men: the boat. A great flat dead shape going out over the water, pushing the river aside, the rope above her singing faintly in its block. The two warmths at the foot of the farthest piles did not move.

It was slow. She went across the current slantwise on her rope with the poles going down and coming up dripping, and the lantern on her mast lit now so that the far bank could see her come, and it was nine minutes by Ward's watch before her far ramp went down onto the far stage and the six horses went off her, and seven more before she came back empty with her lantern swaying. Sixteen minutes. Ward had said twenty. Emeric had said twelve. Ferra, at the long table, had said she would write down whichever was right.

At four minutes to eleven, with the ferry still coming back across the dark toward them, he felt one of the warmths under the stage shift.

It was a small thing. The farther one, on the last pile: it slid along the timber a yard upward, and settled again. The lowest knob on that pile had gone. Running water eats it from the back. He had frozen them sixty-six minutes ago, in a river slower than the race, and the race had taken the first at fifty-one, and he lay in the mud and did not move his face and thought that Tess would want to know that, and would write it down, and would be right to.

The ferry came in to the stage. The ramp banged down.

The carriage went on alone.

The four riders round it drew off. The coachman got down and led the greys by their heads down the hard and onto the stage and along it, with the planks booming under the wheels; and the ferryman and his man stood one each side of the ramp with their hands up, the way men guide a cart onto a boat they have guided a thousand carts onto, and the high wheels went up the ramp and onto the tarred deck, and the greys stood. The coachman stayed at their heads. The sergeant got down off the box and stood by the carriage door with his hand on the door's rail and his other hand on his sword. The ramp came up. The ferryman and his man took the poles.

Four men on the deck. One in the box.

The boy on the stage's end held the eave lantern out over the water at arm's length, with both hands, to light the ferry off, the way he must have been told to do for every ferry since he could hold one.

She went out.

Under the stage, as her black bottom went out over them, Emeric felt both warmths let go of the piles and come up off the mud, and go with her.



He let it go out of his hand then, the other thing, the one he had come for; the water.

It was the one he was worst at. Hedda had told him so with the bucket's worth he had lifted out of her well slopping down his front, and had not troubled to be kind about it. Water was three times the work for him that fire was, and he felt every one of the three. But it did not have to be much water. The river went past the ferry's hull at a slow man's walk, and a man coming up beside a moving boat in the dark would be carried along her side and off her stern before he got a hand to her rail. So the river under her, for a few yards round, for a few minutes, was not to go anywhere.

The glyph came at his palm under the water, a water ring this time, larger than the fire's, and he crushed it down small and dim among the reed roots and held it there until it closed. Then there was nothing to see. There was only the work.

It went out from his hand through the river the way the sense goes out, thin, and found the ferry's dead flat shape eighty yards off and getting farther, and settled round her under the water like a hand round a cup; and he held the water there. Not still. Nothing that big was still. Slack. A patch of river a few yards across under her hull that was not going to the sea for as long as he said so, while the rest of the river went round it on both sides; and as she went on along her rope he moved it with her, under her, a yard at a time.

It hurt. It hurt behind the eyes first, where the sense always hurt, and then down the right arm into the bare hand in the water, a dull pull at every joint like an ache coming on in wet weather, and it went on hurting and did not get worse, which was the best that could be said of it. Ward's hand came onto his left shoulder through the wool and stayed there. Ward could not feel the water. He could feel Emeric's shoulder.

Out at the edge of the slack, very small and dulled, two warmths came up on either side of the boat.

He did not see it. He felt it through the river and his own hand. The warmths came up beside her hull in the slack water as gently as a man puts his hand on a sleeping dog, one on her upstream side and one on her down. They held there a breath, against her side, under her rail. Then the upstream one went up.

On the near bank the boy's lantern was still out over the water, and in its light and the light of the mast lantern, small and far, Emeric saw the ferryman's man on the upstream pole go backward off the rail into the river with his pole still in his hands, without a cry, like a man who has leaned out too far; and the pole come up out of the water and slide along the deck, and somebody black and streaming come in over the rail where he had been.

The mast lantern went out.

After that it was the river and the dark, eighty yards off, and the sounds that came back across the water flattened by it: a shout, short, cut off; the greys trampling on the deck and one of them screaming; a man's voice, quite cheerful and not loud, saying something he could not catch that ended in "sit there"; a heavy splash. Another. Then a sound he knew from the race, which was the sound of a rope's strands parting under a knife, one, two, three, and the long soft whip of the rope going up off the block and away over the water toward the far bank, singing as it went.

He let the slack go.

He felt the river take her. The whole slow body of it came back under her flat bottom from both sides at once, and she turned, ponderously, the way a big thing turns when it is let go of, and her blunt end came round downriver, and she began to drift. Then there was a pole going down into the river from her deck on the downstream side, and coming up, and going down; and the dead flat shape of her went on away from him down the dark faster than the current, with a man on her who had always wanted to be a ferryman.

He took his hand out of the water. It would not close.



The near bank knew.

It knew all at once, the way a crowd knows: the boy on the stage's end saying something high and thin, and then the captain's voice, enormous; and every lantern on the hard came round to the water, and the van, the sixteen and the four who had ridden round the carriage, came down onto the stage's shore end and the bank's edge and bunched there, men and horses ten deep, lanterns held out over the river into nothing, swords coming out, a man at the front pulling off his boots to swim for it with nothing to swim for. The far bank's six were shouting across two hundred yards of black water and could not cross it, because the only thing that crossed it was gone.

Ward took his hand off Emeric's shoulder.

He had the flash stone out of the pouch at his chest in its fist-sized case, and the brass pin through its eye. He looked at the bunched lanterns at the stage's end, twenty-five paces off.

"Eyes," he said, not loudly. On the rise the six had their own count from the race, the ferry's light out and then ten, and would have their faces in the mud behind their guns. Emeric shut his eyes inside the hood.

Ward pulled the pin and threw.

He threw underhand, flat and low, the way he threw a ball for the yard boys' dog, and it went out of the reeds and over the hard and into the middle of the bunched horses at the water's edge; and Emeric, with his eyes shut and his face turned into the mud, saw it anyway, through his eyelids and through the narrow pane and through the hood, a white so white it was nearly blue, gone as soon as it came; and then the noise, a blow on the whole body at once, through the ground, through the mud, through the wool, a crack like the sky split down the middle with an axe.

When he opened his eyes the near bank was screaming.

The horses at the water's edge had gone mad. Some had gone into the river, with men on them and without. Some had gone back up the hard into the horses behind, rearing, and the twenty in the cutting were a tangle of lanterns swinging and men shouting at horses that could not hear them. A man lay on the stage's planks with his arms round his head. The boy had dropped the eave lantern into the river, and Emeric saw it go down still lit, a yellow smudge going brown and then gone; and saw the boy himself go off the stage's shore end into the reeds across the hard on his hands and knees, and lie down in them, and not move.

Up on the rise the six fired.

It was the sound Ferra had written down at the long table, a book shut hard on a table, six of them, not together, close together. Six of the lanterns still lit on the hard went out, one after another, the glass bursting and the oil going down in a spill of little blue flames across the gravel and dying. The six reloaded, lying down. The levers clicked. Six more books shut. Six more lanterns. After that there was not much light left on that bank but the ferry house's window and the lantern on the Church men's wagon, and the small blue fires on the gravel going out.

The wagon was moving.

Its driver had not gone mad with the rest. He had stood up on the box at the flash and taken his whip to his pair, and they were going hard round the top of the hard for the cutting and away, the lantern swinging wild on the tilt and the three Church men riding close on either side. Getting out. Getting something out.

Ward had the gravity stone in his hand, six ounces of grey in its cup of iron with its own pin through it. He rose to his knees in the reeds, drew the pin, and threw, long this time, overarm, with the whole of his back in it, and the cup went turning through the dark and came down on the gravel under the wagon's front wheels as it came round; and Ward said, very quietly, "One."

The wagon stopped as if the ground had reached up and taken hold of it. The pair went down onto their knees in the traces and then onto their bellies, necks stretched out along the gravel, eyes rolling white, not screaming, because they did not have the breath to scream. The driver was flung down off the box and lay flat with his arms out. The tilt's hoops cracked. The lantern dropped straight down off its hook and smashed, and did not roll. And the three Church men went down with their horses, the horses folding under them and the men coming out of the saddles onto the road and lying there in a ring round the wagon, pressed to the gravel like leaves pressed in a book, ten yards every way.

"Two," said Ward. "Three."

Nothing within ten yards of that small grey cup would lift a sword for a count of sixty. Ward had said so at the long table. Sixty was a long time on a road at night.

"Four. Five."

Below them the near bank was trying to be an escort again and not managing it. The captain was roaring for lanterns, for horses, for the men in the water; the far bank was roaring back; the men in the river were getting out of it in their coats and boots, hauling each other by the collar; somewhere out in the dark downriver a man was laughing, not loudly, and then a pole went into the water. Nobody on that bank knew yet where the shots had come from. The cutting was full of loose horses going up it.

"Nine. Ten."

Emeric was looking at the road.

He had not meant to. He had meant to look at the ferry going away, and at the stage, and at the rise where the six lay and Saban sat with the handset in his lap, waiting to say the second of three words. He looked at the road instead, above the hard, between the pinned wagon and the cutting, where a rider had been sitting a grey horse with a space before him and a space behind.

He was still there.

He was outside the stone's ten yards, a little way up the road from it, perhaps fifteen. The flash had gone off forty yards from him and every horse on that bank had gone mad at it, and his grey had not. It stood where it had stood on the road with its feet square and its head level, as if it had been told to. Loose horses went past it up the cutting in a stream on both sides, close enough to touch, and it did not so much as turn an ear. The man on it sat as he had sat coming down the cutting, easily, with the reins in his left hand and his right hand closed on his thigh, and his hood up, and his face turned toward the river.

He did not run. Everybody else on that bank who could run was running, or swimming, or roaring; and he sat on his grey in the dark on the king's road and did not do anything at all.

"Sixteen," said Ward. His voice had not changed. His note had. Beside Emeric in the reeds it had gone so still that it was hardly a note at all, only a wall with nobody behind it.

"Seventeen."

The man on the grey put his hood back.

Emeric did not see his face. There was no light left to see it by. He saw the hood go back, and a pale shape where a head was, turned toward the river, toward the reeds, toward the stage; not looking for anything, it seemed to him afterward, so much as listening, the way a man listens for a step he expects.

"Eighteen."

And then something opened over the bank.

It came through the mesh. Nothing came through the mesh; the mesh was a wall between a man and a field, he had lain under it for an hour with the world in a blanket; and this came through it. Not a sound. Not a light. Not anything that a man could have pointed to on that road in the dark. It was in the air over the hard and the stage and the reeds and the river, all at once, everywhere, the way the pressure comes into the ears on the stair up into the Band, but not in the ears: in the chest, behind the breastbone, in the roots of the teeth, in the bare hand lying in the mud that would not close. A heaviness, as if a door had been opened onto somewhere very deep and the air of that place was coming out into this one. A reaching. Thin and fine and enormous and patient, going out over the bank from the man on the grey horse in every direction at once, the way the sense went out, the way his own sense went out on a good night, only it was not his, and it was not anything like his, and it did not stop at the room or the house or the river. It kept going.

He had never felt it in his life. Ward had; he knew that from Ward's note, which had done a great many things beside him over the years, and never this.

Beside him in the reeds, Ward had stopped counting.
