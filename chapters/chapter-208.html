Chapter 208 - The Last Draw

Ilsa brought the count up from the old weigh house on the first Monday of the winter, as she brought it on the first Monday of every month, with the damp of her room still on the paper.

She had walked it the week before, every lane on the island, with last month's list in one hand and a pencil in the other, the way she had walked it every month for years. She knocked on doors. She asked who slept there. She wrote it down. It took her six days, and on the seventh she sat in her room over the weigh house with the lists spread on the floor and added them, twice, and on the eighth she climbed the keep stair and put the figure on Ferra's table.

Ferra read it, and then read the one under it, which was the second quarter's, kept by the ganger of each street and copied fair by Hobb.

The island stood at two thousand three hundred and some. It had been two thousand six hundred at the summer count before last, the summer the wall closed. It had gone down by a street at a time since, on the draws alone: the fourth draw in the late summer, into the first quarter's new streets under the second tower, and the fifth, a month early, in the autumn, into the second quarter. Whole streets had gone. Their neighbours had gone with them, the same neighbour on the left and the same on the right, because that was how Ilsa drew them.

The second quarter stood at a little under two thousand, and full. Its first streets were stone, hand-laid by Hewitt's gangs. Its later streets were the banded grey of the Walker, with Hewitt's dressed corners standing out pale at the end of every house. The two kinds stood side by side along the same ring road, and the families in them had stopped remarking on it by the second week.

And the camp on the foundry bank, which Ferra had not been asked to count but counted anyway, had grown again since the autumn. It always grew. Every month there were men in it from Milford and Wayford and Grayrock and places further off, come for the wage and the rail, and some of them had sent for their families, and the families were in the lean-tos.

She laid the two figures side by side on the table, the island's and the second quarter's, and looked at them, and did not like what they said, which was that the island was emptying already and had been for a year, and that nobody at the long-room table had yet decided anything about it.

She took them to the table that evening. She said them plainly, as she said any figure, and then said the thing under them, because it was hers to say and somebody had to.

"The island's going," she said. "Not because anybody's told it to. Because every street that goes takes the next street's reason to stay. The draws are doing it on their own. At this rate the island's half empty in two years, and we'll have got there without once saying at this table that we meant to." She put her hand on the island's figure. "The question isn't whether. It's what it empties into."

Nobody answered at once. Then Saban said, "And where the camp goes," and she wrote that on the edge of her sheet, because it was the other half of the same sum.

"The second quarter's full," Ferra said. "The island's two thousand three hundred and falling, and the camp's more every month. There has to be a third quarter. The drawing always had one. It has to be built, now, not when there's time."

"What limits it?" Emeric said.

He meant the pace. Ferra knew he did. A year ago she would have said masons, and given Hewitt's count, and it would have been true; every job on the flat had wanted dressed stone at once, and there had not been men enough in four baronies to dress it. It was not true now. The Walker stood in the second quarter under its canvas for the winter, where the frost had stopped it, and in the spring it would walk again, and the rail works had been asked in the autumn whether it could build a second. Hewitt's men were in the yard all winter dressing quoins, heads and sills for the spring, a winter's stock of corners for a summer's walls.

"Boats," Ferra said.

Emeric looked at her.

"The Walker's skin is the grey. The dock's is the grey. Both of them are lime off the blue stone and the mountain's ash, and both of them come the same way, in the sulphur boats' ballast, from Emberfell, when the sulphur boats come." She turned a page. "Hewitt can dress a winter's quoins. The rail works can build a second frame. The camp can find us men. None of that puts one more barrow of the grey on the quay. The Walker ate it faster than the boats brought it every week it ran, and the dock ate the rest, and in the spring there'll be two Walkers." She laid her pen down. "The limit's the sea lane. It's how many boats Rowena's harbour sends, and how much blue stone the mountain can burn, and whether the weather lets them come."

Emeric did not answer. He took out the small book he carried, the one with the two columns in it, and wrote in the second column, one line. Ferra was close enough to see it. Burn it here.

Nobody asked. Bram, across the table, looked at the book and then at nothing.

"The island becomes the school," Emeric said.

He said it the way he said the things he had already decided and wanted argued with: not loudly, and looking round the table while he said it, one face at a time.

"Orrin's school is in the old exchange already. I looked at that building my first week here and thought it was made for reading in, and it was. Armand's forty have a founding stone on the flat and nothing on it. Imani teaches her students in the ward, on the island. The printing is on the island. The pattern store is on the island. The works are across the water from it, a bridge away." He opened his hands on the table. "The learning's there already. It's in the buildings nobody's going to leave empty on purpose, and the ones that are going to be empty anyway. Let it have the whole island."

Then he took a sheet out from under the day book, rolled, and unrolled it on the table, and put the inkstand on one corner and his hand on the other.

It was the island. Ferra knew it at once by its shape, the long lozenge between the two branches with the keep at its head and the Left Bridge off its western side. But it was not the island she walked every day. The lanes were not lanes. The lanes behind the market were long halls, with high windows drawn in along their sides, and the market square was a green, and the tannery lane where the fire had been was a row of something with glass roofs. On the east side, where there had only ever been the wall and the water, there were two bridges, going over to the foundry bank, and the works on the far bank were drawn in as if they were part of the same thing, with more glass roofs, and a long building by the water with a wheel. The keep was the keep. The church was the church, and the churchyard by the east wall, and the old exchange, drawn exactly as they were, the only things on the sheet that were. Everything else was a place that did not exist.

"Twenty years," Emeric said. "Maybe more. Not now. A school for every age. Children who read. Children with the gift. Whoever passes the paper, from anywhere. Imani's physicians. Bram's journeymen. And a place to build whatever they learn to build, on the far bank, where the works already are."

Nobody at the table had ever seen a drawing of a town that did not exist, except the one on the long-room wall of New Kaldo. They looked at it for some time.

Armand saw it before Emeric had finished. Ferra watched him do it.

He had leaned forward over his stick when the sheet came out, to see it better, and he had stopped leaning and gone quite still halfway through Emeric saying children with the gift. He was looking at the keep. Then at the water drawn round the island on both sides, and the one bridge on the west, and the new bridges on the east. Then back at the keep.

"An island," he said. "One bridge, or three. A keep at the head of it. Mages in the keep, and children with the gift in the halls below it, taught from printed books by masters who measure them twice." He did not look up. "They will call it a tower."

"It is a tower," Emeric said.

Armand did not say yes. He sat back in his chair and put both hands on the head of his stick and looked at the drawing from further away, the way he had looked at the drawing of the six-sided columns in the autumn: as something that might be pretty, and might be a great deal more, and that he had been alive long enough not to decide about in an evening.

He had come south with a letter he could not write the middle of. He had brought the Circle's own mages south after him, one cart at a time, on the strength of it, and they were on the flat now, raising a wall. Everybody at the table knew what the tower in the north would call a second tower in the south, with its own books and its own measures and the Circle's own mages in it. Nobody said it. Armand least of all.

"I shall want to think about it," he said, and that was all.

Ferra looked at the drawing for a long time. She looked at it long enough that the others stopped looking at it and started looking at her, and she let them.

"There's nobody in it," she said.

Emeric waited. He knew her well enough to know there was more, and to know she would say it when she had it.

"I don't object to the school," she said. "I think it's right. I think it's the best thing anybody's drawn at this table, and I've costed most of them." She put her finger on the paper, not on any building; on the empty space in the margin, where he had written, in his small hand, the levers. No re-letting of barony houses. Same rent or less in New Kaldo. Owners bought at a fair price. Whole streets together. The market, the church, the work. Her finger was on the first line.

"This," she said. "'We stop re-letting.'"

"The plague houses."

"The barony's houses. All of them on the island that the barony took in the plague years when nobody was left to take them, and has let since at a Copper a week to whoever came." She did not need the book for it. "When one empties, we don't let it again. That's all the line says. It's a small line."

"It is."

"Then I'll tell you what it means at the far end," Ferra said. "Not this year. Three years from now. Four." She looked at the drawing and not at him. "Every house that empties and isn't let again is a family the baker doesn't sell to. One a week, then two. The baker's still there. He's still baking. He's baking for fewer. The chandler on the corner shuts first, because a chandler can't live on half a street, and then there's no lamp oil in that lane, and the families left in it walk to the market street for it, and one day the market street isn't there either, because the market's on the flat. The watch walks the lanes still, but there are fewer lanes worth walking. The well's still there, but nobody's mended the rope. And the last families on the island live in a half-dark town, with the lights out on either side of them, in houses nobody around them is living in. Nobody told them to go." She lifted her finger off the paper. "They go anyway. Because staying got worse, a Copper at a time, every year, until it was worse than going."

Nobody said anything.

"You won't have put anybody out," Ferra said. "You'll never have to. Nobody will ever be able to stand at the cross and say the prince put them out of their house. You'll have let it get bad until they left." She looked at him at last. "That's the same thing. With a longer road."

He did not argue. She had thought he might. She had thought he might say the island was emptying anyway, which was true, and that she had said so herself an hour ago, which was also true, and that a school would bring life to it that a half-empty town never could, which was true as well. He had all of those, and she could see him have them, and he did not say any of them.

He took a clean sheet off the pile at his elbow, and dipped his pen, and began to write.

He wrote it in his own hand, which he did for very few things now. He wrote the day book's business and the drawings and his notebook in his own hand, and everything else went to the clerks. Ferra had seen him write in his own hand for a promise once before, years ago, in the ward, with Imani standing over him with her arms folded, when she had made him put the date the hospital would be built on a sheet of paper and sign it and give it to her, and she had kept it in her apron for a year and brought it out at this table on the day it came due.

He wrote:

The well on the market street, the gas lamps on the market street, the watch on the island's lanes and its gate, and the ward, stay open, lit, walked and kept, until the last family on the island has gone of its own will or has died in its own bed.

While there are fewer than a hundred households on the island, the barony pays the baker on the market street whatever it costs him to keep his oven lit and his door open.

Any man or woman paying a Copper a week for a barony house on the island pays a Copper a week for a barony house in New Kaldo, whatever that house would let for, for as long as they live in it.

He signed it. He turned it round and pushed it across the table to her.

Ferra read it twice. Then she took it, and turned to a clean page in her own book, and priced it.

The well and the lamps and the watch she could price in her head; the barony paid for them already, and would go on paying the same whether the island held two thousand or two. The ward was Imani's and was not going anywhere. The baker she priced against his flour and his wood and his rent and a year of a hundred households, and then fifty, and then twenty, and wrote the figure with a ring round it, because she did not know yet how long the last twenty would stay.

The rent was not small. She had known it would not be. A new house in the second quarter, stone or grey, with a tap and a shower room and a drain and a school at the end of the street, would let on the flat at four Copper a week, perhaps five, when the quarter was full and people were asking. Every family that came off a barony plague house at a Copper a week would pay a Copper a week for it, for as long as they lived in it. Every one of those houses. Three Copper, four, the week, each, that the barony would not have, for twenty years, thirty, as long as anybody in those families lived.

She wrote the figure. She looked at it for some time. It was the largest single promise she had ever written against the barony's income that was not a debt.

She wrote it anyway. Under it she wrote the date, and E.'s hand. Signed. And then, because it was true and she thought somebody ought to write it down, one line more.

Houses are not why anybody stays on the island now.

It was not a thing she had been asked to write. It was the answer to the question she had asked at the start, about what limited the pace, and it was not the same answer she had given. The Walker could put up a street by the summer. The rent was the rent. Whoever stayed on the island after this would stay because they wanted to, and whoever went would go the same way, at whatever pace they chose, and no frame on rails in the second quarter would make that pace one day faster. She thought that was right. She thought it was the only thing on the page that was entirely right.

The notice was printed on the Thursday, three hundred copies, on the good paper.

It said what the sheet said, in plain words, in large letters, with a picture across the top of it for the people who could not read, which was fewer than it had been but still a great many. The picture was Orrin's idea and a girl in the finishing room had cut the plate. On the left, the island, with its keep and its wall and the water round it. An arrow. On the right, a street of new houses, with smoke from every chimney and a tap at the corner. And under the arrow, back on the island, small, the old exchange, with a door, and at the door, going in, three children.

Emeric read it at the market cross himself on the Saturday, at noon, when the market was fullest.

He had read things at that cross before. He had read the ditch there, the day the town learned where its dead had been coming from and what he meant to do about it; he had stood on the cross's step and read it aloud off the sheet in front of everybody, and let them say what they thought of it to his face for most of an afternoon, and answered every question and nothing that was not a question. He did the same now. He stood on the step with the notice in his hand and Ward behind him and read it, all of it, slowly, in the voice he used at the cross, which was not the voice he used at the table.

Ferra stood at the side of the square by the market hall with her book, where she had stood for the ditch.

The square listened. It was cold, and the market was full, and people had come for bread and cloth and eggs and not for a prince, and they listened anyway, because it was the island and their houses and they had known since the fourth draw that something was going to be said. When he read the part about the well and the lamps and the watch, an old man by the fish stall said, loudly, "Till we're dead, is it," and Emeric said, "Till you're dead, if you stay," and the old man said, "Well, that's fair," and some of the square laughed. When he read the part about the Copper a week, there was a noise from the back of the square that was not quite a word, a sort of long breath going out of a great many people at once. Ferra wrote the noise down, as well as she could. She did not have a word for it either.

When he read the part about the school, nobody made any noise at all. They looked at the picture on the notices in their hands, at the three children going in at the old exchange's door.

He finished. He did not make a speech after it. He never did. He stood on the step with the notice in his hand and waited, the way he had waited for the ditch.

Somebody at the back of the square, behind the fish stall, a woman's voice, not angry, only wanting to know, called out over the heads.

"And if we don't?"

The square went quiet again. Emeric looked for the voice, and could not find it, and answered the square instead.

"Then you don't," he said.

He stood on the step a moment longer. Nobody asked anything else. After a while the market noise came back, slowly, a stall at a time, as people remembered what they had come for, and he came down off the step and Ward came down behind him, and they walked back through the square to the keep, and people let them through, and looked at them going, and went back to their bread.

Ferra wrote both lines in the book, under the date. The woman's question, and his answer. She wrote them exactly, because they were exactly what had been said, and because she thought that in twenty years somebody would come to this book wanting to know what the island had been promised, and should find it, and find it in the island's own voice.

And if we don't?

Then you don't.

She closed the book, and stood for a moment longer by the market hall, looking at the cross and the step and the square filling up again with people who had come to buy eggs; and then she went back to the keep to price a baker.
