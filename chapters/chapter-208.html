Chapter 208 - The Cellar

The door was behind the rags.

The north end of the Staple house stood on three bays of old vaulted cellar, the oldest part of the building, older than the charter, and the five had filled all three with the trade: bales of rag, pressed and corded and stacked to the curve of the vault, waiting to go down the hill to the vats, with a narrow way left between the stacks for a man with a lamp. It smelled of old linen and damp and mice. Declan's men went down the narrow ways with lanterns, tapping the walls with the butts of their swords, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find by tapping.

Emeric found it with his hand.

He walked the back wall of the third bay with his palm flat on the stone and his sense pushed down through it, and felt the stillness come closer under his feet, step by step, the way warmth comes closer to a hand moving along a wall toward a hidden flue; and at the end of the bay, behind a stack of bales that reached the vault, it was nearest. He stood there with his hand on the stone. Ward stood behind him and did not ask.

"Move these," said Emeric.

Osric and two of the column moved them. It took a quarter of an hour; the bales were pressed hard and heavy as men, and they had to be carried down the narrow way one at a time and stacked again in the next bay. Behind them was an arch, bricked up. Old work, to look at: the bricks the same dark red as the rest of the cellar, the same soot on them. But the mortar between them was pale. New. Ten years, perhaps. Twenty. And when Ward put his ear to it and rapped it with his knuckle, it did not sound like a wall.

It was a door. The five had mortared a facing of brick onto a door of iron, so that the door was a wall, and had hung it on hinges sunk in the arch's own stone where no lamp would ever find them; and one brick at the height of a man's hand, near the left side, was loose. Ward pulled it out. Behind it was a keyhole.

The keys were on Marrow's belt. Rylan's man brought them from the bench in the passage, where Marrow had not moved; and the third key on the ring went into the lock as if it had been doing it every night for twenty years, which it had.

The door swung in on its sunk hinges without a sound. Somebody kept them oiled.



The smell came up the stair before they went down it.

He knew it. He had met it once before, at the bottom of another stair in another city, with two leather buckets of water in his hands and a canvas apron on: tallow and wet stone, and under both of them the thing he had not been able to name until he was nearly at the bottom, and then could. The smell of bodies working harder than bodies can. The ward at Kaldo in the first week after the war. The rite room under the Circle, on a winter night, with nineteen men on the floor.

And lime.

The stair went down a long way. Stone, and old, and worn hollow in the middle of every step, the way the Circle's stair had been worn, by a great many feet going down and fewer coming up. At the bottom there was a short passage, and a door on the left standing half open with light in it; and in the light, three men.

They were sitting on the floor of a little guardroom with their backs against the far wall, where they had been sitting, Emeric understood, since the flash went off upstairs and the house began to shout: three ordinary men in leather aprons, with a lamp on a table and a jug and a game of something with pegs half played, and a cudgel on the floor in front of the nearest that he did not reach for. They looked at the black suits in the doorway with the faces of men who have been waiting all night to be found and have imagined, in the waiting, a great many worse things than what had come.

Emeric did not look at them. He did not trust what his face would do if he looked at them. He went on down the passage, past the guardroom door, to the end, where there was another door, open, and a room.

Ward went into the guardroom behind him. He heard Ward say something flat and short, and the cudgel go skittering across the floor, and nothing else.



The room had no windows.

It had a door, and a drain in the middle of the floor, and lamps on the walls. Some of the lamps were light stones in iron cages, the kind the rich used, and gave a cold white light; some were tallow, in brass saucers, and smoked. It was a long room with a low vaulted ceiling, longer than the long panelled room above it, and it was very clean. Somebody scrubbed this floor. He could see the marks of the brush on the flags, in long arcs, the way a woman scrubs a kitchen.

In the middle of the floor, round the drain, a ring had been painted on the stone. White, with a black edge, a yard wide and twelve across, and at eight places round it, evenly spaced, a mark: a sign he did not know. He knew the number. He had stood against a wall in the rite room under the Circle with a ladle, on a winter night, and counted the mages who did the work, four at the heads of the rows and four against the wall behind them, waiting their turn; and there had been eight. Here there were no mages. At each one there was an iron stand the height of a man's waist, and on each stand, in a cradle of iron, a stone. Eight stones round a ring, where eight men should have stood.

Along the left wall, in two rows of four, there were chairs.

Oak. Heavy, high-backed, plain, like the chairs in a magistrate's court, bolted to the floor through their feet. Each one had straps of iron and leather at the wrists and the ankles and across the chest, and at the top of the back, where a man's neck would rest against it if he were strapped in and let his head fall back, an iron cradle, shaped to the curve of a neck, and in each cradle a stone. They were not glowing. They were not doing anything. Seven of them.

The eighth was.

Along the right wall there were racks. Shelves of oak from floor to vault, the whole length of the room, like the racks in a wine merchant's cellar; and on every shelf, in a little hollow cut to hold it, a stone. Hundreds of them. Small and large, grey and blue and clouded; every one charged. He could see it with his eyes in the light stones' cold glow, a faint light in each, like a coal under ash. With his sense he could see it the way he would see a city at night from the ship: hundreds of small hard brilliant points of light in rows, so bright together that they hurt behind the eyes.

At the head of the room, where in the capital the bishop had sat in his carved chair with his book open on his knees, there was a carved chair. A good one, walnut, with arms that ended in lions' paws, and a cushion of red velvet on the seat, and a footstool, and beside it a little table with a cup on it, and a decanter, and a book.

And through a low arch at the far end, past the ring, another room, darker. He could see the edge of straw on its floor in the light from this one. Pallets. And beyond that, at the very back, a low door of planks with a bar across it, from under which came the smell of lime.

He stood in the doorway and spread his sense into the room.

Nothing came back.

That was what he would remember, afterward, when everything else about that night had worn down into something he could hold. Not the chairs. Not the ring. The nothing. He pushed his sense into that room and it went out from him through the cold white light and the smoke of the tallow, and it found the racks, blazing, and the eight stones round the ring, and the seven in the cradles; and past them, through the low arch, in the dark room with the straw, it found nothing at all. A dead place. A place with the feel of a dead zone in it, the dead flat silence he had felt on the bridge in the war and again on the north road last November, a crack and a wind that moved nothing, and after it the world gone dull and blank to the edge of his sense.

Except that it was not a dead zone. The racks were blazing. The stones were full. The air of the room was thick with mana, rich with it, the way the air of the ward smelled of carbolic. He could have filled his own well from that air standing where he stood.

All the mana in that room was in the stones.

None of it was in the people.

He had felt them from the passage above, faintly, a dozen and more, so thin he had taken them from the ship for nothing. He felt them now from twenty feet, with nothing between, and they were not much more. Notes so worn and so faint that they hardly rose above the stone they lay on. The notes of people who had been emptied every morning for years, and had been let fill again overnight only so far as would keep them alive to be emptied again in the morning. Like the bottom of a well that has been drawn every day for years. Damp, and no more.

And the eighth chair.



There was a girl in it.

He did not see her at first, because she was so small in the chair that the chair hid her: the last chair in the second row, at the end nearest the arch, with its high back to the door. He saw the stone first. The stone in the cradle at the top of the chair's back was glowing, faintly, a slow uneven pulse of light, and with his sense he felt it pull. The way a mouth pulls on a straw. Slow, and steady, and patient, and not stopping.

He went round the chair.

Ten, he thought, or a small twelve; it was hard to tell, because the person in the chair did not look like any age. She was strapped at the wrists and the ankles and across the chest with the straps at their last holes, and they were still loose on her; her head lay back against the iron cradle with the stone at the nape of her neck, and her hair, which had been dark once, was grey at the roots, and her face was the colour of the wall. Her eyes were half open. She was breathing. He could see her breathe, just. He could feel her, just: a note so thin it was barely a thread, going out through the back of her neck into the stone.

Somebody had put her in the chair at midnight, for the night's yield, and strapped her, and set the stone; and then the flash had gone off upstairs, and the shouting had started, and the three men in leather aprons had gone into their guardroom and sat down against the wall and waited to be found. And nobody had come back to take her out.

The stone had gone on pulling. It did not know about flashes. It had been cut to pull, and it pulled.

He put his hand on the strap at her wrist.

She moved. Her head came forward off the cradle a little way, the way a sleeper's head moves when a door opens, and her half-open eyes found him, a black helmetless shape in a black suit standing over her chair; and he saw her see him, and decide what he was, from the only things she knew.

"I'm on forty-one," she said.

Her voice was a whisper with nothing under it. She said it carefully, the way a child says a lesson she has been made to learn.

"I know I'm slow." She looked at him. "I can do more. I can do more tonight. Please don't write me slow. Please."

He undid the strap at her wrist. His hands did it. He watched them do it from a long way off, and did not trust them, and they were steady, and the buckle came free; and then the other wrist, and the ankles, and the one across her chest, which was the hardest, because she flinched from his hand when it came near the buckle there, as if she knew exactly what hands near that strap meant. Then he reached behind her head to the cradle.

The stone was warm.

It came out of its cradle into his palm warm and heavy and full, the way a stone is when it has just been filled, and he knew from whom, because there was nobody else in that chair. Everything that had been going out of the back of her neck was in his hand now, every bit the stone had taken since midnight, packed into a thing the size of a hen's egg, humming faintly against his palm like a held bird.

The girl in the chair breathed out, a long breath, as the pull stopped. Then she looked at the stone in his hand. Then she looked at him again, and her face did something small and terrible, which was to be afraid that she had done something wrong.

"Is it the cart?" she said.

He did not answer. He could not have. He knelt down on the scrubbed floor in front of the chair, because his knees had decided it for him, and put his free hand very carefully round one of hers on the arm of the chair, the way he would have put it round a bird; and her hand was so cold. It was the coldest living thing he had ever touched.

He looked past her, through the low arch, into the dark room with the straw.

They were lying on the pallets in two rows, under thin grey blankets, the way the nineteen had lain in the capital: on their backs, with their arms at their sides, very still, the way people lie still when they have been told to and are frightened of what happens if they don't. Fourteen of them. He counted, because he could not stop himself. Men, most of them, young, and white-haired, all of them, white or grey, the way the girl was going grey at the roots; and among the men, women; and at the end of the second row, against the wall, a smaller shape under a blanket that was not a man, or a woman. A boy. On the wall above the boy's pallet, scratched into the stone with something, a nail or a buckle, were marks. Tallies. Rows of them, in fives, four strokes and a bar, the way Emeric tallied, the way he had once shown Ferra how to tally over a book nobody could read; going along the wall from the pallet to the corner, row under row, and round the corner, and on.

Somebody had been counting days.



He knew what it was.

He did not need the ledger in his coat, the one Ward had taken unburned out of Marrow's arms. He did not need Strade's tallies or Ottoline at the lamp reading them to him, or Ferra's columns, or the five marks in Sethe's margin. He knelt on the floor of that room with a girl's hand in his and her life in his other palm, warm, and the whole of it came to him at once, every piece, the way the whole of a machine comes to you when you have been staring at its parts for weeks and suddenly see the shaft that runs through them all.

Held against rent. A debt made on paper, and a man taken for it, by the old law, with a magistrate's seal; and his wife, and his children, because the old law did not say a man's household could not be held with him, and nobody had ever thought to make it say so. A letter home in a clerk's hand: he has gone to work for the Staple, he is well, he sends his love. Brought here, down the stair worn hollow in the middle, and laid on straw.

Intake.

And every morning, the chairs. The straps, and the cradle, and the stone at the neck, and the slow patient pull, until the stone was full and the person in the chair was empty, emptier than any person should ever be, emptier than a body is built to go; and then back to the straw, to fill again overnight, as far as a body fills, and in the morning, the chairs.

Yield.

And the stones, full, up the stair and into a small closed cart with two horses and a lame man driving, across the square at two in the morning, once a week, south, to be sold to anybody who would buy a full stone and not ask where its charge came from, which was everybody. A charge is a charge.

And a third of them kept. Kept for the carved chair at the head of the room, with the red velvet cushion, and the footstool, and the cup and the decanter on the little table; where one of the five would come down the stair at night, Placit with his close white beard or Hollin with his soft jaw, and sit, and take a stone from the rack in each hand, and draw it into himself, slowly, as a man drinks good wine, the charge of a stranger's emptied body going into an old man's veins and making them younger. Placit was seventy. Emeric had dined across a table from him in May and looked at the straight back and the pale clear eyes and thought: good bones, for seventy.

Kept.

And the ring. Eight stones round a painted circle where eight mages should have stood. A rite. Somebody had sold them a rite, the knight's rite of the Circle, the book of it, and they had tried it, down here, on the youngest of their held men, with stones in the casters' places, because a knight was worth a great deal more than a stone and a knight that you held was worth more than anything; and it had never once worked, because stones could not do what eight men did through a long night in shifts, and they had gone on trying.

And the low door with the bar across it, and the smell of lime.

Losses.

And the thing that was worst, that he had not seen until he knelt there with it in front of him, though it had been in front of him for months: that it was his. Not the chairs. The idea. A body fed mana slowly, every day, a little at a time, grows to hold more. Slow induction. He had found it in his first years in Kaldo by watching his own people live beside his own stones, and it had been the thing he was proudest of in all this world: that a commoner could grow, gently, without the rite, without the Circle, without anyone's leave, just by living in a town where the stones were. That a whole barony could rise. He had built a town on it. And the other face of it was here. Draw a body empty every morning and let it fill every night, and the body grows to hold more, the same way, faster; and the more it holds, the more you can draw. Strade's Yield had risen every year, a little, in its neat upright figures, and Ferra had added it and not known what it meant. It meant that the people on the straw had grown. That they had been grown. On purpose. Like a crop.

His own best idea, built here out of human beings, in a scrubbed room under a house on his own road, for twenty years, while he went to dinner upstairs.



He had thought he knew this world.

That was the thing. He had thought he had learned it. He had come into it eight years ago in a body that had drunk itself half to death and had looked at it with an engineer's eyes, and seen how much of it was hard and cruel and stupid, and had set about making a corner of it less so; and somewhere along the way he had thought he had made his peace with the dark parts. He had stood against the wall of the rite room in the capital with a ladle and watched men break in the night, and gone home, and slept. He had killed. He had killed men with fire across a river and over a wall, in the war, and shot a knight in the back at five paces through the joint of his plate; and he had killed a man in the hold of a boat with his legs, with his hands tied behind him, and felt him stop. Always because they were trying to kill him or somebody he would not let them kill, always with a reason he could write down afterward and read and believe. He had thought that was what it was to live here. That you learned the cold, the way you learn cold water, by getting in and staying in until your body stops telling you it is cold.

He had not learned it.

He knelt on the scrubbed floor with the girl's hand in his and understood that he had not learned anything at all. That he had only never been shown.

It came up in him slowly at first. He felt it come. It came up from somewhere below his stomach, low, the way the heat comes up through the floor of a forge when the fire under it has been banked all night and somebody opens the draught; a heat that was not his and was entirely his, that rose, and filled him, and did not stop at the top of him but kept on rising. And with it a clearness. Everything in the room very clear, very sharp, as if somebody had cleaned a window. The brush marks on the flags. A small shoe, a child's, worn through at the toe, lying on its side by the drain. A doll on the boy's pallet, made of rag, of the Staple's own rag, a twist of grey linen with a knot for a head. The cup on the little table by the carved chair, with a dark ring of wine dried in the bottom of it. Somebody had sat in that chair tonight, before the raid, and had a glass of wine, and drawn a stone, and gone upstairs to burn the books.

His jaw was shut.

He noticed it the way he noticed the shoe, from a long way off: shut the way a vice shuts, his back teeth meeting with a weight he could hear inside his own skull, a small fine creaking in the enamel like ice on a pond in the first hour of a frost. He knew that sound. He knew, with the part of him that still stood a long way off and noticed things, exactly how much more it would take. One notch. If he let go of whatever was holding him one notch further, he would bite down through his own teeth, and feel them go, and not care.

He held on to that. He held on to it as if it were the only thing in the room. His teeth, and the creak in them, and the girl's cold hand.

It was not enough.

Because it was not only the five. That was what was coming up in him now, past his teeth, past everything: it was not only five old men with good bones. It was the charter on the wall upstairs in its frame, with its great red seal, signed by a king who had never asked what it was for, and renewed by every king since, and paid for with a tenth on the quarter days that the crown's clerks counted and were glad of. It was the magistrate who dined with Placit and signed whatever was put in front of him. It was the law that let a man be held against a debt, and did not say his children could not. It was the Church, that had spent a thousand years making sure no commoner ever learned to cut a sign on a stone and push his own mana into it, so that when five old men came down a stair with straps, the people on the straw had nothing in their hands at all. It was a world built, from the top to the bottom, on the understanding that the people at the bottom of it were stuff. Rag, to be pressed and corded and weighed at the junction. Mana, to be drawn and sold and drunk.

And it was him. Four years he had held Wayford. Four years of Aster's narrow hand every week on his table, barns and boats, true to the line. He had paid at the chain in April and smiled while he paid. He had dined in that long panelled room upstairs in a gold-laced coat with a cup in his hand, asking for a share. A share. He had sat at Placit's table drinking water and laughing too loudly while, under his feet, under the floor, under the rag, a girl lay on straw and counted the strokes on a wall.

He had built a whole town to stand on. And he had never once looked down.



The lamps went first.

He saw it happen and did not do it. Every tallow flame in the room stood up at once, straight, the way a flame stands up when a door is shut on a draught; and then grew. A finger tall. A hand. The length of a forearm, white at the root and yellow at the tip, roaring softly, and the brass saucers under them began to glow dull red. The light stones in their cages did not grow, because they could not; they flared, all together, white, so bright that the room had no shadows in it at all, and one of them cracked across with a sound like a knuckle.

The air in the room went hot, the way the air of the forge cellar went hot when the lattice was at a white heat and Ferra took off her jacket. He felt it on his face. He felt the sweat start on the walls, on the old stone, running down in threads into the brush marks on the floor. The iron straps on the empty chairs began to smoke where the leather met them.

And on the walls, round him, in the white light, he saw them come.

Glyphs. Not one. Dozens. Writing themselves on the air an inch from the stone all round the room, faint and then less faint, red-gold and violet, the fire's sign and the weight's sign over and over, half-drawn, broken, drawing themselves and unwriting themselves and drawing themselves again, the way a man's hand writes the same word over and over in the margin of a page when he is not thinking about his hand. He was not casting. He was not doing anything. They were coming out of him the way the heat was, and the light; the way sweat comes out of a body that has been asked to do too much.

Behind him, in the doorway, he heard Ward say his name.

Then the weight came.

It came down on the room all at once, like the lid of a box. He felt it go out of him, down through his knees into the floor and out through the stone in every direction, a great plain downward pull that did not choose and did not care. Ward went down onto one knee in the doorway with a sound of plate on stone. In the guardroom down the passage the three men in their leather aprons went flat. The girl in the chair beside him made a small sound and her head went back against the cradle. Dust came down out of the vault in a sheet, all at once, as if it had been poured.

And it went on going out. Up. Through the cellars and the rag bays and the floor of the passage where Marrow lay on his bench, and into the house; he felt it go, and could not call it back, the way you cannot call back a shout. In the long panelled room upstairs, twenty people who had been sitting on chairs under guard went down onto the boards together with a single dull sound that he heard through two floors, the five among them, Placit's cup going out of his hand and across the floor. In the yard, the horses screamed and fell. Declan's men went to their knees in the dirt with their swords in their hands. In the stable, Rylan went down on top of Garrin in the straw, and held him there, because Rylan held on to things.

It did not stay still. That was the worst of it, afterward, for the people who were in it. It came and went with his heart. With every beat of it, the weight came down harder, and let up, and came down; and the house above him began to shake. Not the way a house shakes in a wind. The way the ground shakes when something very heavy is being dropped on it again and again from a great height. Earth had never been his. Earth was Paskin's. This was only weight, his weight, pounding on the ground with every beat of his heart, and the ground answered it the only way ground can. The flags of the cellar floor cracked across under the ring, a long black line through the white paint. Plaster came down upstairs in sheets. A chimney stack on the north end of the roof shook itself loose of its flashing and leaned. Across the yard the stable's shutters burst open, one after another, bang and bang and bang, in time.

And under it, through it, the other thing, that had no element and no name.

His mana was going out of him. Not as weight, not as heat; plain, untold, the way he had once pushed it out into a jar of glass to see what it would do. It went out of him into the room and the house and the yard like a wind. A wind with no air in it, that stirred nothing and moved no dust and that every person in that house with any faculty at all felt on his face like a gale. The racks along the wall began to hum. The stones in them that were not full brightened, one after another, drinking it, until they were full and could not drink any more; and the full ones sang, very faintly, a high thin note on the edge of hearing, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass.

And the door.

He felt it open. The room, his room, the long quiet place with its light that came from nowhere, behind his eyes, in the stone on his wrist. It was not his to open now; it opened. It swung wide, all the way, wider than it had ever opened, and it reached. Out along the gale of his own mana, the way the hair-width door reached when he held it open on a note he knew, except that now it did not hold to one note; it went out to all of them. Every note his mana touched, it touched. Ward's, kneeling. Rylan's, in the straw. Declan's and Osric's and the forty in the yard. The three in the guardroom. The five upstairs, on their faces on the boards. Marrow's, torn, on his bench. Garrin's. The fourteen on the straw, so thin. The girl's. It reached for all of them at once, without asking, without contact, without consent, a door the size of a house trying to take a house into itself.

And it hurt them. He felt it hurt them. All of them, together, the same pain, a nail driven in behind the eyes: in the yard men dropped their swords and put their hands to their heads; upstairs somebody was screaming, Hollin, high and thin, over and over; in the doorway Ward, on one knee, put the heel of his gauntlet to his forehead and made a sound through his teeth. And it hurt him. The same nail, behind his own eyes, a hundred times over, every head it touched driven back into his.

He thought it was the anger. He thought, with the small part of him that still stood a long way off and noticed things, that this was simply what anger was, when it was this large. He did not know, then, what else it might be.

Because the small part of him had noticed something else.

The fourteen on the straw were dying.

He could feel it. Under the weight, under every beat of it, they could not push back. Ward could; Ward was a knight, and his body pushed back against anything that came at it from outside, the way the house knight's had pushed back against the stone in the long room. Declan's men could, a little; they had lived for years in a town full of stones and had grown, the way his whole barony had grown. But the people on the straw had been emptied every morning for years. They had nothing to push back with. The weight came down on them with every beat of his heart and pressed them into the pallets and the stone under the pallets, and he felt their notes, already so thin, stutter under it. A man in the first row, by the arch, was not breathing in time with the others; was breathing short, short, short, like a man with a sack of flour on his chest. A woman's note in the second row flickered and came back and flickered. And the girl in the chair beside him, whose hand he was still holding, had stopped looking at him; her eyes had gone past him, to the ceiling, and her breath came very small.

He was killing them.

He had come down a stair to take them out of the dark, and he was killing them. With his own weight. With the thing he felt for them.

He tried to stop.

He tried the way you try to open a hand that is on fire and has closed on the thing that is burning it. He reached for the weight and it was not there to reach for; it was him, it was every beat of his heart. He reached for the heat and it ran through his fingers. He tried the anchor, the old one, Armand's: hold something hard enough to hurt, and come back to the hurt. He held the girl's stone in his fist so hard that its edges cut his palm, and the hurt came, and he could not come back to it; he went past it, out, into the gale, into the house, into the door. He bit down. He felt his teeth creak. He did not let them go the last notch; he held that, that one thing, and could not hold anything else.

"Emeric."

Ward. Ward was coming. He felt him through all of it, the slow even note, a wall, lit to the top; rising off his knee in the doorway the way he had risen in the long room, slowly, as if he were climbing out of deep water in his armour; and taking a step into the room. Into the heat and the weight and the gale. Another. His face, without the helm, was grey and running with sweat and there was blood coming out of his nose and he was looking at Emeric the way he had looked at him across a yard at five in the morning a thousand times, before a bout. Another step.

"Emeric. Look at me."

He could not. He could see Ward and could not look at him. Ward was a long way off across a room that was twelve feet wide, and coming very slowly, and the man by the arch was breathing short, short, short.



Something fell out of the sky into the yard.

He felt it, the way he was feeling everything, through the gale: a note, high above, three hundred feet up, where the ship was, a note he knew. Dense. Bright. A great deal of mana in a body that had been told all its life to hold a ledger and nothing else. And it was falling. Not the way a man falls off a deck. Slowly, at first, as if somebody were letting it down on a pillow of nothing; and then, as it came down into the field of his weight, not slowly at all. He felt his own weight take it and pull. He felt it fight. He felt the pillow under it go thin and break, and it hit the yard hard, and stayed down for the space of one beat of his heart, and two.

And got up.

It came across the yard. He felt it come, against the weight, the way a mage comes against weight: not like Ward, from inside, but pushing outward, round itself, a little space of not-falling that it carried with it and that cost it dearly at every step. Into the house. Down the passage, past Marrow's bench. Down through the rag bays. Down the stair, the long stair, worn hollow in the middle; slowly, on its knees, he thought, by the rhythm of it, down the steps on its knees with its hands on the stone.

Into the doorway, beside Ward.

Vantic.

Her grey was black with the yard's dirt down one side and torn at the knee, and the knee under it was bleeding through the tear. Her hair had come down. There was blood on her lip, and coming from her nose, the nail behind the eyes driven into her too, as into everyone. She was on her feet. Barely. Carrying her little space of not-falling round her like a woman carrying a lamp in a high wind, with her bare left hand out in front of her, palm down, fingers spread, quite steady, the way he had seen it once on a hill road at Grayrock, holding a frame of old timbers off a boy.

She looked at him. Once. At his face, across the room, through the white light and the heat and the gale. He did not know what she saw. She did not stop to tell him.

She looked past him, at the arch, and the straw, and the man breathing short by the arch; and she understood the whole room in one look, the way Ferra understood a page.

Then she went to the rack.

Two steps. Each of them cost her, he could feel it, more than the whole stair had. She took a stone off the nearest shelf. A big one, the size of a fist, clouded and blue, one of the full ones, singing its thin high note; filled, he knew without knowing how he knew, from one of the people on the straw. She held it in both hands against her chest. She looked at him again, over it.

And pushed.

He felt her do it. Everything she had, all of it, every drop of the mana her body had been carrying in secret all its life, the House's factor, the quiet mage, poured into a stone that was already full. A stone takes what it can hold and no more; that was the first thing anybody learned. It does not burst of its own accord. It has to be made to. She made it. Her face went white and then grey and her lips drew back from her teeth and the stone in her hands lit, from within, a blue so bright he saw the bones of her fingers through it, and the singing note went up and up past the top of hearing.

The stone cracked.

It was a small sound. He would remember that, too. After all of it, the heat and the light and the house shaking itself to pieces over their heads, it was a small sound, like a cup breaking in another room.

Then the wind.

It came out of her hands and went through the room, through him, through Ward, through the walls and the house and the yard, in a single silent rush; a wind that moved nothing. No dust stirred. No flame bent. And behind it, everywhere it had gone, nothing.



The weight stopped.

It did not lift. It was simply not there, all at once, as if it had never been, and his body, braced against it, rocked forward on his knees and caught itself. The heat stopped. The tallow flames dropped back into their saucers in the same breath, small and yellow and ordinary, guttering in the smoke. The light stones went out. All of them, together, every cold white cage on every wall, dark. The racks stopped singing. The glyphs were gone off the walls as if somebody had wiped a slate.

The gale stopped.

And the door, his door, the room behind his eyes, slammed. He felt it go, the way a door goes in a wind, cut off at the hinge, and with it every note it had been reaching for; and the nail behind his eyes stayed, but stopped being driven in.

And his sense was gone.

Since the night in the line room when Rylan sat the board and Emeric first felt the edge of a man's note through a wall, he had kept it open, thin, all day, every day, a film over the whole world; the way you keep your eyes open. He had stopped knowing it was there. He had lost it once before, on the north road last November, for half a mile of dark under a lady's coach, with men coming at him and no time to think about anything else. Here there was nothing to think about but the loss. It was not there, and the world went flat.

That was the only word for it. Flat. The room was the room: stone, and smoke, and the tallow lamps guttering, and the dark cages on the walls, and Ward on one knee in the middle of the floor with blood on his lip. He could see them. He could not feel them. He reached out of himself for the people on the straw, for their thin notes, for the girl's, and there was nothing to reach with; it was like reaching for a cup with a hand that has gone to sleep. The racks along the wall were only shelves of grey stones in the smoky light, dull, dead to him. Vantic was only a woman standing by a rack with a handful of grey dust running out between her fingers.

It was very quiet.

And he was still angry.

That was what the dead zone could not touch, and he learned it there, on his knees on the cracked floor in the dark: a dead zone stops what goes out of a man. It stops the glyph and the weight and the heat and the wind and the door. It does not stop what stays in. Ward's body was still lit, inside; he could see it in the way Ward moved, the knight's way, the weight carried evenly. And his own was still lit too. The rage had not gone anywhere. It had only lost all its doors but one, and it went where it could go, which was down, into his arms and his back and his legs, into the knight's half of him that he had kept hidden from nearly everybody he knew; and he felt it fill him to the fingertips, hot and enormous, and had nowhere to put it.

His left hand was on the arm of the girl's chair. He was holding it to get up. The arm of the chair was oak, with a strap of iron along the top of it, riveted, an inch wide and a quarter thick.

He got up. The iron bent in his hand as he did it. He felt it go, under his palm, like a strip of lead, and did not look at it.

Across the room, by the rack, with the grey dust running out between her fingers, Vantic looked at it.

He turned round. He went past Ward, who was getting up off his knee, and past Vantic, and out through the door of the room into the passage, toward the stair. He knew what was at the top of the stair. The rag bays, and the passage, and the long panelled room, and on the floor of the long panelled room, in the dark now that the light stones had gone out, twenty people, and among them five old men who had come down this stair at night and sat in a carved chair and drunk a girl's life out of a stone like wine. He knew the way. He knew exactly how many steps there were. He knew what his hands could do now, with nothing but the inside of him to do it with; what they had done to a strip of iron. He did not need weight. He did not need fire. He would go up the stair, and into that room, and he would do it with his hands, slowly, and he would not stop, and nobody in that house, nobody in this kingdom, would be able to make him.

He put his foot on the first step.

Ward was on the third.

He had not seen Ward pass him. He had not seen Ward move at all; he had been looking at the top of the stair. But Ward was there, on the third step, in the dark, between him and the top, in his black suit with his helm off and the blood on his lip, standing the way he stood in the yard at five in the morning: square, quite still, his hands empty at his sides.

"No," said Ward.

Emeric looked up at him.

"Move."

"No." Ward did not move. His voice was perfectly flat. It was the voice he used for everything, for the turn and the stair and the weather. "You will not go up there. Not tonight. Not like this."

"They sat in a chair," said Emeric. He heard his own voice and did not know it. "Down here. They sat in a chair with a cushion and drank them. Children. Ward. Children." He took the next step. "Move."

"I know what they did. I was in the room." Ward did not move. "And if you go up that stair now, you will kill five men with your hands in the dark, and it will be the worst thing anybody in this house has ever seen, and tomorrow the town will say the prince went mad in the night and murdered five old men who had a charter from the king. And they will be half right." He was quiet. "And the girl in that chair will still be in that chair. And the boy on the straw. And whatever is behind that door with the lime."

"I don't care what the town says."

"Yes," said Ward. "You do. That is why you are you."

Emeric stood on the second step with his hands open at his sides. They were shaking. Not from fear. From the size of what was in them.

"You can go through me," said Ward. Very low. "I know you can. I have known it since the yard at Emberfell. I have known it longer than that." He did not look away. "So go through me, if you are going. I will not stop you. I cannot." He paused. "Or listen to me, once, as you used to at five in the morning. You make them pay in the square. In the light. In front of every soul in Wayford, with these people's own mouths telling what was done to them, so that nobody in this kingdom can ever say it was a story. Not on this stair. Not in the dark." He was quiet. "Forward, Emeric. Not down there. Not up here. Forward."

Behind them, down the passage, one of the three men in the guardroom was talking. He had been talking for some time; Emeric heard him now. Babbling. On his knees in the guardroom door with his hands up, an ordinary man of fifty in a leather apron with his face wet, saying over and over that they would tell, Highness, they would tell all of it, everything, every name, every night, only don't, Highness, don't, in the name of God, don't.

Emeric turned his head and looked at him.

The man stopped talking.

"You'll tell it," said Emeric. "All of it. In the square. In front of everybody. Every name and every night." He heard his voice come out of him, cold now, from the bottom of something. "And you'll live. All three of you. That's my word." He looked at the man. "I'll keep it. You'll wish I hadn't."

He turned back to Ward.

He stood on the second step and looked up at the man who had stood in his doorway at five in the morning for eight years, and taught him the turn, and put his own cloak round his shoulders in the first grey light after the rite and walked him home through the empty streets of the capital, and stood on the third step of a stair in the dark with blood on his lip and his hands empty.

"They die in Wayford," said Emeric. "All five. By my order. Not by writ."

Ward looked at him for a long breath.

"Yes, Highness," he said.

He came down off the third step. He put his hand on Emeric's shoulder, once, heavily, so that Emeric felt it through the suit; and left it there for the time it took to breathe in and out; and took it away.

Emeric went back into the room.



He did not remember, afterward, deciding what to do. He remembered doing it.

He had in his head, from the other life, the whole of a medical school's knowledge of what a body needs when it has been taken further down than it can go: warmth, and water, and sugar, and air, and to be turned on its side if it cannot hold its own head, and to be watched. He had never in his life needed it so badly and had so little else to give. No stone would work in that room. No fire would light from his hand. He had a cracked floor and tallow lamps and his own two hands, which had just bent iron, and were very steady now.

He knelt by the man who had been breathing short. Older than the rest, grey to the scalp, with a face folded in on itself. He was breathing still; short, and shallow, and slowing. Emeric turned him on his side on the pallet and put his own hand flat on the man's back between the shoulder blades and felt for the breath, and counted it, as Imani counted a wrist; and when it slowed too far he pressed, gently, in time, until it came back. He did it for a long while. Declan's men came down the stair with lanterns, because Ward had sent for them, and stood in the doorway with their faces going white one after another; and Ward sent them up again for blankets, every blanket in the house, and water, and the honey from the Staple's kitchen, and every candle. They came back down with their arms full and their faces set, and did what he told them, and did not look at the chairs.

Vantic was on her knees by the girl.

She had taken the girl out of the chair. He did not see her do it. When he looked up from the man on the pallet she was sitting on the cracked floor by the chair with her back against its leg, with her torn grey skirt spread on the stone and the girl lying across her lap wrapped in her own coat, the House's dark coat with no arms on it, and her bare hand on the girl's grey hair. She was not doing anything with the hand. She could not have done anything with it, in that room, now. She was only keeping it there. She was talking, very low, to the girl, in the voice of a woman reading figures aloud to herself, and the girl was looking up at her.

The boy on the last pallet was awake. He had been awake, Emeric thought, all through it. He was lying on his side under his grey blanket with his rag doll held against his chest in both hands, and his eyes open, watching the room; watching the black suits and the lanterns and the men with their arms full of blankets, without moving, the way a hare watches from a furrow. When Emeric came to him he did not flinch. He looked up.

"Are you the cart?" said the boy.

"No."

The boy looked at him a while longer, considering it.

"Perrin said one day it'd be somebody who wasn't the cart," he said. "He said to count till it was." He looked past Emeric at the wall above his pallet, at the rows of strokes in fives going away along the stone to the corner and round it. "I counted."

Emeric looked at the wall.

"I know you did," he said.

He got the boy's blanket round him, and a second one over it, and lifted his head, which was very light, and gave him water with honey in it from a cup, a little at a time. The boy drank all of it. Then he put his head down on his doll and went to sleep at once, the way Imani slept, as if he had found the door to it in the dark.



Vantic came and stood beside him, at the end, when the man by the arch was breathing in time again and the girl was asleep in a nest of blankets on a pallet beside her mother, who had woken, and seen her, and reached out one grey hand to the girl's grey hair and not let go of it.

She stood by the boy's pallet with her coat gone and her grey torn and her knee bleeding through it, and her hair down, and her bare left hand at her side. He could not feel her at all. It was the strangest thing he had felt that night, of all of it, not to feel her: to see a woman standing beside him, so close he could have touched her sleeve, and have no more sense of what she was than of the stone wall behind her.

"You came down without a suit," he said.

"I came down without a great many things." She looked at her left hand. There was still a little grey dust in the creases of the palm, from the stone. She wiped it on what was left of her skirt. "I told Fletcher to take her up and east. Half a mile, and keep going. He argued. Vesk didn't." She was quiet. "If I'd broken it with the ship over the house she'd have come down through the roof on all of you."

"I know."

"I thought you might not, just then." She did not look at him. "You were busy."

He did not say anything.

"Ward saw," said Vantic.

"Ward saw nothing," said Ward, from the doorway, where he was standing with a lantern; and went out again, along the passage, toward the guardroom.

Vantic watched him go. Then she looked down at the girl's chair, the eighth, the last in the second row, at the end nearest the arch, standing empty now with its straps undone and hanging. At its left arm. At the strip of iron along the top of the arm, an inch wide and a quarter thick, riveted at both ends, that was bent in the middle down into the oak in the shape of a man's palm and four fingers, as cleanly as if it had been pressed into wax.

She looked at it for some time.

"I'd have someone straighten that," said Vantic, "before the healer comes down. She'll ask what did it." She turned away. "And I'll have nothing to tell her."

She went and sat down on the cracked floor by the girl's pallet with her back against the wall, and put her hand on the blanket over the girl's feet, and did not say anything else.



He went up into the yard at the first grey of the morning, because Ward made him.

The dead zone ended at the stable. He found the edge of it walking across the yard toward the stable gate with Ward's lantern: a place on the cobbles, between one step and the next, where the world came back. He had not known it would feel like anything. It felt like hearing comes back after a bell has been struck too close to your ear: all at once, all of it, too much, the notes of every horse in the stable and every man in the yard and Rylan on his bucket in the stable door and Garrin trussed in the straw behind him, all rushing back into the place where they had been, so loud after the silence that he stopped on the cobbles with the lantern and put his free hand over his eyes.

His sense was there. All of it. Not bruised, not thin, not dark; there, the way it always was, the film over everything. He stood on the cobbles and felt it, and was so glad of it that it frightened him.

The headache was there too. It had not gone with the door. It sat behind his eyes like a nail, and when he looked round the yard in the grey light, at Declan's men sitting against the walls with their heads in their hands, at Osric pressing the heels of his palms into his eyes, at Rylan in the stable door with two fingers pressed to his temple, he understood that it was the same nail. Everybody's. Every man in that house who had been on his knees an hour ago had the same pain in the same place behind the eyes.

Marrow had bled from the nose and the ears when Emeric dragged him into the room against his will, and so had Emeric. Forced, it costs one of them or both. He had learned that in the strongroom four hours ago, on his back in the ash.

He had not dragged anybody tonight. He had not meant to. The door had opened on its own, and reached; and he had thought, at the time, that it was only what anger was, when it got that large.

He stood in the yard with the nail behind his eyes and Ward's lantern in his hand and looked at forty men with their heads in their hands, and wondered whether it was.



The ship came in at seven, out of the south, low and fast, with her stones bright.

She had gone home in the dark at Ward's lamp: three long flashes and three short, which in Ward's book meant the healer, everything she has, now. She came back with it. She came down over the junction with her legs out and settled on the capital road below the chain, outside the edge of the dead zone, because Fletcher would not put her an inch inside it whatever anybody said; and her hatch opened before her legs had stopped moving, and Imani came down the ladder.

She came down it with her bag in one hand, and two of her nurses behind her with more bags, and behind them a crate of blankets and a crate of bottles coming down on the tackle, and last, Ferra, with the book. Imani did not look for him. She looked at the house. She looked at the leaning chimney and the burst shutters and the plaster dust on everything, white, like a frost; and at Ward, who had come down to the road to meet her, and said four words to her that Emeric could not hear.

She went past him into the house without stopping. Without looking at him at all. She went in at the door with her bag and her nurses behind her at a pace that was nearly a run and did not come out again for two hours.

He knew why. He would have done the same. He sat down on the mounting block by the yard gate, because his legs had been telling him for some time that they would like to, and watched the door she had gone in at, and was glad, with the whole of himself, that there was somebody in that cellar now who knew what she was doing.

Ferra came straight to him.

She came across the yard with the book against her chest and stopped in front of the mounting block and looked at him. All over. Quickly, the way she looked down a column for the figure that was wrong; at his face, which he had not seen, and his hands, which had dried blood on the right palm where the girl's stone had cut it, and his jaw, which he understood from her face was still shut. She did not say anything. She put the book down on the mounting block beside him, which he had never seen her do with the book anywhere, and sat down on the other side of it, close, so that her shoulder was against his arm.

They sat there.

After a while, she took his right hand, the cut one, off his knee, and turned it over in her lap, and looked at the cut, and did not do anything about it. She held it.

"Ward told me," she said. "On the road. What's down there." She was looking at his hand. "Some of it. He said I'd want to see the rest myself, later, and that I shouldn't, and that I would anyway."

"Don't."

"I will." She did not look up. "It's going in the book. All of it. I can't put it in the book from what Ward says." She turned his hand a little in hers. "Later."

He did not say anything. He could not. His jaw was shut and he could feel what was behind it, pressing; not the rage now, or not only. The other thing that comes after it. He sat on the mounting block with his teeth together and his eyes on the house door and held it there, behind his teeth, all of it, because if he opened his mouth it would come out, and he could not let it come out. Not here. Not in a yard full of his own men with their heads in their hands, and the five upstairs under guard behind a window, and Ferra beside him. Somebody in this yard had to be the thing that did not break. Somebody had to be the wall. It had always been him; it had been him since the gate, since the first winter, since the first time a man in Kaldo had looked at him to see what to do. If the wall cried, what did the people behind it do.

Imani came out at nine.

She came out of the house door with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder and her apron gone somewhere and her hair tied up with a strip of somebody's blanket, and stood on the step, and looked across the yard; and found him; and came. She did not hurry. She came across the cobbles at the pace she walked the length of the ward at the end of a bad night, and stopped in front of the mounting block, and looked down at him.

"They're alive," she said. "All fifteen. The man by the arch will live, I think. The little girl will live. The boy is eating." She was quiet. "They're not going to be what they were. Some of them. I don't know which yet. I'll know in a week." She looked at him. "You turned the one by the arch on his side and breathed for him. For an hour, Declan says."

He did not answer.

"That's why he's alive," said Imani.

She knelt down on the cobbles in front of him. In the dirt, in her ward dress, with her knees in the dust where the horses had fallen. She took his face in both her hands, as she had on the slip, and looked at it in the grey morning light; the left eye, and the right. And did not let go.

"Your jaw," she said. "Open it. You'll crack a tooth. I can hear it from here."

He could not.

"Emeric." She did not take her hands away. "Open your mouth."

"Four years," he said.

It came out through his teeth. That was all that came, at first. Then the rest came after it, low, without his leave, the way the weight had gone out of him in the cellar.

"Four years I've held Wayford. Four years. Aster's letters every week on my table. Barns and boats. I read every one. I read the price of rope. I paid at that chain in April and I smiled at the man while I paid. I sat at that table upstairs," he did not look at the house, "in May, in my coat, with a cup, and asked them for a share. A share. And laughed. And under my feet, under the floor, under the rag, there was a girl on straw counting strokes on a wall. I sat on top of her and laughed." His teeth were shut again. He opened them. "I built a whole town to stand on and I never once looked down. Not once. I was so pleased with what I'd built that I never looked down."

"Nobody looked down," said Ferra, beside him. Very quietly. "Nobody."

"I'm not nobody."

"No," said Ferra. "You're not." She did not let go of his hand. "And I had Sethe's boxes for three and a half years and didn't open them, and they'd not have shown me this; I've read them; nothing in them says this. And Aster kept one set of true books in that hall for fifteen years under Sethe, and the books didn't show it. And Ottoline watched the cart cross under her window for twenty and thought it was silver." She turned his hand over. "It wasn't in anybody's book. It was under a floor, behind brick, behind a door that was a wall. They built it so nobody would look down. That was the whole point of it." She looked at him. "You found it. You went down a stair and found it. That's the part that's yours."

He shut his eyes.

"Some of them are dead," he said. "There's a door at the back. With a bar on it. The lime." He could not go on with that. "If I'd looked. A year ago. Two."

"Then you'd have found fewer dead behind it," said Imani. "Not none. Fewer." She did not soften it; she never did. "And you'll carry that, and I'll not tell you not to, because you'd not listen and you'd be right not to. But you'll carry it next to the fifteen who are alive in there. Not instead of them." Her thumbs moved, once, on his cheekbones. "You don't get to only carry the dead ones. That's too easy. That's making it easy on yourself." She looked at him. "That's the fourth term."

His jaw was shut again. He could feel his eyes. He could feel what was behind them, under the nail, coming up, the way the heat had come up in the cellar from somewhere low; and he held it. He held it with everything he had left, which was not much. Men in the yard. The column against the walls, with their heads in their hands, looking at him from under them. Rylan in the stable door. The window upstairs. He could not. Not here. He was the thing that held. If he let it go, here, in front of all of them, he did not know what he would be afterward.

Imani watched him hold it. She watched his face from a hand's breadth away, with her hands on it, and he saw her see exactly what he was doing and why; and she did not tell him to stop. She did not tell him anything.

"You can, you know," she said at last, very low, only for him. "Nobody here would tell. Ferra wouldn't put it in the book."

"I would," said Ferra. "I'd put it in the book." She was quiet. "I'd put it in under 'courage'."

"Not yet," said Emeric. His voice was not his. "Not here."

Imani looked at him a breath longer. Then she nodded, once, as if he had passed, or as if he had not and she had decided to let it go for now; and leaned in, there on her knees in the dust of the yard in front of the whole column, and kissed his forehead, slowly, and left her mouth there.

"Then later," said Ferra, beside him. She put his hand back on his own knee, carefully, as if she were setting down something that might spill, and kept hers on top of it. "I'll be there later. Whenever it is." She did not look at him. "I'll not be counting anything."



Imani went back in. Ferra went with her, with the book, to the cellar, because she had said she would, and because she would. He watched them go in at the door together, the tall one with her sleeves rolled and the small one with the book against her chest, and the door shut behind them.

He sat on the mounting block in the morning sun and looked at the Staple house.

One house. Old, and grey, and good stone, with a chain across its two roads and a weathervane on its ridge in the shape of a bale and a chimney leaning now that had not leaned last night. One house, in one barony, in the north of one kingdom. With a charter on its wall, from a king.

The kingdom had a hundred baronies. More. And the duchies over them, and the counties between, and in every one of them a lord, and a hall, and a strongroom, and a road with something across it; and under every hall, cellars. Old ones, vaulted, full of the trade, whatever the trade was there. He had never been down into any of them. Nobody had ever asked him to.

Who was to say what was under them.

Not Corbin. He did not think it of Corbin; Corbin wanted a chair, not a cellar. But he had not thought it of five old men with good bones either, and he had dined with them. Who was to say what a man would do, with a charter and a road and a magistrate who dined with him, and a law that let him hold another man's children against a debt, and a Church that made very sure the children had nothing in their hands. Who was to say what was under the next house, and the next, and the next, in a kingdom where nobody ever looked down because the whole of it was built so that nobody would.

Not five men. That was the thing he understood, on the mounting block, in the sun, with the nail behind his eyes. It had never been five men. It was the house. The whole house. The shape of the thing, from the top of it to the bottom, that made the people at the bottom into rag.

He did not know yet what to do about it. He knew he was going to do something. He sat on the mounting block and looked at the Staple house, and began, there, that morning, very slowly, to count.
