Chapter 249 - Planting

He took the stone out of the brooch on the morning of the sixteenth, a Friday, at his own table, with the shutter open on a clear sky and the snow of the week before gone to water in the ward below.

The box was where it had always been, on the shelf, on its square of black velvet: Bram's silver star, eight points, cleaned and cleaned again since the sea, and in the middle of it, in four claws, the grey stone. He turned the star over. The chip was on the back, against where the coat had been, where Bram had set it so it would not show: a small bite out of the stone's edge, old, smooth with age, as if somebody had once dropped it on a hard floor a long time before anybody had thought to call it worth anything.

He had a pair of Bram's smallest pliers. He opened the claws one at a time, a hair each, carefully, so that he would not mark the silver, and the stone came loose and lay in his palm.

It did not light. It did not do anything at all. It lay in his hand grey and cool and a little heavier than it looked, as it had lain in his coat at Emberfell, and the fine raised net over its every face caught the morning light from the shutter. He had looked at that net under a lens a hundred times. He looked at it now without one, and saw a coat.

He put the empty star back in its box, on its velvet, with its four claws open, and closed the lid.



They planted it at ten, in the winter garden, with the screens drawn back and the sun coming through the glass.

Four of them. Ferra with her book, because there would be a figure. Bram, because he had set the stone in its star and had said, when Emeric told him on the Wednesday what the star was for now, that he would see it out of the star and into the ground or not at all. Armand, carried down the keep stair and across the ward in his chair by two of the garrison, with a rug over his knees and Imani's leave for an hour; and Emeric.

They went in through the pass-box one at a time, as the door said.

The bare circle in the middle of the dome lay as it had lain the week before, raked, in its ring of white stones, among Imani's green beds. Bram had dug it that morning, at six, by himself: a hole the depth of his forearm, in the middle of the circle, and filled it again with earth he had brought in a barrow from the old charcoal ground above Ivo's glass house, black and fine and sifted, and soaked it, and let it drain. He stood beside it now with his hands at his sides and his apron off, which nobody had seen at a working hour in nine years.

Emeric knelt on the edge of the circle.

He had thought about how to do it. He had thought about it at his table for three days, as he thought about everything, and had read every page in his head he had ever read about seeds, and come to nothing better than what a farmer's boy would have told him. You made a hole with your finger. You put the seed in it, the right way up if it had a right way up, and a seed this old and this strange could not be expected to tell you which way that was. You covered it, and firmed the earth over it with your palm, not hard, and you watered it, and you went away.

He made the hole with his finger, two joints deep, in the middle of the black earth. He held the stone over it, between his finger and thumb, and turned it, looking for anything that might be up or down; and found nothing but the chip, the old wound in the coat, on one side. So he laid it in the hole on its side, with the chip upward, toward the glass, toward the sun and the thick air that was coming; because if the chip was where it would wake, it should wake toward something.

He covered it. He firmed the earth with his palm.

Nobody said anything. It was not a thing that anybody at that edge of the ring of stones would have known how to say. Armand sat in his chair with his old hands folded on the rug, and looked at the black earth with his lips pressed together, as he had looked once at a woman in a passage putting a duke on the floor, and had not moved, because she had not needed him. Bram stood at the circle's other side with his big hands open at his sides and his face doing nothing. Ferra did not open her book.

Emeric knelt with his palm flat on the earth over the stone, and did not take it away.

He did not remember her. He had never remembered her. He knew her as a word other people said; as a woman in a grey gown on a floor beside a slate, in a passage he could not see; as a stone in a box and a stone in his band and a stone in Bram's star; as two notes hummed under his breath that he had not known were hers. There was a wall where the rest of her should have been, and nothing had ever opened it, and he had not expected the planting of a seed to open it either, and it did not.

He took his hand away.

Ferra, beside him, put her hand on his shoulder. She left it there while he got up off his knees, and took it away when he was standing, and opened her book.

"Sixteenth of April," she said, and wrote it. "Ten in the morning. The stone from the brooch." Then she wrote one more thing, small, which he did not lean to see, and which he never asked her about, and which he read in her book a long time afterward, at a table she was not at, and it was only a name.



The charging was at noon, and the first cartridge cracked a pane.

The collar was on the north side of the ring, low, an iron sleeve through the steel with a threaded mouth outside in the ward and a valve inside the dome; and the cartridges were the vial rig's, the same steel bottles the size of a man's forearm that the Hod brought down from the Band in their iron racks every week and Bram's men screwed into the cellar's niche. Ferra had counted how many the dome would want to reach her line from the ward's air, and how fast, and had written it in red on the sheet beside the line: four cartridges, one an hour, and then one a day to hold it.

Bram screwed the first into the collar from outside at noon exactly, with Emeric and Ferra and Armand inside the dome to read it, and turned the valve.

The mana came into the dome through the collar's mouth as a jet. That was the trouble, and nobody had seen it coming, because nobody had ever put liquid mana into a sealed room through a pipe before. It came out of the cartridge as liquid and met the dome's air at the collar's mouth and went to mist at once, as it did; but it came out fast, under the cartridge's own pressure, and the mist went across the dome in a straight hard blue jet a yard long before it spread, and hit the glass.

One triangle, low on the south side, a yard above the ring. The jet hit it square. And the glass, which held mana, took the jet's whole charge into itself all at once in a patch the size of a hand, more than glass was ever meant to hold in one place, and cracked: a sharp small sound like a knuckle on a table, and a white star in the pane where the jet had hit, with three thin lines running out of it to the pane's edges.

Bram turned the valve off from outside before anybody inside could shout to him. He had been watching through the glass, as he watched everything.

Nobody moved. The pane held. It had cracked, but it held in its iron frame on its bead of pitch, and the mist that had come in spread through the dome's air slowly and evenly now that the jet had stopped, a faint blue going grey, and was gone; and Emeric's sense, open in the dome, felt the air round them go a little thicker, a little warmer, like a room where somebody has lit a fire in the next room.

Bram came in through the pass-box, one at a time, and looked at the cracked pane, and then at the collar's mouth, and then at the pane again, and measured the distance between the two with his eye.

"It's a hose," he said. "With the nozzle off. You don't put a hose on a seedbed with the nozzle off. You put a rose on it." He went out again.

He came back in a quarter of an hour with a sheet of iron from the works' scrap, cut into a disc the size of a dinner plate, and a hand drill, and a pocketful of bolts; and he knelt at the collar's mouth inside the dome and began to drill the disc, holes the size of a pea, dozens of them, all over it, so that it was like the rose on a watering can; and then to fix it on three bolts a hand's width in front of the collar's mouth, square to it, so that anything coming out of the collar would hit the disc first, and go through its holes, and come out the other side as a hundred small soft jets going every way at once instead of one hard one going one way.

A baffle. He did not call it that. He called it a rose.

It took him the better part of half an hour, kneeling on the stone edging by the collar with his drill, and Emeric stood beside the black circle with nothing to do and his hands wanting to do something, anything, that was not reaching for the next cartridge.

There was a jar on the edge of Imani's comfrey bed, a stone jar of water she kept there for her trowel. He picked it up and held it in both hands.

Armand's rule. The heat must have somewhere to go that isn't you. He did not put his hand in the water this time. He held the jar, and found the wet black earth of the circle at his feet with his sense, the soaked earth over the stone, cold, heavy, with all the water Bram had poured into it at six; and he pulled the heat out of the water in the jar and sent it down past his own hands into the earth at his feet, without it once stopping in him, and he was only the way it went.

The jar went cold in his hands. Then colder. A skin of ice came across the top of the water, and then down from the skin, white, into the jar, and the stone jar began to sweat on the outside in the dome's warm air and the sweat froze on it in a white fur.

When Bram stood up from the collar and dusted his knees, the jar was solid. He turned it over in his hands. Nothing came out of it.

Bram looked at the jar.

"How long did that take you?" he said.

"As long as the rose took you."

"At midwinter," said Bram, "you burned your thumb on a pan and Armand had to tell you why." He took the jar out of Emeric's hands and looked at the white fur on it, and the ice inside it, and gave it back. "That's quicker than a meal. That's the length of a job." Which, from Bram, was more than he had said about any piece of magic Emeric had done in nine years.



The second cartridge went in through the rose, and the dome took it the way a field takes a soft rain.

No jet. A faint blue breath out of a hundred small holes at once, going every way, spreading, gone. The cracked pane did not crack further. Emeric stood in the middle of the dome by the black circle and felt the air thicken round him, slowly, evenly, like warm water rising in a bath; and Armand, in his chair by the circle's edge with his eyes shut, felt it too, and sat with his old face turned up toward the glass, reading it.

The third, an hour later.

It was after the third that Emeric felt the want.

It came up in him as Armand had said it would, at a table in March with Ferra's gauge book open: plainly, without any argument, like hunger. The air was thick now. It was thick the way the Band's foot was thick, a thing he could feel on his skin, and in it, under the black earth, a grey seed with a broken coat lay in the wet dark and did nothing at all. And he had seven cartridges in the rack outside the pass-box, and a niche in the cellar with more, and a ship that brought more down every week; and if the thick air was what woke it, more of the thick air would wake it faster. It was the most obvious thing in the world. You did not water a field with a cup when you had a river.

He went to the inner door of the pass-box.

"No," said Armand, with his eyes shut.

Emeric stopped with his hand on the door.

"Two more," said Emeric. "Only two. It'll be over the line by a hair, for an hour, and then the dome will bleed it down through the glass to the line again by tonight. A hair over the line for an hour."

"A hair over the line for an hour," said Armand, still with his eyes shut, "is the jar. In your Vey's drawing. The hand with the jar." He opened his eyes. "You knew it on the day you read it. You told me on the night you read it. Soak it. Don't pour it. And now you're standing at a door with your hand on it, wanting to pour." He did not raise his voice. "I said you would. I said it on the night I went up for the number. A man who's spent his life building things to go faster can't stand in a room with a thing that's growing and not want to make it go faster. It's not a fault. It's what you are." He looked at Ferra. "That's why it's her line."

Ferra had her book open at the gauge page, and her finger on the red line, and she was looking at him.

She did not say anything. She did not need to. She held the book up, open, so that he could see the red line across the page from where he stood at the door, and the figure beside it, and the word DOME, in her square capitals, in ink.

He took his hand off the door.

"The fourth at two," said Ferra. "Then one a day. At the line. Not over it."

"At the line."

"And when does it come up?"

He looked at the black earth in its ring of white stones.

"I don't know," he said. "Weeks. Months. A seed that's slept for however long this one's slept doesn't wake because we're watching it." He was quiet. "And if it wakes, and if it grows, I don't know when a tree like that bears. A year. Two. Three. Vey didn't say. The carver didn't say anything Vey could read."

Ferra wrote it. One to three years, she wrote, and a question mark; and then she drew a box round the question mark, not a ring, which was her sign for a thing that would be answered by waiting and by nothing else.



The works sent its report up the hill that afternoon, in Bram's square foreman's hand, and Ferra signed it at the counting room table at five without reading more than its totals, because she had read every line of it already as each line was done.

The ships were caulked. The K Discovery and hull two had gone into the covered shed in turn that fortnight, and Fletcher's men had lined each of them inside her inner shell with sheet iron, soldered at every seam, as the Hod had been lined in the autumn, so that what the Band put into the air outside came through to the men inside as a slow seep and not a flood; and on each ship's pump, where the air from the Band came in squeezed and cold, Tobe had fitted a coil of copper pipe in a jacket of the plant's cold, a cold trap, that wrung the mana out of the squeezed air before it reached the cabin, the way a cold glass sweats on a summer day, and dripped it, liquid, blue, into a tank. The tank was one of the four glass bodies the bigger room had drawn in March, in an iron jacket, at the stone-keeper's station beside each ship's latch, with a brass head on it and a dome and a horn: the ship's own inducer. In the Band it caught the drip and filled. Below the Band it held the cabin at Imani's mark, so that a ship carried the Band down with her. And in a dead zone, it fired, through its horn, into the gap between the iron and the matrix ring, where the stones were, because mana let go anywhere else inside the iron would have woken the lamps and not the lift. Hull two and the capital's lift hull had theirs. The Hod had hers beside the latch in her oak house. Every ship Kaldo flew from that week on had one, and every ship it ever built would have one from the keel.

Ferra had priced the iron for the two of them against what the iron cost them in lift, which came to something near a ton, and signed for it; and had said, signing, that a ton was a cheap price for nobody bleeding, and that she would like that sentence remembered the next time anybody at the table asked her why she had signed for something.

And at the works, the pot room had been begun. A shed of its own beside bay three, with its own steam engine and its own dynamo, so that the lamps of Kaldo kept theirs; and down its middle, a row of iron pots on brick, each lined with carbon, each the size of a washtub, each with a thick carbon block hanging into it from above on a chain, for the current to come down; and under every pot, set into the brick, a fire stone, so that if the current ever stopped in the night the melt in the pots would not freeze solid round the carbon and crack the pot, as Tess had made very sure he understood a melt would. The alumina was to come from Grayrock's bauxite, a red earth Bryda's men had been throwing on the spoil heap for as long as anybody could remember, cooked in the caustic from the chlorine works; and the bath from fluorspar and the acid works, once Tess had worked out how, and from the bigger room until she had.

Ferra had priced the first kilo of the light metal out of the pot room before there was any pot room to make it in. She had priced it above silver. It was, that year, the truth.



Rylan carried his daughter into the ward a little before the late bell that night.

Emeric was there because Imani had sent for him. Not for the girl; for the gloves. She had worn them for a week, as she had said she would, on everything, the dressings and the boils and a baby with a fist stuck in a jug; and she had found what was wrong with them, three things, and wanted to tell him at once while she had them in her head, and had sent a boy up the hill at ten. He had come down. He had been in the dispensary for half an hour with the gloves on the bench between them, and the three things, and her list, when the outer door of the ward went back against the wall.

Rylan did not shout. He came down the ward's passage with Wren in his arms, fast, at a walk, which from Rylan was worse than running, and Nessa behind him with a lantern and her face like paper; and he did not say anything at all until he was in the dispensary door.

"She's bad," he said. "She's been bad since yesterday. We thought it was her stomach. She's bad."

Wren was fourteen, and nearly as tall as her mother, and Rylan carried her as if she weighed nothing. She had her knees drawn up and her arms round her middle and her face against his shoulder, and she was grey, and sweating, and when he moved she made a sound that Emeric had not heard her make since she was small.

Imani was already up.

"The bed in the end room," she said. "Put her down on her back. Slowly. Don't straighten her legs; let her keep them how she wants them." She was moving as she said it, past him, into the passage. "Nessa. When did it start? Where?"

"Yesterday morning. In the middle." Nessa put her hand on her own stomach, at the navel. "Here. She said it was here, and she was sick, once, and wouldn't eat, and we thought, a bad egg, a chill. And then in the night it went." Her hand moved down and out, to the right, low, above the hip. "Here. And she's hot. And since tonight she won't let anybody touch her."

Imani stopped in the end room's door.

She did not say anything for a breath. She looked at Wren on the bed, with her knees up, grey, sweating, and at Nessa's hand still pressed low on her own right side; and Emeric, behind her, saw her face, and saw that she knew. Not guessed. Knew, the way she knew a dose; from her register, from her ward, from every child who had ever lain on that bed with her knees up and her hand on her right side.

"Right-side fever," said Imani, very quietly. "Second day."

She said it to him. She did not say the rest of it. She did not need to. He saw it in her face: what she had seen on that bed before, young people, mostly, between ten and twenty; the pain in the middle and then on the right; the fever; and then the third day, when some of them got up and were well, and some of them made a hard lump in their side and lived, and some of them, quite suddenly, in the night, went quiet and hot and grey all over, and their bellies went hard as a board, and they died, because something inside them had burst. And she could do nothing for any of them but keep them still, and give them nothing by mouth, and give them poppy for the pain, and wait, and see which it would be.

"Let me look," said Emeric.



He looked the way he had told her to look, in the dispensary, in January. With his hands, and with her stethoscope, and slowly.

He knelt beside the bed and spoke to Wren first, by her name, so that she would know his voice; and asked her if he could put his hand on her belly, and she said yes through her teeth, and Rylan, at the bed's head with his hand on her hair, said yes over her.

"Imani's cup," he said, and held out his hand, and Imani put the stethoscope into it without a word.

He put the silver cup on Wren's belly, low, on the left side first, where it did not hurt, and listened. A full minute, by the clock on the end room's wall. Then the middle. Then the right.

"Listen," he said, and gave Imani the plugs.

She listened. A full minute. Then she took them out of her ears and looked at him.

"Nothing," she said.

"Nothing. No gurgle. No drain. Since I don't know when." He gave her back the stethoscope. "Quiet belly. Something's so sore in there that the gut's stopped moving round it."

Then his hands.

He had never done it. Not with his hands. He had done it in his head out of the books the trial had given him, ten thousand times, on bellies that were paper; and his hands followed what his head had, as they had always followed it, slowly, every motion thought through before it was made, so that he made no slip. But he had never once put his hand on a sick child's belly and felt for the thing that would kill her.

He put his fingers flat on the left side, low, and pressed, slowly, gently. Wren gasped. Not on the left. She put her own hand to her right side.

"Where does it hurt?"

"Here." Right side, low.

"I pushed on the other side."

"It hurts here," said Wren, through her teeth, as if he were stupid.

He looked up at Imani. Press on the left and it hurts on the right: because pressing on the left pushed the gut along inside, and the gut pushed on the sore thing on the right. Imani had seen it. He saw her see it, and file the reason away behind her eyes as she filed every reason.

Then he found the point.

He drew a line in his head from the top of the hip bone at the front to the navel, and went a third of the way along it from the hip, and put two fingers there, flat, and pressed slowly, slowly down. Wren's face went tight. He held the pressure there, steady; and then, without warning, lifted his fingers away, fast.

Wren screamed.

It was short and sharp and it went through the end room and out into the passage, and Rylan's hand on her hair went white at the knuckles, and Nessa made a sound; and Emeric said, quietly, "I'm sorry, that was the worst one, there's only one more," and meant it.

"It hurt more when you let go," said Imani. It was not a question.

"It hurts more when I let go. Because when I let go, the belly springs back, and the lining of the belly, the skin inside it that wraps round the gut, rubs on the sore place. The lining's angry. That means the sore thing's touching it." He put his hand on Wren's right knee. "Wren. Lift your leg for me. Straight. Against my hand."

She lifted it an inch and stopped, and put her head back on the pillow, and wept.

"That's enough," he said. "That's all. No more pressing. I promise." He took his hands off her and sat back on his heels.

"There's a thing in there," he said, to Imani, and to Rylan, and to Nessa, and to Wren, all at once, plainly. "Everybody has it. A little blind finger of gut, the size of my little finger, that hangs off the big gut down here on the right where it turns. It doesn't do anything much. It's called the appendix." He held up his little finger. "Sometimes it gets blocked. When it does, it swells, and it gets sore, and it gets hot, and the blood can't get through it properly, and the end of it starts to die. And when it dies enough, it bursts. And everything that was in it goes into the belly." He looked at Imani. "That's the third night. That's what you've seen on this bed."

"Yes," said Imani.

"It's going to burst," said Emeric. "Not tonight, maybe. Tomorrow. Inside a day. And if it bursts, she'll die." He held Nessa's eyes, and then Rylan's. "Unless it comes out first. Tonight."



"No," said Imani.

She said it at once, and flatly, standing at the bed's foot with her arms folded. She did not say it to Rylan or to Nessa. She said it to him.

"You'll not open her," said Imani. "I'll not let you open her. Not on my ward."

"If I don't, she'll..."

"I know what she'll do. I've seen it. I've seen it more than you've seen anything." Her voice did not rise. It went quieter, and harder, which in Imani was worse. "And I've seen every belly that's ever been opened in this ward, and in my father's practice before it, and in every ward I ever heard of. Cut by a sword. Cut by a plough. Cut by a surgeon taking out a stone. And the fever took every one of them. Every one. On the third day, the fourth, the belly goes hot and hard and they die of it, slower than if you'd left them, and in more pain." She held his eyes. "You'll not cut this child open and kill her of the fever instead of the burst. You'll not make me watch it. I'd rather give her the poppy and sit with her and see if she's one of the ones who gets up."

Nobody in the end room said anything. Rylan had not taken his hand off his daughter's hair.

Emeric did not argue. He did not stand up. He stayed on his heels by the bed, where he was, and answered her, one thing at a time, the way she would have answered a nurse.

"The fever's not from the cut," he said. "It's from what goes into the cut. Dirt on the blade. Dirt on the hands. Dirt in the air. Things too small to see, the same things that make the matter in a wound and the red line up the arm. You know that already; you've washed your hands in carbolic every morning for eight years because I told you, and your ward's wounds don't fester the way they did, and you've counted it in your register." He held up his hand, and counted on it. "The steel's boiled. The new instruments, the stainless ones off the plate; they've been in your boiling pan since they came, and I'll boil them again now in front of you. The carbolic, on her skin, and on everything that touches her. The ether, so she doesn't move and doesn't feel it. And these." He nodded at the dispensary, where the gloves lay on the bench. "Nothing gets through them. Not blood. Not water. Not what you can't see in either."

Imani said nothing.

"And I won't cut the muscle," he said. "I'll go through it. The belly wall here is three sheets of muscle, one over the other, each one with its grain running a different way. I'll cut the skin, a cut the length of my finger, here." He drew the line in the air over Wren, short, slanting, at the point where she had screamed. "And then not cut the muscles at all. Part them. Each one along its own grain, with the handles of the forceps, the way you'd part the strands of a rope. And when I'm done and I let them go, they fall back over each other like the three of them were never parted, and they hold, because nothing's been cut across." He looked at her. "Small. Clean. Quick, if I'm slow. And out before it bursts."

Imani looked at Wren on the bed. At the girl's grey sweating face, and her knees drawn up, and her father's white knuckles in her hair.

She was quiet for a long breath.

"If she dies of the fever," she said, "on the fourth day, with her belly hard, I'll know it was this."

"Yes."

"And so will you."

"Yes."

"And if you don't open her, and it bursts tomorrow, I'll know it was that." She turned her head and looked at Rylan, and then at Nessa, and something passed between the three of them that Emeric was not part of and did not try to be. Then she looked back at him.

"It's my ward," said Imani. "My yes." She unfolded her arms. "Yes."



He asked before he looked. It was his rule, and he kept it, even then.

"I want to see inside her," he said to Rylan and Nessa, with Imani boiling the steel in the dispensary and the nurses sent running. "Before I cut. Not with my hands; with the other thing. The sense. It doesn't go into her. It doesn't touch her. She won't feel it. But it's looking, and I'd not look without your leave." He looked at Wren. "Or hers."

Rylan, who had felt Emeric's sense on a wall at night, and in a boat's hold with his son, and knew what it was, said yes at once.

Wren, with her face in the pillow, said, "Just get it out," which he took for yes.

He knelt by the bed and put his sense into her.

Not his mana. His sense: the thing he kept open always, the thing that told him where people were in the dark and who they were by their note. With a grown man, a knight, a mage, a body that held its mana thick, the sense came back off the skin the way sound came back off a wall, and he got the man's shape and his note and nothing more. With Armand, last autumn, he had had to send his own mana into the old man's chest to see the heart, and it had cost them both. But Wren was a child. A commoner's child, though her father was a knight, with a child's thin mana, thinner than anybody's in that room; and when he pushed his sense at her close and hard, at the place on her right side, it did not come back off her skin.

It went through.

It went into her as a lamp goes into fog, and came back to him from inside, shaded: the bright places where the blood ran thick, the dimmer places where it did not, the faint grey of the hip bone at the edge. The great coil of the gut, a dim knotted rope. And low on the right, where it turned, something brighter than the gut round it, thick, swollen, a finger's length, glowing with the blood packed into it.

It was blurred. It was like looking at a thing through a window streaming with rain. He could see that it was there, and that it was swollen, and that it was bright, and not much more; not where it lay against the gut, nor how far its end had gone, nor how close the whole of it was to the thing he was afraid of.

So he went into the room.

It took no time at all, outside. In the end room, Rylan saw him close his eyes for the space of a breath. In the room it was long enough to do what he needed: the room where time ran slow, his room, through the stone in his band, with its long table and its shelves and everything he had ever read lying open on them. He took the blur in with him. He laid it on the table and looked at it in the room's light.

Still blurred.

He came out, and opened his eyes, and looked at Imani in the doorway with the boiled steel in its pan.

"Your hands," he said. "What they found. When you touched her, before I came in. Before I did." He held out his own hand to her, palm up. "Can I have it? I'm asking."

Imani looked at his hand.

She had not forgotten. He saw that she had not. A man's heel in October in this ward, and his sense sliding off the heel into her and into a pier, and green water through the planks, and her father's voice, and the door slamming, and the headache they had both had for a day. And on the Wednesday after, in the dispensary, flat: Ask next time.

He was asking.

She put the pan down on the end of the bed and came and put her hand in his.

"Only what I felt in her," she said. "Not anything else."

"Only that."

She gave it to him. He felt her give it: not the slide of the first time, not him going in, but her putting it out, deliberately, the way she would have put a card into his hand. What her fingers had found on Wren's belly when Rylan laid her down: the hard, guarded wall of the muscle on the right, tight as a drum skin, and under it, at the point a third of the way from the hip bone, a place that was hotter than the rest, and thicker, and where her fingertips had felt, very faintly, through the guarding, the edge of something swollen that should not have been there.

He took it into the room with him.

He laid it on the table over the blur. Her hands, on his sight. And over both of them, a third thing: the quiet. The patch of nothing the stethoscope had heard, low on the right, where the gut had stopped moving, laid over the others like a sheet of tracing paper on a drawing.

The blur went.

Not all of it. But the sense gave him the shape and her hands gave him the edges and the quiet gave him where it lay, and the three of them on top of one another came together on the room's table as three drawings of the same thing on glass come together when you hold them up to a lamp and line them up: and there it was. A swollen finger of gut, bright with packed blood, lying across the edge of the big gut where it turned, with its end tucked down behind it. And the end was dim. The last third of it, the tip, was going grey, and the grey was spreading back up it, very slowly, toward the bright; and at the very tip, at the end, the grey had gone almost dark.

Close. Not yet. Close.

It cost him. It cost him a headache that came on all at once behind his eyes like a hand closing there, worse for the third layer than for the second; and he opened his eyes in the end room with the lamp too bright and Imani's hand still in his.

"Tell me what you saw," said Imani.

"It's lying behind the gut. The end's going dark. Hours, not a day." He let go of her hand. "Your hands showed me where it lies. I'd not have had the edge of it without them."

Imani looked at him a breath longer, at his face, not the child's.

"All of it," she said. "Not the pretty parts."

"That was all of it," said Emeric, and stood up. "Gloves."



Imani gave the ether.

She had given it a hundred times since the first time, when he had told her how and she had not believed him; a cone of folded linen over the nose and mouth, the bottle, the drops on the linen one at a time, her fingers on the pulse at the jaw, her voice in the patient's ear, telling her to breathe, and count, and breathe. Wren counted to nine, and went quiet, and her knees came down.

The nurses washed the girl's belly with carbolic, three times, from the middle outward. Every instrument came out of the boiling pan onto a cloth that had been boiled. Emeric and Imani scrubbed at the basin side by side, to the elbow, in the carbolic, with the brush, for the count of three hundred, which was the rule he had given her eight years ago and she had kept since; and then the gloves. He had a pair, from Tess's second dipping, made on formers cast from his own hands the week before because Imani had said that if she was to have gloves he was to have gloves, and if he was going to put his hands in anybody in her ward he was going to put them in in rubber. They went on wet, from a bowl of carbolic, cold, and drew tight to the wrists.

He had a scalpel off the plate in his hand, one of the two hundred.

He stood over Wren for the space of a breath.

He knew everything. That was the strange thing, and he would think about it afterward on more nights than one: he knew, standing there, every motion of what he was about to do, every layer, every vessel, every tie, the way Bram knew a weld; he had known it since the trial, with the certainty of a man who has done a thing ten thousand times. And he had never done it once. His hands followed his head. They always had. But they followed it slowly, every motion thought through before it was made, and there was nothing his head could do to make them fast.

So he was slow.

The skin first. One cut, slanting, the length of his finger, at the point where she had screamed; through the skin, and the yellow layer under it, and down to the first sheet of muscle, which lay across the cut with its grain running down and in, pale, shining. Imani's forceps took the edges back. He did not cut the muscle. He put the closed points of a pair of forceps against it, along its grain, and opened them, slowly, and the fibres parted along their own line, as the strands of a rope part under a fid, and showed the second sheet under them, with its grain running the other way. He parted that. Then the third, thin, its grain running across. Then, under all three, the lining: thin, grey, faintly shining, the skin inside the belly that wrapped the gut, and that he could see through, a little, the way you can see through wet paper. He lifted it in a fold between two forceps, so that nothing under it could be caught, and nicked it, and opened it.

A little cloudy fluid came up out of the opening. Not much. Not pus. Not yet. He saw Imani see it, and saw her not say anything.

He put two fingers in.

It was warm, inside, and wet, and the gut was soft under his fingers, smooth, moving very slightly with Wren's breathing under the ether. He did not look for it with his eyes. There was nothing to see through a cut that size. He looked for it the way the books said, and the way his room had showed him: he found the big gut where it turned, by the long flat bands of muscle that ran along its outside, and followed the bands down with his fingertip to where they met, at the bottom of the turn; and where the bands met, the finger of gut began. He could feel it. Hot. Thick. Swollen as a sausage. Tucked down behind the big gut, exactly where the room had shown him it lay.

He brought it up. Slowly, slowly, two fingers behind it, easing it out from behind the big gut and up through the cut in the lining, a hair at a time, because the end of it was the thing he was afraid of and he did not know how near to bursting the end was; and it came up into the lamplight, out of the wound, and lay on the gauze Imani had ready: red, and fat, and shining, the length of his finger, with its tip a dull dirty grey.

Nobody in the room breathed.

"There," he said. "That's it."

Imani looked at it. She looked at it as if it might move, the thing that had been killing children on her end bed for as long as she had kept a ward, out in the air, in the lamplight, on a gauze, where she could see it.

Then she held out her hand for the next instrument before he could ask, because she had seen what he was going to do next as clearly as he had, and he put the clamp into it.

He tied off the little fold of tissue that carried its blood, in two places, with silk off Imani's own reel, and cut between the ties. He crushed the finger at its root, where it met the big gut, with the clamp, and tied it there, twice, hard; and cut it off above the ties. It came away in the forceps, whole. It had not burst. He laid it in the basin a nurse held out, and felt something in his chest let go that had been held since the dispensary.

The stump he turned in, under a ring of fine stitches round it in the wall of the big gut, so that the tied end sank into the gut's own wall and was covered, as you would turn the end of a sausage skin in; and then he eased the big gut back down into the belly where it lived, and looked at the lining for any bleeding, and found none, and closed the lining.

Then he stopped.

His hands were steady. They had been steady the whole time. But he had been at it for most of an hour, and he was slow, and he knew he was slow, and he knew what was left: the three sheets of muscle to lie back down, and the yellow layer, and the skin, and every stitch of it to be small and even and close and tied so that it held and did not pucker.

He looked at Imani.

"You close," he said.

Imani did not say anything. She took the needle holder out of his hand, and a needle, and the silk, and went to work.

She did not look at what she was doing. That was what he would remember. She looked at it once, at the start, at the line of the cut; and then her eyes went somewhere else, half shut, unfocused, and her hands did it. The muscles fell back over each other along their grain as he had said they would, and she laid a few loose stitches to hold each in its place, quickly, without looking. Then the skin. A row of stitches, small, even, each one tied with a turn and a flick of her fingers that he could not have followed if he had been looking for it, each knot laid exactly beside the last, so that when she had finished there was a slanting line the length of his finger on the girl's pale belly, closed, neat, flat, like a seam in a good coat.

He had seen where her hands learned to feel a knot. In October. On a pier, through green water, by accident: a big dark man with grey in his hair and a net across his knees, and a small girl in front of him with a piece of the same net across hers, frowning; and the man's voice. Don't look at it. Feel it with the end of your finger.

He did not say anything about it. He would not have said anything about it if she had asked him.

She cut the last thread, and stood back, and pulled the gloves off her hands, inside out, as Tess had pulled them off the formers.

"There," said Imani.



Rylan had stood outside the end room's door the whole time.

He had not sat down. He had not leaned on the wall. He had stood in the ward's passage with his back to the door and his arms folded, in the dark, between the door and the stair, as if something might come up the stair that he would need to be between; the way Ward stood at every door Emeric had ever slept behind. Nessa sat on the floor of the passage beside him with her back to the wall and her knees drawn up and the lantern beside her, and did not speak.

Emeric came out at a little after one, in his shirt, with his gloves in his hand.

Rylan did not move. He looked at Emeric's face.

"It's out," said Emeric. "Whole. It didn't burst." He held up his finger, his little finger, and then put it down, because his hand had started, at last, very slightly, to shake. "She'll be sore for a week. She'll have a line here the length of my finger for the rest of her life. And she'll be up in ten days."

Rylan looked at him a breath longer.

Then he unfolded his arms and put both his hands on Emeric's shoulders, and held them there, hard, without saying anything, and took them away; and went past him into the end room, to his daughter, and Nessa went after him with the lantern.



Wren came up out of the ether at a little before dawn.

She came up slowly, as they did, a long way, from somewhere deep, with her eyes opening and shutting and her mouth working, and Imani on one side of the bed with her fingers on the girl's pulse and Nessa on the other with the girl's hand; and Emeric, who had not gone home, standing at the foot of the bed with his shoulder against the doorframe.

Her eyes found him.

They did not find her mother or her father or Imani first. They found the man at the foot of the bed, in the grey light, and stayed on him, unfocused, puzzled, and he saw her look at him the way she had looked at him in her mother's house at four years old, ten years ago, the first summer he came to Kaldo: with the flat unimpressed curiosity of a child who had not yet been told that princes were supposed to be interesting.

"Are you a real prince?" said Wren.

Nessa put her hand over her mouth.

"Duke, now," said Emeric.

Wren thought about this, slowly, from very far away, with her eyes half shut.

"Where's your crown?" she said, and went back to sleep before he could answer.

"I left it in the carriage," said Emeric, to nobody.

Rylan, in the chair at the bed's head, put his face down on the blanket by his daughter's hand and laughed into it, without any sound, until his shoulders shook, and went on.



The basin in the cutting room was the one they had scrubbed at, a long stone trough with the brushes on its edge and the carbolic in its jar, and he went back to it at dawn because his hands smelled of the gloves and the carbolic and the inside of a girl's belly, and he wanted them not to.

Imani was there before him. She had her sleeves rolled to the shoulder and her own gloves, the ones she had pulled off inside out at the end, in the water, turned the right way, rinsing them, as Tess had told her to: in clean water, and then dried in the air on their formers, and dusted with chalk, so that they would go on again. She did not look round when he came in. She was looking at the gloves.

"Three things wrong with them," she said. "I came down to tell you last night. There are four now. They tear at the thumb if you pull them on too fast. I nearly put mine through at the start." She turned one over in the water. "And they held. Everything else, they held."

He came and stood beside her at the trough, and looked at her: her bare arms in the water, and her hair half down out of its tie from the night, and the grey light from the high window on her face, which was tired and was not the face she wore in the ward. He had watched her close that wound without looking at it. He had watched her say yes.

He reached for her.

She did not look up from the gloves.

"Wash first," said Imani.

He stopped with his hand an inch from her waist.

"You smell of carbolic and the inside of Rylan's daughter," said Imani, to the water, "and so do I, and I'll not be reached for at my own basin by a man who hasn't scrubbed to the elbow for the count of three hundred." She lifted a glove out of the water and held it up to the window to look for holes. "You know the rule. You made it."

He washed. To the elbow, with the brush, in the carbolic, for the count of three hundred, while she rinsed the gloves beside him and did not look at him once; and at three hundred he dried his hands on the clean towel and put them down on the trough's stone edge.

Then she put the glove down, and dried her own hands, and turned round, and put her arms round his neck, and leaned on him, the whole of her weight, as she had never leaned on him in that ward; and stood there, against him, with her face in his shoulder, in the grey light, and did not say anything at all.

He held her. Her heart under his hand was going fast, and she could not hide it, and did not try.

"Now," she said, into his shoulder, after a long time.



He did not sleep. He went up the hill at six to the yard, because it was the morning for the drill and Ward would be there, and he had not missed a morning in nine years that he had been in Kaldo for, and he did not mean to miss this one because of a night.

Ward was there, with the two ash blades, in the grey.

He looked at Emeric's face, and at his hands, and at the carbolic smell that came off him across three yards of cold air; and said nothing at all, and threw him the narrow blade.

They went through the forms. Slowly, the first time, as they always did. Then faster. And at the end of the third pass, without warning, as he had done every morning for nine years, Ward came in with the downslash: his oldest cut, the ash blade coming down from over his head with his whole weight behind it, so fast that the art was to have moved before it started.

Emeric was not there when it came down.

He was half a step to the left and inside Ward's arms, as he had been once, six years ago, the first time; and his own blade was already going, across Ward's ribs under the raised arms, a clean full cut, hard. And on that morning six years ago he had stopped dead, with his blade against Ward's side, thinking he had broken the rib, and Ward's blade had come up off the sand and lain along his neck.

He did not stop.

The cut went in and through and his blade was already coming back, round, high, before the ash blade had finished going into the sand; and it was at Ward's neck, resting there, along the side of it, before Ward's own blade had come up an inch.

Ward stood still.

He stood with the ash blade's point in the sand and Emeric's narrow blade along the side of his neck, in the grey light, for some time. Then he looked down at the blade at his neck, and along it, at the hand on its hilt, which was steady.

"There," said Ward.

Emeric took the blade away.

"Nine years," said Ward. He picked the ash blade up out of the sand and looked at its point. "Six since the first time you got inside. And every time since, you stopped to see what you had done. Half a breath. Every time." He wiped the point on his sleeve. "That half a breath is a man's life, on a field." He looked at Emeric. "It is gone."

"I didn't stop to look."

"No." Ward put the blade under his arm. "You had already looked. Before you cut." He turned to go in. "Whatever you did last night. Do it again."



Imani found him at noon in the dispensary, asleep in her chair with his head on her bench beside the gloves, and did not wake him for an hour.

When she did, she had a jar on the bench in front of him. A tall glass jar from Ivo's, with a ground stopper, full of clear spirit from the still house; and in the spirit, hanging on a thread from the stopper, the thing from the gauze. Grey-red, now, and shrunken a little, the length of his finger, with its dark tip.

"For the school," said Imani. "So that every child in Kaldo knows what it looks like before they're old enough to have one go bad." She turned the jar so that the label faced him. She had written it in her own hand: APPENDIX. W., 14. 16 APRIL. And under it, smaller: Taken out whole. Did not burst.

She put the register beside the jar, open, at a new page.

She had ruled a new column. All down the page, beside the names of every child who had ever come to her end bed with the right-side fever since the first summer, with the day it had begun and what had become of them, she had ruled a new column, at the right-hand edge, with a heading at its top.

OPENED.

Every line in it was empty but the last.

"I'll teach the nurses this week," she said. "The cup on the belly. The point. Let go fast. Press left, it hurts right. The leg." She sat down on the stool across the bench from him. "All of it. So that the next time a child comes in at night with her knees up, whoever's on knows what it is on the first day, and doesn't wait for the third." She looked at the jar. "And so that I'm not the only one."

He looked at the column. At the one line in it that was not empty.

Imani was looking at him across the bench. She had not slept either. She had the dark rings under her eyes that she had had on the morning after the culvert, and her hair was still half down, and she was looking at him the way she had looked at him in the end room in the night, at his face and not the child's, when he came out of the room behind his eyes with the blur gone.

"You could see inside her," said Imani. "I couldn't."
