Chapter 313 - No Loans

Ferra asked him on the tenth of September, at night, in the map room, with the capital standing in light on the table between them; which was three weeks after she had said she would, and he understood when she did it that she had waited on purpose.

He had come up to look at the close again. He came up most nights now, late, when the Works Office was empty and the watchman two floors down had done his round and gone back to his kettle, and lit the ground tray and the first dozen layers and stood over them with his hands on the table's rim. He did not do anything. He looked. The river grey-blue, the streets buff, the wall dull red round the whole of it, and the roofs going up layer on layer out of the stone in the dark like a town seen from a hill at dusk; and in the middle of them the pale yellow ground of the Church, and on it the long cross of the nave, and the two porch towers with Venn's guns standing on them in two bright upright strokes, and the bell tower off the south side on its own, eighteen layers tall, that Emeric had lit from memory and a walker's given read and two looks from a ship at height. The ring was drawn round it at four hundred yards. Inside the ring, nine thousand people on an ordinary day. He knew it by heart and looked at it anyway.

He had his sense out, as always, and felt her come up the stair: the quick bright note, a little tired, the step that did not hurry and did not dawdle either. She did not knock. She came round the end of the table to the head chair, where Tess's control stone sat under its glass lid on the arm, and did not sit in it; she sat on the arm of the next one, with the grey book in her lap, and looked at the capital with him for a while.

"It's been three weeks," she said.

He knew at once. She had said it in this room on the night she put the capital's plaster on the tray, with the cathedral lit for the first time between them: ask me again in a week, and in a fortnight, and if it's the same plan in a fortnight it's a plan. She had written it in her book. He had seen her write it. He had thought about it on the seventh day and the fourteenth, and both times waited for her to say it, and both times she had not.

"You said a week."

"I said a week and a fortnight. Then the proclamation came, and Saban came, and you went up the keep stair with a slip of paper and came down again in the morning with a face I hadn't seen on you since the plague year." She turned a page in the grey book without looking at it. "So I didn't ask on the fourteenth. It would have been a different question. I wanted to ask it after the things that change it, not before."

"And now?"

"Now I'm asking." She looked up at him. "Is it the same plan?"

He looked at the close. He made himself do it properly, the way she would have done it, line by line, as if it were somebody else's sum. Every soul out before anything falls. The day and the hour and the distance printed and dropped. The count. The rule at the head of it. And then the things Saban had brought home in a hat band: a chest the size of a cradle under every pair of Venn's; forty-four men in grey with stones that took a post off at forty paces; a soldier asleep on every kitchen floor in the lower town, and a householder's paper for his board, to be paid after.

"No," he said. "It's not the same."

She waited.

"It's worse," he said. "When I drew it in August the capital was a city with a cathedral in it. Now it's a city he's built to make us kill people. Every barracks empty. Every gun on a tower with a chest underneath it, so the minute we come near enough to be any use he can put the whole city dead, wall to wall, and every lamp and stone in it out, and me with them, as near as matters." He did not take his eyes off the yellow ground. "The cathedral's the only thing in it now that he can't fill with people's children. He's filled everything else. That's not anger. I've been angry. I'd know." He put his finger on the bell tower. "It's the one target he's left us. So it's still first."

Ferra let that sit. She was not looking at the close. She was looking at him.

"That's the same plan," she said at last. "With more reasons."

"Yes."

"Then it's a plan." She took her old brown book out from under the grey one, the book she had written in on the twentieth of August, and found the page; and under her own line from that night, ask again, a week, she wrote the date, and then, in her small upright figures, asked, and then: same. Reasons added: towers, chests, quartering. Not anger. F. She capped the pen. "I'll hold you to the rule. Nothing falls with anybody in it."

"I know."

"I don't mean the leaflets. I mean when you're hanging over it with the whole fleet behind you, and it's past noon, and some old canon won't come down off the bell tower, and Ward's telling you the Lieutenant's had six hours to move his guns." She stood up off the chair arm. "That's when I mean."

"I know when you mean."

"Good." She tucked the book under her arm. Then she stood a moment longer, looking at the lit town on the table, the roofs and the river and the dull red wall; and her face did something he did not expect, and was gone before he could put a name to it.

"I'm taking the war to Vantic in the morning," she said. "All of it. What it costs and what we've got. She'll want to see it before you ask her for anything, and I'd rather she saw it from me." She paused at the head of the stair. "You'll want to come. Don't speak till she's read it."



Before the morning, there was Saban.

He came up the hill at seven with a slip that had not come down in a laundry bag. It had come down the coast instead, from the port at Kesrath, on a fishing boat out of Rowena's water that put in at Kaldo's north quay twice a year for net twine and had carried Saban's letters both ways since the year of the camp; and it was in his man's hand, a cramped quick hand like a clerk's in a hurry, on the back of a chandler's bill.

"You'll remember the forty sail," said Saban, in the study, to Emeric and the king. Harrold had come down early for his tea and stayed, as he did now, in the window seat with the cup, as if the study were a room he passed through on the way to somewhere else. "Kesrath's. In the roads since March. Varek's been on the strait across from them with half the crown's host the whole summer, and that's why he wasn't in the capital in April, and why he's not in it now."

"I remember," said Harrold.

"My man in Kesrath's port has been wondering since June who pays forty ships to sit at anchor for six months and do nothing. Kesrath's treasury can't. Everybody in Kesrath knows Kesrath's treasury can't; they say so in the taverns, which is how a man finds out what a treasury can do." Saban put the slip down. "So he went where the crews are paid. Every month a pay chest goes out to the ships in the roads from the harbourmaster's house. He stood a round in a tavern for a purser's mate off one of them, and then another, and asked to see what the man had been paid in, because he said he was a coin dealer and a man ought to know what he was carrying." He laid a coin on the desk beside the slip. It was not the token. It was a common silver piece, Kesrathi, a fish on the face of it. "Most of it was this. Kesrath's own. Some of it wasn't." He laid a second beside the first.

It was the size of a Silver Rand and twice as thick. A lamp on the face, a flame in a bowl. A walled house with a tower on the back. And round the rim, in square letters, what the Light holds, it keeps.

"Not that one," said Saban. "That's Doran's, that I've had in my waistcoat since July. My man couldn't buy one off the mate for love. But he drew it, and he drew the rim, and it's this. Ostrel's, out of the mother house. In a Kesrathi sailor's pay." He picked the token up again and put it back in his waistcoat. "Not a great deal of it. One in eight of what he saw, my man thinks. But you don't strike your silver in Ostrel and send it to a foreign harbour to pay a foreign king's sailors to sit still unless you want them sat still. And where they're sat, they keep Varek sat across from them."

Harrold put his cup down on the sill.

"They paid to keep my Marshal on the strait," he said.

"Since March, Majesty. Before the sitting. Before anybody in the capital knew there'd be a sitting, bar the men who'd bought the vote."

Nobody said anything for a time. Out in the yard the knights were at the forms, slow, in the early light; Emeric could feel Ward's note at the rack and the line of them moving together like a field of barley in one wind.

"Varek should know," said Emeric.

"Varek should know," Harrold agreed. "Varek won't move." He picked his cup up again. "He'll read it, and he'll believe it, and he'll still not move, because forty sail at anchor are forty sail at anchor whoever paid for them, and if he takes his men off that shore Kesrath will be across it inside a week. They'll have paid for that too." He drank. "Write to him. Tell him what Saban's man found. Tell him I said he's right to stay, before he has to write and tell me so."

The letter went the same day, by the same boat's road in reverse, in Ferra's figures. Varek's answer did not come for a fortnight, and when it came it was six lines, in a soldier's hand that pressed through the paper, and the last of them said only that the sail were still real.



The tithe house stood on the square across from the records hall, where it had stood since before Emeric came south: a narrow old building of grey stone with a newer door, and a brass rail down the long front room behind which the House's clerks sat at their desks in a row like birds on a wire. The House had kept its Kaldo office there since the covenant. Since Vantic took the House and moved its seat south, the building had been the House; the capital office in the street behind the palace was her eldest brother's now, with the crown's book in it and none of the rest, and its brass plate had been taken down and put up again with one word fewer on it.

Vantic saw them in the room behind the rail, at nine, before the doors opened. She was at her desk in her grey with her gloves on, as she always was, and she had cleared the desk of everything but a blotter and a pen. She did not offer them anything. She held out one gloved hand, and Ferra put the sum into it.

It was eleven sheets. Emeric had watched Ferra make it over four nights, at her table, with the grey book open on one side and the duchy's year books on the other, and had been told twice to go away; he knew its shape without having been let read it. What Kaldo had, on one side. What the war would cost, on the other, as near as anybody could say what a war would cost: the ships in the air and the coal and the Band runs to fill them, the shells and the powder and the rifles and the cartridges, the food, the field hospital Imani wanted built before and not after, the pay. And on the second sheet, where the pay began, the men it was for.

Three thousand on the rolls, in Ferra's upright figures, since Keller's letter took the old number off them: three thousand regulars, drilled, armed, and paid. Three hundred knights. Twelve hundred to crew the ships. The reserve behind them, uncounted, a few thousand works hands who had drilled on their rest days for years with rifles out of Ward's armoury and would go back to their benches if the war was short.

Vantic read the second sheet twice. She read the rest of it once, steadily, turning each sheet face down on the blotter as she finished it, the way Saban turned his slips; and Ferra sat across the desk with her hands folded on her knee and watched her read, and Emeric, who had been told not to speak, did not.

It took her the better part of an hour. At the end of it she squared the eleven sheets face down and laid her gloved hand flat on top of them.

"You've left the river dam in," she said.

"It's in the second column," said Ferra. "As a thing we keep building."

"I saw where it is." Vantic did not take her hand off the sheets. "And the towers. All of them. The twelfth fitted out by spring, and three more begun."

"Yes."

"And the margin, with everything left in, is a seventh."

"A seventh if nothing goes wrong," said Ferra. "A tenth if the things I've guessed go wrong. I've marked which."

"You have." Vantic looked at her. They had sat across a desk from each other, those two, for years, through the covenant and the repayment and the plague and Ferra's books opened to the House every quarter day; and Emeric, who had sat in rooms with both of them all that time, still could not always tell what passed between them in a look. Something did. Ferra's chin came up a little.

Then Vantic turned to him.

"Your Grace," she said. "Ask."

He had meant to ask it well. He had thought about it walking across the square: what to say first, how much of the why. In the event he only said it.

"Credit for the fleet," he said. "Against the mine and the trade. And notes. Paper, under the House's name, to pay the men, so that the coin stays in the chest for the things you can't pay for with paper." He made himself go on. "Not much. Not a tenth of it. Enough that Ferra's margin is a margin, and not a guess."

Vantic did not answer at once. She sat with her hand on the sheets.

"I'll give you an answer tomorrow," she said. "In writing."

He had expected that. He had not expected the way Ferra let her breath out beside him, very quietly, through her nose, as if she had heard something in it that he had not.



It was on the way out that it happened, and nobody but Emeric noticed, because it was over in a breath.

The doors had opened while they sat in the back. The long front room had its morning in it: the queue along the rail, carters and wives and a man from the dye works in his apron, and at the end desk a clerk with a sack of coin from the north road tipped out on a felt tray, counting it into tens and weighing each ten on a little brass balance before he wrote it in. Ferra stopped at his desk in passing, as she stopped at anybody's desk where money was being counted, and the clerk, who knew her, half rose; and she put out her hand and picked one coin off the counted pile, a half, a capital half with the old king's head on it worn nearly flat, and turned it over in her fingers the way she might have picked up a pebble on a beach.

And stopped.

Emeric was beside her and felt it, because his sense was on her as it was always on her, a little: a check in her note, small, like a foot that has come down on a stair that is not quite where it was. She was looking at the half in her palm. She did not weigh it in her hand, or hold it to the light, or look at its edge. She only held it, flat, and looked at it with her brows drawn down a little, as she looked at a column that she had not added yet and knew was wrong.

"Weigh that one," she said, "by itself."

The clerk did. He put it alone in the brass pan and set the half weight against it, and the beam went down on the weight's side and stayed there; and he looked at it, and took the coin out and turned it to the window, and ran his thumbnail round the rim, and found where somebody had taken a thin paring off a third of the edge and milled the cut over with a file until you could not see it unless you knew to look.

"A grain and a half light, Mistress," he said. "Near two." He looked at her. "How did you..."

"It felt wrong," said Ferra.

She put it down on his desk, apart from the rest. She did not look at Emeric. She went on out of the door into the square with the grey book under her arm, and he followed her, and halfway across the square she said, without turning her head, "Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to ask how I knew." She walked. "I don't know how I knew. It was wrong in my hand. I've handled clipped coin since I was nine and I've never once known it in my hand before the scale." She stopped by the plane trees, where the line had stood to look at their names in August, and looked at her own palm. "I'll write it down. I'll see if it happens again. If it doesn't, it was a guess and I was lucky."

"And if it does?"

"Then I'll want to know why," said Ferra, "and I'll find out, and I'll write that down too." She put her hand in her pocket. "Go and look at your ship. You've a face like a man waiting for a letter."



The letter came at eight the next morning, to the castle, in the House's own paper, by a clerk who waited at the gate for no answer and walked back down the hill.

It was one sheet, in Vantic's hand, which was small and very even and had no flourish anywhere in it.

The House of Ducatis would not lend to the duchy of Kaldo against the war it meant to make, nor issue notes under its name to pay the duchy's soldiers, nor buy the duchy's paper from any other house that did. Its reasons were in a second sheet, attached, and were the House's and not hers. It would continue to hold the duchy's accounts and the deposits of every person in the four baronies, and to do every ordinary business for them it did now. It had made the same answer, on the same terms, a fortnight before, to the chancery of His Highness the Lieutenant of the Realm, in answer to a like request from that office, and had written to the chancery that it would make the same answer to anybody. She remained, the letter said, with respect, His Grace's servant; and under it her own name, Livia Ducatis, written small, as she wrote it on nothing else he had ever seen.

He did not read the second sheet. He put both sheets in his coat and went down the hill.



She saw him at once. She had known he would come, he thought afterwards; she had cleared the desk again.

"Sit down, Your Grace."

"I'll stand."

"Then I'll sit." She did. She folded her gloved hands on the blotter and looked at him across it with the face she had given him on the twenty-fourth of April, when he asked her who bought the paper, and he found that he had come down the hill wanting her to look, just once, as if it cost her something, and that she was not going to.

"You've read Ferra's sum," he said. "You read all of it. You know what we've got and what it'll take."

"I know to the copper what you've got. Ferra doesn't put anything in a sum she can't stand behind." She did not look down at the place on the desk where the sheets had been. "You can pay for this war out of what you own, Your Grace. The mine and the trade and the market deck, and the reserve. With a seventh over, if Ferra's right. She's usually right. With a tenth, if she isn't. You don't need the House's money. You asked for it so you wouldn't have to find out whether the seventh holds."

"Yes."

"That's a reason to want it. It isn't a reason to have it." She let that sit. "And it isn't why I said no. I'd have said no if Ferra's sheet had shown you short by half. You'll want the reasons. You've not read them; you've got them in your coat. Shall I say them, or will you stand there?"

He had them in his coat. He did not take them out.

"Say them," he said.

"Four." She did not count them on her fingers. She was not Ward. "One. If the Lieutenant's army were at Kaldo's gate tomorrow, I would lend you the whole of the House to hold it. Every Gold in the vault and every note I can print, at no share at all, and stand on the wall myself with the clerks. A house that won't lend to keep its own depositors' doors shut isn't a bank, it's a strongbox with opinions. But that isn't what's in Ferra's sheet. Ferra's sheet is ten ships flying seven hundred miles to another man's city to knock his church down. That's not a defence. That's a war you choose. And the House doesn't pay for wars that men choose, because there is no end to them, and they're always going to be short."

He opened his mouth, and shut it.

"Two," said Vantic. "A house that lends to one side of a war between two princes is a party to it. Not in a sermon; in law. If you lost, and you could lose, and you know you could or you wouldn't have asked me for a margin, the first thing your brother does after he has his throne back is take this house as a rebel's banker. Everything in it. Every carter's Silver in the long room and every widow's savings and the three hundred parishes' seed corn. Every depositor in four baronies, ruined, because their bank chose a prince." She did not raise her voice. "I don't hold their money so I can bet it on you. I hold it so it's there in the morning."

"He'd take it anyway."

"He'd want to. He'd find it very hard. He'd have to stand up in front of the council and say he'd seized the bank of a hundred thousand people who'd done nothing, because it was in the wrong town." She folded her hands the other way. "Lords bank with us too, Your Grace. Not many, in the north. Enough that he'd be asked."

"Three."

"Three, I refused him. You'll have read that. His chancery wrote in the week after the escheat, asking the House to carry the crown's war paper, since the crown's book in the capital couldn't carry all of it. I said no by return." For the first time something moved at the corner of her mouth, and it was not amusement. "So he went to my brother. He was always going to go to my brother. My brother's book is the crown's book, and it'll carry anything he puts in it, and it'll go on carrying it until the day it can't, and then everybody who ever lent through it will find out at once." She looked at him steadily. "When this is over, whoever wins, there has to be one house left in this kingdom that both sides trust. That both sides know said no to them. Because the morning after the war somebody is going to have to lend the crown the money to mend what you've broken, and pay what your brother's run up, and it had better not be a house that helped either of you do it."

Emeric stood at her desk with his hands at his sides.

"And four," he said.

Vantic looked at him a long moment.

"Four is you," she said. "And you won't like four. So I'll say it once." She did not lower her eyes. "If you win, Your Grace, you will very probably be king. Not this year. Soon. Your father won't sit on that bench by the wall for ever, and there's nobody else he'd give it to, and everybody in this room and in that capital knows it. And a king who won his war with a bank behind him learns one thing from it, which is that wars can be paid for with paper. He doesn't mean to learn it. He just does." She unfolded her hands. "I'd rather you learned the other thing. That you paid for it with what you had, and it hurt, and you counted it."

He did not say anything. He found he had nothing he wanted to say that he would not be sorry for, and he was not sure he would be sorry.

So he said one of them anyway.

"You'd have lent it him," he said, "if he'd asked you nicely and paid the share."

Vantic did not move at all.

"I refused him a fortnight before I refused you," she said. "I've told you so in writing, and now to your face." She picked up her pen, and put it down again, square to the blotter. "Good morning, Your Grace."



He was angry for the rest of the day, and knew it, and could not stop.

He went to the airship yard because it was the farthest place from the square that was still his, and stood in the long shed under the K Amara's black flank, where she lay in her cradle with her lift stones in and her trim stones going in, and Fletcher's men up her sides on the staging like flies on a wall; and Fletcher came and stood beside him for a while and looked at his face and then at the ship and asked him nothing at all, which was why Fletcher was Fletcher. He went to the works and stood at the end of Bram's floor, where the flight suits hung in their rows on the racks, matte and empty, their nodes dark under the violet glass; and counted them, because he could not help it, and made it a hundred and forty. He went up the hill again by the Gate Avenue and stopped under the twelfth tower, where the glaziers were at work on the eighth floor and the crane on its crown was swinging a crate of panes up out of the street; and stood with his head back and watched the crate go up, and thought about margins.

Ferra found him there at four.

She did not say anything about Vantic. She did not say that she had known on the morning she put the sheets in Vantic's hand what the answer would be, though he understood by then that she had; that was what her breath had been, beside him, at the word tomorrow. She stood under the tower beside him and looked up at the crate too.

"I've been at it since nine," she said. "Since you went down the hill."

"At what?"

"Paying for it." She opened the grey book. It had a new page, ruled, with the date at the head. "She's right that we can. She's right that it's a seventh. I'd rather it was a fifth, so I've found you the difference." She ran her finger down the page without reading it to him. "The mine's next quarter, sold forward to the Grayrock smelters at this quarter's price; they'll take it, they've been asking for two years. The market deck's first year, sold forward too, city by city, the way the K Amara's meant to sell it in peacetime; Saban's got buyers in four towns already who'll pay now for cloth they'll get in the spring. The trade stays the trade." She turned the book so he could see the last line. "And the towers."

He looked up.

"The twelfth gets its glass," said Ferra, "because the glass is bought and stacked and it'll break if it stands. Then it stops. The fitting out, the lifts, the floors. It stands the winter a shell with a crane on its head, and the three that aren't begun aren't begun. The men go to Fletcher and Bram." She shut the book. "It's not nothing. It's two hundred men off one job and on another, and the town looking up at that crane every morning wondering why it's stopped. They'll know why."

"They'll know it's the war."

"They'll know the war costs something." She looked at him. "That's the point. That's her point, if you want it. I didn't need her to tell me. But she was right to."

The crane swung the crate in at the eighth floor and somebody inside took it. Up on the crown of the tower the craneman leaned out of his cab and shouted something down to the street, and a boy at the foot of it shouted back.

"I said something to her I shouldn't have," said Emeric.

"I know. Her clerk told mine." Ferra put the book under her arm. "You can tell her tomorrow. Not today. Today you're still cross, and she'd know, and she'd think less of you for coming before you meant it." She took his arm, briefly, the way she took it on a stair, and let it go. "Come home. Ward wants you. He's got a list."



Ward's list was the knights.

They met in the long room after supper, with the lamps lit and the windows shut because the evening had turned cold all at once, the way September turned in Kaldo. Ward had the rolls on the table, the new ones in Ottoline's ruling, with three hundred names on the knights' pages and a column against each for what he had: the rite or the yard or the Proving; the suit or no suit; the hours.

"A hundred and nine of these have flown," said Ward. "Off the Hod and the aprons and the island. A hundred and nine in three years. The rest have stood on a deck." He put his finger on the column. "The Church has ships. You told this table so in July, and you drew one, and Armand said there was no such thing, and you were right. If they come up at us, they'll come up at us in the air. And a knight who has never been in the air is a man in plate standing on a deck, waiting to be thrown off it."

"Then they fly," said Emeric.

Ward looked at him.

"All of them," said Emeric. "Every knight on that roll. Not the aprons; not the hundred and nine. Three hundred. Every one of them in a suit and off a rail and into the air before the fleet goes north, and back onto a deck that's moving, at night, without lights." He found he was not angry any more, or that the anger had gone into this and was useful here. "We've ten weeks. Perhaps eleven."

"We've a hundred and forty suits," said Bram.

"How many can you make?"

Bram looked at the ceiling, as he did when he was adding, and was not adding; he was looking at his floor in his head, the racks and the presses and the girls at the node benches. "With the yard's men off the towers," he said slowly, "and Tess's line on the hearts. Twelve a week. Fifteen, if nobody wants anything else." He looked at Ferra. "Somebody'll want something else."

"Somebody always does," said Ferra. She was writing. "Twelve a week from the fifteenth is three hundred by the second week of December. Fifteen is three hundred by the end of November." She looked up. "When does the fleet go?"

"Before December."

"Then it's fifteen," said Ferra, "and nobody wants anything else," and wrote it down, and Bram said something in his beard that was not quite agreement.

"Where do they learn?" said Imani. "Not here. You can't drop three hundred men in plate over a town that's watching."

"The island," said Ward.

Emeric looked at him; and Ward looked back, and Emeric saw that he had had this on the list as well, further down, and had been waiting for Emeric to get to it, which was Ward's way when he knew he was right and wanted his lord to have got there by himself.

"Two hundred miles of sea under every drill," said Ward. "Nobody to see. The pods are there, the moorings are there, there's a bay to fall into and boats to fish them out of it. Fennick can burst a stone over the water as often as Ferra lets him." He turned the roll round on the table. "The first hundred go out on the Hod and hull two at the end of the week. Wicket's been asked."

"Asked what?" said Armand, from the head of the table.

"To teach them to swim," said Ward. "Half of them can't."

Armand looked delighted. "Knights," he said. "In the shallows. With a fisherman."

"An old fisherman."

"Even better," said Armand, and drank his wine, and would not be drawn on why.



He woke in the night, in the dark, with the thing worked out, the way a sum sometimes worked itself out while he slept and was lying there finished when he woke.

It was not what she had said. It was the order she had said it in. She had told him one, two and three, which were the House's, and kept four, which was his, for last; and four was the only one that was not about the House at all. One, two and three were why she could not lend. Four was why she was glad not to. She had not refused him in spite of thinking he would be king. She had refused him because she did. She had looked at him across her clean desk and seen a king who would one day be able to print his way into a war whenever a war seemed short, and she had taken the press away from him before he knew he had one.

He lay and looked at the ceiling. Imani was asleep against his back, warm, with one hand flat between his shoulder blades as if she had been taking his pulse there and drifted off in the middle of it. Ferra was asleep on his other side with her face in the pillow and her book on the floor where it had slid off the bed.

He had a thing he wanted to tell Vantic, and it was not an apology, though there would be one of those too. It was that he had not known until four in the morning that anybody had ever done that for him; taken a thing out of his hands, on purpose, because of what he might do with it later and not what he had done.

He went down the hill at nine. She saw him in the back. He said the second thing first, because it was easier, and she listened to it with her gloved hands folded on her blotter, and when he had finished she inclined her head a quarter of an inch.

"Yes," she said. "That's four."

Then he said the first thing, and she listened to that too.

"I know," she said. "It was a fair thing to think of a banker. Most of them would have." She picked up her pen. "Not this one. Go and build your ships, Your Grace. I'll be here in the morning."



The first hundred went out to South Sentinel Island on the Hod and hull two on the twentieth, with their suits in canvas bags at their feet and their faces very set; and old Wicket went out with them, on the Hod, in his oilskins, sitting on a coil of line by the stern rail with his pipe going upside down against the wind, refusing to look over the side.

"I've been to sea," he told Ned Tallis, who had asked. "I've been to sea fifty-one years. I've never been to sea upside down and I'm not starting."

Emeric went out with them, because Ward did, and because he wanted to see it begin. He watched from the island's bluff on the first morning as Wicket stood up to his waist in the bay's shallows, in the cold clear water over the white sand, in his oilskin trousers and his shirt and his hat, and twenty knights stood in a row in front of him up to their chests in their shirts, and he told them, without taking his pipe out of his mouth, that the sea did not care who they were. Then he showed them how to lie on it. Half of them sank. One of them, a big full-traced sergeant from Grayrock with a neck like a bollard, went straight to the bottom like a dropped anvil and stood there, looking up, until Wicket reached down and took him by the hair.

"Breathe in," Wicket told him, when he had him up and coughing. "Then don't breathe out. You're full of iron, man. You'll want all the air God gave you."

Ward stood on the beach with his book and wrote it all down and said nothing. Emeric saw that his mouth had gone very slightly crooked at one corner and stayed so for some time.

He went down the shell track to the Dust House in the afternoon to find Tess.

She was at the bench under the window with the presses behind her, sitting on a high stool with a stick hooked over the edge of the bench beside her, because she stood now when she worked and sat when her knees told her to, and had made Bram build her a stool to her new height so that she could do both. The bench was covered in tiles. They were little square tiles, the size of a thumb's length, printed in the Motion Shop on Bram's dust press out of every kind of dust the island's mill had sorted, each one labelled on its back in her hand; and she was laying a glaze over them, one kind on another, with a fine brush out of a pot, and setting them in a row along the bench to dry, each with its keystone set in a dimple in one corner like a jewel in a ring.

"I'm finding out what they do," she said, without looking round. "Stacked. Nobody's ever stacked them. Bram's done every one by itself, and every one does the thing it does, and nobody's put one on top of another to see." She set a tile down. "It's very boring. Mostly nothing happens."

The kettle was on the little stove by the door, beginning to sing. She had a meter on the bench beside her, one of Merrin's, a needle in a brass case with two leads on clips, because she put a meter on anything she was testing in case it did something she had not asked it to; and the leads were clipped to the tile in front of her, a fire-dust glaze, dull orange, laid over a lightning-dust tile printed grey, both keyed. The needle sat on its pin at nothing.

Emeric took the kettle off the stove, because it was boiling and she had not noticed, and set it on the bench to make the tea; and put it down, as it happened, a hand's breadth from the tile.

The needle moved.

It did not move far. It came off its pin a hair and sat there, trembling a little, the way a needle sits on a weak cell.

Tess was looking at it. She had stopped with the brush in her hand. Emeric had not seen it; he was pouring. Then he saw her face and looked where she was looking.

"Move the kettle," said Tess.

He moved it away. The needle went back to its pin. He moved it back. The needle came off.

"Again," said Tess.

He did it three times. Each time the kettle came near the tile the needle stood up off the pin, and each time it went away the needle lay down. Tess put the brush in the pot. She reached out with both hands, and picked the tile up off the bench with its leads still clipped to it, and turned it over, grey side up, orange side down, and set it back where it had been beside the kettle.

The needle went the other way.

It went off its pin backwards, to the left, below nothing, and sat there trembling.

Tess looked at it. Then she looked at the tile, at the place where the orange glaze met the grey under it, which was nothing at all to look at, a line along the edge of a tile so thin you could not have put a fingernail into it.

"It's the edge," she said.

"What is?"

"It's not the fire. The fire's on top, it's just warm. It's not the lightning. The lightning's just lying there. It's where they touch." She turned the tile over again, and the needle went right again. "It's the edge. It's coming out of the edge. And it goes the way the edge goes."

He knew what it was. He had a word for it, and the word was in a book in his head, and he did not say it; he did not have to. He watched her pull her bench book across and open it at a clean page and write across the top, in her square capitals, very large, SEAMS; and under it, the date; and under that, fire over lightning, and an arrow, and current, and then, smaller, kettle, and then, after a moment, E. made the tea.

"I did make the tea," he said.

"You did." She was ruling a grid. Eight across, eight down. She wrote the names of the stones down the side, and then along the top, in the same order. "You'll have to make a lot more of it," she said. "There's sixty-four of these."



The K Endeavor went north on the dark of the moon, in the third week of September, with the month's empty tanks and one more thing aboard than usual; and Emeric went with her, because the one more thing was for him.

She was the oil ship and had been for two years: once a month, on the moonless night, north in the Band at her own pace with her side pods empty, and down to the cove under the Isle's west cliffs, where she hung a few feet over the swell in the dark while Teague went down the line in his brass helmet and canvas dress to couple the hose to the riser, and the sea itself pushed a month's oil up out of the bell on the bottom into her pods. Then up, and home before dawn. Nothing to hear. Nothing to see, if the night was dark enough and the crew put their pipes out. Cade had her helm. He had sworn at every one of the runs, Fennick said, in a low continuous undertone, from the moment they left the Band to the moment they climbed back into it, and claimed it was the only thing that kept her steady.

Fennick was aboard tonight. He was the one more thing, he and the tin he had on his knees on the bench in the K Endeavor's hold, wrapped in oilcloth, with its long waxed fuse coiled round it like a sleeping snake.

"One stick," he said, to nobody, for the third time since Kaldo. "One. She's close in to the face down there, close, the rock's that tight round them you'd think it was poured. One's plenty." He patted the tin. "I'd like two. I'm not getting two."

"You're not getting two," said Emeric.

"I know I'm not."

It was the third time. Ferra had said so at the table, plainly, with the stone book open in front of her: twice since the winter Fennick had put a charge down on the seam under those cliffs, for the pin stones, and twice the sea had thumped. Every stone that was cut there was cut in a dead zone that the blast blew out of the rock, and a dead zone did not go quietly. Anybody with a sense on the Isle would feel it go, like a door slammed in a house at night. Anybody awake on Kesrath's tower at the island's far end, with the fire fields and the lightning fields round its foot and a high mage on its top who had sat over those fields for thirty years, would feel it. Twice was a thing a man might put down to the sea. Three times was a habit.

"The oil runs are silent," Ferra had said. "The stone runs never will be."

"So we don't do many," Emeric had said, and she had looked at him over the stone book as if to say that she had said that, two years ago, and had he forgotten.

This was one. One stone, for the Well. For the shell it would carry four miles up, and the pin to hold it there against the wind when the war was over the capital and nobody could come to it; one stone, cut and coded and given to Tess to write. The station would go up whether it had a shell or not. It would not go up safely without one.

The cove came up under them a little after one.

He felt it before Cade said it: the Isle's mana rising under the hull as she came down out of the Band, the thin even taste of the sea thickening all at once into the island's, iron and salt and old stone and somewhere under the cliffs the hot close prickle of the lightning fields to the east, and under all of it, under the water, the seam. He had felt the seam twice from the deck of this ship. It was like nothing else he knew: a cold, held, waiting density, as if something under the sea had drawn a breath and was keeping it, and had been keeping it since before there were men.

The K Endeavor came down to hang over the swell, and Cade stopped swearing, which meant he had her where he wanted her. The hatch in her belly came open. Below it in the dark the sea moved, black, slow, and the air that came up through the hatch was cold and smelled of weed.

Teague went down the line first, to couple the hose, as he did every month; and the hold was quiet while the oil came up, a low long gurgle in the pods like a big animal drinking, and Emeric stood at the hatch's edge and watched the line go down into the water and felt Teague's note go down with it, steady and small, a long quiet lighterman's son humming to himself inside a brass helmet on the bottom of the sea.

When the pods were full and the hose was off, Teague came up for the tin.

That was the dangerous part, Fennick said, and Fennick was the man who knew. He handed the tin down the line into Teague's canvas glove at the water, and Teague took it down to the foot of the cliff where the seam ran out under the rock in a face as clear as old ice, and found the biggest lump in the face by touch and by the lamp in its sealed tin on his chest, and wedged the charge against it, and lit the fuse with the match that Fennick had made for him, which burned under water. And then he came away from it as fast as a man can come away from anything in a canvas dress on the bottom of the sea, which was not fast.

The fuse was two minutes. Fennick counted them aloud under his breath at the hatch.

And this was why Emeric was there, and not in his bed.

When the stone took the blast and burst, a breath after it, and blew the mana out of the rock all round it, everything in the dead zone it left behind would stop. The seam would stop guarding itself. Teague would have three minutes, a little more, before the rock breathed back in from round about and the stones he had not cut woke up again, and in those three minutes he would cut one, as he had cut two in December; and then it would lie in his glove on the bottom of the sea with its own mana coming back into it out of the water, waking. In December they had drained what they cut, a little, by hand, Teague's own bare hand on it in the hatch, before it woke enough to fight, and Teague had gone about humming like a knight for two days. That was fine for a stone that was going to sit in a bucket in Kaldo till somebody had time to code it.

This one was going to be a shell. It wanted every grain of its charge kept. It wanted taking from Teague's glove the moment it came up out of the water, before it was awake, and putting to sleep from the inside, one line, before it knew it had been moved. And it wanted doing a hundred and fifty yards from where the ship could hang, because the cliff's foot was in the surf, and no ship could hang in surf, and no boat could go in there in the dark without a lamp and a noise.

"Ninety," said Fennick, at the hatch.

Emeric went down the line.

He went down it hand over hand to the last knot, a yard above the water, and hung there in the dark with the swell going under his boots, black, lifting and falling; and then he let go of the line and stepped down onto it.

He had done it in the Mere in August, at night, with Rylan sitting in a boat at the edge of the reeds in case, and once in the bay off the island at dawn with Fletcher in the boat instead, who had taken his hat off and not put it back on for some minutes. It was not the step he used on a wall, which pressed a man to a surface; on water that only sank him faster. It was the other thing. He pushed down, through the soles of his boots, against the weight of the water under each one, the way a held stone pushed against the ground, so that a patch of sea the size of a boot took his weight for a breath the way ice takes a skater's; and then that water ran out from under the push, as water did, and the next boot had to be down on fresh water before it went.

So he walked. He could not stand still. Standing still, he would be in to the knee in three breaths and to the waist in ten. He walked across the cove in the dark toward the foot of the cliffs, quickly, with his knees loose and his weight low the way he had worked out in the bay, on the face of each swell as it lifted, short steps; and each step left a small dimple in the black water behind him that filled in as he lifted his foot, and the cold came in over the tops of his boots to the ankle and stayed there. There was no wind on him from it. There was no glyph. There was nothing at all to see, if anybody had been on the cliff to see it, but a man walking on the sea at night with wet boots.

"Hundred and ten," said Fennick, far behind him, under his breath.

He came to Teague's line, where it ran down off the float into the water at the cliff's foot, and found the float by feel, and walked a slow small circle round it because he could not stop, and put his sense down through the water to the bottom.

Teague was there. Thirty feet down, on the rock, at the foot of the face, his note gone still and careful, not humming now; and away to the left of him, against the clear face, the tin, and the small hot spark of the fuse inside the oilcloth, burning down in the water.

Fennick's count reached a hundred and twenty.

The stone went.

He felt it as a blow, the way he had felt the overloaded stones under Aldane's men on the stream ground, and the cellar, and the yard: a fist of mana, slamming out of the rock from one point, in every direction at once; and then the wind. It came up through the water and out of it into the air, past him, through him, a wind that moved nothing, not a hair on his head nor a ripple on the black swell under his feet, only the mana itself, all of it, going out from the cliff in a ring as fast as the ring could go, and leaving behind it nothing.

His feet went through the sea.

He had known they would. He had told himself so on the line, and told Fennick so, and told Ferra so before she would let him go at all, and she had made him say the rest of it back to her. In a dead zone the push was nothing. The water had nothing to push against. He went in to the waist before he had finished knowing it, and to the chest, cold as a slap, and caught Teague's float with both arms as it went by his face, and hung on it in the dead black water while the wind went over and away, and could not feel anything at all: not Teague, not the stone, not the ship, not Fennick's count, not his own hands on the float. Nothing. The stream ground. A man in the dark with his eyes put out.

He counted. Ferra had made him promise to count, aloud, so that he would know how long it had been and not guess, and he did it with his chin on the float and his teeth shut so that they would not chatter. Ten. Twenty. Somewhere below him in the nothing Teague was cutting. He could not feel it. Forty. He could hear it: very faint, through the water and the float, the knock of the pick going in, like a man splitting kindling three rooms away. Eighty. The knock stopped. A hundred.

At a hundred and ninety the sea came back.

It came back as Teague had said it would in December, like the tide, like somebody leaning on a door: the mana rolling in from round about, out of the rock and the water and the air, filling the place where it had been, and his sense came back with it in a rush, all of it at once, so that he gasped on the float. The ship, high and steady over the cove, with Fennick at the hatch and Cade swearing again. The cliff. The seam, waking, stone by stone along the face, sullen. And Teague, coming up the line below him, slowly, hand over hand, with something in his left glove held against his chest that was waking too.

He got his feet back. He pushed down on the sea and the sea took him, and he came up out of the water to the knees, to the shins, to the ankles, streaming, and stood on it, walking his small circle round the float with his clothes pouring water off him into the dark; and Teague's helmet came up out of the swell at the float beside him, and the glass of it turned toward him, and stopped.

Inside it, by the light of the battery lamp on his chest, Teague's long quiet face was staring at Emeric's boots.

Emeric held out his hand.

Teague gave him the stone.

It was the size of a goose's egg and clear as water, faint blue at the thick edge, and it was waking in his palm. He felt it come up out of its sleep the way a man comes up out of his, all at once and angry, the way the two in December had been angry in the bucket, gathering itself, beginning to push back against the hand that held it; and he did not let it. He went into it from the inside, through the skin of it, as fast as he had ever gone into anything, and found the door that every stone had in it, that a stone left open until somebody wrote on it, and wrote one line on the inside of the door before the stone could shut it.

Sleep.

It slept.

He stood on the sea with it in his hand and breathed for a while. Teague hung on the float in his helmet and watched him do it. Then Teague lifted one canvas glove off the float, slowly, and put two fingers to the glass of his helmet in the place where his forehead was, the way the port's lightermen touched their caps to a man on the quay; and went back down the line for the pick.

They were in the Band by half past two. Emeric sat in the hold in a blanket with his teeth going and a cup of Cade's tea in his hands that had more of something else in it than tea, and the stone asleep in a padded box on his knees, and Fennick sitting opposite looking at him with his white salt eyebrows drawn down.

"You went in," said Fennick.

"I went in."

"To the chest. I saw you go." Fennick looked at the box. "Ferra said you would."

"Ferra said I would."

"She told me to watch you do it and tell her how long." Fennick was quiet a moment. "Hundred and ninety. I'll tell her a hundred and eighty. She'll know."

From the helm, up the ladder, Cade's voice came down, not swearing.

"Light on the tower," he said.

Emeric put the cup down and went up.

The K Endeavor was climbing, high and quiet, with her stones dimmed down to nothing and the Isle falling away behind and below her in the dark. Off her stern quarter, far to the east along the island's long black back, where Kesrath's tower stood on its headland over the fire fields, there was a light. One light, very small, high up, where the top of the tower would be. There had been no light there when they came down. Emeric had looked.

It did not move. It did not go out. It burned on the top of the tower, steady, a single point of lamplight in the dark over the sea; and while he watched it, a second light came up below it, and a third, lower, along the headland, at the watch platforms; and then one more, far down at the shore, at the place where Kesrath kept its boats.

"He felt it," said Cade.

"He felt it," said Emeric.

They watched the lights until the Isle went under the curve of the dark and they could not see them any more. Then Cade turned the K Endeavor south for home, and began, very quietly, to swear again.
