Chapter 203 - Thin Smoke

The gale came up the coast on the ninth day after the equinox, as Fletcher had said it would, and sat down on Kaldo like a dog on a doorstep.

Tessaly Rook walked the wall in it anyway. She had walked a wall every evening of her working life, the whole length of whatever wall she had, with her hand on the stone, and she did not see why a wind should stop her. At Carrow Ford it had taken her a quarter of an hour. Here it took the better part of two, and she had to lean into the gusts on the western arc where nothing broke them, with her hood tied under her chin and her skirts bound up, and on the second evening Rafe came with her because he had seen her go out and did not think she should go out alone. She did not tell him she had walked walls in worse. She let him come.

The flat below them had stopped. Every gang had gone in. The mortar boards stood upended against the scaffolds with stones on them, and the scaffolds themselves were lashed and groaning, and on the gate towers the canvas the masons had rigged over the green courses cracked like sailcloth and held. In the second quarter, where they had been putting up houses a new way since the last weeks of the summer, the great iron frame the wallers called the Walker stood on its rails over the half-built row with a sheet of tarred canvas lashed over its carriage and its hoppers, like a horse blanketed in a field. Nothing moved on it. Its lamps on their masts at the row's ends were dark. Hewitt's setters had been out on the first morning of the gale, in oilskins, setting quoins by hand in the rain along the next row, because a quoin did not need the machine; and on the second morning Weekes had sent them home, because a quoin set in that wind would not stay set.

She had watched the Walker for three weeks before the gale came. She had stood by its rails at the end of her own work, the way half the flat did, and watched it lay its grey out of that square iron mouth, course on course, and she had put her hand, once, when the works man was not looking, flat on the iron collar round the nozzle, and felt nothing. Nothing at all. Iron, cold, wet with the mist. It was a die, the works man had told her when he did look, and she had said of course it was, and taken her hand away.

It was a very good die. It made the mortar stand up on its own.

On the third evening of the gale the lights over the dock were still on.

She saw them from the south arc, as she saw them every evening, over the fold of the land where the yard lay at the ridge's foot with its wall round it. Four white lights on masts, hard as stars, even in the rain, with the rain going through their glow in long bright slants. She had been looking at those lights every evening since the late summer, when they came on for the first time, and she had told herself every evening that she was not looking at them.

She knew what they were not. She had known since the first white lights came on at the quarry, on their two masts over the face where the night gang cut stone, and she had walked up there after dark and stood under one and reached for it the way any mage reaches for a working, and found nothing to reach. No stone. Not a dead stone or a sleeping one; nothing. The light came out of a thing of glass and brass that had no working in it anywhere, down a wire from a shed by the sawmill race, where a wheel turned in the water, and the wheel was only a wheel. She had stood under that light for a long time. She had not told Armand, because there had been nothing to tell. A wheel turned a thing that made light. That was all it was. It was only very strange.

And the dock's lights were like the quarry's. She was sure of that, though she had never been near them. They had the same hard white colour, not the yellow of oil or the blue-white of a lamp stone. They came down a wire. She had watched the wire go up on its poles through the summer, a mile of it, from the yard gate up the slope and in at the south gate and round the ring road to the second tower's pump house, where it turned a pump; the whole flat had watched that. So there was something at the yard that did what the sawmill's wheel did, and it was not a wheel, because there was no race at the ridge foot for a wheel to turn in.

There was a stack. She could see it from here on a clear evening, over the yard wall, a brick chimney on a stone house in the upper corner of the yard by the rock. It was an engine house. She knew engine houses. She had lived three seasons on the flat beside the rail works and the converter and the quarry engine, and she knew what a stack looked like when the fire under it was driving something: black, and thick, and leaning off down the wind, and every time the load came on it went blacker.

The stack at the yard had never once looked like that. It was a thread. All summer, with four white lights on masts burning all night at the foot of it, and a night gang working under them, and a wire going up the slope to turn a pump, it had put out a thread of grey you had to look for, the kind of smoke a fire makes when it is banked for the night and turning nothing.

She had looked at that thread for the whole of the late summer and decided not to think about it.

On the fourth evening of the gale the lights over the dock were out.

She stood on the south arc with Rafe beside her and the rain driving into her face and looked at the place where they had been. The fold of the land was black. The yard was black. There was a lantern or two moving down there, and a glow from somebody's window, and nothing else.

And the stack was smoking.

She could not see it well in the dark and the rain. She could see enough. There was a light at the foot of it now, the red of a firebox with its door open, going on and off as somebody fed it, and above that, against the little paleness the town put on the underside of the cloud, a thick dark column bending off to the north on the gale, the way a stack's smoke bends when the fire is driving hard.

"They've lit it up," Rafe said. "At the yard."

"Yes."

"But the lights are out."

"Yes," said Tessaly.

She walked the rest of the wall. At the north gate the relay house was lit, and through its shutter she could hear the key going on the telegraph, the little dry clicking of it on its crocks that she heard every evening, telling somebody something up the line to Wayford. It had not stopped. On the east arc she stopped at the second tower, below her on the canal's edge, and put her hand on the parapet and felt for the tower as she always did, and it was full. Its pump was turning in its house. She could hear it through the wind, a low steady knock, and the float on the tower's gauge board stood where it always stood at dusk. The first quarter's windows were lit up and down its streets, lamp oil and candles, the way they had always been lit. Somebody was frying onions. The taps would be running.

Out on the island, four miles off across the fold and the lake, the gas lamps on the market street burned their small steady yellow, as they had since the spring. They had never been on the wire.

So: the pumps ran, and the telegraph clicked, and the gas burned, and the towers were full, and the dock was dark, and the works across the water was dark where the night shift should have been, and the Walker stood under its canvas. It was the city as it had been in the spring. Not worse. She noted that. Somebody had built it so that when the lights went out, nothing else did.

And the stack at the yard burned black to turn a pump, which was a thing a stack did. That was all it was doing. There was nothing at all left over for the lights.

It was dark four nights. On the fifth day the wind went round into the north-west and dropped, and by dusk the sky was clearing from the west in long cold bars of green, and the flat stank of wet lime and was loud with men pulling canvas off things.

That night she did not walk the wall, because she was tired, and because she had decided on the fourth night that she did not want to see. In the morning she walked it anyway, early, before the gangs were out, and that evening she walked it again, and at dusk she was on the south arc with Rafe, and the lights over the dock came on.

All four. Hard and white, as if they had never been out. Down in the yard, under them, she could hear the night gang coming back to the dock's walls, the ring of a hammer on a chisel, a man shouting for a line.

She looked at the stack. She had to look for it. It was a thread.

Rafe said something about the lights being back, and she said yes, and he said something else, and she did not hear what it was. She was standing very still with her hand on the parapet, the way she stood with her hand on a wall she had raised, feeling for a thing in the stone she could not name. She could feel nothing from here. It was a mile off, behind a wall, and whatever was in the engine house was shut in iron, as the Walker's collar was, as everything in Kaldo that mattered seemed to be shut in iron. She did not need to feel it.

There had been four nights with a fire burning black under that stack and no light over the dock. And now there was light over the dock and a thread of smoke. Whatever lit the dock was not the fire. It had not been the fire all summer. The fire was the thing that ran when the other thing stopped.

She thought of the tower on the first quarter's corner, and the day she had laid her hand on its stone while it was building and tasted something in the water she had no name for, and taken her hand away and not asked. She thought of the assessment hall, and Master Hebden writing one word very neatly in the column for the place of raising. She thought of the night she came home from the north and went into Armand's study and shut the door and told him all of it, every word, the ninth stone and the tenth and the boy's clean arms, and sat in his chair afterward with her hands shaking while he wrote something in a box of Ferra's with his back to her. She had promised herself on the stair going down that she would never again go anywhere without telling him why.

She did not say anything to Rafe.

She walked the rest of the wall with her hand on it, and went down the north stair, and went to her lodging, and did not go to Armand's. She told herself it was because it was late. She lay awake a long time listening to the Walker, a mile off across the second quarter, begin to click again under its lamps.

It was the first thing she had kept from him since the study.

The autumn quarter day fell a week after the gale, and Vantic came for it in the House's carriage, with her clerk and her courier and her small book, and sat across the loan table from Ferra in the counting room, and read Ferra's book.

Ferra watched her do it. She had watched her do it on every quarter day for eight years, and for most of them she had watched the way you watch weather, because it came and you had to be ready for it. Since the winter she watched differently. Since the winter she knew where the copying went.

Vantic read the way she always read, pen in hand, steady, a page at a time, copying into her own book the lines the House's instrument asked for and nothing it did not. The instrument asked for a great deal. It asked for the works, line by line, because the works were what the House had lent against: what they cost to run, what they made, what they sold it for, what was owed on them and to whom. It had asked for that since the first draw, and Ferra had given it, in the ordinary form, every quarter, because it was owed and because a book that hid a line from its creditor was a book that would be read by a magistrate in the end.

The works now had a line for the engine house at the yard. It had had one since the late summer. Ferra had written it herself in the House's form, as she wrote every line, and it said what was true. Coal, by the ton, the week. The engine's hours. The drivers' wages and the watchers'. And under it, as a separate line because it was a separate cost, the lamps: carbons for the arc lamps over the dock and in the works, by the dozen, the week; the glass for them; the wire. The pourers' wages were in the works' general line with the night gang's. The flasks were nowhere. They were not the works'. They were the barony's, drawn from the band, entered in a column that did not go to any creditor, because the Crown had never counted what came down out of the sky and Ferra had never been asked to.

She watched Vantic come to the engine house line and copy it. Coal, a small figure. Hours. Wages. Then the lamps, a large one, because a carbon rod in an arc lamp lasted a night or two and the dock burned four all night, and the works burned more.

Vantic's pen did not stop. It did not hesitate. She copied the two lines the way she copied every line, and went on to the paper works.

She walked the works in the afternoon, as she always did on a quarter day, and Ferra went with her, as she always did, because a creditor walking the thing she had lent against with nobody beside her was a creditor who would write down things she had misunderstood.

The paper works first, where Vantic asked the price of rag against straw, and was told it, and wrote it down. The converter, cold today between blows, where she stood and looked up at it for a while and did not ask how long a blow took, because she had asked Bram once and had it in her book. The rail works, where a gang was cutting rail for the Walker's next row in the second quarter. She asked what the Walker was. Jorrin said it was a frame for laying house walls. She asked what it cost. Jorrin told her. She wrote it down.

Then down the slope, in the thin cold sun after the gale, to the yard.

Bram was at the yard gate. He was usually at the yard gate on a quarter day in the afternoon, because Bram had a way of being where a question would come, so as to answer it himself and not have somebody answer it worse. He was in his leather apron with his arms folded, and he looked at Vantic coming down the road with her book the way he looked at weather too.

Vantic stopped in front of him and looked past him through the gate, at the yard: the stone engine house in the upper corner by the rock with its stack, the four masts over the dock hole, the dock's grey walls going up course by course with a gang on them, Fletcher's sheds, and a long shape along the far wall under canvas that might have been anything.

"How many tons does the engine house burn in a week?" she said.

Bram told her. He told her the true figure, because it was the true figure, and because Bram did not know how to tell anybody anything else. It was the coal the engine had burned in the week just gone, which had been an ordinary week, banked and turning nothing, with the fire kept in against need. It was a small figure.

Vantic wrote it down.

She did not ask how many lamps. She did not ask what the engine turned, or or how a fire that small drove a dynamo that lit a dock all night. She looked at the stack for a moment, where the thread of grey went up off it into the clear cold air and bent a little with the wind. Then she closed her book on her finger and said, "Thank you, Master Bram," as she said it every quarter day, and turned back up the road.

Ferra walked beside her. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing either of them could say that the other would not have to write down.

Hollis was at the top of the slope with the farm's straw carts.

He had brought them round from the old farm by the Grayrock road to the paper works, as he did every week now that the paper works took straw, three carts of last year's barley straw baled for the vats, and he was sitting on the shaft of the first while the carters unloaded, with his hat on the back of his head, eating an apple. He had been a farmer in Kaldo for forty years and had kept the barony's farm for most of the last ten, and he looked at everybody who came up the road from the yard the way he looked at a sky: to see what it would do.

He watched Vantic go by with her book and her clerk and not look at him. He watched her all the way along the road to where the House's carriage waited by the works gate, and watched her get in, and watched the carriage go. Then he threw the apple core into the ditch.

"I've seen that woman before," he said. He did not say it to Ferra. He said it to nobody, to the road, the way an old man says a thing he has been waiting to remember. "Years back. Before anybody here knew her name. On the foundry pile, it was. The winter before the House's money came. In a plain cloak, with the hood up. Standing on the slag with her hands in her sleeves, looking at the furnace house, the way you'd look at a beast you were thinking of buying." He scratched his jaw. "I took her for somebody's widow come to see where the money went. Then the next spring she came up the road in that carriage, and I thought, well, there's your widow." He looked at the road where the carriage had gone. "Never did see her on the pile again."

Ferra had her book under her arm. She did not open it. She stood beside the straw cart for a moment longer than she needed to, and then said she hoped the straw was dry, and Hollis said it was drier than the last lot, and she went on up to the works.

She did not write it down. There was nowhere in any book she kept for it to go.

She sat with her own book that evening, in the counting room, after the House's carriage had gone out of the Western Gate on the road north and the courier the other way.

She turned to the engine house line. She looked at it the way she had tried all afternoon not to look at it: as a stranger would. As a clerk would, at a desk in a house on the coast a day past Milford, with the week's bag open in front of him and an old man waiting at the other end of the room to be read to.

Coal: a small figure. Lamps: a large one.

Anybody who had ever costed a lamp could read it. You did not need to know what a dynamo was. You only needed to have kept the books of anything, a mill, a lighthouse, a ship, and to know in your hands that light was bought with fuel, and that a great deal of light was bought with a great deal of fuel, and that when the two lines on a page did not agree, one of them was a lie or there was a third line somewhere that nobody had written.

There was no lie on the page. She had written both lines herself and both were true. And there was a third line, and it was in a column that did not go to any creditor, and it said flasks, by the count: twelve down with hull two on the first clear night after the gale, and three days' stock on the shelf by the lock again. It would never leave this room.

The copy of the first two would be on the quay at the fishing harbour on the Thursday tide. It would be in a bag on a cutter going west, and in a house on the coast, and in front of the old man who had once gone up the Circle's stairs to ask what a mage cost by the year and written it down in a small book. She had known that since the winter. Everybody at the table had known it since the winter, and had agreed, because Emeric asked it of them, to know it and do nothing.

And there was nothing she could have done. That was what she sat with. She could not have kept the line out of the book, because it was owed, and a hidden line was a thing Vantic would find, because finding hidden lines was what Vantic was for. She could not have asked Vantic not to copy it, because to ask that she would have had to say why, and to say why she would have had to say who read it, and nobody at the table was ever going to say that. Emeric had decided so on the night the roll came, and they had all agreed, and they had been right. They were still right. It had simply turned out to cost something, and this was the first of what it cost.

A house with a stack that lights a city and burns what it burns will not add up, she had said to him in the summer, in the still week, with this book open between them. Not in my book. And my book is copied.

It had not added up. And it was copied.

She closed the book. She sat for a while with her hand flat on the cover. Then she opened it again, to the engine house line, and took up her pen, and sat with the pen over the page.

She did not write anything. She closed it again.

Emeric was at the long-room window when she went up, with the lamps behind him turned low and the shutters open on the cold.

The window looked south. From it you could see over the island's roofs and the lake and the fold of the land beyond, to where the yard lay at the ridge foot, and on a clear night the dock's lights showed over the fold, four hard white points, very small at that distance, like a constellation somebody had put up low on purpose. He was looking at them. He had his hands behind his back.

She stood in the doorway. She had meant to tell him, and found as she stood there that she did not need to.

"She copied the engine house line," he said, without turning round.

"Both of them."

"And asked Bram the tonnage."

"And wrote it down. And didn't ask about the lamps."

He nodded. He did not say anything else for a while, and neither did she. Down in the town the bell went for the end of the evening.

"Hollis says he saw her on the foundry pile," Ferra said. "The first winter. In a plain cloak. Before anybody knew her."

He turned his head at that, not all the way. Then he looked back at the lights.

"Did you write it down?"

"No."

"No," he said. He was quiet a moment. "I could have kept that line off her table," he said. "In the winter. I could have had it every quarter. All I had to do was tell her I knew who she was writing to."

"And then she'd have known you knew."

"And then she'd have had nothing in Kaldo that was hers."

Ferra did not answer that. There was nothing in it to answer. It was only true, the way the two lines in her book were true, and it did not add up any better than they did.

He had decided it a year ago, at this table, with his finger on a clerk's copy of an empty space beside a name. He had asked nothing of anybody for it. And tonight, without anybody asking him, he had paid the first of it, standing at a window, looking at four small lights he could not now take back out of a bag on a cutter on the Thursday tide.

"Goodnight, Ferra," he said, after a while.

"Goodnight."

She went down to the counting room and put her book in the press and locked it. On the way she passed the stair window that looked south too, and did not stop at it, and then went back two steps and stopped.

They were still there. Four of them, very small, hard and white.
