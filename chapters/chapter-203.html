Chapter 203 - Laundry

For three weeks nobody in Wayford wrote a word.

Not a word that came through. The knots were up, all of them; Emeric had felt each one from the ship on the night, and Bram had tested the chain from the barn gable to Ambry's back room on the next night with a wet sheet held up at an attic window, and the printer's bed had taken the strokes off the last knot as cleanly as it had ever taken a pattern off its stone in the cellar at home. The printers sat by it in turns, day and night, with their stack of sized sheets and their box. The box stayed empty.

Six houses had six cases of the black. Not one of them was writing with it.

"They've shelved it," said Saban, on the eighth day, at the counting-room table in Sethe's hall. He had his feet up on a second chair and his hat over his eyes, which was how Saban thought. "Somebody told them to. You don't get five households all deciding on their own not to use a free ink."

"Strade," said Emeric.

"Placit." Saban did not take the hat off. "Strade would tell them to pour it in the river. Placit tells them to put it on a shelf, and wait, and see what the prince does next. Placit's older." He was quiet. "And Placit's right. If you were going to do anything with that ink, you'd do it in the first month, when it's new. Wait three months and it's just ink."

So they waited.

It was the hardest thing he did all summer, and he had fallen off a keep wall twice. He went to the vats with the Keelings and to Ambry's with the punches and the type metal, and stood in Hesketh's barn while the first loads of straw went onto the carts for the Keeling yard; he went up the hill to the junction and paid it, again, smiling, and let the grey coat with the cudgel see him smile. He dined with Hollin, who liked him more each time. He did not go near Ambry's back room by day at all. At night he lay awake in Sethe's old bed in Sethe's old room, which still smelled of Sethe, and listened with his sense spread as thin and wide as he could spread it toward the six roofs he could not feel from there, and heard nothing.

On the twentieth day, at a quarter past ten in the morning, the printer's bed began to move.



The male printer brought him the page himself. That was against the third condition and he knew it, and did it anyway, because it was the first, and because he had sat by the bed for twenty days and wanted somebody to see it; a long, stooped, earnest young man named in Ferra's book as clerk the second, whose face, when he put the sheet into Emeric's hand in the yard behind Ambry's, was the face of a man presenting a firstborn.

It was a list. In a big sloped showy hand, very fast, with a great many flourishes on the capitals; Hollin's hand, unmistakable, the hand that had written forty years of dinner letters to Sethe. Grey on the sized sheet, faint, but every stroke in it, every flourish, in the order it had been made.

Shirts, linen, fine, 14. Shirts, linen, plain, 6. Stocks, 9. Small clothes, 12 pr. Nightshirts, 4, one torn at the left shoulder, TELL HER. Handkerchiefs, 22, the blue ones separate. Stockings, silk, 8 pr, the ninth pair is MISSING and I want to know where.

Emeric read it twice, standing in the yard.

"It's his laundry," he said.

"Yes, Highness." The young man's face had not changed. "He's run out of his own ink. Old iron gall, from Ambry's front shop. Ambry says he bought his last jar in March and he always buys again in June, and he didn't, because he'd a case of yours on the shelf and Placit told him not to touch it." He looked at the page with pride. "And this morning he wanted to count his shirts. And there was nothing else in the house to count them with."

Emeric looked at the ninth pair of silk stockings, MISSING, in the grey, and began to laugh, and could not stop.

He laughed in the yard behind Ambry's ink house until he had to sit down on the mounting block, and the young printer stood beside him holding the page with the face of a man whose firstborn has been mocked; and when Ferra came in through the yard gate with her key at the hour for the box, she stopped and looked at both of them, and at the page, and read it upside down from where she stood, and put her hand over her mouth.

"Twenty days," said Emeric, wiping his eyes. "Twenty days, Ferra. A ship and nine knots and the mana printer out of the cellar and a man on a barn gable over a midden. For a fat man's shirts."

"It's a beginning," said Ferra, behind her hand.

"It's his laundry."

"It's his laundry," said Ferra, "in his hand, in your ink, on your bed, a mile from his house, the morning he wrote it." She took her hand away from her mouth and her face was perfectly composed, except at the corners. "Struck," she said, and held out her hand to the printer for the page. "Under the third. It doesn't touch the Staple." She looked at it again. "Though I'd like to know about the ninth pair."



After the laundry, everything came.

Not all at once. Hollin first, because Hollin had opened the case; and once a man has filled his inkwell from a bottle he does not stop to think which bottle the next time. Hollin wrote a great deal. He wrote to his tailor in the capital. He wrote to his wine merchant, and to a woman in the capital who was not his wife, at length, in a style that the female printer read once and put face down in the box with a set face and did not discuss. He wrote to Placit, twice in the first week, short, about the prince, in a tone of great fondness and mild contempt. He wrote his household's orders in the black too, as he had the laundry, in that fast flourishing hand; and his steward, who wrote at Hollin's desk on Hollin's paper when Hollin was out, wrote with whatever was in Hollin's inkwell.

Then Vennor's wife, who had liked the black since the night she tested it, and wrote a great deal more than her husband, and all of it to her daughter.

Then, in the last week of July, Dray.

Dray did not write. Emeric had been right about that much. But Dray had a clerk, a sour little man in the clerk's room off the hall full of guns, who took Dray's letters down as Dray walked up and down shouting them; and the clerk had found the case of bottles with the three bars on their shoulders in the servants' passage where it had been put on the night of the dinner, and had looked at it, and had thought, like any clerk, that it was a great deal better than the brown stuff his master gave him. So Dray's letters came through in the clerk's small cramped careful hand, word for word, with Dray's oaths left in.

The printers read everything.

That was the third condition, and it was the part nobody had wanted to think about at the long table in May, and everybody had to think about by the end of July. Somebody had to read it to know what to strike. So the printers read it: the tailor and the wine and the woman in the capital, Vennor's wife's long anxious letters to a daughter who was ill in the north and getting worse, Dray's shouting. Every page. They read it at the bed as it came off, face down, in the box; and twice a day Ferra took the box to Sethe's counting room and read it again, all of it, and struck what did not touch the Staple, and copied what did, by hand, into a book of her own that never left her room.

On the twenty-ninth of July the female printer asked to be taken off.

She asked Ferra, in the counting room, at night, standing very straight in front of Ferra's table with her hands folded. She was a quiet capable woman of forty with grown children of her own whom Ferra had chosen out of the whole counting room at Kaldo, because, Ferra had said, she was the steadiest reader Ferra had ever had, and could read a man's letter to his dying daughter and strike it and not carry it home.

She had read Vennor's wife's letter to her daughter that morning. The daughter had died. The letter was the mother's answer to the letter saying so.

"I can't," said the printer. "I'm sorry. I thought I could. I've struck forty of hers, and I was all right, and today I wasn't." She did not cry. She stood very straight. "I keep thinking she doesn't know. That somebody read it. That a stranger read it, before the girl's own father did." She looked at the floor. "She'd never forgive us. I'd never forgive us. I'm sorry."

Ferra looked at her for a long while.

"You'll go home on Thursday," she said at last. "On the train. With your wage to the end of the summer and a line in my book that you did this work better than anybody I could have chosen, and asked to stop, which is the right thing to do with work you can't carry." She stood up. "Nobody will say a word to you about it. Ever. I'll see to it."

"Who'll read hers?"

"I will," said Ferra.



He found her at three in the morning, in the counting room, reading them.

The lamp turned low on her table, and the box beside it, and a pile of pages face up in front of her in the grey ink, in a round hand he knew from a slip of paper on a dinner table: the round anxious hand of Vennor's wife, three weeks of it. Ferra was reading the last of them. She had her hand flat on the table beside the page and she was not moving at all, and her face in the lamplight was the face she wore at a column that would not add, perfectly still, with everything behind it working.

He did not say anything. He came round the table and sat down beside her, close, on the bench, and reached across her and took half the pile.

"Those are mine," said Ferra.

"Not tonight."

"They're mine. The third condition is mine. I said I'd answer for it." Her hand on the table did not move. "I told her I would."

"You told the printer you'd read them. You didn't say alone." He turned the first page over. "Condition three doesn't say alone either. I've read it forty times. You wrote it."

Ferra was silent.

Then, after a while, she leaned her shoulder against his, very slightly, and left it there, and went on reading. He read too. He read the round anxious hand of a woman he had dined with, writing to a girl he had never seen who was dying in a house in the north of a cough, about the garden, and the weir, and the dog, and what the doctor had said, and what she had said back to the doctor, and whether the girl was eating; and then the hand changed, on the last page, and was not round any more.

He struck it. He drew the line through it himself, with Ferra's pen, the way she drew it, once, corner to corner. She watched him do it and did not say anything. Then she took the pen back and wrote in the margin, small, in her own hand, the date and the words: Struck. Read by F. and E. Not copied.

She put her head down on his shoulder, there at the table, and closed her eyes.

"I don't like this," said Ferra.

"I know."

"I wrote the five. I wanted it. I wanted it more than you did, when it came to it. I wanted to see what they wrote." She did not open her eyes. "And I'm good at it. That's the worst part. I can read a dead girl's mother and strike her and turn the page and find the line about the Staple in the next one. I'm very good at it."

"I know that too."

"Don't agree with me."

"I'm not agreeing. I'm keeping you company." He put his arm round her on the bench. "That's not the same."

She was quiet a while longer. Then she opened her eyes, and turned her face up, and kissed him, not the way she had kissed him in Sethe's chair, but slowly, tiredly, gently, with her hand on his face; the way you would kiss somebody to make sure they were there.

"Fun later," said Ferra against his mouth, very low. "Much later. When this is done." She put her head back down on his shoulder. "Stay till the lamp goes out."

He stayed till the lamp went out, and after.



Dray's page came on the thirty-first.

Ferra brought it to him in the yard at Ambry's, which she did not do, and held it out without a word, and he read it there in the sun by the mounting block. Dray's clerk's cramped hand, every word, with the oaths left in. A letter to Dray's brother, in the capital. It was not about the Staple at all.

Dray's wife had gone. Ten days ago, with a bag and her little boy, while Dray was at Hollin's for the night; she had gone to her sister's, whose husband was a lighterman, in the dock quarter at Kaldo. Dray knew where; he had always known where. Her sister had married beneath them both, and he had never let his wife visit her. He would go and fetch her back. He would send two men down the road for her at the end of the week, and they would bring her back, and the boy, and this time, he said, in the clerk's small careful letters, she would not be walking anywhere again for a good long while. He had been too soft with her. His brother had always said so.

Emeric read it twice, in the sun.

"Struck," said Ferra, beside him. Her voice was perfectly level. "Under the third. It doesn't touch the Staple."

"Ferra."

"I know." She took the page back out of his hand, carefully, by its corner. "Imani asked me, in May, at the long table. What if what's struck is a crime. And I said I'd find a reason that wasn't a knot." She folded the page, once. "So I'm finding one."

She did it by telephone, from the posting house, that afternoon, on the line to Kaldo, in a voice so ordinary that the posting house girl at the board would have had to be a great deal cleverer than she was to hear anything in it. She asked the ward for Imani. She asked Imani, when she came, how the fever in the dock quarter was; there had been a low fever among the lightermen's children since June, every summer, and the ward did a round of the dock quarter for it once a fortnight. She said that Saban had heard there was a woman newly come to the dock quarter from the north, with a little boy, staying with her sister in the lighterman's row below the east bridge; and that the boy might not have been looked at, coming from a town where there had been fever too. She thought, she said, that the ward might want to know. That was all. And then she talked about the cost of linen for the ward's beds for a quarter of an hour, and rang off.

Imani went down to the lighterman's row the same evening.

He heard about it afterward, in pieces; from Imani, on the telephone, three nights later, and from Rylan's line on the wire, and from Ferra, who had known how it would go before it went. Imani found the house. She found the boy, who did not have the fever, and looked at him anyway, all over, as she looked at every child, and found what was on his back, and on his mother's arms when the mother pushed her sleeves up to hold him still; and did not say anything about it to either of them. She sat in the lighterman's kitchen for an hour and drank the sister's tea. Then she asked the woman to come up to the ward in the morning and bring the boy, for a proper look, in a proper light. And in the morning, at the ward, in her room with the door shut, she told the woman that the ward needed a woman who could sew and keep a linen press, at a wage, with a room of her own off the ward's back passage where the nurses slept, and the boy could go to the school. Did she want it?

The woman had wanted it very much.

"Is she safe?" said Emeric, on the telephone, three nights later, at the posting house board, with the girl gone to bed and Ward at the door.

"She's in the room at the end of the back passage," said Imani. Her voice came thin and far down the wire, with a hum under it, as if she were speaking from the bottom of a well; and close, too, at the same time, warm, in his ear. "With the boy. With a lock on the inside of the door that she turns herself. She sleeps like the dead, she says." The hum. "Rylan's been told two men are coming down the road. He's very pleased about it, Osric says. He's been reading the gate law aloud to the garrison for practice."

"What did you say to her? In the morning. With the door shut."

"I'm not telling you." He heard her smile. "Come home and I'll whisper it."

"I'm coming home on Thursday."

"I know. Ferra telephoned. She tells me everything, it's in the terms." A pause, and the hum on the line. "She said you read half of Vennor's wife's letters with her. At three in the morning."

"Yes."

"Good." The hum. "She'd not have asked you."

"I know."

"You're learning," said Imani. Then, lower, so that he had to press the earpiece to his head to hear it: "Thursday. I'll be on the slip. Without a lamp this time." And then, before he could answer, in her ward voice, loud and brisk: "And without a bag, so I'll have both hands, and I'll not drop anything. Goodnight. Sleep." And the line went dead in his ear with a click; and he stood at the board in the dark posting house with the earpiece in his hand, grinning like a boy, until Ward at the door cleared his throat.

Dray's two men came down the capital road eight days later, on horses, with a cart. Rylan met them at the South Gate. He had a list from the gate tower of every man who had a pass to Kaldo that month, and their names were not on it, and Kaldo was a walled town with a gate and a law about strangers with no business at its gate, which had been written the winter Finn was taken; and Rylan read the law to them at the gate, all of it, slowly, in his pleasant voice, with six of the garrison behind him in their black. They asked to see a woman in the dock quarter. Rylan asked her name. They told him. Rylan said he would send to ask if she wished to see them; and sent, and the answer came back from the ward in Imani's hand, two words; and he read it to them.

They went back up the road.

Emeric never did find out what the two words were. Imani would not say, and Rylan, asked, only smiled his pleasant smile and said that a gate law was a gate law.



Adlow's page came on the second of August, and he got it wrong.

It came off the bed at a little after four in the afternoon, Hollin's knot, Hollin's boiling, the needle on the office board standing where Hollin's needle stood: a short page, quickly written, unsigned. And the female printer was gone home by then, and the male one was alone at the bed and three hours into his watch, and he put it face down in the box as he had put every page; and Ferra was at Ottoline's shop on the square, and Emeric was in the yard, waiting for her, and the printer, who had been told a hundred times and had forgotten once, brought it out into the yard to him, because it was short, and because the prince was there.

Thursday. Both carts. The north lane, after the moon's down, not before. The grey boxes from the strongroom first, then the plate. Nobody from the house. Nobody to know till it's done.

Emeric read it in the sun.

The strongroom. The grey boxes. He had stood in Hollin's hall and seen the strongroom door at the end of it, iron-bound, under a lamp, and heard Hollin say at dinner that every house of the five kept its own books of the trade in its own strongroom, by the charter, and opened them to each other once a year. Grey boxes. The Staple's books. Hollin was moving the Staple's books out of his strongroom by night on Thursday, by the north lane, with nobody from the house to know.

Somebody had told Hollin to. Placit, probably. Somebody had started, at last, to be afraid.

He did not wait for Ferra. That was the whole of it, afterward, when he went over it in the dark as he went over everything: he did not wait twenty minutes in a yard for Ferra to come back from Ottoline's, and look at the page with her eyes, which always looked at the hand first. He gave the page back to the printer and sent for Saban, and Saban sent for his man in Wayford, the bow-legged groom from the posting house, and on Thursday night, an hour after the moon went down, two carts came out of the back gate of Hollin's yard into the north lane with their wheels muffled in sacking and a single man walking at the first horse's head with a dark lantern; and at the bend of the lane where it went under the trees, the groom and two of Saban's carters stood across it with a lantern of their own and asked, very civilly, what toll the carts had paid at the junction, since there was a charter, and the charter didn't say except at night.

The man at the horse's head looked at them for a long moment in the lantern light.

Then he turned the carts round, without a word, and took them back into Hollin's yard, and shut the gate.

It was Adlow. The groom knew him: everybody at the posting house knew Hollin's steward, a thin pale careful man who drank alone. And the grey boxes in the carts, which the groom saw under the sacking for the length of the turning, were not the Staple's books at all. They were Hollin's silver. The plate. And Hollin's own wine, by the cask, the old wine from the capital that Hollin told stories about at dinner, and some of Hollin's wife's things in a trunk; going out of the back gate at night, on Thursday, with nobody from the house to know, because Hollin was dining at Placit's on Thursday with his wife and would not be home till two.

Ferra read the page that night. She read it once, in Sethe's counting room, by the lamp, and then held it to the lamp, and looked at the hand.

"This isn't his," she said.

He had known it since the groom came back. He said nothing.

"Look at the capitals." She put her finger on the T of Thursday. No flourish. A plain upright stroke, quick and thin, a clerk's T. "Hollin never wrote a capital in his life without a tail on it. This is a clerk. Somebody who writes at Hollin's desk, in Hollin's ink, when Hollin's out." She was quiet. "His steward. It's the steward, isn't it. Robbing him. And you sent a man to stop the steward's carts on the one night that only the steward's own letter named."

"Yes."

"So now the steward knows." Ferra put the page down. "He doesn't know how. He's not a fool, Saban says he's not a fool. He wrote that page at Hollin's desk on Monday, alone, in a locked room, and burned the blotter. And on Thursday night three strangers were standing across the north lane in the dark at the one place and the one hour he'd written down, asking about a toll." She looked at the page. "He'll go over it and over it. Who saw. Who could have. And the only thing in that room that's new since May," said Ferra, "is the ink."

The black stopped coming from Hollin's house on the sixth of August.

All of it. Not only Adlow's; Hollin's own, the tailor and the woman in the capital and the fond contemptuous notes to Placit, stopped together, the same day, as if a tap had been turned. Saban's groom heard the reason a week later at the posting house, from Hollin's cook: the prince's fine ink had gone bad, the steward said, gone thick in the bottles and rotten, and the steward had poured the whole case into the river himself, and bought Hollin a new jar of good iron gall from Ambry's front shop with his own money, out of loyalty. Hollin had been very touched. He had given the steward a Gold.

Adlow did not tell Hollin anything else. Adlow was a thief; he could not. But Hollin's house was dark now, on the printer's bed, and would stay dark, and a pale careful man who drank alone was going over and over a page he had written on a Monday, and would, sooner or later, wonder aloud to somebody about a prince's ink.

Ferra wrote it in her book that night. Not in the condition book; in the book. He watched her do it, across the table, by the lamp, in her small exact hand, and she did not hide the page from him and did not soften it.

2 August. Hollin's knot. A page in A.'s hand, read as H.'s by E. without the hand being checked. Acted on before F. had seen it. A. warned. Hollin's house lost to the net, 6 August. The error is E.'s.

She blotted it, and turned the book round on the table so that he could read it, and waited.

He read it. He did not reach for the pen. He had sat across this same table from her in April and not struck a line she had written about herself, because it was true; and this was true too, every word of it, and it was his.

"Then it's written," he said.

Ferra looked at him a long breath across the lamp. Then she reached across the table and put her hand over his, on the book, over the line, and left it there.

"You saw the strongroom," she said, quietly, "and the grey boxes and the night and the north lane, and it added up, and you didn't look at the hand." She did not take her hand away. "I look at the hand first. Every time. That's what I'm for."

"I know."

"Then wait for me next time," said Ferra. "Twenty minutes. In a yard." She took her hand off his. "I'll always come back from Ottoline's."
