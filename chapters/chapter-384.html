Chapter 384 - Seconds

Waylon swallowed his stone on the sixteenth, a Monday, in the dome, on the board on the tree's north side that Zale had lain on six weeks before, with Alina sitting on the gravel at the foot of it reading aloud to him from a book about the tides that nobody else in the dome found at all interesting.

"Go on," she said, when he'd held the stone in his fingers for long enough. "I'm at the good part."

"There's a good part?"

"The neap tides," said Alina, and turned the page.

He swallowed it. Imani sat down with her book and her watch. Alina went on reading about the neap tides, steadily, in her clear flat voice, long after he'd gone under and couldn't hear her, and nobody suggested she stop.

The chart had been on the back of a tide table in December. By the twentieth of February it was on a sheet of drafting paper four feet across, pinned to the long room's east wall where the map of the Isle had hung, and Ferra stood in front of it on the Friday morning with a pointer she'd borrowed from Sela's schoolroom and didn't need.

It was a pyramid. It had always been going to be a pyramid; that was how Kaldo had been run since the second year, tier on tier, every desk with someone over it and someone under, and everyone able to see the line from their desk to the top. But the top had always been a name. Now the top was a box, and in the box, in Ferra's hand, was C., and over the box, in a separate box with a dotted line round it, H., regent, the crown's matters, and over that, at the very top, small, with a line going off the edge of the paper into nowhere, E., by relay.

"Every one of us names a second," said Ferra. "Every one who sails. In writing. With authority over their work, in their own hand, signed and in this book." She tapped the book on the table with the pointer. "Not someone to keep the chair warm. Someone to do the job. Finish what we've started. Start what we haven't."

"And if they can't?" said Ward.

"Then they go up the line." Ferra moved the pointer. "A second decides inside their own work. Everything. Nobody comes to them and says, but what would Bram have done. Bram's not here. They are." Down to the next tier. "Across works, when one second's work runs into another's, it goes to Corbin. The chair." Up. "The crown's matters, anything with the king's name on it, a treaty, a law, an envoy, the regent. And over the regent, the king, by relay, if the relay's up and the king's not at the bottom of the sea."

"Which he will be," said Saban.

"Which he will be, a good deal of the time. So the line stops at the regent most days, and the regent's to know that." Ferra put the pointer down. "Weekly, every second sends a line on the relay net, in cipher, to the K Amara. What's done, what's stuck, what's wanted. Monthly, a ledger. The real one, not the one you'd like me to see."

"You'll be reading eighty ledgers a month on a ship," said Imani.

"I read eighty ledgers a month now," said Ferra. "On a chair. I'll get used to the motion."

They went round the table.

It took most of the morning. It should have taken an hour; Ferra had had every name in her book since December. But each of them had to say it, out loud, and sign it, and each of them, when it came to it, found it harder than they'd expected to say a name and mean the whole of what came with it.

Imani went first, because she'd said she would. "The ward to Mattie Dunne," she said. Dunne was the senior physician, a square grey woman of fifty who'd come down from the capital in year nine to see whether Imani was a fraud and had stayed. "With Hollin Ashe in Bettany's place, because Bettany sails with me and somebody has to do the nights. Dunne decides who's admitted and who's cut and what we stop doing because it doesn't work. Not me. Her." She signed. "And the register stays where it is, and nobody touches it, and the Sand Room never sees it. That's not hers to change. That's mine and it's in the book."

Armand: "My chairs to Lysa Orme." His best student, who'd taken the grammar from him in two years when it had taken him twenty, and who was frightened of nothing in the world except him. "She'll teach the stones better than I did. She's not old." He signed, in his long thin hand, and then added, under it, without being asked, She will think she's not ready. She is.

Ward: "The knights to Osric. With Declan and Rylan under him. Declan the ridges and the roads, Rylan the walls and the town." He looked down the table at Osric, who'd been brought in for this and was sitting at the far end very straight in his good coat. "He'll have a hundred and eighty, and the frames, and the fire company. And he'll make a better job of it than I would, because he'll ask Tess."

"I'll ask Tess," said Osric.

"I know you will. That's why it's you."

Ferra: "The counting house to Ilsa." She didn't say anything else. She signed it, and then sat looking at her own name for a moment, and then turned the page.

And Bram, when it came to him, said "Tess," and signed, and that was all he said, and Ferra didn't push.

Linus Vance brought the corridor book in at eleven, under his arm, in a wooden box with a lid, and laid it on the table in front of the stewards.

They'd come down for it. The Stewards' Council had been called to Kaldo for the last week of February, and because of the phones half of them had said they needn't come at all and Ferra had said they would. Bryda from Grayrock, in her travelling coat. Paxton from Milford, who had brought his own list. Aster from Wayford. And the new stewards from the holdings that had come up under the uplift, six of them, who'd never sat at this table and were trying very hard not to look at the king.

"It's not a map," said Linus. He was a dry man who'd been surveying Randillia since before most of them were born and had never once been in a hurry. He opened the box. Inside, in sections, bound in boards, were the sheets. "It's where nothing goes."

He'd been at it since the summer. Every road that would one day want to be a railway. Every valley a canal could go up. Every line a power cable could run from Milford's towers to the holdings without crossing a lord's park or a church's graveyard or the old-race footings in the west hills that nobody had dug yet. Inside Randillia, and inside Duvrenne, by Duvrenne's leave, as far as Kesrath's border and no further. On every one of them, a band drawn in green, a few hundred yards wide, with a note: Keep clear. Railway, later. Linus, July. Canal, later. Line from the towers. Don't build here.

"Every time a man builds a house in the wrong place," said Linus, "the railway costs another hundred marks to go round it. Every time a lord plants an orchard across a valley, the canal's a mile longer. Fifty years from now somebody's going to want to put a road from Wayford to the old capital in a straight line and find a town in the way that wasn't there when they could have stopped it." He put his hand on the box. "So. This is the line. You keep it clear. You don't sell it, you don't let it be built on, you don't let anybody plant across it. And when somebody asks you why, you tell them Linus said so and he's dead, if I am."

"You're not dead," said Bryda.

"I'm old. Give it time."

Each steward took their section. Paxton took his and opened it at once and began to argue with it, in a low voice, about a green band across the east side of the bench fields that he'd had other plans for. Linus let him. He'd known Paxton longer than anyone.

There was one section left in the box. Linus didn't hand it to anyone. He lifted it out, a thick one in the crown's blue boards, and set it down in front of the king.

"The demesne," he said. "The old capital and the farm ring. It's got no steward. So it's yours."

Emeric opened it. The green bands came up the south road through the suburbs and stopped dead at the outer wall, because inside it there was nowhere left to draw one. Linus had drawn the lower city as it stood, the lanes as thin black slots between the roofs, and across the whole of it, in pencil, he'd written No room. Under, not through.

"Under," said Emeric.

"That's all it'll take," said Linus.

Emeric turned the sheet so the table could see it. "Do you know why it doesn't smell?" Most of them had been to the capital at least once. Nobody answered. "A hundred and twenty thousand people on a river bend, and you can walk the lower city in August and not hold your nose. That's utility mages. Water up, waste out. The crown pays them out of the court's purse, and they're very good, and it's the only thing keeping the water clean." He put a finger on the hill. "The court's coming down here. Not all of it. The half that spends money. The purse gets thin, the mages go where they're paid, or there's one dead zone over that hill on a hot day. And then it's a hundred and twenty thousand people drinking their own dead."

Imani had gone still at the end of the table. She'd been at Kaldo's burial rows the first summer. She didn't say anything.

"And who pays to dig up the old capital?" said Bryda. "Because I'd like it said out loud that it isn't Grayrock."

"The demesne pays," said Ferra, without looking up. "A hundred and fifty thousand people and a farm ring. It's just never been asked to pay for anything."

"Keller has the land and the courts," said Emeric. "Weekes goes up at the thaw with two gangs." Weekes had built the palace and complained about every course of it, and had never once been wrong about a level. "Filter beds. Mains. A sewer along the river, below the town. The mages stay. They run on top of it, the way our stones run on top of the pumps. When they can't, it still works." He shut the section. "Then lamps. Then a tram, if the lanes'll take one."

"They won't," said Linus.

"Then something else."

"They'll hate it," said Aster quietly. He'd have them through Wayford on the train, every one of them, going up and down, and he'd hear it first. "The lords up there. Trenches in front of their doors for two years."

"They'll hate the trenches," said Emeric. "Then they'll turn a tap."

Ferra drew a new box on the chart, under the dotted one, with a line up to H., and wrote in it Weekes, the demesne. Then she looked at it, and crossed out the demesne, and wrote the old capital, because that was what everybody was going to call it anyway.

And then, because it was there in her book and she'd been waiting to ask it all morning, Ferra turned to Saban.

"Who minds your shop?"

The table looked at her.

Saban had a shop. Everybody had always known that and nobody had ever thought about it. Before Kaldo, before Emeric came down the coast road with his cart and his chest of dead stones, Saban had been a trader on Quay Street with a counter and a back room and a sign that said nothing at all, and he'd sat across that counter from a great many men who didn't answer him and priced every silence to a copper. He still had it. He'd never given it up. It was where the Church's letters came, and the bishops' cousins' orders for bicycles, and the merchants from the free crowns with their premiums; and it was where Saban went every morning at seven before he did anything else, and where he'd been going while the six had been building a kingdom round him, and while he'd been running the kingdom's Trade from the long room in the afternoons.

"I mind my shop," said Saban.

"You mind the kingdom's trade. That's the Trade desk. That's the chart." Ferra put her finger on it, on the box under Corbin's with S. in it and the line going down from it to a box with no name. "You've a second on the chart, here, for the Trade desk. You've never told anybody who it is. I've written S.'s man on it since December." She looked at him. "And you've a shop on Quay Street that's had its door open every morning for thirty years. Who minds it while you're minding the Church?"

Saban turned his hat round once in his hands.

"The same person," he said.

"Who?"

He looked at her for a long time. Then he sighed, the sigh of a man giving up a thing he'd been keeping for no reason except that he'd always kept it, and got up, and went to the long room's door, and opened it, and said to somebody in the corridor, "You can come in. She's found you."

A young woman came in. She was perhaps twenty-five, round-faced and short and plainly dressed, with ink on her fingers and a pencil behind each ear, and she stood just inside the door of the long room with her hands folded in front of her and looked at the king and the six and the stewards and Linus Vance's box with perfect composure, the way a cat looks at a room it's decided it owns.

"This is Hetty Quill," said Saban. "She's minded the shop since she was seventeen. She's minded the Trade desk since the war. She read the Velmoor bishop's cousin's hand before I did, and she was right." He sat down again. "She's my second. On the chart, and the shop, and everything else. She's never met any of you because I didn't want any of you to have met her."

"Why not?" said Ward.

"Because a man who wants to know what Kaldo's trade is doing comes to the shop," said Saban, "and finds a girl behind the counter who seems a bit slow and makes him a cup of tea. And he talks." He put his hat on the table. "That's a very good second. It's a very bad thing to have in the long room."

Hetty Quill looked at Ferra.

"I'm not slow," she said.

"I'd not thought you were," said Ferra.

"Good," said Hetty Quill, and came to the table and took the pen from Saban's hand without asking and signed her own name under his in the book, in a small rounded unremarkable hand that nobody would ever have looked at twice.

Ilsa took the counting house that afternoon. There was no ceremony. Ferra had said there wasn't to be. She walked down Gate Avenue at three with Ilsa beside her, and into the counting house, and up the stairs to the long room on the first floor where Hobb and Wenna and Dace were at their desks with the day's books, and across to her own desk by the window, where the light came in over the square.

It was a plain desk. Oak, scarred, with a drawer that stuck. She'd had it since the counting house was two rooms over a chandler's. Everything on it was where it had always been: the inkstand, the pen tray, the stack of the month's ledgers squared to the edge, the little brass weight she used to hold a page flat, and nothing else at all.

She took her own pen out of the tray and put it in her pocket.

"It's yours," she said.

Ilsa looked at the desk. She'd kept Grayrock's books under Ferra for years and the counting house's second ledger for three, and she'd sat at the desk beside this one every working day of those three years, and Emeric, who'd come down with them, saw that she hadn't once in all that time sat in this chair.

"It's your desk," said Ilsa.

"It's a desk." Ferra pulled the chair out. "Sit in it."

Ilsa sat in it. Hobb and Wenna and Dace, at their desks, had all stopped writing and were pretending they hadn't. The light came in over the square.

"The drawer sticks," said Ferra. "Lift it at the left as you pull."

"I know," said Ilsa. "I've watched you do it for three years."

"Then you know." Ferra stood back from the desk and looked at it, with Ilsa in it, for what Emeric thought was a long time and was probably a few seconds. Then she said to the room, "Hobb, the Wayford returns are a day late, chase them. Wenna, the Isle's first escrow's the twenty-eighth, I want it in the House's vault by the twenty-sixth, and if Vantic says it's in her book and not her vault, ring me. Dace." She stopped. "Dace, you're fine. Carry on." And to Ilsa, without looking at her: "Weekly line on the Friday. Ledger on the first. I'll know if it's the one you'd like me to see."

"I know you will," said Ilsa.

And Ferra went out, and down the stairs, and into the square, and stood at the bottom of the counting house steps in the cold for a moment with her hands in her coat pockets, the right one closed round her own pen, before she walked on.

The Bank Article had been going since the Thursday after the Compact, every Thursday, in Vantic's office on the first floor of the House, with Bram's grandmother's tea caddy on the table between them.

It was a small tin box with a painted lid, a shepherdess on it gone pale with handling, and inside it, folded small, a single sheet of paper money from the old capital's mint in the bad year, when Bram's grandmother had been a girl. A hundred marks, it said. It had bought her a loaf, in the end. Bram had kept it all his life. He'd brought it into the long room on a winter night years ago, when the House's paper was new and Kaldo was deciding whether to trust it, and put it on the table without a word, and Saban had picked it up and said the three guards, and nobody had forgotten.

Emeric went with Ferra on the nineteenth, because Vantic had asked for the king for an hour, and sat at the end of the table while the two of them worked.

They were on the limit. Against what. That was the whole of the argument and they'd been having it for five Thursdays.

"Not metal," said Vantic. "Metal's what killed this." She put her fingertip on the caddy's lid. "The old mint printed against silver it didn't have. And when it found it didn't have it, it printed more, because that's what a mint does when a king's at war and the silver's gone. Tie a note to metal and you've tied it to whatever's in the ground and whoever's digging."

"And to what's past the wall," said Ferra. "If a people out there uses silver for pots and pays in it by the ship."

"Exactly that. Every coin at home worth half by spring and nobody meant any harm." Vantic sat back. "So not metal. What the realm makes. What it can tax. What it owes and what it's owed. The paper's good because the crown takes it for the tax and the House takes it for the debt and the bread's in the shop when you get there with it."

"And the limit?"

"The limit's a number. A sum against the realm's taxes, published, every month, in the records hall. And above that sum not one more note, not for a war, not for a famine, not for a king's wedding." Vantic picked up her pen. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless a board says so. Not the king. Not the House." She wrote. "Five seats. By term, staggered, so nobody appoints them all at once. No crown man sits alone. Nobody who holds a partnership in any House, mine included, may sit. And they publish." She stopped writing and looked at what she'd written. "An Issue Board. It prints. The House banks. Two different things."

Ferra was very still.

"That writes the House out of the printing," she said.

"Not yet. Not at once. The House prints under the charter for its term, the way the charter sheet says, the one with the king's name blank. When the term runs out, the printing goes to the Board, and the House goes back to being a bank." Vantic put the pen down. "I've read forty years of the old mint's ledgers, Ferra. I know what happens to a pricing model that outlives the woman who built it. Somebody inherits it who doesn't understand it and uses it to buy a war." She smiled slightly. "I'm not leaving my brother Tullio's grandchildren a printing press."

"You're writing yourself out of the seat."

"I'm writing myself out of the seat," said Vantic. "And I'll charge you for it. The House's fee for the charter term goes up a quarter. It's worth it. You know it's worth it. Argue me down."

Ferra argued her down. It took the rest of the hour. Emeric sat at the end of the table and didn't say anything, which, he realised about halfway through, was what Vantic had asked the king for an hour for: so that it would be written, afterward, in the Article's own history, that the king had been in the room when the House wrote itself out of the printing and had said nothing at all, and that this was the point.

"It won't be finished before you sail," said Vantic, at the end, when she'd got her quarter down to an eighth.

"No."

"And there'll be no paper under it till you're back." Vantic closed the folder. "Not with the auditor at sea and the Core's books shut. Five years to pay the fleet off, I've said, and I'll not say it twice because you'll hold me to it. I'm not launching a currency in the middle of that with the two people who'd have to defend it a thousand miles away."

"Then we'll finish it when I'm back."

"We'll finish it when you're back," said Vantic. "And in the meantime Ilsa can come on Thursdays and read it. She'll find all the places you missed."

"She'll find all the places you hid," said Ferra.

"Those too," said Vantic, pleased.

"Who fights it?" said Emeric. "When it comes."

Vantic didn't stop to think, which meant she'd done the thinking some time ago.

"Everybody who's paid when the coin's short." She laid a finger on the caddy's lid, and moved it once for each. "My brother. The crown's debt's in his book and half the old lords' mortgages with it, and a paper anybody can count is a debt he can't price. Stannary. The tin house bought the mint's farm along with its rank, off a king who needed silver more than he needed his own mint, and it's had a copper off every pound struck since. Paper isn't struck. Fairhythe. Every cargo on the Tarrow is weighed on their quays and paid over their tables, and they take their bit at the table. And the Cocan March, because every pilgrim to Ostrel changes his silver at Pryor's Gate at the marquis's rate, and there's silver in those hills the crown's never been told the weight of."

"Four houses," said Ferra.

"Four houses and everyone who owes them." Vantic took her finger off the lid. "They'll go to law for some of it. Law's what they do to Kaldo; they like law, it's slow. But a man who loses an argument writes a pamphlet. A man who loses a toll hires men."

"To do what?" said Emeric. "Walk at the towers?"

"Not at you." She looked at him as if he'd said something younger than he was. "At whoever's standing in their market when you're not."

He said he'd think about it when he was back. She said that was what she'd expected him to say, and said nothing else about it. Ferra, who'd been writing the whole time, drew a line under the four names in her book and wrote one word over them, because a folder needed a name and none of the four would ever have called themselves anything at all. Consortium.

She closed the folder and put it in her bag, and stood, and then sat down again, which with Vantic meant the thing she'd actually come for was next.

"One more," she said. "Not for the Article. For the House." She took a card out of her coat, a citizen's card, plain, its chip a grey dot under the glass, and laid it on the table beside the caddy. "Do you know how many of these came back across my counters last year? Not stolen. Lost."

"No," said Emeric.

"Four hundred and twelve. Over the side of a boat. Down a privy. Through a hole in a coat pocket. Through the wash, which I didn't know a chip could survive, and it can't." She turned the card over with one finger. "Every one of them a man at a teller's window with no card, only his word and his duress word, and a clerk deciding whether the word's good for his wages. The word works. The carbon copies work. It takes forty minutes and it's the worst forty minutes of his month." She looked up. "When you're back, I want a card a man can't lose."

Emeric looked at the card for a while.

"Then he doesn't carry it," he said. "He wears it."

Ferra stopped writing.

"A band. Like the council bands, only everybody's." He touched his own wrist, where there was nothing. "Round the wrist. The same chip, his note and nobody else's. Closed, so it doesn't come off in the wash or over the side, and it opens to his hand and no one else's. And while it's there anyway..." He stopped, and then said it, because he'd been thinking it since the Isle. "A grain of stasis stone. Cut very small. Set to catch only the loud things. A spell thrown at him in the street. Not a fist, not a knife. A spell."

"A shield," said Vantic. "On a clerk."

"On everybody."

"A fist of shard," said Ferra, "is a barony." She hadn't picked her pencil back up. "That's not a figure of speech. I've seen the Isle's books."

"A grain isn't a fist. Two and a half thousand to the fist, about, if Tess does the cutting."

"And the Isle gives us how many fists a year?"

"Not enough. Not yet." He thought of the divers going down under the west cliffs in January, the Compact barely a month old, the seam lawful for the first time in living memory. "When the Compact's run a year, and the divers are paid properly, and nobody's stealing from it, more. Enough for the town in a few years. Not before."

Vantic was still looking at her card. "And the money?"

"Stays in your vault. The band doesn't hold a coin. It doesn't hold a number. It holds his note, the same as the card does now. Steal it and you've stolen a bracelet."

"Then I like it." She opened the folder again, found the Article's last sheet, and wrote in its margin, small. "I'd want it in writing that it never holds a number. Not his account. Not his balance. Nothing a thief or a clerk or a king could read off his wrist."

"You'll have it in writing."

"I have it in writing," said Vantic, and turned the sheet round so they could see.

After. The citizen's band. Never a number. V.

She put the folder back in her bag and stood, and this time she stayed standing, with one gloved hand on the back of the chair. There wasn't anything else she'd come for. It was only something she had been carrying since November.

"Your sister's line," she said. "In the Board's book. I went down and read it the week after." She was looking at the caddy, not at him. "Not birth. She has hidden what she is all her life because of it."

"Prudence put that in."

"I know who put it in." Vantic was quiet for a moment. "I was seven. The old man's room, under the roof. There was a lamp stone in it older than he was, and every winter afternoon about three it went dim, and he'd swear at it and get up and change it. And one afternoon it went dim, and I was cold and bored and I wanted it not to. And it didn't."

Ferra didn't move.

"He didn't say anything that day. He finished his column. Then he came and sat on the edge of his desk, which he never did, and asked me how long I'd been able to do that. I said I didn't know I could. He said, good, then you don't know now." The corner of her mouth moved. "And then he told me what the House does with a gifted child. It sends her up to the Circle with a letter, or it marries her into a house that wants the blood, and either way it stops counting her. Nobody ever wrote it down. Nobody ever needed to. He'd watched them do it twice." She took her hand off the chair. "He said a gift is the first column they'll read and the last one they'll forgive. Keep it out of the book. So I did."

"Your father," said Emeric.

"I've never asked my father what he knows." She buttoned her coat. "It's the one book in the House I've never wanted to price." At the door she stopped. "The old man showed me the lamp properly the next week. Two fingers, and from the side, so nobody across a room sees your hand do anything. He said if I was going to do it I could at least stop doing it badly."

She went down the stairs. Ferra looked at the door for a while after it shut.

"Seven," she said.

"Yes."

Ferra picked her pencil up and wrote her own line under the day's, and didn't turn the book round, which meant he'd see it later and argue with it.

Bram did it that night at the Works. Badly, and briefly, the way Bram did things.

Emeric only saw it because he'd come back for a drawing. It was gone nine and the Works was dark except for the drawing office's lamp at the end of the long gallery, and in the lamp, at the big table, Tess, with her stick against the table's edge and a sheet of the dam's second stage in front of her and a pencil in her teeth.

And Bram, in the doorway.

Emeric stopped in the shadow of the gallery where neither of them could see him, and didn't go any further.

Bram stood in the drawing office's door for a while. He had his coat on and his old hat, and the Works' great ring of keys in his hand, the iron ring the size of a dinner plate with every key to every door in the place on it, the forge house and the turbine hall and the dynamo house and the cold store and the gate. He'd carried it since the Works was a shed. Emeric had never in sixteen years seen it anywhere but on his belt.

Tess hadn't looked up. She knew he was there. Everybody always knew Bram was there.

"Dam's wrong," said Bram.

"It's not wrong. It's the second stage."

"Second stage is wrong. The spillway's too narrow. You'll get a spring flood over the top of it in year one and spend year two mending it."

Tess took the pencil out of her teeth. "I know. I'm widening it."

"Good." Bram came into the room. He put the ring of keys on the table, on top of the dam, with a clank that made the lamp's flame jump. "Here."

Tess looked at the keys.

She didn't touch them. She looked at them for a long time, the iron ring, the old keys and the new ones, the big one for the gate with the brass worn off its bow by seventeen years of Bram's thumb; and Emeric, in the dark of the gallery, saw her face go still in a way he'd only seen it go once before, in the dynamo house, on the floor, the day the dynamo came apart, before she'd known whether she was going to live.

"You said Tess," she said. "In the long room. Ferra told me. You said Tess, and signed, and didn't say anything else."

"Didn't need to."

"You didn't need to." She was still looking at the keys. "Bram."

"It's yours," said Bram. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at a point on the drawing office wall above her head where a shelf of old patterns had been since the room was built. "Works. All of it. The dam and the towers and the frames and the fibre and whatever that is you've got on your bench that thinks in light. Dagny's lot under you. Cade'll do what you tell him if you tell him in the morning. Merrin'll argue." He stopped. "Don't let anybody paint anything. They'll want to paint it. Don't let them."

"I won't let them paint anything."

"Right."

He turned to go. He got as far as the door.

And then he stopped in it, with his back to her, and stood there; and Emeric saw his big shoulders move once under the coat, the way a man's shoulders move when he takes a breath to say a thing and decides against it, and then decides against deciding.

"You were always having it," said Bram, to the door. "Since you were twelve. Since you came in at that gate with nothing and stood in my yard and asked me what the engine was for." He still didn't turn round. "I'd not have taken you in if I'd not known. I want you to know that. I'd not have taken in any child. I took you in because I'd seen you look at the engine."

Tess didn't say anything. Her hand had gone out across the table and closed on the ring of keys.

"That's all," said Bram.

And then, because he was Bram, and it had all gone on far too long, he turned his head just far enough to look back at her over his shoulder, and turned one big hand over at his side, palm up, the way he'd done across the long room in July when Ferra had written her name; and turned it back.

And went out, past Emeric in the dark of the gallery without seeing him, or seeing him and not stopping, which with Bram was the same thing; and down the gallery, and out of the Works by the side door, and the door banged behind him in the wind.

Tess sat at the table in the lamplight with the keys in her hand.

After a while she picked up the pencil again and put it back in her teeth, and moved the ring of keys off the drawing onto the table beside it, carefully, as if it were heavier than it was; and pulled the dam toward her; and began, very slowly, to widen the spillway.

Emeric went home without his drawing.

He put it in front of the six on the Saturday, because Ferra had written it in her book, and things in Ferra's book didn't stay between three people for long.

One sheet. In the middle of it, drawn twice, a band round a wrist: once from above, once cut through, the way he drew everything for the Works, with the parts named in small capitals and lines running out to them. Round the drawing, in his hand, what it did. Under that, longer, what it didn't. And across the top, underlined twice: After.

"Not before we sail," he said. "Not before we're back. Not before the Isle can pay for it. I want you to have seen it, that's all. So nobody's surprised in three years."

It went round the table.

Imani had it first, because she was nearest. She read the whole of it, the didn'ts as carefully as the dids, and then turned it sideways and looked at the cut-through drawing of the wrist for longer than anybody else would.

"It sits on the wrist," she said. "All day. All night."

"All day. All night."

"Over the artery." She put her finger on the place in the drawing. "Then it could count a pulse."

"Not this one. To count a pulse you need things we can't make yet." He nodded at the stack of Tess's books on the side table, the finished ones, roped in fours. "Twenty-two's the start of them. The dust one."

Imani took the pencil out of Ferra's hand without asking and wrote in the margin beside the wrist, small: pulse. Then, after a moment, under it: warm?

"It's February," she said, when he looked. "Half my ward's old men who can't keep warm in their own beds."

"Not this one either." But he looked at the word longer than he needed to, and she saw him do it, and passed the sheet on.

Ward read it in the time it took him to read anything, which was not long. "Does it stop a knife?"

"No. A spell. A knife, a fist, a bolt, a ball out of a gun, all straight through. It's not armour."

"Then it's not for me." Ward pushed it along the table. "It's for everyone who isn't me."

"That's the idea."

Saban read it twice, the second time with his finger going down the didn'ts. Then he sat back.

"A heartbeat is a lighthouse," he said.

He'd known Saban would go there first. He'd gone there first himself, at two in the morning, and come back the long way round. "It doesn't beat. It doesn't send anything and it can't be told anything. Nobody can talk to it, so nobody can tell it to stop. It sits there and does nothing at all, for years, unless somebody throws a spell at the man wearing it. Then it shouts. Once."

"And the Sand Room hears the shout."

"The Sand Room hears the shout. Where, and who threw it."

Saban thought about that for a while. "Then it's a second door into a room I already don't like."

"It's not a door. It's a man shouting in the street. You'd hear it with your own ears if you were close enough."

"I don't like it," said Saban. "But that's an honest answer." He passed it on.

Armand held it at arm's length, because his spectacles were in his other coat. He read it the way he read a student's proof, slowly, and set it down, and folded his hands on top of it.

"You'll want a law before you want this," he said. "Not after."

"What law?"

"What it's for." Armand looked down the table at him. "You're going to put a shield against spells on every wrist in the town. There aren't any spells in the town's streets. Not yet. There will be, because you've spent sixteen years making a town full of people who'll be able to throw them. The water. The stones. The Roll." He tapped the sheet. "Write down what a man may not throw at another man in Kaldo, and what it costs him, before you give the other man the shield. Otherwise the shield's the law. And a shield is a very poor judge."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Write it," said Emeric. "You. When we're back."

"I'll be dead."

"You've said that every year since you came south."

"And one year I'll be right," said Armand. But he took a pen out of his coat and wrote across the foot of the sheet, in his long thin hand, The law first. A.

Bram looked at the cut-through drawing for a long time, the way he looked at anything with parts, turning it a quarter round on the table to see it from the other side.

"Who makes it?"

"Tess."

"Right," said Bram, and pushed it on.

Ferra had it last. She'd seen it on the Thursday; she read it again anyway, all of it, the margins too, and then she turned it face down and squared it on the corner of the table, the way she did with sheets that were decided and not yet paid for, and wrote in the book.

21 Feb. E.'s citizen's band, from V.'s question. Shown to the six. After the return; not before the Isle pays for it. I.: pulse, warm. W.: not for knights. S.: a lighthouse; answered. A.: the law first. B.: Tess. To the after pile. F.

"Is there an after pile?" said Imani.

"There is now," said Ferra.

Ferra woke at two and his side of the bed was cold and the lamp was on in the little room across the landing.

She went and stood in the door in her nightgown with her arms folded. He was at the table under the window, as he'd been most nights since October, with a stack of written pages at his left elbow taller than the ink bottle and the carbon sheet between the leaves of the one he was writing, so that every line he wrote went down twice. He didn't look up.

"What's this one called?" she said.

It was what she'd asked in the summer, the first time, on the fourth night of the lamp. He'd said Long division, then. He'd been lying, a bit.

"Twenty-two," he said. "The last one. Measuring things very exactly. Keeping the room clean while you do it." He turned a page over onto the pile. "It's about dust, mostly. How there's always more of it than you think, and how small it can be and still ruin everything." He wrote another line. "Tess'll like it. She hates dust."

"How far are you?"

"Forty pages from the end of it." He put the pen down, finally, and rubbed his eyes. "Then that's the second shelf done. Then there's only the first shelf's new bits. The past-thats." He looked at the stack. "Three weeks."

"You sail in six."

"Three weeks," he said again. "Then I'll sleep."

She didn't say anything. She came into the room and stood behind his chair and put her hands on his shoulders, and looked down past his head at the page under his hand, at his writing going across it small and even and steady as it always did, as if he'd never been tired in his life; and at the bottom of the page, under a diagram of something she didn't understand with a great many very fine lines, a note in the margin in a different ink.

T.: you'll want to skip this bit. Don't.

She left her hands where they were until he'd finished the page.
