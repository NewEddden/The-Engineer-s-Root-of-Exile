Chapter 320 - Leaflets

Hobb set the words on the eighteenth of November, and the forme that had stood in its chase since August with a border round it and nothing in the middle was full by noon.

He set them himself. He would not let his compositors do it, though they were faster than he was now and he knew it; he stood at the case under the printing works' north window from six in the morning with his stick and his composing stick, picking the letters out one at a time with his old fingers and setting them, and reading each line back upside down and backward as he had read type for fifty years, and justifying it, and laying it in the galley. Ferra had given him the words on two sheets in her large plain hand, the front and the back, at five, at his door, in the dark. He had read them through once on the step under his lamp. Then he had gone in and taken his apron off its hook and not spoken to anybody until noon.

The front was short. Ferra had made it short. She had cut it at the long table four times in a week, and each time it had been read aloud and each time somebody had found a word in it that could go, until there was nothing in it that a carter could not read in the time it took a sheet to fall from the sky into his yard.

TO THE PEOPLE OF THE CAPITAL.

Then four lines. That Kaldo made war on the cardinal Augustine, and on the men who had sold the council's verdict against the king, and on nobody else. That Kaldo made no war on the faithful, nor on the people of the city, nor on the Lieutenant's soldiers who had been put to sleep in their houses, nor on their houses. That the cathedral would fall at noon on the day after this sheet came down, and that no soul should be in it, or within six hundred yards of it, from first light. That every gate of the city would be open, and every road out of the close.

And under the four lines, filling the bottom half of the sheet, the map: the capital as the sand table had it, walls and river and streets, in Hobb's woodcut, cut from Linus's survey by Hobb's best block cutter in a fortnight of evenings, and the ring cut again in three nights after the king's six hundred; the cathedral in the middle of it, solid black, with a ring drawn round it at six hundred yards, dotted, and inside the ring every street and lane and court named, so that a woman in the lower town could put her finger on her own door and see whether it was in or out. And the hour, in a box, in the largest type Hobb had. NOON.

The back was Doran's, and Armand's.

Doran's letter was on the left half, in his name, his parish, and his forty-one years. It was not long. It said that he was a priest of the Light and had been since he was nineteen, and had kept his faith through the plague and the war and the heresy case, and was writing to the faithful of the capital as one of them. It said that the Light was never in the stone. Not in that house, or any house. That the men who had told them it was, so that they would be afraid to leave a house when it was going to fall, had lied to them, and had known it. That it was no sin to go out of a church; it was no sin to stand in a street and watch it come down; and if a man found he was glad, God would forgive him that too, because God had seen what had been done in that house. And it ended with a thing Doran had written at the bottom of the page that afternoon, after the rest was fair copied, in a smaller hand, that Ferra had wanted to cut and had not been allowed to: that he would pray for every soul in the capital on the morning, the ones in the streets and the ones in the close and the one in the cardinal's house, and asked them to pray for him.

Armand's was on the right half, and it had no prayers in it.

It was headed with his name and his rank: Armand, Master of the Tower, High mage. Under it, in Hobb's smallest type, it was a column of figures, and nothing else that was not a figure or a name for a figure. What the rite was: that every winter, in the capital, from first snow to the Ascent, the Church and the tower took the sons of the realm and put them on a floor and filled them in a night until their bodies cracked, and that nine in ten died on that floor and one in ten stood up a knight. The count of those who had gone to the floor in Armand's own rotation, the years he had stood on it, by his books, and the count of those who had died there. Then the yard: that in Kaldo, for years now, knights had been made without the floor, slowly, in a yard, under a captain and a medic; that the yard had buried nobody it had fed; and that those in Kaldo who had died trying it in secret, against every warning, were written by name in Kaldo's own death book, which the close itself had read aloud in its case against the duke. Then the Proving: that in the last year, of sixty-one who had been prepared in Kaldo and gone to the floor because the rite was still the only road to a knighthood in the realm, twenty-two had stood; a little over one in three, against the floor's one in ten. Then the king: that His Majesty had been told all of this, in writing, under seal, in the year eleven, and had sent his own men south to be prepared; and that in the year eleven and the year twelve, the Church had kept the floor open, every winter, and fed the Light to every boy it could get, and counted none of them.

And at the foot, in larger type, alone on its line, the last thing Armand had written; the thing he had written last because he had said at the table that a man should end a proof with the one line that a person who read nothing else would remember.

WE COUNTED. THEY DID NOT.

There was no method in it. Not a word of how the yard was done, or the feeding, or the dose, or the stones; Armand had made sure of that, and Ward had read it twice to make sure Armand had made sure. The result, not the road. The road was for after, when there was a crown that could run it safely and a promise written beside it. Tonight there was only the count.

Hobb pulled the first proof at one. He brought it up to the castle himself, on his stick, damp, between two sheets of blotting paper, and laid it on the long room table in front of Ferra and stood back from it.

Ferra read it. She read the front and turned it over and read the back, slowly, with her finger, every line, as she read any proof; and found one letter upside down in Armand's column, a six that was a nine, and put a mark against it. Then she looked up at Hobb.

"Forty thousand," she said.

"By the twenty-second," said Hobb. "Both presses. Both shifts." He looked at the proof. "I've printed every sheet this town's put out since the first Almanac, Mistress. I've printed the census and the death book and the duke's laws and the Long Table menus. I printed the heresy sheet they built the case on." He picked up his stick. "This is the first one I've printed that'll be read by people who'll die if they don't read it."

He went back down the hill to his presses. They ran for four days and nights.



Emeric went into the capital on the twenty-first.

Ward had said no. He had said it once, as he said everything once, at the long table, with three fingers on the board; and then he had said what he would have instead, if Emeric was set on it, and Emeric was. Not Saban, who could not go, whose name was on the chancery's list. Not Ward himself, whose face had stood at the king's elbow for years before Kaldo and was known to every gate captain in the city by sight. Not Armand, who was a tower master and would be known to every mage in it by his note the moment he walked through a gate, young or old. Not Ferra; Ferra's name was on the heresy case, and the capital had looked at her for a whole evening at the investiture beside him. Imani's name was on it too; but at the investiture she had been one brown woman in grey among four hundred guests and had spent the evening at the back of the hall with a lord's fainting daughter, and nobody in the capital who mattered had looked at her twice. Bram's was on nothing at all. And Mira and Bren, in carters' clothes, because Ward would not let him go into that city without two of the three, and because Arthur's height was a thing a gate man would remember.

"Why?" Ward had said, at the end. "Tell me why. Not the table. Me."

"Because I'm going to count it," Emeric had said. "On the day. From the K Discovery, a mile up, every soul in that building, with every delver aboard. And I've never once been inside the close with my sense open. I don't know what it feels like. I don't know which walls are thick and which rooms are under which and where a man could be that I'd miss." He had put his hand flat on the board. "I want to stand in it. Once. Before. So that on the day I know what empty feels like."

Ward had looked at him a long time.

"Two days," he had said. "In and out. And if anything's wrong at a gate, you turn round."

The K Discovery put them down in a field of stubble eleven miles south of the capital at three in the morning, in the dark, behind a wood, the way she had put the ground team down behind the wood at the river road; and climbed again into the low cloud over the hills to wait. Saban's man met them at the field's gate with a cart. He was a plain man of forty in a carter's smock, with a long patient face and a voice like a man reading a list; and he looked at the five of them in the dark by the cart's lamp, one at a time, and at Emeric last and longest.

"You'll want your hat down," he said. "Further than that." He reached up without asking and pulled the brim of Emeric's knitted cap down to his brows, and looked again. "And the eyes."

Ivo had ground him spectacles for it. Round wire ones, with the glass smoked dark, like a man who has a weakness in the eyes from a forge or a fever and cannot stand the light; and Emeric put them on, and the world went the colour of strong tea, and Saban's man looked at him through the lamp and nodded slowly.

"You'll do," he said. "Carter's man with bad eyes. Keep your mouth shut and your face down and let the lady do your talking." He looked at Imani. "You're his sister. You're bringing him to the city to see a surgeon about his eyes. That's why you're in a cart with cloth in it from Grayrock and three hands to unload it." He climbed up onto the cart's seat. "Nobody at the South Gate'll ask more than that. They're not there to stop people coming in. They're there to stop the wrong people going out."

His hair was under the cap. All of it, the whole weight of his locs, coiled up and pinned and stuffed into the knitted wool, which was tight and hot and made his scalp itch. The capital had seen his hair once, four years ago, at the investiture, gathered back with Abloh's black cord and gold cuffs on its ends, under the great hall's lamps, on a duke in black; it had not seen it stuffed into a carter's cap. And it had never seen his eyes behind anything at all. He was thirty-five now, and broad in the shoulders, in a smock with straw on it, and his gold eyes were behind smoked glass, and that was the whole of the disguise and Saban's man said it was enough: people saw the cap and the glass and the smock, and nothing under them. Saban's man looked at him once more on the cart's seat, and seemed satisfied.

They went in by the South Gate at eight, with the market carts, in a queue.

It was the gate he had gone out of. He had gone out of it in a carriage, thirteen years before, in April, in the morning, with the curtain drawn and Ward riding beside the wheel, and had not once looked back at it. He looked at it now through his smoked glass from the cart's tail as they waited: the twin round towers, the arch between them, the gate captain in the arch with his book, a married man with his children's street written in a press in the Lieutenant's office. The captain looked at the cart. He looked at Saban's man, whom he had seen go in and out a hundred times. He looked at Imani on the seat, a brown woman in a plain grey travelling coat with her hair tied up under a scarf, and asked her business, and she told him, in a voice Emeric had never heard her use, small and anxious and provincial, a woman from the country who had never been in the city and was frightened of it and was here because her brother's eyes were bad. The captain looked at the brother at the cart's tail with his hat down and his dark spectacles, and at the two hands beside him, a big young man with a heavy face and a girl with a plait.

"Surgeon's on Tanner's Row," he said. "Ask for Mellor. He's the only one won't rob you." And waved them through.

Emeric sat on the cart's tail as it went in under the arch, and did not look up at it.

He felt the towers.

He had his sense out as he always had it out, and the capital came into it as the cart rolled in through the gate: a hundred and twenty thousand notes, more than he had ever felt in one place, a roar of them, like standing under a waterfall; and over the roar, above it, on the towers, cold and still and very close, the chests. He had known they would be there. Saban had told him in August and he had drawn the rings on the map in his head. He had not known what they would feel like. On the South Gate's two towers, one in each, above the arch, on the floor below the Venn's: a chest the size of a cradle, iron-banded, full of stones that were not doing anything, asleep, held in, the way the cove's seam had felt from the K Endeavor's deck, a breath held under the sea. And beside each, the small sour grey note of a man asleep on a straw tick. A breaker.

And beyond the gate towers, on the old gate towers along the wall, more; and on the four towers of the palace wall up the hill, four more. He could feel them all round the city, in a ring, wherever a pair of Venn's stood, cold, held, waiting, a necklace of them; and in the middle of the city, on the two porch towers of the cathedral, the last two, the biggest, under the Venn's.

If any one of the men on those ticks woke and broke what was in his chest, the ring round that tower would go out. If all of them did, together, on an order, the whole city would go out; every lamp, every stone, every note he could feel, gone, as if somebody had put a hand over his eyes and another over his ears. And he would be sitting on the tail of a cart in the middle of a hundred and twenty thousand people with no sense at all, as he had stood on the stream ground, blind, a man in the dark; with Imani on the seat in front of him and Bram and Mira and Bren beside him, and none of them able to feel a thing either, and a gate behind them that was there to stop the wrong people going out.

He sat on the cart's tail and breathed. Imani, on the seat, did not turn round. But her hand came back behind her, low, along the cart's side, where nobody could see it, and found his knee, and pressed it, once, as she pressed a pulse she already knew; and went back to the reins.



The cathedral filled the sky.

He had forgotten that. He had grown up under it. He had walked across its square on feast days at his father's side and stood at its altar rail and been named at its font, and he had seen it from the air from a ship three times in two years, small, a grey cross on a pale yellow ground among the roofs. He had lit it on the sand table in the map room layer by layer, to twenty-two layers, from memory and flights and Saban's walkers, and stood over it every night for three months. He had forgotten what it was to stand at the foot of it.

It went up out of the square in front of them like a cliff. The west front, grey, black with two hundred years of the city's smoke, with its three doors in their deep arches and the saints standing in rows in the stone round them; and over the doors the great round window, and over that the gable, and either side of the gable the porch towers going up and up, a hundred and eighty feet, with the Venn's on their tops, small at that height, upright, like two dark fingers raised against the sky. Off to the right, on the south side of the nave, standing on its own across a strip of grass, the bell tower; and the bells in it were going for the noon office, the big one and the three small, a slow heavy tolling that came down through the square and up through the soles of his boots.

Six hundred yards. His father's six hundred. He had paced it in his head on the sand table. Standing in the square, he could see how much of the city it was.

The square was full. People going in to the office, through the three doors, a steady stream: women with baskets, men in their work aprons, old men on sticks, mothers with children by the hand, soldiers in the Lieutenant's coats among them, off duty, with their caps in their hands. And on the porch steps, along the whole width of the west front, under the saints, the ones who did not go in: the beggars, the sick, the lame, on the steps in a long ragged row as they had been on every feast day of his childhood, with their bowls, and their sores, and their crutches laid beside them, waiting for the close to feed them at the north door after the office.

Saban's man left the cart in a yard off the square with a boy to mind it, and they went in with the crowd.



The nave was the length of a street and the height of the sky.

He had his sense open in it, wide, the whole of it, the way he would have it open on the day from a mile up; and he stood at the back of the nave by a pillar with his cap off, because every man's cap was off, and his locs pinned up in a knot at the back of his head under the scarf Imani had tied over it for him in the yard, a carter's man with sore eyes come in to pray for them; and he read the building.

It was enormous. He had known it would be; he had not known what it would be like to read. Stone everywhere, thick, old, the walls of the nave four yards through at the foot and the piers between the aisles thicker, so that his sense went into them and came out the other side slowly, as if wading; and above, the vault, a great ribbed ceiling of stone eighty feet up, that his sense went into and lost itself in like a man looking up into a forest. Under his feet, the crypt. He could feel it: the floor was not the bottom. There were rooms under the floor, low, vaulted, a great many of them, going away under the nave and the choir toward the east end, and in them, here and there, small cold quiet things that were not notes, that had been notes a long time ago, the dead of two hundred years in their places. None of them was his. The royal dead lay under the palace chapel across the city, not here; and his mother was not among them any more, and he had promised not to look.

And the people. Two thousand of them in the nave, perhaps more, standing, the notes of them filling the stone like water in a basin; and round the edges, in the aisles and the side chapels and up in the galleries over the aisles, the close's own, the canons and the lay brothers and the choir and the vestry women, and the ones he had not known were there: in a passage inside the thickness of the north wall, three men walking, slowly, in single file, with lamps. In a room over the north porch, a woman asleep. In a cell at the foot of the south-west tower's stair, behind a door that had not been opened for a long time, something that was a note, very faint, an old man, alone, not moving, and very much alive.

He marked them. He marked every one of them. The passage in the wall. The room over the porch. The cell at the foot of the stair. The places a man could be that a man reading from a mile up would miss, because they were inside stone that was too thick, or under it; and he put each one on the sand table in his head, where he would put it on the real one when he came home, and drew a ring round it.

Bram, beside him, was not reading people. Bram was reading the stone. Emeric could feel him doing it, slow and heavy and thorough, his sense going up the piers and along the ribs of the vault and down into the crypt the way his hands went over a casting, looking for where it was strong and where it was carrying and where it would go; and his face, in the grey light from the great window, was a face Emeric had seen on him once before, in the shed, the night he took the spirit level off its pegs.

"It's beautiful," said Bram, very low, to nobody.

The office began.



It was not Augustine who said it.

Saban's man had told them that at the field's gate, in the dark: that the cardinal was back. That he had come into the city by the north road in the first week of November, in a closed carriage, from Ostrel, with another cardinal beside him; and that he had gone up to the hill house above the falls, and not come down, and not said an office in the cathedral since, nor been seen in the close; and that the other one, the new one, said them all.

The new one came out of the choir to the altar at the noon bell, under the great round window at the east end, in the cardinal's red, with the close's canons in their white behind him in two lines; and the nave went still.

He was young. That was the first thing. Not young as the canons were young, the novices at the end of the lines with their thin necks; young as a man is young who has never once in his life been ill, or hungry, or out in weather he did not choose. Perhaps thirty. Tall, and straight, with fair hair cut close to a fine head and a face that looked as if it had been made by somebody who knew exactly what a face was for; and on his breast, on a chain of gold, a pendant the size of a child's palm: the Church's sign, the Eye, gilded, open, with the light from the window on it. And over the altar behind him, a great deal larger, hung from the vault on gold chains so long that they went up into the dark, the same Eye, gilded, a yard across, open, looking down the nave at all of them.

"Gregory," said Saban's man, beside Emeric, without moving his lips. "From Ostrel. The pope's bench."

His voice, when he began the office, was the most beautiful voice Emeric had ever heard in a man.

It went down the nave without being raised and filled it to the vault, low and warm and certain, like a hand laid on the back of the neck. He said the office as it had always been said, in the old words, the words of Emeric's childhood; and the people answered him as they had always answered, and the bells were quiet, and the light came down through the great window onto the altar and the gilded Eye and the young man under it, and the nave was entirely still. Emeric stood at the back by his pillar with his cap in his hand and listened to it, and did not understand for a while why his hands were cold.

Then the office was done, and Gregory came down from the altar to the rail.

The sick were brought to him. Not the ones on the steps. Three, out of the front of the nave, who had been there since dawn, who had been chosen; Saban's man said so afterward, that the close chose, that every feast day the canons went along the porch steps at first light and picked three to be brought in, and the rest stayed on the steps. A man on a board, carried by two lay brothers, with his leg bound up in rags from the knee to the foot and the rags dark. An old woman who could not straighten her back. A girl of eight or nine on her mother's hip, with a burn on her arm from wrist to elbow, wrapped, that she held out from her body as if it belonged to somebody else.

Gregory knelt to the man on the board first.

He put his hand on the man's forehead. Not on the leg; on the forehead, gently, the way a father puts his hand on a child's head who has a fever. And he bent his fair head down close to the man's, and spoke to him, low, so that only the front of the nave heard; and in the hush the words came down the stone to the back as clearly as if he had said them in Emeric's ear.

"Open the door, child. Hold nothing back from Him. Do you give yourself to the Light?"

"I do," said the man on the board.

"Then be still. Let your pain fall silent before it."

And Emeric felt it.

He had pulled his own sense in before the man at the rail began, as Armand had taught him in the first year to listen without leaning, a thread laid flat along the floor and not thrown; and a delver with his own thread inside another man's head is deaf to the room behind him. Emeric knew that from the inside. He had been that deaf himself, at Ferra's table, with his father's memory in his hands.

He felt it as he would have felt a hand go into his own chest. Every delver in the cathedral felt it, he understood afterward, and there were three of them at the back of the nave by one pillar, and at least one more at the altar rail; but he felt it most, because he had his sense open wider than any of them and was listening for exactly this and had not known it. It was the delve. It was the thing he did. The thing he had done for Harrold at Ferra's table in July, to show his father a day; the thing he had done to Grier with two fingers on a wrist; the thing Bram did in a stone and Tess in a lattice and Imani in a man's mind on a ward. It went out from Gregory's hand on the man's forehead, a thread, fine and sure and very strong, the strongest he had ever felt from anybody who was not himself; and it went in. Into the man. Through the door the man had just been told to open, that he had opened, because a priest had asked him in the old words in God's house and he had said I do.

And inside the man, the mana moved.

That was the tell. Not out of Gregory's hand, as a mage's casting came out of a mage's hand with its glyph hanging in the air for every soul to see; there was no glyph. There was nothing to see. Inside. The man's own mana, and something more that Gregory sent down the thread after it, gathering in the man's body, going down the leg under the rags, finding the wound. Emeric felt it find the wound. He felt it go to the place and spend itself there, quickly, too quickly, all at once, the way a man pours water on a fire without looking where it goes; and the pain went out of the man's note like a lamp turned down.

The man on the board cried out. Not in pain. He cried out and put his hands over his face and wept.

Gregory took his hand off the man's forehead and stood up, and turned to the nave, and said, in that voice, gently, "The Light has heard him."

And the nave said, Amen; two thousand voices, all at once, so that the sound went up into the vault and came back down; and the lay brothers lifted the board, and the man on it was still weeping, and they carried him away down the side aisle toward the north door, and as he went past the pillar where Emeric stood Emeric felt the leg.

The pain was gone. The skin was closed. He could feel that: the wound under the rags was not open any more, it had a skin over it, new, thin, pulled across it like a sheet over a bed nobody had made. And under the skin, the rot was still there. All of it. Deep, in the muscle and the bone, dark and slow and spreading, exactly where it had been before, with nothing done to it at all except that it could not be felt any more, and could not be seen, and could not drain.

Imani had felt it too.

She was beside him at the pillar with her scarf over her hair and her hands folded in front of her like a country woman at an office. She had not moved. Her face was perfectly calm. But her note had gone white, all of it, in one breath, the way it had gone white once before that Emeric knew of, on a night in the plague year in a house in the lower ward with a child in her arms; and her hands, folded in front of her, were pressing each other so hard that the knuckles stood out.

Gregory went to the old woman. Then to the girl with the burn. Emeric made himself feel both of them. He made himself feel the door asked and opened, I do, and the thread go in, and the pain go out; and the old woman's back come straight for the length of the nave, under a weight of something Gregory had put in her that would wear off by night, and the girl's burn skin over under its wrap, closed, pink, clean, with the deep part of it under the new skin not mended at all.

The girl's mother wept. The nave said, Amen.

And Emeric stood at the back by his pillar with his cap in his hand and his smoked glass over his eyes, and understood what the Church was.

Not the verdict. He had known about the verdict since April. Not the rite, or the floor, or the nine in ten; he had known about the floor for years, and so had Armand, and it was on the back of forty thousand sheets in Hobb's works tonight. Not Augustine, and the channel, and a closed right hand. He had known about all of those.

This. That they did it here. In the open. In front of everybody, on a feast day, in the old words, to the poor and the sick and a little girl with a burn, who came because they were told the Light would mend them, and said I do because a priest asked them to, and opened the door into themselves because God's house was where you opened it. And a young man in red went in through the door with the thing that Emeric did, the thing that a handful of people in Kaldo could do and that Emeric had kept secret for thirteen years because he was afraid of what anybody would do with it if they knew it could be done; and turned their pain off, and put a skin over the rot, and stood up and said the Light had heard them. And they wept, and thanked God, and were carried out of the north door to die of what was under the skin, later, at home, and be told when they died that their faith had not been strong enough.

They called it God. They had always called it God. It was the delve.

He did not know how long he stood there. Bram's hand came onto his arm at last, heavy, and Mira's on the other side, and they took him out of the north door into the grey light with the crowd, and he went.



It was on the way back to the cart, in a lane off the square, that the bell rang.

It rang once. Not in his ears; in his sense. It came out of nowhere into the roar of the city's notes, a single clear small sound, like a bell rung once somewhere far off in a fog, and he stopped in the lane so suddenly that Bren walked into his back.

He knew it. He knew it at once, the way a man knows his own name called across a market: a bead. The grey one, off Old Edrin's band. Blue beads and a grey one on a wire, that Armand had strung with his own old hands and Emeric had cut from the inside, the grey one, so that it would call through the bond if Edrin ever needed calling for; and that had been taken off Edrin's wrist two years ago in a dark lane in a dead field, while he went for a pie, by a kind hand and a young man's voice, with his cuff buttoned for him afterward and his supper left beside him. They had looked for it for a month and never felt it. It had not called once in two years. It was calling now because somebody, somewhere, had just taken it out of whatever kept it quiet, for a breath; out of iron, perhaps. And it rang, once, with a direction in it, as clear as a man pointing.

Then it stopped. Whoever had taken it out had put it back.

"North," said Emeric. "Up the hill. Toward the palace end. Close." He did not move. "Mira."

Mira went. She did not ask what. She had been with him at the river road; she knew his face when he felt a thing. She went up the lane at a walk with her plait down her back and her carter's smock and her hands in her pockets, as if she had remembered an errand, toward where he had pointed, and Bren made to go after her and Emeric put a hand on his arm.

She was back in a quarter of an hour.

"Big house," she said, low, falling in beside them as if she had never left. "Three streets up. The one with the green door and the lions on the gateposts. There's a jeweller's man just come out of it with his box under his arm, and a servant shutting the door behind him." She did not look back. "There's a shield over the door. Gold, with a black bend."

"Aldane," said Saban's man.

Emeric stood in the lane and thought about it for exactly as long as it took Bren to stop looking at him.

Aldane had Edrin's bead. Aldane had had it since the lane, perhaps, or since somebody sold it to him; and Aldane had kept it in iron, because he could feel that it was a thing that was more than a stone and could not tell what, and Aldane was not a man who would go to the Church to ask, because the Church would take it off him. And this morning Aldane had shown it to a jeweller, to see whether a jeweller could tell him; and the jeweller had held it under a cloth, and it had rung, once, for as long as the cloth was off; and gone back in its iron, and the jeweller had gone away with nothing.

He could take it back. He could have Saban's man's people in that house by tomorrow night. And then Aldane would know that the thing in his iron box had been a thing somebody could find in the middle of a city of a hundred and twenty thousand, from a lane, in a breath; and Aldane would tell somebody, because that was what Aldane did with anything he had that was worth something; and the somebody would think about what else of Kaldo's might ring.

"Leave it," he said.

Saban's man looked at him.

"Leave it where it is," said Emeric. "Tell Saban. Somebody on that house. Not inside it; on it. I want to know when Aldane goes out of that door with men behind him, a day before he goes." He started walking again. "And tell Edrin we found it. And that it's staying where it is, for now."

"He'll ask why."

"He's got his new one," said Emeric. "He'll understand."



They were in the K Discovery's hold by two in the morning, and in the air, and going south; and Imani had not said a word since the north door.

She sat on the bench in the hold with her scarf in her lap and her hands on it, and looked at the deck plates. Bram was asleep against the bulkhead with his mouth open. Mira and Bren were up on deck with Fletcher. Emeric sat beside her and did not say anything either, because he did not know what to say, and because he knew she would say it when she had it.

"The man on the board," she said at last, somewhere over the hills.

"Yes."

"His leg's rotten. To the bone. It'll take him in a week. Less." She did not look up. "And the skin's closed over it. So it can't drain. So nobody can see it. So he'll go home and lie in his bed and thank God, and his wife will thank God, and when it takes him they'll say his faith wasn't strong enough." She turned the scarf over in her hands. "I've seen it. I've seen it a dozen times, on the ward, in the plague year and since. People who came to me from the capital with a thing that had been healed. Clean skin. Rotten under. I thought it was their doctors. I thought the capital's doctors were butchers." She looked at her hands. "It wasn't the doctors."

"No."

"It was that. The Light." She was quiet. "Can I do it?"

He looked at her.

"Not that," said Imani. "Not the door. Not the I do. God, not that." She said it fast and hard. "The other part. The part where it goes in and finds the place and spends itself there. Can I do that?"

He had known she would ask that too. Not today; some day. Since August, since the cracked stone, since the night in his lap when she worked out she was heavy. He had watched her do things with her hollow sense on the ward for two years that nobody else in the world could do, slowly, by reading, and he had watched her start, without knowing it, to do more than read.

"You've told me twice," he said, "that you can feel heat. On the ward. Over a thing that's gone bad. Before you look."

"Yes."

"Today. On the porch steps. Going in."

Imani looked up at him at last.

"A woman with a leg," she said slowly. "On the fourth step. Under the saints. I felt it before I saw it. It was hot. Under my hand, almost, and I was a yard off." She stopped. "I nearly stopped. To look at it."

"And your scan's slow on yourself. You told me. A week, for your number."

"Yes."

"That's the start of it," he said. "The heat's you feeling where the body's spending. Where it's already trying to mend, and can't, because it's got nothing to mend with. And the slow scan's you. Your own mana, more of it than there was, getting in the frame's way." He held her eyes. "You don't need a door. You need a body to know you're there, and to let you; and you need to know it so well that when you send something into it you can send it to the one place, and not everywhere. And you need to spend it there slowly. Not pour it. Feed it." He stopped. "He poured it. He poured it all in at once to turn the pain off, because the pain was all anybody would see, and he didn't care what it did underneath. You'd do the opposite. You'd feed what's underneath, and let the pain stay as long as it needs to, so you could see it was getting better."

Imani looked at him in the hold's dim lamp.

"Teach me," she said.



He taught her on the twenty-third, on the ward, at six in the morning, with a dock man's hand.

The dock man had come in in the night with a hook through the web of his thumb and four days of it going bad before his wife made him come: red to the wrist, hot, swollen tight, with a line going up the inside of the arm that Imani did not like at all. She had cleaned it and opened it and drained it and given him Hester's broth, the mould water out of Hester's cheese cellar that killed what was in a wound if anything would; and he lay on the cot in the end bay with his arm on a board and his face grey, and looked at the two of them standing over him as if they were going to cut it off.

"Nobody's cutting anything," said Imani. "I'm going to put my hand on it. It'll feel warm. If it hurts, you say so, and I stop." She looked at him. "You can say no."

The dock man looked at her. Then at Emeric.

"Is it the Light?" he said.

"No," said Imani. "It's me."

The dock man thought about it, and then put his arm a little further toward her on the board.

She laid her hand on it, above the wound, where the heat was. Emeric stood behind her with his own hand flat between her shoulder blades, as she had laid hers between his a hundred nights in the dark, and his sense in her, beside hers, following; and he showed her.

Not much. Not the whole of it; there was not time for the whole of it and she would not have wanted it. Only the first thing: that she had more in her than she had had, and that it was hers, and that it would go where she sent it if she knew where she was sending it. Her sense was already in the dock man's hand. It had been in the hand since she laid hers on it, reading, slowly, as she read everything, the swollen flesh and the drained wound and the bad heat, hollow, from inside. He showed her how to take a little of what was in her, a very little, and send it down her own arm and out of her palm and into the hand, along the road her reading had already made. And where to put it: not on the wound. On the flesh round the wound, the living flesh, that was fighting what was in it and losing because it was tired. And how fast: slowly. A trickle. Like water into dry ground, as Ferra had fed the Cradle's stones.

Imani did it.

He felt her do it. It was clumsy, the first time; it went too far in and too fast and she pulled it back with a hiss through her teeth, and the dock man said, warm, that's warm, in a small voice; and then she did it again, slower, and it went where she sent it, a thread, very thin, into the tired red flesh round the wound, and stayed there, and spent itself, a little at a time.

She held it for the length of a slow count of a hundred. Then she took her hand away and sat down on the stool by the cot, rather suddenly, and put her head down between her knees.

"That's what it costs," said Emeric.

"I know." Her voice came up from between her knees. "I can feel it. It's like running." She sat up. Her face was grey under the brown, and her eyes were very bright. "Did it do anything?"

"Look."

She looked. The red had not gone. The heat had not gone. But the line up the inside of the dock man's arm, the line she had not liked, was paler by a shade; and the flesh round the wound, under her own reading, was not tired in the same way. It was working.

The dock man was looking at his arm too.

"It still hurts," he said.

"Good," said Imani. "It's meant to. It'll hurt less tomorrow, and less the day after, and you'll be able to see why." She stood up. "I'll do it again at noon, and tonight. If you'll let me."

"I'll let you," said the dock man.



She taught the class at nine.

Not the thing itself. Nobody on her ward but Imani could do the thing itself, and nobody would, she said, for years, perhaps ever; it wanted the hollow sense, and the hollow sense wanted what Imani had and they had not. But she stood in front of her medicine students in the long room off the ward, the ninety from Commencement and the twenty older ones who taught them, on benches, with their slates, and told them what she had seen in the cathedral, plainly, without the names. A man brought to a rail with a rotten leg, and asked, in the old words, to open himself, and he said I do; and a priest went in, and turned off his pain, and closed the skin, and stood up and said the Light had heard him. And the man had been carried out weeping to die.

The long room was very quiet.

"He was never told," said Imani. "Not what was going to be done to him. Not what it would cost him, or what it couldn't do. Not whether he had anything in him to spend. He was asked to open a door, and he opened it, because he was frightened and in pain and a man in red asked him in God's house." She looked at them. "And the man in red never once read him first. Never looked at what he had. Just went in and spent."

She picked up the chalk, and wrote on the board behind her, in her capitals, one word.

ROOM.

"Every body has a number," she said. "How much it can hold. Master Armand reads it. I read it, slowly. You'll learn to guess at it. That's not this." She put her finger on the word. "This is how much is empty. Today. Now. Whether a body's got anything left in it to spend on mending, or whether it's spent it all already on staying alive, and you'll kill it if you ask it for more." She put the chalk down. "Before you do anything to anybody, a cut, a draught, a bone set, anything that'll ask a body to work: you find out its room. You read it, or you ask it, or you guess and say you're guessing. And then you tell the person. What you're going to do, and what it'll cost them, and that they can say no." She looked along the benches. "Then you ask."

A girl on the front bench put her hand up. Edda Rowe, from Grayrock, in her let-down dress, with her slate.

"And if they can't answer, Mistress? If they're out of their wits, or a baby, or..."

"Then you read harder," said Imani, "and you write down what you read, and you write down why you did what you did, so somebody can check you afterward." She looked at the girl for a moment. "And you never, ever, call it anything but what it is."

Edda Rowe wrote ROOM on her slate in large letters, and underlined it twice.



Imani refused the K Amara that evening.

Emeric had put her name on the K Amara's rolls for the war, at the head of the surgeons' list, in October, without asking her; because the K Amara was the flagship and would be in the middle of everything, and he wanted her where he was, and where the shell was, and where the guns were between her and anybody who wanted to hurt her. He had known when he wrote it that she would see it. He had not known what she would say.

She came up the K Endeavor's gangway at the yard at seven, where he was with Cade going over the hospital hull's racks, with Hester behind her carrying a crate of stoppered jars against her chest like a baby, and two of the ward's orderlies behind Hester with two more.

"I've crossed myself off," she said. "Your black barge. I've written myself onto this one."

Cade, beside him, became very interested in a rack.

"Imani..."

"Your tower has a view," said Imani. "Mine has the wounded." She stepped aside for Hester, who went past her down the hold with the jars of broth and began, without waiting to be told, to stow them in the racks that had been built for liquid mana, in their plated sleeves. "The K Amara's where the fighting is. That's right; you'll want to be there. The K Endeavor's where they'll bring them after. That's where I'll be." She looked at him. "You'll have Bettany. She's better than me with a saw. I'm better than anybody with what comes after the saw."

He opened his mouth.

"And I can do a thing now," said Imani, "that I couldn't do on the twenty-first. I'm not doing it from a tower." She folded her arms. "You can fight me on it. I'd rather you didn't. I'd rather you went and looked at your guns and came back at nine and ate something, because you've had nothing since the hill."

He looked at her for a long time, standing at the head of the hospital hull's hold in her ward apron with her arms folded and Hester's jars going into the racks behind her one by one.

He did not fight her on it.

"Nine," he said.

"Nine," said Imani. On her way past him to the hatch she took his wrist, briefly, hard, between finger and thumb, the way she took a pulse between two patients on a full ward, without looking up from the racks; and let it go, and went down into the hold to tell Hester she was stowing the broth upside down.



The leaflets came up the hill on the twenty-fourth, in bales.

Forty thousand sheets, quarter-folio, in two hundred bales of two hundred each, tied with Hobb's string and stamped on the outside of every bale, in red, by Hobb's own hand: DO NOT OPEN BEFORE THE DAY. They came up on four drays from the printing works, slowly, through the town, with the drivers walking at the horses' heads, and the town stopped work to watch them go by. Nobody in the town knew what was in them. Everybody in the town knew.

They went into hull two's hold at the yard, in racks, behind a door with Ward's lock on it.

Emeric stood in the hold when the last bale was in, alone, with the door open behind him and the yard's lamps coming on outside, and looked at them: two hundred bales of paper, stacked in the dark, smelling of Hobb's ink. He took one sheet out of the bale that had been opened for the count, and held it up to the light from the door, and read it, both sides, as Ferra had read the proof, every line, with his finger.

NOON. Six hundred yards. Every gate open. The Light was never in the stone. We counted. They did not.

He folded it, and put it inside his coat, in the place where until Hollow Night he had carried a slip with a number on it that Ferra had read to him from four miles down, before a boy in a black sack took it off him; and went out, and shut the door, and Ward's lock clicked.
