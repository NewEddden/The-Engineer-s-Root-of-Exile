Chapter 217 - The Oak

On the third morning after the battle the stream ground still smelled of it.

Emeric was in the quartermaster's tent with Osric and the ransom list when the rider came in.

Osric was reading it to him, slowly, with his finger under each name, and heard the horse first. Emeric turned, and spread his sense out of the tent the little way it went these days without effort, and found a note coming across the camp at a run, thin and frayed at the edges the way a man's note went when he had been frightened all night and then ridden hard on it. One of the levy. Bennet's picket, from the north road. He knew the note before he knew the face.

The boy came in under the flap with mud to the hip and his rifle still slung, and stood there, and opened his mouth, and nothing came out of it. Osric got up and put him on the stool and gave him the cup off the table, which had been Emeric's, and was cold.

"Take your time," said Emeric, and did not mean it.

"Cressing ford," said the boy. "Last night. Second watch." He drank. "They came down the bank, sir. Out of the alders. Horse. We thought they'd all gone north. Everybody said they'd all gone north."

"How many?"

"I don't know. Thirty. Forty. In the dark." He put the cup down on his knee and held it there with both hands. "Bennet's got a sword cut on his arm. Long one. He's at the ford still, he wouldn't come. Dicky Marsh is dead." He swallowed. "And they took five. I saw them go. Tied on behind the horses, running. I was in the water under the bank. I didn't shoot. I'd have hit our lads." He looked up. "I didn't shoot, sir."

"You were right not to," said Emeric. "Which five?"

The boy told him. Jory Cobley, the cooper's boy off the river street. Hugh Ashby. Kit Bastow and his brother Abner, who had joined together and stood in the same rank and had been told not to by everybody. And Arthur.

Osric said, "Oh," very quietly, beside him.



Ward was at the earthwork when Emeric found him, watching a party fill in the last of the trench the horse had never reached. He heard it to the end without turning round, which was how he heard everything, and when it was finished he said, "Aldane's rearguard."

"His rearguard went north on the twenty-seventh. Saban had it from three people."

"Most of it went north on the twenty-seventh." Ward looked at the trench. "A rearguard leaves somebody behind. That is what it is for. Somebody who knows the country, and has a grudge, and has not been told to stop." He turned then. "Five men taken alive at night and run off behind horses. They did not kill them at the ford. They could have."

"I know they could have."

"Then they want them for something."

Emeric was already walking.

He did not remember, afterward, deciding any of the next part. He remembered Declan's face when he took every mounted man off the burial details and the bridge and sent them out in pairs on every road north of the stream. He remembered Linus on his knees in the map tent with the survey sheets weighted down with rifle cartridges, drawing a red ring round the ford, and a second ring, and saying in a reasonable voice that inside twelve miles of Cressing there were thirty-one farms and nine hamlets and two manors and Lisle's hunting lodge. He remembered Saban in the doorway, pulling on his gloves, saying he would take the lodge himself because the steward owed him money.

And he remembered riding.

He rode to the ford, where Bennet sat on a stone with his arm bound and would not go back, and Dicky Marsh lay under a cloak with his boots put straight; and he followed the cut ground up the lane to the north road, where every cart and fleeing horseman of three days had gone, and the track went into all the others. He stood in the road and spread his sense as far as it would go. A few hundred yards. On a quiet day perhaps half a mile, thinning at the edge until he could not tell a man from a cow from a hedge full of wrens. He knew Arthur's note. Small and bright and quick. He would know it anywhere he could reach.

He could not reach it. He got back on the horse.

He went to every farm on Linus's first ring himself, because the sense went where he went and nobody else's did. He rode into yards and spread it and rode out again. At the third farm the farmer came out with a fork, because he was a Lisle tenant and Emeric was the man who had just killed four hundred of his betters in a morning, and stood in his own yard holding it, very white, and his wife behind him in the door holding a child. Emeric looked at the fork. He did not get down. He asked whether horse had come by in the night with men on foot behind, and the farmer said nothing, and Emeric heard himself say, quite quietly, that if the farmer had seen them and did not say so he would come back with the levy and take the farm apart to the footings and the farmer with it.

The farmer said they had heard horses. Toward the river. Before midnight. They had not looked out.

Emeric thanked him and rode out of the yard. Halfway down the lane he stopped the horse and sat on it in the road and found that his hands on the reins were shaking, and that he had meant it. Not as a thing said to frighten a man. He had meant it. If the farmer had said no, he would have done it.



Emeric came into camp at dusk on his third horse, because he had ridden the first two until they would not go, and went to the map tent, and stood at the trestles with the cold coming off him and looked at Linus's rings.

"You have been to twenty-two farms," Ward said.

"Twenty-three."

"Twenty-two. You went to the mill farm at Hollow End twice." He did not look up from the sheet. "You will not find them by riding. They are not hiding them."

"What?"

"A party that wants to hide five men cuts their throats at the ford and leaves them in the water." Ward put a cross on the sheet, near Hollow End, and wrote the hour beside it. "They did not. They took them alive and ran them behind horses through the dark, which is slow, and leaves a track, and costs them. That is a party that wants five men alive somewhere particular, by a particular time." He laid the pencil down. "They want them seen."

Emeric looked at him.

"By whom?"

"By you, perhaps. By the road, certainly." Ward put his finger on the survey, on the north road, where it came up out of the river land toward Lisle's border and Aldane's beyond it, and moved it along. "Somewhere on that road. Somewhere a carter will pass in the morning, and a drover, and a priest, and tell it in the next town. They lost a hundred and eighty-eight knights to a levy of farmers in a quarter of an hour. They cannot answer that in the field. So they will answer it on a board."

The tent flap went back and Rylan came in with a girl.

"Tell him what you told me," said Rylan. "Go on. He won't bite. He's had his supper."

Emeric had not had his supper. The girl looked at him doubtfully.

"There's a cart under the oak," she said. "At Cressing Cross. Where the two roads go. There's men on it."

"Standing on it," said Rylan, and his voice did something Emeric had not heard it do before. "Tell him the rest."

"Standing on it, with ropes on. Up to the branch." She shifted the basket. "And a board nailed on the tree with writing, and three men with a fire. In brown. They gave me a penny for the eggs, only they didn't take the eggs, they said to take them home and tell my da what I saw, and tell everybody, and say they're going at first light." She looked at the floor of the tent. "Da said not to come here. I came anyway."

"Going," said Emeric. "Who's going?"

"The cart," said the girl. "They said they're driving it off at first light. So everybody'd see."



Rylan had had the board read to him by the girl, who could not read, and so had only the shape of it: that the men on the cart were outlaws, and had slain knights of the realm against all law, and were to be hanged as the law of the march allowed. Ward heard it with his face shut.

"It's bait," said Rylan.

"It is also bait." Ward put two fingers on the oak. "Three men and a fire, at a crossroads, in open country, a quarter mile inside Lisle's line. If we come in force, we have crossed his border under arms, after a battle, and Lisle writes to the crown tonight. If he comes alone," and he did not look at Emeric, "a prince goes into a lord's country in the dark and does whatever he does, and in the morning there are three dead men in brown and a burnt cart and nobody to say how, and a story. Either way the five are a price."

Emeric could be there in a quarter of an hour. Not on a horse. He knew it the way he knew his own hand; the glide off the high ground behind the camp, over the river land in the dark, the air turning under him and the weight letting go. He could be over the oak before the three in brown had finished their supper. He could lift five men off a cart and hold them in the air all night, if he had to. He could put three men in brown flat on their faces in the grass and keep them there until they wept.

And in the morning there would be a story about a prince who flew.

"No," said Ward, who had been watching his face.

"I didn't say anything."

"No," said Ward again. "Not in the air. Not where a road can see. You have done one thing tonight already that you will think about." He meant the farmer, and Emeric did not ask how he knew. "Do not do a second."

Rylan had been standing with his arms folded, looking at the oak on the survey, and now he unfolded them.

"It's not a dead field," he said.

They looked at him.

"It's not." He touched the inside of his own left forearm, through the sleeve. "Three lads in brown, a fire and a cart. No grey mages. Nobody's been bursting stones up there, or Emeric'd have felt it all day like a hole in his teeth. It's open country, it's flat, the girl says there's a hedge on the drove road right up to a gate, and sixty paces of grass from the gate to the oak, and there's thirty-nine lads in this camp with a glyph on their arm that works." He looked at Ward. "In the quarry it didn't. At the stream it didn't. Up there it will."

"Thirty-eight," said Ward. "Ashby is on the cart as well."

"Thirty-eight, then."

"Sixty paces," said Ward. "From the gate to the cart. In the open."

"Six steps," said Rylan. "Feathered. If they've kept their bands charged, and they have, because I've had them at it every night since the stream." He shrugged. "Six steps and stop, and you're at the horses' heads before the horses know you've left the gate."

"Twenty," said Ward. "Not thirty-eight. The best twenty. At the gate before the sky changes. Two to each horse's head, one to the box, the rest to the cart bed, under the men. Not to the ropes. To the men. Lift them. The ropes come after." He looked up. "Nobody fires. A shot in the dark and the horses go, and they go with five men behind them. Rifles stay at the gate with a rank who will not use them unless I say."

"You're coming," said Rylan. It was not a question.

"I am coming." Ward looked at Emeric for the first time since the girl had come in. "And so is he. On the ground. On his belly, in the ditch, behind the hedge. With his hands where I can see them."



They went at midnight.

Twenty of Rylan's tattooed and twenty rifles went up the old drove road in file, sunk between its hedges, with the frost already ringing underfoot and nobody speaking. Ward walked in front in his plate with the crest off and a farmer's cloak over it that hid nothing.

Emeric walked near the front behind Ward, and held his sense in.

He did not think about the cart.

About two in the morning the hedge on the right fell away, and there was a gap, and through the gap, a long way off across the dark fields, a light. Small and red. A fire, low, banked for the night.

Ward put his hand up and the file stopped behind him.

They went the last half mile on their bellies.



The ditch was full of last year's leaves and a skin of ice that broke under him. He lay in it with the frozen leaves against his cheek and the hedge above him, hawthorn, black and bare and as thick as a wall, the stems bent over and laid along low down and woven, someone's good work from twenty years ago; and through a gap in the root of it, where a badger or a fox had gone through, he could see the crossroads.

The gate first. A field gate on the drove road, five bars, grey wood, shut. Beyond it the grass, white already with the frost, sixty paces of it, more or less, the girl's sixty, running level to the crossing. The two roads, pale in the starlight. And at the crossing, where the four corners met, the oak.

It was a crossroads oak, the kind that had been big before anybody in Kaldo had a name, wider than it was high, its lowest limb running out level over the north road as thick as a man's body. On that limb there were five ropes. And under the ropes, the cart.

It was a farm cart, a big four-wheeled one with high boards, the kind that carried hay; and it had been backed in under the limb, with two horses in the shafts facing up the north road, standing with their heads low and blankets over their backs, asleep on their feet, the way horses sleep. On the bed of the cart stood five men.

They were standing. That was the first thing, and the thing he went on seeing afterward, for weeks, when he shut his eyes: that they were standing, all five, in a row on the boards, with their hands tied behind them and the ropes going up from their necks to the limb, too short to let them sit. They had been standing since the afternoon. Two of them were leaning against each other, shoulder to shoulder, and he thought that would be the Bastow brothers, and found he was right, because the one on the end, the bigger one, had his head turned and was talking very low to the other, on and on, and the other was nodding. Next to them a man with his head down. Next to him a man who kept shifting his feet, a little lift of one and then the other, very small, all the time, and Emeric knew it was Jory Cobley because Jory had shifted his feet like that in the line at every drill for a year and been told off for it by Ward every time.

And at the far end, nearest the fire, standing straight, Arthur.

He was in his department coat. Somebody had taken his boots. He was standing on the frozen boards of the cart bed in his stockings, with his hands behind him and the rope going up from his neck into the dark of the oak, and his head up, looking north up the road toward nothing, and not moving at all.

By the fire, below and to the left, three men. One asleep under a horse blanket. One sitting up with his back to the oak's great trunk and a cudgel across his knees. One standing, stamping, with his hands under his arms, looking at the cart. Above them, on the trunk of the oak, a board, white, nailed at the height of a man's face, with black writing on it that Emeric could not read from here and did not need to.

He lay in the ditch with his face in the leaves and looked at Arthur's stockinged feet on the boards of the cart, and the thing began.

He felt it start. That was the difference. In the cellar he had felt it when it was already coming up past his stomach, past his teeth, and there had been nothing to do by then but hold on to his teeth. This time he felt it start, low down, the way you feel the first turn of a wheel through the floor of a mill before you hear it: a warmth that was his and not his, and with it, before anything else, the clearness. As if a window had been wiped.

He could see the grain of the gate. He could see, at sixty paces, in starlight, the frost on the shoulders of Arthur's coat, and that one of the buttons was gone, the third, and a thread hanging from where it had been. He could see the cudgel on the knees of the man against the trunk, and the knot in it, and the man's hand on it, a big hand with a ring on the smallest finger. He could see every twig of the oak against the stars. Everything very clear and very near and very quiet.

The fire stood up.

Not much. A hand's height. The banked red of it lifted off the ash and stood, yellow, straight, the way a flame stands when a door shuts on a draught; and the standing man by it said something and looked down at it, puzzled, and held his hands out to it, glad.

In the ditch the ice under Emeric's cheek went soft.

He felt the levy behind him in the dark down the length of the ditch as a row of notes, close, because they were close, forty men and Rylan and Ward lying in the leaves within twenty yards of him; and he felt the first of them feel it. A man ten yards back put his hand to his head. Another drew a breath through his teeth. Rylan, who was beside the gate, turned his head very slowly and looked back along the ditch, and his face in the starlight was perfectly still. And under all of it, behind his eyes, in the long quiet room with its light from nowhere, Emeric felt the door lean on its hinges.

Not open. Lean. The way a door leans when the wind is on the far side of it and the latch has not yet given.

This was the moment before.

He knew it. He knew it the way Armand had said a man learns to know the moment before he loses his temper, the half breath when it is still a thing coming and not yet a thing come; and he had not learned it in years, he had learned it in a cellar and a sickroom and a hospital chair with an old man's hands on his stick, and it was not enough, it could not possibly be enough. And here it was anyway. Clear as the frost on Arthur's coat. The wiped window, the fire standing up, the door leaning. In a breath it would not lean. In a breath it would go, out along his mana, across sixty paces of frozen grass, and take hold of every note it touched, the three men by the fire, the horses, the five on the cart, Arthur; and drive a nail in behind their eyes, all of them, at once, the same nail; and the horses in the shafts, who had never in their lives felt anything like it, would scream and go.

Up the north road. With the cart behind them.

And he had not even been angry yet. He had only been looking at a boy's feet.

Ward's hand was moving.

He felt it, the way he felt everything about Ward, that slow even note that was a wall lit to the top, a yard off in the ditch, and the arm of it coming up out of the leaves, the gauntlet, the heavy hand on its way to his shoulder, as it had come across a cellar floor through a gale with blood running out of Ward's nose, unhurried. It would land in a moment. It would land and it would say, as it had said on the stair, Forward, and the thing would stop because Ward had stopped it.

He did not wait for it.

He did not try to hold the door shut. He had tried that, once, with all his strength, and it had gone past his teeth. Armand had told him: you cannot hold a thing like that by strength. You walk out of the room.

So he walked out of the room.

He took his eyes off Arthur's feet. That was all it was, at first; it was such a small thing that it seemed it could not possibly work. He took his eyes off the boy's stockings and the button and the thread and put them on the gate. Five bars. Grey. The top bar split along the grain from the hinge end, and somebody had mended it with a strip of iron and four nails, and one of the nails had rusted out. He let the clearness go on being clear, and did not lean on it, did not let it be about anything; it was a gate, and a nail hole, and the frost on the gate. And then, behind his eyes, he did not push at the door, and he did not hold it. He turned his back on it. He stood in his own long room with his back to the door and looked the other way, at the plain end of the room where the light came from, and was in no hurry to be anywhere, and let the wind blow on the far side of it all it liked.

The fire sank.

He heard it before he saw it: a small settling of ash. The flame went down off its hand's height into the embers and was red again, banked, low; and the man by it said something disappointed and squatted down and poked at it with a stick. The ice under Emeric's cheek was water now, and stayed water, and began, slowly, very slowly, to stiffen again. Behind him in the ditch the man who had put his hand to his head took it away.

And the door, which had been leaning, stopped leaning.

It did not slam, as it had in the cellar when the dead zone cut it off at the hinge. It settled. Like any door when the wind drops. It settled back against its frame and was a door, his, shut, the latch where it should be. He felt that with his back to it. He did not turn round to look.

He breathed out.

His jaw ached. He found that his back teeth had been together, hard, and he had not felt them go. He let them go. There was a taste of iron in his mouth and he thought his nose was bleeding, and put the back of his wrist to it in the dark, and it was not; he had bitten the inside of his cheek. That was all. That was the whole of the bill.

Ward's hand had stopped an inch above his shoulder.

He could feel it there, in the dark, the gauntlet not quite on him, the heat of the man's arm through the farmer's cloak. It stayed there. Emeric did not move. He lay in the wet leaves with his cheek on the softening ice and his eyes on a nail hole in a gate, and felt Ward look at him, the whole slow even wall of Ward's attention on him in the dark, for as long as it took the man by the fire to put his stick down and pull his blanket round him.

Then the hand went down. Back into the leaves, slowly, the way it had come up. Ward lay beside him in the ditch and looked through the hedge at the oak, and said nothing at all.



They waited.

Emeric kept his eyes on the gate.

The man with the cudgel slept. The man under the blanket woke and sat up, and the standing man lay down in his place.

About half past five Rylan came along the ditch on his elbows, like an otter, and lay down on Emeric's other side with his mouth by his ear.

"Was that you," he breathed, "earlier?"

"Yes."

Rylan was quiet a while.

"Half my lads thought it was the frost," he said. "Coming into their teeth." He considered. "I told them it was the frost."

"It wasn't."

"I know it wasn't, you great idiot. I've been on the wrong end of it in a stable." He lay there, breathing. "It stopped, though."

"It stopped."

"Didn't see Ward stop it."

Emeric said nothing. Rylan waited, and when nothing came, he reached over and knocked his knuckle very lightly twice against the back of Emeric's head, the way he knocked on Finn's when Finn had got a sum right without being shown, and then went back along the ditch on his elbows to the gate.

The sky over the north road began to go grey.



By the fire the three in brown were up, kicking the embers together and blowing on their hands, and the blankets came off the horses. Emeric could see faces on the cart now. Arthur's had a long dark mark down one side where somebody had hit him at the ford. His eyes were open. He was looking up the north road.

Along the ditch, in the leaves, Emeric felt the twenty get up onto their knees.

The gate was shut. He had looked at it all night. Ward was crouched at the hinge end of it now, by the post, with one gauntlet on the bottom bar.

The man with the ring climbed up on the box of the cart.

He sat down, and arranged a sack under himself against the cold, and took up the reins, and looked back over his shoulder at the five men standing on the bed behind him with the ropes going up from their necks. He said something to them. Emeric could not hear it. The bigger Bastow brother spat, and missed.

The man on the box took up the whip from its socket and shook out the lash.

Rylan, at the gate, said, "Now," in an ordinary voice; and Ward stood up.

The gate came off its hinges with a crack like a shot. Ward heaved it up and over and put it down on the far side of the ditch, all five bars of it, as a man puts down a hurdle, and stepped back out of the way; and the twenty came up out of the ditch past him through the gap and into the field, and they did not run.

They stepped. They came through the gap at a hard ordinary run, three paces, four, men running across a field; and then each of them, one after another, as Rylan had taught them, took the half step. A man was there, by the gate, running; and then he was twenty paces on, still running, as though the ground between had been folded up like a sheet and put away. And another half step. And another. A row of men running across a frozen field who kept on arriving where they should not have been, and in each of them, as they went, Emeric felt the glyph wake on the forearm, a small fierce flare of the cold point at the wrist, and go out, and wake again.

The two men by the oak had turned at the crack of the gate. One of them shouted. The man on the box turned on his seat and saw them coming, and saw how they were coming, and his mouth opened; and he did what any man would have done, what he had been sent there to do, and brought the whip down across both horses' backs as hard as he could, and yelled.

The horses went.

They went the way horses go when they have been standing in the cold all night and somebody lays a whip across them out of nowhere, all at once, both together, heads up and haunches down, and the cart lurched forward a foot. A foot and a half. Emeric saw the five ropes go tight. He saw all five men on the bed go back on their heels as the boards went out from under their feet. He saw Arthur's chin come up.

And the first two of the levy arrived at the horses' heads.

They did not come up the road. They were simply there, out of the last half step, one at each bridle, a farmer's son and a carter, with their whole weight on the bits and their heels dug into the frozen road, the way you stop a bolting team if you have ever stopped one, which both of them had; and the near horse reared, and the carter went up with it and came down still holding on, and the cart stopped. Rocked. Stopped. Two feet on from where it had been.

On the bed, the five were hanging.

Not dropped. The cart had not gone far enough for a drop. They had gone off the back of their heels with the boards moving under them and they were half on the bed and half off it, with their feet scrabbling at the tailboard and the ropes taking their weight, and the ropes were short, and they were choking. Emeric saw Hugh Ashby's legs kick. He saw the younger Bastow go off the tailboard altogether and swing, and turn, and kick.

And then there were men under them.

They came up the back of the cart out of the last step, four, five, eight of them, and there was a man under each pair of legs before the legs had kicked twice; one of the levy got his shoulders under Abner Bastow's knees and stood up with him, simply stood up, a grown man across his shoulders and his hands locked round the boy's shins, and the rope went slack. Another had Hugh Ashby round the thighs. Two of them had the bigger Bastow between them, lifting. Jory Cobley's feet found the boards again by themselves and somebody's arms were round his waist, holding him up on them.

And the one man of the twenty who ran instead of stepping, which Rylan had always said there would be, a lanky lad from Milford who had done it in the yard every single night, came out of the last half step going far too fast, and missed the cart altogether, and went straight across the north road at full stretch and into the ditch on the far side with a noise like a sack of turnips.

Arthur was standing.

He had gone back on his heels with the others when the cart lurched, and caught himself. Emeric did not know how. His feet had come off the boards and found the boards again, his stockinged feet on the frozen planks, his toes, the balls of his feet, and he was standing, up on his toes on the very end of the bed, with the rope dragging his chin up into the grey light and his whole body stretched up along it like a man reaching for something on a high shelf. He was holding himself there. He was not kicking. He was standing still on the ends of his toes on a cart bed, with his neck in a rope, and holding himself up by nothing but the muscle of his own legs, and waiting.

Rylan took him round the knees from behind and lifted, and Arthur came down onto his heels with a long rough sound in his throat, and the rope went slack.

The knives came out. The ropes were cut; there was no finesse about it, men sawing at hemp a hand's width above five necks, and the ends of the ropes swinging up into the oak's limb as they parted. The man on the box had been pulled off it backwards by somebody and was face down on the road with a knee in the middle of his back. The two in brown by the oak had not run. They were standing against the trunk under their board with their hands up, very still, with six rifles on them from the gate.

It had taken, from the crack of the gate, perhaps the time it takes to say a grace, not hurrying.

Emeric was on his feet. He did not know when he had stood up. He was walking across the field toward the cart, not fast, on the frozen grass, and he kept his sense where it was, close round him like a coat, and he did not look at anything but the cart.



Arthur was sitting on the tailboard when he got there, with his tied hands still behind him because nobody had got round to them, and a cut rope's end lying on his shoulder like a scarf. There was a red mark round his neck, raw, with the skin broken on one side under the jaw where the knot had been. His stockings were black at the toes and torn through. He was shaking. Not shivering; shaking, in long hard waves, all through him.

He looked up when Emeric came, and his face did a number of things at once and then decided on one.

"They took my boots," he said.

"I saw."

"They were good boots. They were the department's." His teeth were going. "Ward'll have them off my pay."

"Ward won't."

"He'll want to." Arthur looked down at his feet, at the black torn stockings, as though they belonged to somebody else. "I couldn't feel them after about midnight. I thought, if I can't feel them, I'll not know when the cart goes. So I kept them going. Up and down. On my toes. All night. So I'd know." He looked at Emeric. "Jory was doing it too. I told him to keep on."

Emeric went round behind him and got out his knife and cut the cord on his wrists. It took him three tries. His hands were not good. Arthur brought his arms round in front of him and looked at his own wrists, and then put both hands up to his neck, and touched it, very carefully, with the tips of his fingers, as though checking it was there.

"Did you feel it?" Emeric said.

"Feel what?"

"Earlier. In the night. About two. Anything."

Arthur thought about it.

"The fire went up," he said slowly. "I was watching it. It went up and I thought, they've put wood on, that's nice for them." He frowned. "And my teeth went funny. Just for a bit. I thought it was the cold." He looked up. "Was it you?"

Emeric did not answer, and Arthur looked at his face, and then, oddly, nodded, as though that settled something, and looked away up the north road.

Somebody brought a coat. Somebody else brought a pair of boots, off the man in brown who had been on the box, who was sitting up in the road now with his hands tied, in his stockings, looking at the levy as if he had never seen men before. The boots were too big. Arthur put them on anyway. Hugh Ashby was crying, without any noise, into his hands.

Ward came round the cart.

He had the board. He had pulled it off the oak, nails and all, and he was carrying it under his arm, face in. He stopped in front of the tailboard and looked down the row: Ashby, the Bastows, Cobley. Arthur. He looked at each of them for the same length of time, and then at Arthur's neck, and then at Arthur's feet in the driver's boots.

"Cobley," he said.

Jory looked up.

"You moved your feet all night."

"Yes, sir. Sorry, sir."

"Do not be sorry," said Ward. "Do it at drill." And turned away to the prisoners.



They brought them back to the stream ground in the cart, the five of them, lying in the bed of it on straw under the levy's coats, with the horses that had nearly hanged them pulling them home, and a farmer's son at each bridle the whole nine miles, because the farmer's sons would not give the bridles up.

Emeric walked behind it. Ward walked beside him. They did not speak for the first five miles.

At a ford, where the cart stopped for the horses to drink, Ward said, without looking round, "The board."

"What about it?"

"Saban will want it. To copy." Ward shifted it under his arm. "He will want Ambry to print it on the open press beside the picture of a cart with no men hanging from it, and the date, and send it north to every town that would have heard the other story by tonight." He paused. "It will do more than the battle."

"Then give it to Saban."

"I will." Ward looked at the water going round the horses' legs. "I wished you to know that I would."

That was all he said about the night. Emeric waited, at the ford and after, for the rest of it, for Ward to say something about the ditch, the fire, the hand an inch off his shoulder; and it did not come, and it did not come, and somewhere on the long last mile into camp with the sun up and the frost going off the fields in a white smoke, Emeric understood that it was not going to, and that this was the most Ward had ever said to him.



He went home to Kaldo on the night of the thirtieth.

He went south in the dark, in the hull's cold belly with a sack of letters at his feet and Ned Tallis at the levers saying nothing, which was Ned's gift; and came down on the slip behind the works at a quarter to one, and walked up through the sleeping town to the north well.

There was a light in Sela's kitchen. There always was. He had never once in eight years passed it at night and found it dark.

He knocked, and she opened it, and looked at him on her step at one in the morning in the coat he had worn to the oak, with the ditch still on the knees of it; and he watched her face go, all at once, the colour leaving it from the top down like water leaving a glass.

"He's alive," Emeric said. "He's well. He's at the stream ground. He's well."

Sela stood in the door and held onto the frame of it.

Then she said, "Come in, then, you'll let the warm out," and stood aside, and went to the hearth and put the kettle over, and stood with her back to him while it came up, for rather longer than a kettle takes.

He told her all of it. The ford. The five. The farms. The girl with the eggs. The oak, and the ropes, and five men standing on a cart bed in the cold all night. He told her about Arthur on his toes. He did not tell her about the fire standing up, or the door. That was not hers. Everything else was.

She listened without a sound. Only once, when he came to the boots, her mouth moved.

When he had finished she sat for a while, with her hands folded on the table, looking at the row of slates.

"On his toes," she said.

"All night."

"So he'd know when it went." She nodded slowly. "He'd think of that. He'd think of a thing like that. His father was the same. Thinking, when anybody else would be screaming." She looked at her hands. "His brother wasn't."

"Nobody came," said Sela. "Last time." She was looking at the slates. "Nobody came and sat at my table at one in the morning and told me how it was. They sent a man from the castle with a paper in the spring, a year after. He read it at the door. He couldn't read it very well." She was quiet. "I don't know where his brother lies. I don't know if he was cold. I don't know if somebody got there in time or not in time." She unfolded her hands and put them flat on the table. "You came."

"I should have kept him out of it," Emeric said.

It came out of him before he had decided to say it, and once it was out he found that it was what he had flown south to say.

"Stop that," said Sela.

He stopped.

"You didn't let him," she said. "That's the first thing. He's nobody's to let. He went for the levy the day it opened, and he didn't ask me, and he didn't ask you, and when I said what I said about it, which I'll not tell you, he said he'd heard me, and went anyway. And when Rylan's lot came round about the arm, he never told me that at all. I had it from Jory Cobley's mother at the pump. I went up there to box his ears and he showed me it. All bound up. Sore." She was quiet. "He was so pleased with it. Trying not to show me."

"I saw that," said Emeric. "In the yard."

"Yes. Well." Sela put both her hands round her cup, which she had not drunk from. "I've had him nineteen years. I know what he looks like when he's decided a thing." She looked into the cup. "He stood on a cart all night with a rope round his neck and kept his feet going so he'd know. He kept Jory going. He kept the Bastow boy from falling." She said it flatly, the way she chalked the hours on the wall. "That's not a boy. I'd like it to be. But I'd be a fool, and I'm not one." She looked up. "He's a man. He's his own. You'll not keep him out of anything, and neither will I, and it's past time I stopped trying."

The kettle had boiled while she was talking and nobody had taken it off. It hissed on the hook. Neither of them moved for it.

"So I'll ask you one thing," said Sela. "One. And then you'll drink your tea and go and sleep somewhere, because you look like a dog that's been dragged."

"Ask."

"Take care of him." She did not look away. "Not keep him. Take care of him. The way you'd take care of your own, if you had one. That's all. When it comes to it, and it will, because he's in Ward's department and you're who you are, and there's more of this coming, I'm not daft. When it comes to it, you look after him like he was yours." She held his eyes. "That's what I'm asking."

"Yes," he said.

Sela looked at him a moment longer, as she had in the summer when he had said yes about Garrin; as if he had passed something. Then she got up and took the kettle off, and made the tea, and put it in front of him, and sat back down and took up the next slate, and wet her rag, and began to wipe.

"His boots," she said after a while, without looking up. "You said they had his boots."

"They did."

"Did you get them back?"

"No," said Emeric. "He's wearing the driver's."

Sela wiped the slate.

"Good," she said.



There was one candle lit in the counting room when he came up the keep stair at three. Ferra was at her end of the long table with the camp returns open in front of her and a blanket round her shoulders. She did not look up when he came in. She finished the line she was writing, and blotted it, and then looked up.

"Ned Tallis said you'd come south in the hull and gone straight to the north well," she said. "He said you never spoke the whole way. Ned thought that a bad sign." She put the pen in the stand. "So I sat up."

"You didn't have to."

"I'll not argue that at three in the morning." She looked at him. At the knees of his coat. "You've been in a ditch."

"Most of the night."

"Sit down."

He sat down on the bench beside her, and she unwound the blanket from her shoulders and put half of it round his, without comment, and turned back to the returns. He sat under it. It was warm from her. He could feel, through it, the line of her shoulder against his, and she did not move it away.

After a while she turned a page of the camp book toward him and put her finger on a line. It was the levy roll for the twenty-ninth, in Osric's new hand, large and careful and rather crooked. Five names, and against each one, in Osric's capitals, TAKEN AT CRESSING FORD.

Under the five names, on the line below, in her own small exact hand, she had written: 30 Nov. Correction to the above. Returned, all five. F.

She had not struck anything through. She never did.

"I wrote that at one," she said, "on Ned's word, before I had yours." She looked at it. "I'd have had to write another under it if Ned was wrong."

"Ned wasn't wrong."

"No." She took her finger off the page. "I'm glad I needn't."

They sat. The candle burned. Somewhere down in the keep a door shut, and the cold ticked in the window lead. After a while, without looking at him, without any change in her voice at all, Ferra reached under the blanket and found his hand, which was still not good, and held it on her knee under the table, and went on reading the camp returns with her other hand, turning the pages one at a time, until the candle was a finger shorter and he had stopped shaking, and then for some while after.
