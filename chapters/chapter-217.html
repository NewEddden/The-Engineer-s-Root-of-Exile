Chapter 217 - The Banner

The speech came south in somebody's handwriting, which was the point of it.

Saban laid it on the long-room table in the last week of the summer, a single sheet folded twice, written close on both sides in a clerk's hand so neat and so even that for a moment Emeric took it for print. It was not print. It was ink laid down by a pen by a man who had been doing it all his life, every line level, every letter the same height as the one before it, with the capitals drawn in a second ink, a dull red, the way the old books did them. At the foot, in the same red, a small drawing: a hand, open, with a pen across the palm.

"There are a thousand of those," Saban said. "More, by now. I had this one off a bookseller in Wayford who says he was given twenty to put in his window, free, by a man who wouldn't give his name and had ink on both hands. He didn't put them in his window. He sold them." Saban sat down. "A Copper each. He says he could have got two."

"Who wrote it?"

"Aldane said it. At the harvest fair at Corrow, the week after your sitting, from a cart, in the square, to about three thousand people. Then again at two more fairs in the north. Then somebody wrote it down, and then somebody copied it a thousand times, by hand, in a week." Saban tapped the drawing at the foot. "That's their mark. The Inkhands. The copyists of the northern scriptoria. They call themselves that for the stain." He held up his own fingers, which had Kaldo ink on the first two, as everybody's in the long room did by the end of a day. "They've got a banner now. The hand and the pen, in red, on white, the size of a sail. It went behind his cart at Corrow and at the other two, and there were a few hundred of them under it, and the crowd cheered them." He paused. "I'm told the crowd cheered him too. Aldane. At a fair. People who've never cheered a duke in their lives, and whose grandfathers never cheered one either."

Emeric unfolded the sheet and read it.

It was a good speech. He had expected it to be a bad one, the way the file had been a bad story told well, and it was not. It was short and plain and it did not mention demons, or flying things, or the stream ground, or five men at an oak. It did not mention him by name at all until the last lines, and then only once.

It was about hands.

There had been, Aldane said, in the towns along the northern rivers, for as long as there had been books in Randillia, men who made them. Not by any trick. By hand, one letter at a time, in the scriptoria of the abbey towns and in the shops of the booksellers' streets, the psalters and the primers and the rolls of the law and the letters of the lords, every line of it written by a man who had given seven years of his youth to learning to write it straight. Their fathers had done it, and their grandfathers. It was not rich work. It was honest, and it was theirs, and it fed a street.

Now there was a machine in the south that did it without them.

A psalter that had cost a copyist's month cost a morning. A primer cost less than the pen to write it with. The booksellers of the northern towns bought their stock from the south now, in bales, off the carts, printed on paper made from straw by a machine, and the scriptoria stood with their desks empty and their windows dark, and the men who had given seven years to their hands sat at home with nothing in them. The street was not fed.

And the man whose machine it was had been raised to duke for it, in the king's own hall, the week before. Not for a victory. Not for blood. There was no blood in him worth the raising, on either side, and every man in the square knew whose son he was, and whose he wasn't. He had been raised for breaking the crafts of honest men with engines, and for hanging those of his betters who said so.

I do not ask you to hate him, the last lines ran, in the copyist's level hand. I ask you to look at your own hands, and ask what they will be for, when he is done.

That was all. Emeric read it twice. Then he passed it to Ferra, who read it once, and then down the table, and it went round, and nobody said anything until it came back to him.

"Well," said Bram, at last. "He's not wrong about the psalters."

He was not wrong about the psalters. That was the thing Emeric made himself say first, at the table, before anybody else could say something clever, because it was true and the table would take its lead from whatever he said first.

"He's right," he said. "About the work. Ferra."

Ferra did not need to open the book. "The printing office sold eleven thousand primers into the north last year, through the Wayford booksellers and the carriers. Four thousand psalters. The price of a psalter in the north was two Silver before we started. It's a Copper and a half now, on the bookseller's own table, and he makes more on it than he did at two Silver, because he sells twenty for every one." She turned a page she did not need. "I don't know how many copyists there were. Nobody counted them. Nobody counts copyists."

"Somebody will now," said Saban.

"The abbey towns," said Armand, from the far end. He had been quiet all through the reading. "Corrow, Ashby, the two Wendles. That's where they were. I bought my first books from a Wendle shop when I was a boy. There were twenty men at the desks in that shop." He turned his cup on the table. "Those are Aldane's towns. All four. His family has held them for longer than there's been a crown, and the scriptoria paid his tithes and his tolls for all of it."

Emeric had known that. He had known it in the way he knew most of the map north of Wayford, as names on Saban's sheets, and had not put it together until Armand said it aloud in the long room. The press had not emptied the scriptoria of the kingdom. It had emptied the scriptoria of the kingdom, and nearly all the scriptoria of the kingdom were in four towns on the northern rivers, and all four towns were Aldane's. The duke on the cart at Corrow was not speaking for a cause he had found. He was speaking for his own streets, which were dark, and his own tolls, which were down, and his own people, who were hungry, and he was right about every one of them.

"So what do we do?" Ferra said.

Orrin answered before Emeric could. He had come up from the school for the evening, as he did once a week, and sat at the table's foot with his hands folded, looking at the sheet.

"Hire them," he said. "Some of them. I'll have three hundred children when the island school opens, and I've got eleven people who can teach a straight hand. A man who's written a psalter can teach a child to write. A man who's written the law rolls can teach a clerk." He looked round the table. "I'd take twenty tomorrow."

"Not tomorrow," said Emeric.

Orrin looked at him.

"If we send a man north to hire copyists the month after that speech," Emeric said, "we've proved the speech. The duke who broke the crafts of honest men comes round with a purse and buys the men whose crafts he broke, and takes them south, and empties the towns a second time. He'd have it on the banner by the next fair. He'd be right about that too." He put his hand flat on the sheet. "I want them. You should have them. Not this year."

"Then when?"

"When they come on their own. Some will. A man with a trade and no work walks to where the work is. Put a notice in the Wayford booksellers' windows that the island school wants teachers of a hand, at a wage, and say nothing else. No name. No duke. A school wants teachers." He looked at Ferra. "And price it honestly. Not low, so it looks like we're buying cheap. Not high, so it looks like a bribe."

"A copyist's wage," said Ferra, writing. "What he'd have had at a Wendle desk. I'll find out what that was."

"It'll still look like a bribe," said Saban.

"Everything will look like something," Emeric said. "That one will at least be true."

He sat up with the sheet after they had gone.

It was the last line that he kept going back to, and not the one about the hands. It was the line before it. There was no blood in him worth the raising, on either side, and every man in the square knew whose son he was, and whose he wasn't.

On either side. He turned that over under the lamp for a long time.

His father's side was the crown. There was nothing to say about that, and Aldane had not tried to say it; not even the old blood said aloud, in a square, that the king's line was not blood worth the raising. So it was the other side. His mother's. The third queen, who had been raised at the castle by the old king without anybody ever saying where she had come from, and had married his father because his father had wanted to, and had held no house and brought no land and had been, to the old blood, nobody's daughter. Everybody in the square knew whose son he was. Everybody in the square knew she had been nobody's.

Aldane had put her into the speech. He had not named her. He had not needed to. And a speech that was about hands, and was right about hands, had turned in its second-to-last line to say something about a dead woman's blood that had nothing to do with hands at all.

The banner explained contempt. Emeric could see that it did. A duke of a line older than the crown, whose towns were emptying, looking at a third son of a foundling queen raised to his own rank for the machine that emptied them: of course he despised him. Emeric would have despised him too, in Aldane's chair. It was a whole and reasonable contempt, and it was the right size for a man on a cart at a fair.

It was not the right size for the rest of it.

He took a sheet of the notebook paper and wrote it down, the way he wrote down anything that did not add up, in a list, with nothing in it but what he had seen.

The gift. A set of carved wooden soldiers, at the banquet before he went south, the kind you gave a boy half his age. He had enjoyed it at the time. He had thought it was the honest insult of a man who could not be bothered with a decent lie, and he had respected it. He looked at it now, written down, and thought that a man who could not be bothered did not choose a gift. He did not come to a farewell at all.

Grayrock. The day the attainder was read and Grayrock went to Kaldo. Aldane in the chancery anteroom, across the room, by the window, with his steward. Emeric had looked up when the clerk read the name and seen him go still. Not angry. Not surprised. Still, the way a man goes still when a thing he has been carrying in his pocket for a long time turns out to have a hole in it. He had not known then what it meant. He knew now whose money had paid for a survey of that hill eleven years before, and he had been saving that stillness for a long time, in the place where he kept the things he could not use.

The file. Five articles and no Grayrock. The one thing that would have made every lord on the benches sit up, left out, because putting it in would have meant saying how the duke knew what was under that hill.

The field. He wrote the number and looked at it. Forty-one knights. And Harlow, nineteen, the duke's cousin's boy, knighted the spring before, who had died in the first charge below the stream with the rest. That was what Aldane had spent. Forty-one of his own blood and his friends' blood, and a boy he had known since the boy was born, on a quarrel over a toll bridge and a Staple and a few hanged lords who had not been his closest friends. Emeric had done the arithmetic at the time, from the other side of the field, and thought it was the arithmetic of a proud man who had miscounted. He did it again now and it still did not come out. You did not spend forty-one knights and a boy on a toll bridge. You did not spend them on a Staple. You spent them on something you could not name, and hoped they would buy it back.

The banner. A speech about hands, that turned in its second-to-last line to a dead woman's blood.

And one more line, which he wrote and then sat and looked at for a while before he let it stand.

Ward.

Ward, who had said the duke's name at the table once, the day the Staple's books came in, the way a man says the name of someone he has known a long time and not liked. Ward, who had not slept on the road north. Ward, who had stood two paces behind a stool in the king's hall while the duke of Aldane looked at him for two breaths and then looked at the floor. Ward, who had waited before he agreed, in the carriage, that it was contempt for a guard.

Emeric did not know what any of it meant. He noticed that he wanted to open the sense, the way he wanted to reach for a tool, and that it would have told him nothing: there was nothing in the medium in a man's grudge, and nothing in a list. This was the other thing, the thing he had always been worse at. It was a set of true facts about a person, and a gap between them where a reason ought to be, and nothing to fill the gap with but a guess.

He did not guess. He had learned at a yard with forty men in it, a long time ago, what a guess about a man was worth, and it had cost another man his name.

He folded the list and put it in the notebook, in the second column, where he kept the things that were only for him, and put the lamp out. The sheet with the red hand at its foot he left on the table, where Ferra would find it in the morning and file it, because it was a thing somebody had said and it was true in its way, and she would want it in the box.

He would ask Ward. Not tonight, and not at a table. What had happened between the duke of Aldane and his mother. It was a question with her in it, and he had found, over the years, that those were the ones he left longest, and that Ward, who knew the answers to most of them, had never once offered one he was not asked for.
