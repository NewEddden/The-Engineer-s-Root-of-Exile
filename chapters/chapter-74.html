Chapter 74 - One Room

Saban had the room over the inn's archway, because it was the only room in Kaldo where a man could hear his own carts in the yard without getting out of bed.

He was not in bed when Emeric knocked. He was sitting up at the little table under the window in his shirt, with his coat over the back of the one chair and his hat on the floor beside it, a jug at his elbow and his account book open in front of him at a page that was mostly figures and partly gravy. He looked up, saw who it was, and did not get up either. He pointed his pen at the end of the bed.

Emeric sat on it. It sagged further than he expected.

"Ferra says you know where the post goes."

"Does she." Saban wrote a figure, looked at it, and wrote another one under it that was smaller. "Then she'll have told you I charge."

"She said you'd charge me."

"Did she say what?"

"No."

Saban put the pen down and gave the matter his whole attention, the way he gave it to a bale of hides with a doubtful smell. "The first twenty casks off next year's still," he said, "at the price you're asking this year."

"Done."

Saban looked at him with real disappointment. He had plainly been looking forward to the next quarter of an hour. He poured a finger of something brown from the jug into a second cup that had not been clean for some days, and pushed it across the table, and Emeric drank it to be polite and found it was Kaldo's own spirit, from the first run, and that Saban had been charging somebody somewhere a good deal more for it than he had paid.

"You're a terrible man to sell to," Saban said. "Fine. Give me something to draw on."

There was nothing in the room to draw on that was not already written on. Saban went through the loose papers at the back of the account book, discarding them one at a time, and settled on an invoice: two casks of lamp oil to a chandler in Grayrock, delivered in the spring, unpaid. He turned it over and smoothed it flat with the side of his hand.

"Here's you." A dot, at the bottom of the sheet. "Here's your north road." A line from the dot, going up and a little to the left, not straight, because Saban drew roads the way they went and not the way they ought to. "And here's Wayford." A ring, where the line ran into a much heavier one that crossed the whole sheet from bottom to top. "That's the south road. The crown calls it the south road because the crown lives at the top of it. Coast to the capital, the whole length of the kingdom. Relay houses every stage, fresh horses, a rider on every leg. That's the crown's post. Everything else is people's cousins."

He drew a second line from the bottom right corner of the sheet, slanting in, and brought it into the heavy line a little below the ring.

"The coast road. Every crown letter out of the coast towns comes up that and joins the south road under Wayford."

"The salt boatman handed my second copy in at the coast," Emeric said. "To the crown's post. Because it was addressed to the crown."

"Because it said the crown on it, and he's an honest man." Saban said it as a fault. "Never opened a bag that wasn't his in twenty years. That's why he's poor." He put the nib of the pen down hard inside the ring, and left it there, and a little blot spread out from it. "And at Wayford there's the crossing."

Emeric waited.

"The river, and the ferry, and on the town side of the ferry approach there's a stone house with the crown's arms over the door that's older than anything else in Wayford. Every crown bag from below Wayford comes in at the front of it. The coast's, yours, the south farms'. They're opened in the back room. Sorted. Made up again into bags for the capital, and sent on with a fresh rider and a fresh horse, so the crown doesn't have to pay forty men to ride the whole length of the kingdom." He lifted the pen. "My man at the crossing inn drinks with the sorters. Back room, two tables, one window that doesn't open, a stove. They complain about the stove. It smokes."

Emeric looked at the invoice.

He had not needed it drawn, once Saban started talking. He had seen it the way he saw a fault in a drawing, all at once and then everywhere. The first copy up the north road, into the ring. The second down the river, round by the coast, up the coast road, into the ring from below. The third up the north road a week behind the first, into the ring. Three lines on the back of an unpaid bill for lamp oil, and every one of them ran through the same blot.

"Three copies," he said. "Two roads."

"One room," said Saban, with the satisfaction of a man watching a buyer finally understand what he had paid for.



Emeric turned the invoice over and looked at the lamp oil, as if it might have changed.

"In August," he said, "at Ferra's table, you said a letter from Kaldo to anybody had a way of not arriving."

"Did I."

"You did. You carried my letter to the House yourself because of it."

"Then I said it." Saban drank. "I meant the crown. Nobody on the coast writes to the crown, what would they say to it. When the coast says anybody it means the crown, the way a carter says the road is bad and means one mile of it." He shrugged. "Your letters to the coast arrive. Mine do. Your boatman's do. The coast says anybody because the coast never tried."

"Where did the coast hear it?"

"Where everyone hears everything. At the crossing inn." He looked into his cup. "It's a joke there. Kaldo's letters. The sorters say it the way they say a horse is from Milford when it won't go." He considered. "They say it about one or two other places. Not many."

"So what is it?"

"A hole in the road." Saban said it with the flat confidence of a man who had lost goods on most of the roads in the kingdom and found the hole every time. "There's always one. A clerk who's paid to lose a bag and puts it in that stove they complain about. Or a sorter who goes through the bags looking for coin, and when there isn't any he drops the paper in the river on his way home, because a sealed letter looks like money to a man who can't read and like evidence to a man who can." He topped up his own cup and, after a look at Emeric's, decided against topping up his. "Your war's in the river at Wayford, highness. Or it's ash. I'm sorry for it. It's a stupid way to lose a thing like that."

Emeric did not answer.

He folded the invoice in half.

"Can I keep this?"

"It's owed."

"I'll give it back."

"Keep it." Saban went back to his account book. "That chandler's owed me for that oil since the spring. He'll pay it a great deal faster if he hears a prince has his bill."



The forge was hot and the yard outside it was mud, the brown, ankle-deep, sucking mud of the first week of November after a wet October, and Emeric was halfway across it before he noticed he had walked there without deciding to.

Bram was at the anvil with his back to the door, shaping a hinge out of one of the tithe-house hinges, which was the kind of thing that pleased him more than anything new. He did not turn round. Ferra was at her table in the corner, with the lamp already lit against the grey afternoon and the register still open at June beside her, though Hale had sent twice for it.

Emeric had the invoice out of his coat before he reached the table. He had the first sentence ready. He was going to say three copies, two roads, and let her say the last part, because she would want to.

The slip jar was at her elbow, where it always was. Under it, held flat by its weight, was a sheet of paper with a dot at the bottom and a line from the dot going up and a little to the left, not straight. A ring. A heavy line from bottom to top. A second line slanting in from the corner and joining under the ring. A blot inside the ring.

It was on the back of a different invoice. He could see the edge of the writing through the paper: something about salt fish.

He stood there with Saban's lamp oil in his hand.

She had gone yesterday. Of course she had. She had said Saban will know, and then sometime between the forge and her bed she had put her coat on and gone to the inn and paid whatever Saban had decided she would pay, which would have been a good deal less than twenty casks, and come back and put the map under the jar. And this morning she had sat here and done the column and let him go to the inn and buy the same map, at his own price, and walk all the way back through the mud with it held out in front of him like a boy with a frog.

He looked at her. She was already looking at him, and had been, he thought, since he came in at the door. She did not say anything. She watched him work it out, all of it, down to the frog. Then she looked down at her page and went on with the column, and the nib moved and came back, moved and came back.

He put Saban's invoice down on the table next to the other one. They were not quite the same. On his, the coast road joined a little lower.

"His hand shakes after dinner," Ferra said, without looking up. "Yours is right."

Behind them Bram swore at the hinge, kindly.

Emeric went out into the yard again and found that he was in no hurry at all to get out of the mud, and that the mud, on the whole, was not so bad.



Ferra had two sheets for Vantic on the Thursday.

The first was a question, in the form they had settled in September: one line, numbered, with a space under it the width of a line. Whether the cost of sending every report to the crown twice, by two roads, was a running cost of the barony, since the barony had reported to the crown on the day of signing and before it, or a new cost, since it had never before reported twice.

Vantic read it at the trestle in the back room of the exchange. She read it once, the way she read everything, and then she sat with her pen in her hand and did not write anything under it, which Emeric had not seen her do before.

"I won't rule on this," she said.

Ferra waited. So did he.

"The practice didn't exist on the day of signing. It isn't plainly a running cost and it isn't plainly a new work. It's a reasonable question, and either answer would be a reasonable answer, and that's what I don't make." She laid the sheet in her case, flat, not folded. "I'll send it to the House."

"How long?"

"Weeks."

She drew a fresh page toward her and wrote the cover there at the trestle, while they watched, in her clear upright hand, and Emeric read it upside down as he had read her book in the tower. The barony of Kaldo asks the following. The House's factor makes no recommendation. That was all of it, apart from the date. It was so perfectly neutral that it was very nearly an argument for something, and he could not have said for what.

Ferra did not argue. She took the answer the way she took a bad price at the market, which was without any expression at all, and moved her hand to the second sheet, and turned it round, and slid it across the trestle.

It was not a question.

It was written in the House's form, the way Vantic's own rulings came down the hill: the House's line across the top, the date, and under it, in Ferra's small plain hand, two lines.

The fallen span of the aqueduct from the Mere, carried since the spring on a timber trough, is part of a work of the barony that stood on the day of signing. Its making good in stone is repair, and falls outside the limit.

Under that, the clause. Under the clause, the figure: what the stone would cost, what the masons would cost, what the trough had cost to keep up since the spring, and the date the trough would rot through if nobody touched it, which was sooner than Emeric had thought. Ferra had worked it out on the back of something and copied it fair.

Vantic read it. She read it once.

Then she picked up her pen, and initialed it at the foot, and blotted it, and put it in her case beside the other sheet, and did up the strap. From the moment the sheet touched the trestle to the strap going through the buckle, Emeric would have said it was rather less than a minute, and none of it had been spent arguing.

Nobody said anything about it.

Ferra drew the next page of the week toward her. Vantic opened her own book and wrote up the hour. Emeric stood at the window with his back to both of them and looked out at the market, where a woman was trying to sell a goose to a man who did not want a goose, and he thought about what he had just watched.

A ruling Vantic made was a ruling Vantic could be faulted for. Somewhere up the coast there was a room full of partners who read her book, and every line she wrote in it was a line they could hold up and ask about. A ruling Ferra wrote and Vantic initialed was different. It was Kaldo's judgment, with Kaldo's figures under it, sitting in the House's file with the House's factor's mark on the foot to say she had seen it and found nothing wrong. If it was wrong, it was Kaldo's wrong.

Ferra was going to bring her decisions, not questions, from now on. Every one she could. She had worked out what Vantic needed, before Vantic had said a word about needing anything, and she was giving it to her, and Vantic had taken it without a word either.

Behind him he heard Ferra's pen, and then Vantic's, and then neither, and then Ferra's again.

The woman sold the goose.



The wall over the east bridge was the coldest place in Kaldo after dark, and he went up to it anyway, because it was also the quietest.

All eight lamps were lit. The river was high and loud under the bridge, brown with the October rain still coming down out of the hills, and the dynamo house was turning so hard that the lamps burned a shade too white and hummed. He could see the whole run of them from where he stood, round the curve of the wall to the South Gate, each one throwing its circle on the wall walk and on the water below, and in each circle, every so often, a man of the watch walking through with his hands in his armpits.

He had the invoice in his coat. He did not take it out. He did not need to.

Three copies, two roads, one room. A back room with two tables and a window that did not open and a stove that smoked. Somebody in that room had put his hand on three letters about a war, a week apart, and none of them had gone any further. Saban said a clerk paid to lose a bag, or a thief looking for coin. That was what a sensible man would think, and Saban was a sensible man.

Footsteps on the stair, unhurried. He knew them before Ward came up into the lamplight.

Ward stopped beside him at the parapet and looked at the river, because that was what was in front of him. He was in his cloak, with the hood down. He had been round the whole wall once already, by the mud on his boots, and would go round it again before he slept.

"Saban says the crossing," Ward said.

It was not a question, and Emeric did not ask how he knew. Ward knew where Emeric had been most days before Emeric did.

"The crossing at Wayford. The sorting room." Emeric watched the water go under the bridge. "Everything from the south goes through it. All three copies went through it. None of them came out."

Ward was quiet for long enough that a man of the watch came round the curve of the wall, saw who was standing at the parapet, and went back the way he had come.

"You will want to tell someone," Ward said.

"Who?"

Ward did not answer. He turned his head and looked past the lamps, past the last of the lit wall and the dark fields beyond it, up the line of the north road where it went away into the night toward Wayford and everything that lay past Wayford, and he went on looking at it, and said nothing at all.
