Chapter 74 - The Freed Hand

The covenant did not lift. It relaxed, a clause at a time, over most of a year, and every clause that relaxed was one Vantic had argued for in writing to people who were waiting for her to be wrong.

He saw the clauses. They came to him one by one across the year, each on a single sheet in her small exact hand, each with the house's seal at the foot of it, and each said less than the last. In the late spring the house agreed that the extension of an existing road was repair and not new plant, provided the road already ran where it was being extended to. In midsummer it agreed that the removal of an existing service from one building to another was maintenance and not establishment, provided the service had been operating before the loan. At the end of the summer it raised the threshold below which a work counted as upkeep, and raised it a good deal, and did not say why.

He did not see the arguments. He never saw them, then or afterward. Each of those sheets had been bought with a letter from Vantic that he never read, to men in a house he had never visited, and each letter had been read at the far end by somebody who would have been very glad if the argument in it had turned out to be a mistake. Ferra saw the letters, because copies of everything went into the Kaldo book. She read every one. She said nothing to either of them.

He spent each clause the week it came.

The first went on the Grayrock road. It had been mended every season since the war and it ran out a mile short of the hill, at a ford where the ore carts had to be unloaded and carried over by hand. Under the new clause it was not a new road. It was the same road, going on. Bram had the men on it inside ten days, and by harvest the carts went straight up to the tally house door, and Bryda wrote to say so in a letter that contained the number of carts per day before and the number after, and nothing else, and was the warmest letter he had ever had from her.

The second went on Imani. Her ward had been in the church for years, in the side aisle and the room behind it, with Doran's leave and Doran's patience and the bell going over her patients' heads four times a day. Under the new clause it was not a new ward. It was the same ward, moved. There was a grain factor's store on the long street that had stood empty since the factor went bankrupt in the plague years, stone-built and dry, with a door that shut, and in midsummer Imani's fourteen beds went into it, and her register, and her basins, and the ward went on exactly as before except that it no longer had a bell over it. On the paperwork it was called a ward. It was not called a hospital, because a hospital would have been new, and that was the whole of the difference, and Imani knew it, and said so, and moved in anyway.

The third he spent on the north well.

It was small. It was the smallest thing on any list he had, and it came in under the new threshold with room to spare, and nobody but Ferra understood why it was the first thing he chose. The north well was the oldest well on the island and the deepest, and water came up out of it on a rope, a bucket at a time, the way it had come up for three hundred years, and the woman who ran it and ran the shop beside it had been hauling that rope, and making her neighbours haul it, for as long as he had been in Kaldo. Bram put a pump on it. A plain iron pump off the foundry's own patterns, with a long handle a child could work, and a trough, and a drain to carry off the spill so the lane did not turn to mud. It took four days to fit.

Sela watched them fit it from the door of her shop, with her arms folded, and did not say a word to anybody.

He did not tell her it was for her. He did not tell anybody. The week after it was fitted he went into her shop for a cake of soap, which he did perhaps twice a year, and she served him, and charged him full price, the same as she always had, in front of three other customers, and gave him his change, and turned away to the next one.

He came out of the shop and stood for a while in the lane and watched a girl of about seven work the pump handle with both hands, and the water come.

The revenues came current at the end of the summer, and Ferra brought the sheet that said so without the least ceremony.

She laid it on the table in the long room on an ordinary morning, among the ordinary papers, and went to stand at the window. It had four columns on it, one for each barony, and below them a total, and below the total a line for the Ducatis principal and its service, and below that the balance.

Four baronies. A foundry running full on cheap high-grade ore that came down a road with no ford in it. A manufacturing margin, which Kaldo had never had in its life, instead of a harvest margin, which was all it had ever had. The tolls at the junction at Wayford, which belonged to him now, all two rates of them, and came in every quarter to the coin because Pennock was incapable of letting them do otherwise. Grain out of a farm he had rebuilt out of ash, and sold up the river and down it. Soap. Iron. The balance ran the right way. It had run the right way for two quarters, and it would run the right way for the next four if nothing burned, and the service on the loan was covered, and the arrears were gone.

He read it through once. Then he asked her to read it to him, aloud, twice, and she did, both times at exactly the same pace, and did not ask why.

Vantic struck the line herself, in the autumn, in the ledger, in front of both of them.

It was done in her own room. The ledger was the Ducatis book for Kaldo, the one she had brought up the road in the strongbox five years before, and she opened it on the table at the page where the covenant was entered, and read the clause aloud, once, as the formality required. Then she took up a ruler and a pen, and laid the ruler across the clause, and drew a single line through it, from margin to margin, and wrote the date beside the line and her initial beside the date, and sanded it.

That was all. It was the most important administrative act in the nine years he had been in the south, and it took less time than it took to sharpen the pen, and nobody said anything while it was being done.

"The restriction is lifted," Vantic said, and closed the book.

He had rehearsed nothing to say. He found that he did not need to. The debt was not cleared. The principal ran for years yet, and would be serviced every quarter, and she would go on sitting at his table and reading every book he kept, all of them, down to his own purse, for as long as she and her house cared to, because he had made it so and could not unmake it. Nothing about any of that had changed. She was not leaving. He was not free. He was only unfrozen, which was a different thing, and an enormous one.

"Thank you," he said.

"It isn't a kindness, Your Highness. The balance came current." She set the ruler down square to the book. "My house has accepted that a principal serviced in full and on the day is not outstanding, in the sense the covenant means."

That was not what the clause had meant when he signed it. It was what the clause meant now. He did not see, then or ever, what it had cost her to make the house read it that way, a clause at a time, over a year. Ferra, standing by the door, saw it perfectly, and said nothing.

He took the notebook out that night.

It was the longest document he owned. He had started it three years before, on the night he signed the covenant, as a place to put everything he was no longer allowed to do: the clause numbers down the margin, and against every clause, in the second column, what each frozen thing would need on the day the freeze lifted. He had added to it every week since. It had outgrown three bindings.

The port. Fletcher's yard, and Fletcher himself, who had said he would think about it five years ago on a beach at the lower water, and had been building small hulls ever since, most of them under Abloh's canvas, and was sixty now. Abloh. The three streets of Fletcher's village, which in nine years of knowing precisely where Fletcher was, Emeric had never once walked.

The glassworks, with the condition he had written for himself in the back of Ferra's ledger and was now obliged to meet. A furnace that could hold a heat and let it fall, on charcoal and a good stack, with no stone in it.

Then four lines he had written over the last year without explaining them to anybody, each of them one line long, each of them the beginning of something he could see the whole shape of and could not have said where he had seen it. A lathe. A paper mill. A press. A copper test.

And the last line. The shaft, with the sketch from the hillside beside it: two mouths, a fire, and the dead air walking out on its own.

He sat with that one longest.

There was a sealed working in the Grayrock hills, full of air a man could not breathe for two breaths, and under it a body of stone that priced in a unit nobody below a duke had ever held. And as of that morning, as of a ruler and a line through a ledger, he was lawfully permitted to go and open it.

He did not feel triumphant. He had expected to, and he looked for it, and it was not there. What was there instead was a name. The man who had bought the survey of that hill was a duke who had given a disgraced prince a box of carved wooden soldiers at a farewell banquet, and had seemed to find it very funny. Emeric had known the name since the spring. He had not been able to do one thing with it in all that time, because a knife held by both ends cannot be pulled by either. Now one of his two hands was free. He did not yet know what that would do to the other end of the knife, or to the man holding it, and he found that he did not want to guess.

He closed the notebook.

He did not go to bed. He went out, as he did when he could not think, to look at something ordinary.

He walked down through the keep yard and out into the town in the dark, and it was an ordinary autumn night in Kaldo, and everything in it was somebody's.

Bram's forge was dark, because Bram did not work his own forge any more; he had four apprentices and a foundry across the water and he was older than Emeric liked to notice. There was a light in the grain factor's store on the long street, where Imani sat up with somebody, in a building with a door that shut and no bell over it. There was a light in the room at the end of the passage in the keep behind him, where Ferra sat with three clerks' work spread in front of her and a column that ran the right way. There was no light in the room off the foundry office, where Armand kept his bench and two years of baselines and an argument about the constant that he had still not conceded, and would not. There was a light at the etching floor, where Vesk sat late with his four students gone home and a notebook that nobody had ever read. The church was dark. Doran was old now and went to bed early. Rylan's window was dark. And Ward was behind him, of course, twenty paces back, where he always was, having said a word in a room in the spring and never once taken it back.

Sela's shop was shuttered. The new pump stood in the lane beside the well, with its handle down, and the trough full and still under the stars.

And at the end of the lane a light was still burning in Hollis's cottage. Hollis, whose arm had never come back. Whose children had gone to the school, and could read. Hollis, who had stood up at the first muster and challenged a prince to his face over the children, and had not been wrong to. Emeric had never told anyone this and never would, but for eight years, whenever he wanted to know whether the barony was actually better or only looked better, he had asked himself how Hollis was doing. It was the only measure he trusted.

The light in the cottage went out while he watched. He stood in the lane a while longer.

He had arrived here nine years ago with a dead man's memories, a stone in his pocket that he did not understand, and no idea at all what happened, in this kingdom, to a person whose family could not pay to bury them. He had found out. He had found out a great many things. He had built a great many things on what he found out, and some of them had held, and tonight a line had been drawn through a ledger and he could build again.

And he still could not tell a single person where any of it had come from. Not Ferra. Not Ward. Not any of the six who sat at his table. It had not got easier in nine years. Of everything in his life, it was the only thing that had never once improved.
