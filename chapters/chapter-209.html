Chapter 209 - Ashendeep

The last pages came clear in the week after the notice, in the small room, by lamplight, with the frost on the inside of the shutters.

Not the carved signs. Nobody had read a whole line of those yet and Emeric did not expect to for a long time. The margins. Zale had read them in the grey light of the room in his head, an hour of them in a minute, and Ferra had carried every word of his reading out in her own head and set it down within the hour on her ruled sheets, with her strokes and dots and rings; and since then, in the evenings, the two of them had been going back through the copier's brown ink with Zale's readings beside it, sign against word, fixing what could be fixed. By the winter they had most of the back of the book. Not well. Enough.

The copier had got to his island. He had got there in a boat hired off a dry coast, across water a man could row in a day, and he had written very little about the crossing except the price. Then he had written about what he found, in a hand that had got smaller and faster, as if he had been writing by a poor light and did not know how much of it he would have.

A channel, narrow, between two shores, with the island long in the middle of it. Dry. A fort at each end, belonging to two kings. And in the middle, between the forts, on high ground, the outcrops: pale stone breaking up out of the sand in splinters and ridges, like the bones of something enormous lying just under the skin of the ground, and every one of them glass. Men digging at them at night who did not want to be seen digging. And under the outcrops, somewhere, the copier had found a way down.

"That's Zale's sacrifice ground," Ferra said. "The glass that grows out of the ground. The men who go out and don't come back."

"It's the Isle of Shards."

Everybody called it that. Emeric had called it that himself at court, the way everybody did, a neutral name for a rock nobody had wanted enough to quarrel over. In the charter of the kingdom's founding, which he had copied out as a boy in the long form, it had another name: Verandel's Claim, after the first king, whose surveyors had drawn a line down the middle of a river a very long time ago and called everything on one side of it his. And Saban, when Emeric had asked him in the autumn what Duvrenne called it, had said without having to think: Ashendeep.

Emeric had not liked that name the moment he heard it, and it had taken him until now to know why. A claim was a thing you wrote on paper. It said nothing about the place. But somebody had named it ashen and deep, and nobody names a place like that from a chart. You named a place like that when you had worked in it, and burned in it, and gone down into it, and not all of your people had come back up.

They went on the night of the dark moon, in the K Discovery, with eight aboard.

Fletcher at the levers, because Fletcher had built her and would not be left on the ground when she went anywhere new. Ned with the flasks and the racks. Ferra with the log and the pencil on its string, because Emeric had asked for her and she had said a log kept by anybody else was a story. Two of Ward's men at the rails as lookouts. And in the hold, in their harness, three suits of black plate with the capes furled on their shoulders: Ward's, Osric's and his own.

She went down the lower water at dusk on the ebb behind the Marsh Hen, a coaster towing a coaster, no lamps, as hull three had gone for the Milford survey, because Armand's rule stood while there were mages on the flat and there were always mages on the flat now. Five miles out past the bar, in the dark, they cast off the tow. Fletcher took her up.

Emeric felt the band come round them at three miles the way he always felt it: the medium thickening against the sense like water rising up a wall, until the whole of the air was full of it, and the stones in their racks along the veins drank, and the lamps in the deckhouse brightened without anybody touching them. It was very cold. Ferra had her collar up and her hands in her sleeves and the log on her knee.

Four hours, north and east, over the sea, in the dark, with the stars hard and close overhead and nothing below but black. Fletcher steered by the stars and a compass and a sheet of Saban's, laid out on the chart table under a hooded lamp: the coast of Kesrath drawn from a dozen merchants' memories, and the channel, and in the channel the long shape of the island with a fort drawn at each end. Emeric did not trust the sheet. He had sent Saban to find the best one there was, and this was it, and he did not trust it.

At a little past midnight Ferra, at the rail, said, "There," and pointed down, and they saw it: two small lights, very far apart, far below, at the two ends of a long darkness that was darker than the sea round it. The forts. Their watch fires. And between them the island, lying in its channel like a sleeping animal, with nothing on it that showed.

Fletcher brought her round and held her in the band, over the middle of it, and stopped.

They went over the side one after another from the boat door in the hull's belly, Osric first, then Ward, then Emeric, into three miles of black.

The capes went out on the first pull. Emeric felt the battens snap taut from wrist to heel and the air take him, and the fall became a long slant, fast and cold and silent, with the wind roaring in the helm and nothing to see at all but the two watch fires turning slowly far below as he banked. Ahead of him and below, he could not see Osric or Ward. He could feel them, because he had the sense out: two small hard knots in the medium, the hearts of their suits, sliding down the dark ahead of him in a long line.

At a thousand feet he felt the ground come up under the sense before he could see it: sand, thin, the medium lying over it thinly the way it lay over any open ground. Rock under the sand. And ahead, rising out of the flat middle of the island, a long ridge of something that was not rock. It lay in the medium differently. Brighter. Stranger. The outcrops.

They came down on Lighten, a quarter of their weight on the stones, slow, almost without sound: a pressure in the air, no more, and sand stirring under their boots as they touched. Osric first, rolling his cape up onto his shoulders. Ward. Emeric last. They stood together on the sand in the dark with the wind moving over them and the cold of the desert night coming up through their boots, and listened.

Nothing. Wind. Far off to the west, the smallest sound of somebody at the west fort's fire laughing at something. Far off to the east, nothing at all.

Ward touched his shoulder once, and they began to walk.

It took the three of them less than an hour to know that it was not foragers.

Emeric had half expected foragers. He had expected Zale's tavern stories to be what tavern stories were: a few poor men off the dry coast rowing across on moonless nights to break off what they could carry and sell it in the ports for bread. A few men and a few boats and a great deal of fear and nobody in charge.

There was somebody in charge.

They found the landing first, on the island's northern shore, below the outcrops, where the channel came in close under a low cliff: a jetty of timber on stone footings, running out into deep water. It had been built. It had been mended. Emeric put his gauntlet on one of the uprights, and the timber was new, pale, cut this year, bolted with iron to the older timber under it, and the iron was not rusted through. Somebody had replaced that upright in the spring. Somebody had brought the timber across the channel to do it, because there was no tree on the island taller than a man.

From the landing, steps. Cut into the cliff, not worn: cut, square, each one the height of a man's shin, with a rope run up beside them through iron eyes. At the top, a hut of stone with a sailcloth roof, and against its back wall, under the sailcloth, barrels. Ward counted them by touch in the dark. Water, most of them, sealed and marked. Four of meal. Two of something that smelled of lamp oil. Every one of them branded on the head with the same mark: a tower, and a line under it.

And on the inside of the hut's door, where a man going out to work would see it every night, a board. Osric found it by feel and held his helm's slit close to it and shielded the lightest of lamp stones in his hand over it for as long as it took Emeric to read it.

It was not writing. It did not need to be. It was marks. Rows of short strokes cut into the board with a knife, in groups, and every day's stroke crossed through, and at the end of every tenth group a different mark, a little boat, and after the boat a new row began. The newest row had ten strokes, and nine of them were crossed.

Emeric looked at it for a long time.

Men who went out and did not come back. Ten days on the island, working at night, and on the tenth day a boat, and the boat took one lot off and put the next lot on, and the next lot cut a new row. It was a roster. It was the kind of board Bram had nailed up in the works in the first year, so that every man knew his shift without having to ask. Hungry men did not keep a roster. Hungry men came when they were hungry.

And it explained the empty hut, and the empty ridge, and the dark on the whole middle of the island. It was the last night of a row. Whoever had been cutting the ridge for nine nights had downed tools and gone east to the anchorage under the fort to wait for the boat, and tomorrow night the next lot would be here, and the ridge would be full of men. Emeric had picked the dark of the moon for the crossing. He had not known he was also picking the one night in ten when nobody was working.

"Somebody pays for this," Ward said, in his flat voice, very quietly. "Somebody sends the timber and the barrels and the boat. On a calendar."

"Kesrath," said Emeric.

"Or whoever Kesrath answers to."

Emeric did not answer that. The tower on the barrel heads was not Duvrenne's crown's mark. He did not know whose it was. He knew whose coast was across the water, and whose coast answered to whom, and he knew that the woman who ruled that coast had fixed the price of crystal in four kingdoms for twenty years, at a banquet, in front of him, the night he opened his mouth about it and was sent south for it.

He had said, that night, that a price like that meant a deposit nobody had declared. He had been right. He was standing on it.

They went on, inland, under the outcrops, and he let the sense out as far as it would go.

The outcrops were strange in the medium. He had known they would be; the splinters Zale had brought had been strange in his hand, rawer than any cut stone. This was the same, a great deal of it, ridge after ridge of pale glassy stone breaking up out of the sand and running away east and west in the dark, and under the sense it was bright and uneven, like a thing that had been broken off something still growing. He did not stop to think about that. He had other things to find.

Under the sand, a little way south of the outcrops, the sense found water.

Not water now. Where water had been. There was a channel under the island, under the sand, a long shallow trough in the rock that ran across the island's middle from north to south, filled with finer, heavier stuff than the sand above it: silt, and river gravel, rounded, laid down by running water a long time ago and buried since. An old riverbed. The river that the first king's surveyors had drawn their line down the middle of, before there were any charts, when this had not been an island at all but two banks.

He followed its edge with the sense, and stopped.

The edge was sharp. He had expected a slope, the long gentle side of a channel a river had moved away from slowly, over generations, a little every flood, the way rivers crept. It was not a slope. It was a cut. The river had been there, in that bed, and then it had not been there. It had gone somewhere else all at once, in a flood or a season, jumped its banks and cut a new way to the sea on the island's far side and left its old bed to fill with sand. And the line on the charter, which had been drawn down the middle of the old bed, had been left lying in the sand with nothing on either side of it but more sand, and two kings' surveyors, a long time apart, looking at two different rivers and each swearing to his own.

He knew it the way he knew anything the sense gave him: completely, and for nobody else. No court would hear it. No surveyor could check it without digging the island up. It was the same as Wayford, the same as every time the sense had told him a thing he could not prove: he knew, and knowing was worth exactly what he could make of it on his own.

He filed it. Ward, beside him, did not ask.

The way down was under the outcrops' highest ridge, and Kesrath had been working the roof of it for years.

They found the workings first: the pale stone cut away in steps and benches along the ridge's flank, broken, splintered, the spoil heaped below in glittering drifts that crunched under their boots, and wooden props, and tool marks. Somebody had been cutting the glass stone out of that ridge for a long time, a bench at a time, going down.

And under the lowest bench, where the cutting had gone deepest, the sense went hollow.

Emeric stood on the spoil and felt it. The rock went down under his feet, solid, for a man's height, two, and then it did not; there was a space under it. A long space, going down and in, under the ridge, into the dark, with the medium lying in it differently from the air above. Thicker. Not much. Enough. He could not feel the bottom of it. The sense did not reach that far through rock.

There is a way down under the, the copier had written, in the last margin but one, by a bad candle, and the signs go down with it, and I will follow them in the morning.

Kesrath's men were cutting the roof of it. They did not know. They were cutting the glass stone out bench by bench and selling it in the northern ports, a splinter at a time, and every bench took them a little further down, and one night, in some year, a man was going to put his pick through the last of the roof and find the dark under it. Emeric did not know whether that was what had happened to the men who went out and did not come back. He suspected it was not the only thing.

He did not go down. He had not come to go down. He stood on the spoil and let the sense lie on the hollow for a long moment, fixing it, the way he had fixed the Milford squares from the air, so that it would be on his shelf when he wanted it. Then he took it back.

They took samples. Not cargo. One of everything, and nothing Kaldo could not afford to lose, in what three men could carry up a line.

On the western flats, where the island ran out in a dry white plain that crunched like frost, there was a grey shrub growing low and scattered, knee high, with narrow silver leaves, and nothing else growing at all. Osric cut three, close to the ground, and dug under one with his knife until he had the root, and wrapped them in sacking. On the plain itself, the white crust that crunched was a salt that was not salt; Emeric broke a plate of it off with his gauntlet and tasted it, and it was bitter and soapy on the tongue, and he knew what it was and did not say so and put it in a jar. By the western shore, where the sand went dark and stank, there were pools of black that did not move, with a skin on them that shone in the starlight, and a slow bubbling at their edges. Seep oil. They filled a small cask, which took some time, and cut a slab of the thicker black at the edge of the largest pool, which was pitch, as hard as wax in the cold. Under the east cliffs, where birds had nested for longer than there had been kings, Ward filled a sack with what was under them and said nothing about it at all, then or ever. And in a seam of red rock at the foot of the outcrops Emeric found a stone the colour of dried blood, very heavy for its size, and broke off a fist of it, and wrapped it in oilcloth twice, and kept it apart from everything else.

A shrub, a crust, a cask, a slab, a sack, a stone. It was not much. It was not meant to be. He did not know yet what any of it would do in Kaldo's hands. He knew what it would do in his old world's, every piece of it, and that was not the same thing, and the experiments came first.

It was an hour before dawn. They went back to the landing.

The K Discovery came down for them.

That was the part Emeric had argued with Fletcher about, at the long-room table, for most of an evening. The ship could not stay high and drop a line three miles; no line was that long, and none would bear it. She had to come down. She had to come down out of the band, off the stars, in the dark, over an island with two forts on it, and hang low enough over the landing for a line to reach the ground, and take three men and their samples up it, and then climb. Fletcher had said it could be done. He had said it could be done fast. He had not said it could be done unseen, because he did not lie about his ship.

Emeric felt her coming before he saw her, a great dense shape in the medium sliding down out of the thick air high up into the thin air near the ground, her stones burning bright in his sense as they worked. Then he saw her: a darkness against the stars, growing, long and black, blotting them out one by one, and making no sound at all.

The line came down. Osric clipped on the samples and went up first, on Lighten, walking up the line into the dark. Then Ward. Then Emeric, last, with the stone the colour of dried blood inside his breastplate against the heart.

He was halfway up the line, a hundred feet over the landing, with the ship's belly above him and the island below, when he looked east.

The sky was greying. Not light yet. The first grey, low, along the Kesrath shore, so that the channel stood out pale against it and the east cliffs black. And in the anchorage under the east fort, where the channel came in close, a boat was pulling out.

A small boat. A supply boat, broad and low, with oars out on both sides and men at them, heading east across the channel for the Kesrath shore. He counted days in his head before he had decided to. Nine crossed and one not. The tenth day. The boat was on the calendar. It was taking the old lot off, and would be back with the next. It went in his head beside the board and the barrels and the new timber on the landing as one more piece of the same thing: an operation, on a roster, run by somebody who paid.

And one thing was wrong with it.

At the stern, by the steering oar, among the men pulling and the man steering, there was a man who was not working. He was standing. He was standing at the stern rail with his back to the rowers and his face turned up, to the sky, to the west, where the dark was still thick; and he stayed like that, quite still, while the boat pulled out across the channel, and did not turn round, and did not sit down.

Above Emeric, Fletcher took her up. The line went taut, and the island dropped away under his boots, and the ship climbed, fast now, out of the thin air and back into the thick, with Emeric on the end of her like a stone on a string. For a minute, he knew, as she went, she was a long black shape against a greying sky, for anybody on the water who happened to be looking up.

Then she was in the band, and the dark, and gone.

Ferra had seen the boat from the rail. She had the log on her knee and the pencil on its string, and when Emeric came in over the boat door's sill with his helm off and the cold coming off his plate in a cloud, she was writing.

"There was a boat," she said. "Under the east fort. Going east."

"I saw it."

"One man at the stern wasn't rowing." She did not look up. "He watched us go up the whole time. Or he watched the sky. I couldn't tell which."

"Nor could I."

She wrote the hour, by the ship's glass, and the heading of the boat, east by a little south, and supply, on the tenth day, as the board, and then, after a moment, one man standing at the stern, facing the sky. She drew no ring round it. She did not know what it was, and she did not pretend to, and she wrote it down anyway, because it had happened.

Emeric sat down on the bench against the hull with his back to the iron, and let the band take the cold out of the stones and put the warmth back in them, and closed his eyes. Four hours to Kaldo. Six samples in the hold. A roster on a board, a river that had jumped, a hollow under a ridge that a dead man had gone down into by a bad candle.

And a man at a stern rail, looking up. He thought about him once, briefly, the way he thought about a thing he had seen and could not use. A rower resting. A Kesrath overseer counting stars. Nobody.

He did not think about him again for a long time.
