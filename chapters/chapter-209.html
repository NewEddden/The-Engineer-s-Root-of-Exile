Chapter 209 - The Notice

They brought them up at noon.

Imani would not have it done sooner. She had spent the morning on the straw in the low room with her sleeves at her shoulders, going from pallet to pallet with her nurses behind her, and she would not move anybody up a stair that long until every one of them had been warmed through and kept down water, and the man by the arch had breathed on his own for three hours together without a hand on his back. A little before noon she got up off her knees by the last pallet and said that they would go now, on litters, carried, every one of them, and that there would be no cart.

"It's half a mile to Sethe's hall," said Declan. "Downhill. A cart with straw in the bed..."

"No cart," said Imani.

Declan looked at her, and then at the boy on the last pallet, who had heard the word and stopped breathing under his blanket until it had gone by; and went up the stair without another word to have the column cut poles.

The light was the next thing. Nobody had thought of it. The first litter came up out of the stair head into the rag bays, where the shutters had been thrown open in the morning to let out the dust, and the man on it, a young man with white hair, put both his arms over his face and made a sound Emeric had heard once before in his life, from a dog that had been kept in a shed. Imani stopped the litter in the passage. She looked at the shutters, and at the yard door beyond them, standing open on the noon, white; and sent a nurse running for linen. After that every one of them went up out of the dark with a fold of clean linen laid over the eyes, and their own hands holding it there.

"Is it the night?" said the white-haired man under his linen, as they carried him out across the yard. His voice was quite calm. "Is it the hour?"

"It's noon," said Imani, walking beside him.

He thought about that. He did not seem to know what to do with it.

"Noon," he said, as if it were a word somebody had told him once and he had kept without a use for it. And then, after a while: "Which day?"

"The eighteenth of August."

He did not say anything to that at all.



Wayford had come out.

It had been coming out since the flash went off in the night. It had heard the house shake, all of it, from the bridge to the vats; it had seen the chimney lean in the first light, and the ship come in at seven out of the south and settle on the road below the chain; and it had come up the hill road in ones and twos all morning and stood, and nobody had sent it away, so it had stayed. It stood along both sides of the road from the Staple's gate down to the bridge and round into the square: vat men with their arms dyed blue to the elbow, women with baskets they had forgotten to put down, the posting house's ostlers, children on the walls, old Ambry in his ink-black apron at the corner of his own lane. It did not know what it was waiting for. It waited.

And what came out of the gate was forty men in black, two to a pole, carrying fifteen litters down the hill, with a sheet of linen over every face.

The town took off its hats.

Emeric saw it go down the hill in front of them, from the gate to the bridge, the way wind goes across a field of barley: men pulling off their caps and holding them against their chests, women putting a hand flat over their mouths, a boy on a wall being pulled down off it by the back of his shirt. The silence went ahead of the litters all the way. They thought they were looking at the dead. Fifteen dead, carried out of the Staple house by the prince's men after the night the house shook; and every one of them, Emeric understood, walking beside Imani with his cut hand in his pocket, was standing on that road deciding whose.

Halfway down, on one of the litters near the back, a hand came out from under a blanket and moved the linen off a pair of eyes, slowly, to see the sky.

A woman by the bridge screamed. Once. Then the whole hill road was making a sound Emeric had never heard a town make, low, not words, the sound of a great many people breathing in at the same time and not breathing out; and it went down the road behind the litters all the way to the square, and did not stop when they had gone.



Sethe's long hall became a ward in an afternoon.

The trestles went out to the yard and the beds came in: every bed in the hall and the posting house and three houses on the square whose owners came to the door of their own accord and said take it, with the bedding on; and Imani's nurses made them up in two rows down the length of the room, under Sethe's grandfather's antlers and Sethe's grandfather's banners, with a fire lit in each hearth in August. Imani went down the rows with a stick of chalk and a slate for each bed and hung the slate on the bedpost, and wrote on it whatever the bed told her.

Some of them told her at once. The white-haired man who had asked the hour was Perrin, from Tollet, nineteen; he said it in that order, as if he had been saying it to himself every night so as not to lose it, and Imani wrote it down in that order. The man by the arch, grey to the scalp, the one Emeric had breathed for, was Hew, and could not remember the name of his village, and was ashamed of it; Imani told him it would come, and wrote HEW, and left a space under it. Colley. Jem, who was about Perrin's age and would not look at anybody. Senna, a woman with a long plait gone white at the end like a candle wick, who asked Imani very politely whether the lamp would be going out soon, in the middle of the afternoon, with the sun on the floor.

The girl from the chair was Tansy. She told Imani so herself, in a whisper, from the nest of blankets on the bed beside her mother's, as though it were a secret Imani might be trusted with; and then she told her that her mother was Hild, and that her mother could not talk today, and that Imani was not to mind.

The boy with the doll was Robin. He gave Imani his name, and his age, which was twelve, and the place he came from, a Dray holding up the north road; and when Imani asked, carefully, whether there was anybody of his in the hall, he looked at the beds going down the room one after another and said no, his mam wasn't here, in the voice of somebody saying it was raining.

And there was a man on the bed by the far hearth, thirty perhaps, with grey hair and eyes that followed Imani everywhere she went and a mouth that did not open. Not for water. Not for his name. She asked him three times, gently, and he looked at her, and she could see him hear it, and nothing came.

"That's Wat," said Perrin, from the next bed but one, without lifting his head. "He don't talk. He's from Hollin's." He was quiet. "He's been there longest. Four winters, he's been there. He talked the first one, Colley says."

Imani wrote WAT on the slate, and hung it on the bedpost, and put her hand for a breath on the blanket over the silent man's feet, and went on to the next bed.



The scream came at four.

Emeric was in the doorway with a crate of Imani's bottles. Sethe's hall had light stones in iron cages on the walls between the banners, as every rich man's hall in the north had them, and as the afternoon went on and the hall darkened under its high roof a nurse had gone round with a taper and woken them, one after another, as anybody would in any hall in the kingdom; and the cold white light came up along the wall above the beds.

Tansy's mother had been lying with her face to the wall since noon. She turned over at the light. She looked up at the cage on the wall above her daughter's bed, at the small grey stone in it, burning white.

And screamed.

It was not a loud sound. She did not have a loud sound left in her. It went on, though, high and thin and on and on, with her hands over her daughter's body on the next bed and her eyes on the stone; and down the length of the hall, bed after bed, the others heard it and turned and saw what she was looking at, and Emeric watched the same thing happen in fourteen faces, one after another, all the way down the two rows. Senna put her hands over her ears. Colley got out of his bed and tried to get under it.

Imani was at the wall before anybody else had moved. She did not ask anybody to do it. She took the cage off its hook with her bare hands, hot as it was, and carried it out of the hall at a walk, and came back for the next; and by the third her nurses were doing it with her, and Declan's men, and Emeric, every cage on every wall, out of the hall and across the yard and into the coach house, and the coach house door shut on them. Then tallow. Every candle in Sethe's kitchen and every one in the posting house, in saucers, all down the room, yellow and smoking and ordinary.

Hild stopped. She lay with her arms over her daughter and looked at the nearest candle for some time. Then she turned her face back to the wall.

Imani came and stood beside him in the doorway with her palms red.

"No stones," she said. "Anywhere they can see. Not on the walls, not on the nurses, not in your pocket. Not a light, not a warmer, not a band." She looked at his wrist, at the band with its row of beads under his cuff, and then at her own. "Not one."

He took his off. She took hers off. She put them both in the pocket of her apron and went back down the room.



Ferra went down to the cellar at three and came up at six.

She came up with her book and her pencil and a face he did not ask about, and sat down on the mounting block in the yard where he had sat that morning, and wrote. He sat down beside her. She did not stop writing.

"The racks," she said. "Two hundred and eighty-seven. Full, every one. Some small, some the size of a fist." She wrote the number and boxed it. "They're still singing. The full ones. You can hear it if you put your ear to the shelf." She did not look up. "I put my ear to the shelf."

"Nobody draws from them," said Emeric. "Nobody sells them. Nobody touches them."

"Then I'll enter them as what?"

He did not know.

She held her pencil over the line. Then she wrote a word, and looked at it, and he saw her face change. She did not strike it through. Ferra did not strike things through. She wrote a line under it, small, with the date, Correction, and beside that the word she meant. He looked. The first word was held. The second was in trust.

The space after it, where a book would say in trust for whom, she left empty.



The five had been moved at dusk into the Staple's own strongroom: the stone box at the end of the north passage, twenty feet square, with the grate full of ash and the shelves bare to the ceiling and the charter hanging on the wall in its black frame over all of it. The iron door had gone flat across the floor on the night of the raid, when Emeric's breach stone took it off its hinges; Declan's men had hung a stable door in its place that afternoon, on new pins, oak, with iron straps across it and a grate cut in it at the height of a man's face. Declan put two men on it.

At nine Placit sent to ask for the Holder.

The message came up to Sethe's hall by one of Declan's men, who gave it word for word because he had been told to, in a voice that said what he thought of it: Lord Placit wished to speak with His Highness, as one gentleman to another, at His Highness's convenience, on a matter touching the honour of both their houses.

Emeric went.

He did not know why, afterward. He walked up the hill road in the dark with nobody beside him, because he had told Ward to stay at Sethe's hall, and in at the Staple's gate, and through the house, which smelled of plaster dust and ash and horses, along the north passage past the bench where Marrow had lain, to the strongroom door; and stood in front of it.

Through the grate he could see them. Five old men, sitting on the floor of their strongroom in the light of one lamp, with their backs to the bare shelves, in the ash of their own books. Hollin with his knees drawn up and his soft jaw on them. Vennor small in a corner. Dray on his back with his splinted leg on a folded coat, asleep or pretending. Strade upright against the wall, quite still, with his hands folded, looking at nothing. And Placit, in the middle of the floor, under the charter, with his close white beard and his pale eyes, looking at the grate. He had heard the step. He got up. He did it well; he was seventy, and he had drunk a great many stones, and he got up off a floor like a man of fifty.

"Highness," he said. "Thank you for coming."

Emeric did not say anything.

His hands were at his sides. He was aware of them. He was aware of the door, oak and iron, and the bolt on the outside of it, which Declan's man had not yet drawn, and of the twenty feet between the door and the old man under the charter; and of what was in his arms and his back and his hands, still, a day later, under everything, quiet now and not gone. He knew how long twenty feet took. He knew what his hands had done to a strip of iron.

"I think," said Placit, "that there has been a misunderstanding about the nature of the Staple's arrangements. Which I regret. And which, between men of our..."

"The square," said Emeric.

Placit stopped.

"You'll say it in the square." He heard his own voice from a little way off. "Whatever it is. On the twenty-sixth, at noon. Everybody will hear it." He looked at the old man through the grate a breath longer. "Everybody."

He turned and walked back down the north passage. He did not run. He did not let himself walk faster than a walk. Behind him he heard Placit say something else, in the same reasonable voice, and the guard at the door say, flatly, that His Highness had gone.

He stopped in the yard, in the dark, by the stable, and stood there with his hands open until they had stopped doing what they were doing. Then he went back down the hill.



The knots came down that night.

It was the fifth condition, and the five had been taken on the eighteenth, and the eighteenth had three hours left in it. So Osric went up on the pads off the ship as soon as it was dark, in his blacked suit, as he had gone up in June, and went from chimney to chimney across Wayford, and to the weathervane on the Staple's ridge last of all, beside the leaning stack, and brought every knot down in a bag on his back. Vesk drained them on the table in the room behind Ambry's as they came in, one by one, with his thumb on each until it went dull, and pointed at the next with his chin. Ferra stood by with the lamp and counted them into a box. At a quarter to twelve by the church clock Osric came in with the last one, off the weathervane, with a scrape down his cheek from the leaning stack; and she counted it, and wrote in the book under the date:

The five taken this day. Every knot drained. Done. F.

She showed it to him. Then she shut the book, and the net was over, and nobody in Wayford would ever know it had been there.



Ferra read the book after midnight.

The book Ward had taken unburned out of Marrow's arms: the one the five had wanted burned more than anything and Marrow had held on to longest, because, Emeric supposed, Marrow was a clerk, and a clerk cannot burn the only true book in a house. It lay on the table in Sethe's counting room between two candles. It was old, and fat, and falling apart; the calf of its back had split long ago and somebody had sewn the boards on again with harness thread, and the first gatherings had come loose and been sewn back in, and the corners of every page were brown and round with handling. The first forty pages were in a hand Ferra knew from Sethe's boxes: Placit's, long and confident and leaning. After that they were Strade's. Small upright figures, neat as a fence.

She read it the way she read everything, from the first page to the last, without skipping, with a sheet beside her for the sums. He sat across the table and read it upside down as she turned the pages, and did not help, because she did not want help, and because he could not have.

It had more than the tallies had. The tallies had been the totals, copied out a week at a time onto loose sheets. The book had the names.

Every one. Intake: a name, a place, whose land, the debt to the copper, the date, and a note. Lorn, of the Tollet road; Hollin's; one Gold four Silver; the third of June; family told, Staple. Hours, down the facing page: a mark for every morning in a chair, in rows of five. Yield: so many stones, so many grains of weight. Kept. Losses. And a column the tallies had never carried, which Ferra found on the second page and put her finger on and did not take it off: Served.

"Served," she said.

She went back to the beginning.

It took her until four. He watched her do it: page after page, her pencil going down the Intake column and the Served column and the Losses column, the little strokes on her sheet in fives, the totals at the foot of each page carried to the head of the next. Twenty years. The first entry was in Placit's hand, in the spring of a year she worked out on the margin of her sheet to be the twentieth before this one. There were three names on that first page. On the last there were sixteen. Fifteen of them were in Sethe's hall, asleep under candles. The sixteenth had no mark in the Hours after the sixth of August, and nothing against it anywhere else; and Ferra drew a small ring round it in pencil, and went on.

At four she stopped and laid her pencil down beside the sheet, straight.

"Two hundred and ninety-seven," she said. "In. In twenty years." She did not look up. "Sixty-one in Losses. Sixteen held, on the last page, and fifteen of those downstairs. That's seventy-seven." She put her finger on the last total. "Two hundred and twenty Served."

"Served."

"Let go." She turned back to a page in the middle, and showed him: a name in the Intake column, a row of strokes in Hours that went on for nine weeks and stopped, and on the day they stopped, in Strade's small hand, a word in the Served column, and a figure. Served. Debt struck. A season. "They kept them a season. Nine weeks, ten, twelve. Then they struck the debt and let them go home." She turned the page. "And told them what to say."

He looked at the column. Two hundred and twenty names that had gone back up the stair and out of the gate and home, to their own villages, on their own feet. Grey. Twenty years older in a summer. With a debt struck and a story to tell.

"They'd have said they worked the rag," he said slowly. "Sorting. In the bays. That's what the house was. Everybody in Wayford knew the Staple took men on for the rag in a bad year."

"And came home coughing," said Ferra. "Everybody who sorts rag comes home coughing."

She sat back. She looked at the book. For the first time since she had opened it, she did not look at it like a book.

"Aster will have the parish books," she said. "Births and burials. All of them, in the vestry, back to the plague and before." She did not move. "I'll have him bring them in the morning. And I'll go down the Served column, name by name, and find out how many of them are in the burials, and how long after." She was quiet. "I know already. I'll do it anyway. It goes in the book because it's true, not because I'm surprised."

He reached across the table and put his hand on the book, on the open page, flat, beside hers.

She looked at his hand.

"There's a back section," she said. "Turn it over."

He turned the book over and opened it from the back. The last gathering had been sewn in upside down, so that it read from that end, as a merchant keeps his private account at the back of his day book where a clerk turning pages will not come on it. It had a heading, in Placit's hand. The Ring.

Fourteen entries. Twice a year for seven years, at Lady Day and at Michaelmas. A date; a name from the Intake column, always one of the youngest, sixteen, seventeen; and eight stones, by their weights, one for each mark round the painted circle; and a line in Placit's hand, the same line every time, as if he had grown tired of writing anything else.

Did not take.

And on the facing page, in Strade's, against every one of the fourteen names, the same date as the rite, carried across from the Losses.

The last was at Lady Day this year. Twenty-fifth of March. A boy from the Tollet road, sixteen. Did not take.

Emeric shut the book.

He sat with his hand flat on the cover. Ferra sat across from him with her pencil laid straight beside her sheet. Neither of them said anything for a while. The candles burned down a little. Somewhere below, in the long hall, somebody was talking in their sleep, a woman's voice, the same three words over and over, and a nurse's voice answering, low.

"Placit wrote that line," said Ferra at last. "Fourteen times. Did not take." She was looking at the cover of the book under his hand. "As if it were a graft on a pear tree."

Then she got up, and put the book in Sethe's strongbox, and locked it, and blew out one of the candles; and sat down again in the light of the other, with her hands in her lap, and did not go to bed.



Aster brought the parish books at eight.

He brought them himself, three of them, in his arms, up from the vestry of the church on the square, in his brown coat with the mended cuffs; the priest came behind him with a fourth, an old man who had been asked for his burial books by a prince's chancellor at seven in the morning and had decided to carry the oldest one himself. Ferra thanked the priest and sent him away kindly. She kept Aster. She sat him down at the end of the counting room table with the burials open in front of him and the Served column in front of her, and read the names out one at a time, the name and the village and the date the debt was struck; and Aster found them.

Not all. Not the ones from other parishes, who were in other books. But Wayford's own parish ran out along four roads to the edge of three holdings, and a great many of the two hundred and twenty had been Wayford's own; and Aster found them, and read out the date of burial and the cause the priest had written, in the priest's thin hand, and Ferra wrote it on her sheet.

A wasting. A wasting. The cough. The sorters' cough. A wasting, aged twenty-six. The cough. Of a decline. The sorters' cough, aged thirty, leaving four.

It took them until noon.

Emeric stayed for all of it. He sat on the window seat with his back to the square and listened to the two of them, Ferra's voice and Aster's, going back and forth across the table like two people sawing a log, steady, with no change in either voice from the first name to the last; and he watched Aster's face, which was a clerk's face and did not change either, except that once, late in the morning, when Ferra read out a name from the north road and Aster turned the page and found it, he stopped, and put his finger on the line and did not read it.

"I knew him," said Aster. "He kept the ferry at Low Bank. He used to give me a ride across for nothing when I was new." He read the line. "The sorters' cough. Aged thirty-four." He was quiet. "I went to his burying. Sethe's counting room sent four Copper of bread to the widow. I carried it." He looked at the page a while longer. "I put it in the barns and boats. Bread, for a widow at Low Bank. Four Copper. To the day."

Ferra did not say anything. She wrote the line on her sheet, and the date, and read the next name.

At noon she added the column.

"A hundred and four," she said. "Of the two hundred and twenty. Buried in this parish within five years of the day they came home. Most inside two." She put her pencil down. "And the parish isn't the barony. And a man who went home to the north road went home to another priest's book." She looked at Emeric across the room. "I'll write to every priest in the barony. And Hollin's holdings, and Dray's, and the rest. It'll take a month." She was quiet. "It'll be more."

Sixty-one in the lime. A hundred and four in the churchyard. And more in other churchyards, in books he had not read; and the rest, the ones still alive, wherever they were, grey at thirty, coughing, telling anybody who asked that it had been the rag.

He looked out of the window at the square. It was full. It had been full since the litters went through. People standing in knots on the cobbles, talking, not loudly, looking up at Sethe's hall. Here and there in the crowd, he saw, now that he knew to look, a man with grey hair who was not old.



Rylan had asked, on the first day, to carry Garrin's food.

He had asked it in the yard of the Staple house, standing by the stable door where he had sat on his bucket all night, with straw on his knees, in the voice he used for asking whether the yard wanted sweeping. Emeric had looked at him. He remembered a stair under the keep at Kaldo in the first summer, and a cell with one door, and Rylan carrying a prisoner's bowl down it every day by his own hand, because Emeric had said nobody else was to; after the prisoner had said a courier's name in a room upstairs, and a boy on the door had died four hours later.

"Yes," he had said.

So Garrin went down into the coin cellar under the counting house on the square, behind three iron doors, where the barony's silver lay for a night on quarter days before it went south; and twice a day Rylan carried a bowl and a cup down the steps, and set them inside the third door, and came up again. He did not talk to Garrin. He told Emeric so, unasked, each evening, in a single line: took it down, he ate it, nothing said. On the first evening he had told Garrin one thing, standing in the doorway with the empty bowl. He told Emeric afterward what it was. Strade's ledger had a line in the Losses in May, C., carriage; and Ottoline had told them at Sethe's table by the lamp who had driven the Staple's cart across the square once a week for twenty years, grey and lame and with a bad eye. Rylan had told Garrin that Cobb was in the lime.

On the third evening Rylan came up the steps from the coin cellar and did not say his line. He stood in the door of the counting house with the empty bowl and looked across the square at nothing.

"He wants you," he said. "He says he'll say it once."



The coin cellar was a low room of brick with a bench along one wall and an iron ring in the floor. Garrin sat on the bench with his back to the brick and his right arm bound across his chest where Rylan had broken it at the stable gate, and his ankles chained to the ring. He was lean still. Older; eight years older than the night he had gone into the treeline off the capital road with his tea not lifted. Grey in the beard. A face you would forget while you were still looking at it, Petram had said once of another man, and it was nearly true of this one, except for the eyes.

Emeric knew the note before he was on the last step. He had known it across a river. Quiet, and close, and hard, like a fist in a pocket.

Rylan stood in the doorway behind him with the lamp.

Garrin looked at Emeric for a while. Then he looked at Rylan. Then he looked at the floor between his boots.

"Your gate boy," he said. "The first summer. Sela's."

Emeric did not move.

"He ran letters for Cobb. Off the docks. The summer before you came, and that spring. A copper a time. Cobb used dock boys. Everybody did." Garrin's voice was flat and low and very even, a voice that had not been used much in its life and had never been used for this. "He didn't know what they were. He was a boy who wanted a copper. Then his mother got him off the water, on the gate. And then on that man's door." He did not say Petram's name. "And he heard that man say Cobb, through the door. And he knew the name, and he knew what it meant, because he wasn't stupid."

"I saw him," said Emeric. "I saw him know it."

Garrin looked at him a breath. Then back at the floor.

"He came down to the docks that evening," said Garrin, "to find Cobb. Not to run. Not to sell you. To tell him he was done. That he'd not carry for him again." He looked at the floor. "Cobb said the boy would talk by morning. Cobb said it to me. I did what Cobb said, in those days. On the mill road, going home." He paused. "Once. From behind."

Behind Emeric, in the doorway, the lamp did not move at all.

"There was a name on your roster that night," said Garrin. "For the gate change, with him. So he'd not walk it alone. A man nobody'd ever seen. He'd been a name on that roll six years, drawing pay for somebody. Cobb knew which names were names. He saw to it, that night." He shrugged with his good shoulder. "So he walked it alone."

"Why?" said Emeric. "Why tell me."

Garrin looked up at him. For the first time something moved in the even, forgettable face; not much.

"Cobb's in their lime," he said. "They put him down there like a lame horse. Twenty years he drove their cart." He looked at the floor again. "I owe them nothing. I owed him. He's past owing." He was quiet. "And I'll not hang in their square beside them. I'll not be one of five. If I hang, I hang for the boy, in your barony, by your law, for what I did. Not for theirs."

"And the rest?" said Emeric. "Who Cobb answered to. Why Kaldo. Petram said you told him once who the job was for, and why, and it was a story about Kaldo before it was a story about any prince."

"That's not mine," said Garrin. "That's the rest." He leaned his head back against the brick and shut his eyes. "I said I'd say it once. I've said it."

Emeric stood in the coin cellar a while longer. Then he turned and went up the steps past Rylan, who stepped aside to let him by, and did not follow.

Rylan came up a quarter of an hour later. He came out of the counting house door into the evening with the lamp in one hand, unlit now, and stood beside Emeric on the step, and looked at the square, where the knots of people were still standing, and the light was going.

"I could have," he said. "At the gate. With the horses screaming and nobody to see. I had his head in the dung and my knee on his back and his arm in my hand, and I could have." He turned the dead lamp round in his fingers. "I wanted to. I've wanted to for five years. Every night I've gone into Finn's room and looked at him asleep, I've wanted to."

"I know."

"Nessa said, if it comes to it, don't." Rylan looked at the lamp. "She said, His Highness will ask you to bring him in alive, because His Highness will want to know things. And you'll want to know them too, after. She said, you'll want to know who Tam was, and why." He was quiet. "She was right. She usually is. It's very tiresome."

He went down the step into the square with the lamp, toward Sethe's hall, and left Emeric standing on the counting house step in the dusk with the name.

Ferra found it in the morning. She had the old garrison roll of Kaldo in her trunk at Sethe's hall, because she had all the old rolls in her trunk wherever she went; and on the page for that summer, against the gate change on the evening Tam died, there was a second name beside his. She put her finger on it. Then she took the book out of her bag, her own book, and turned back eight years, to the first August, to a page Emeric remembered: sixteen names, struck through, one line each, in her early hand, with a date. Men who drew pay and existed nowhere else.

The name was the ninth.



He wrote the charge that night, and it was three pages long, and it was all true.

He wrote it at Sethe's table in Sethe's counting room with the ledger at his elbow, fast, without stopping, as he had once written to his father from the capital and never sent it. The chairs and the cradles. The ring. Did not take, fourteen times. Sixty-one. A hundred and four. The strokes on the wall above Robin's pallet. Tansy saying please. Placit on the floor of his strongroom asking to be spoken to as one gentleman to another. The charter on the wall, from a king, renewed by every king since. The magistrate who dined with Placit. All of it, every line, in a hand that got larger as it went, until at the bottom of the third page it was hardly his.

Ferra read it in the morning, standing, at the window, by the light. She read all three pages twice. Then she laid them face down on the table, squared them, and sat down, and took a clean sheet, and wrote on it one line.

She turned it round for him.

That they held the people of this barony in secret, unlawfully, and caused their deaths.

"That's what they'll hang for," she said. "Everything else you wrote is true. This is the part they did." She put her hand flat on the face-down pages. "The rest is what the kingdom did. You can't hang a kingdom in a square." She was quiet. "Not yet. Keep these. You'll want them."

He looked at the one line a long while. Then he nodded.

The second part was harder, and they argued it for most of the morning, and it was Ferra who won it, which surprised neither of them. Held against rent. It was the old law, older than the Staple, older than the five houses, and it was on the books of every barony in the north and half the south: a man who owed and could not pay could be taken by his creditor on a magistrate's seal and kept until the debt was worked off, and his household with him, because nobody had ever thought to write that his household could not be. The Staple had not broken that law. It had used it. Every name in the Intake column had a magistrate's seal against it, somewhere, in some drawer.

"So it goes," said Emeric. "In the same notice. On the same day."

"In the four baronies," said Ferra. "Not just Wayford. If it's only Wayford, every lender in Milford takes his debtors across the river." She wrote. "A debt can be taken from a man's goods. From his wages, a part, by a magistrate's order, a Copper in the Silver and no more. Never from his body, and never from his freedom to come and go." She looked up. "And every person held for a debt anywhere in the four baronies is free from the hour it's read. Not from the day the magistrates get round to it. The hour."

"Paxton will write."

"Paxton will write an inventory," said Ferra. "He'll list every debtor in Milford and what each one owes to the copper, and object at the bottom." She wrote the last clause. "Let him."

Then the date, and the hour, and the place. Emeric wrote that part himself, plainly. That on the twenty-sixth day of August, at noon, in the square at Wayford, the five lords of the Staple would be brought out before the people of the barony; that any who had been held might speak, and any who had lost a son or a daughter or a husband or a wife to the Staple's cellar might speak; and that afterward they would hang.

Ferra read it over.

"That's not a trial," she said.

"No."

"Then don't let it look like one." She put her finger on the line. "If they're going to hang whatever's said in that square, then the notice says so. And it says whose order." She looked at him. "Not the barony's. Not the crown's. Yours."

He took the pen back, and wrote it in. By the order of the Holder of Wayford, and not by writ. And signed it under the last line in his own full name, all of it, the way he signed nothing else.



The pictures were his.

He drew them that afternoon on four blocks of pear wood from Ambry's back shop, with a soft pencil, for the old man's apprentice to cut; and Ambry stood behind him and watched, with his ink-black hands folded under his apron, saying nothing, which for Ambry was praise. Wayford could not read. Not most of it. Ferra knew to the soul how much of it could not, because the census had asked, and she had never yet added that column up; but he did not need her to add it to know that a notice in words, posted on a door in Wayford, would be read aloud by one man in ten to the other nine, and that whoever read it aloud would choose what to read.

So: a man with a paper and a seal at a cottage door, taking another man by the arm. And a heavy black cross over the whole of it, corner to corner.

The same man with the same paper, at the same door, leading away a cow, with a sack of meal on his shoulder. No cross.

A door standing open, and a woman and two children walking out of it into a road, with nobody's hand on them.

And the last: the square at Wayford, from above, the way the ship saw it. The church at one end, and the counting house at the other, and the posting house and Ottoline's shop with its clock, so that anybody who had stood in the square would know it. The sun at the top of the sky, over the church tower, for noon. A platform before the counting house, and above it a beam, and on the beam five short lines hanging down. And across the top, for the day, in a row along the sky, small, the way the boy had made them on the wall above his pallet: strokes in fives. Four strokes and a bar, five times over, and one more.

Ambry looked at that one for some time.

"They'll count it," he said. "Everybody in the north can count." He took the block. "My boy will have them cut by dark."

They printed it on Ambry's old press in the front shop, in the new type: the first sheet ever to come off a press in Wayford in Wayford's own letters, cast in the spring in a hand mould the bell founder down by the weir had made, from the steel punches in the box Emeric had pushed across this same table in April. Every e on the sheet was sharp. Ambry set it himself. He would not let his apprentice touch it. He set the one line of Ferra's that mattered in the biggest letters he had, and locked the forme, and pulled the first proof, and held it up to the window; and then he read it, all of it, aloud, in the empty front shop, to nobody, slowly, the way an old man reads who learned late.

"Two thousand," said Ferra, at the door, with her book. "Tonight. For the four baronies."

"You'll have them," said Ambry, not turning round.



Ferra wrote the stay at six.

He saw her do it. She did not hide it. She sat at the end of the counting room table with the proof of the notice beside her, drying, and a clean sheet in front of her, and wrote, in her best clear hand, the large one, the hand she kept for things in the book that were meant to be read by somebody who was not her. He was at the window. He watched her write the heading, and knew it from across the room, because he had written the clause it came from himself, at the long table in the fourth year, and given it to her because he was going to be wrong one day and wanted somebody able to say so; and she had written under it, in this same hand, The Chancellor objects, and he had written Overruled.

The Chancellor stays the order of the Holder of this date, that five lords be hanged at Wayford by his order and not by writ, for these reasons.

She wrote the reasons. He did not go and read them. He could see the length of them from the window: four lines, each a sentence, each with a full stop. Then her name, and the date.

Then she sat with it.

A stay so written, in the barony's book, over her name and the date, held until she lifted it. That was the clause. It was not in the barony's book. It was on a loose sheet beside the barony's book, and the barony's book was open at the page for that day, a hand's breadth from it, with her pencil lying across it.

The light went down the wall of Sethe's counting room. In the square below the window somebody was nailing something; a man's voice called a figure up to another man on a ladder. In the long hall below them, a nurse laughed at something, quickly, and stopped.

Ferra folded the sheet. Once, then again. She opened the barony's book at the back, at the inside of the back board, where there was a pocket she had made of glued linen for loose things that had to be kept and not entered, and put the folded sheet into it. Then she turned to the day's page, and took up her pencil, and wrote one line under the day's entries, small, in her ordinary hand.

A stay was written this day against the order of the 21st, and not entered. F.

She blotted it.

He came from the window and stood behind her chair. He did not say anything. Neither did she. After a while she put her hand up over her own shoulder without turning round, and he took it, and they stayed like that, she sitting and he standing, with the book open on the table in front of them and the line drying, until the clerk came up the stair with the candles.



The letter to Keller went by the post.

He wrote it the same night, to the crown's magistrate in the capital, the man who had sat in a room with its windows set too high to see out of, eight years ago, and sent Wexler to his death by crown order, and told Emeric to his face that it was the crown's judgment and not his. He wrote it in full. The charge, and the cellar, and the notice, and the date. He wrote it plainly and kept nothing out of it that could be got openly, and put nothing into it from the knots, because of the fourth condition. He sealed it. And then he went down to the posting house himself and gave it to the clerk at the counter for the ordinary mail coach north, and paid the ordinary fee, and took the ordinary receipt.

Ferra was in the posting house door when he came out.

"The ship would have that on his table by supper," she said.

"I know."

She looked at him. Then she opened her book and wrote the date and the hour of posting, and the coach.

"The coach is nine days to the capital in summer," she said, writing. "Ten if the long ford's up. It'll be on his table on the thirty-first." She closed the book. "Five days after."

"Yes."

She looked at him a moment longer with her book against her chest. She did not say whether it was right. She went back up the square to Sethe's hall, and he watched her go, and understood that she had put the receipt's number in the book too, and that in some year he could not see yet somebody would read it there.



The voice was Ottoline's idea, though she would have said it was only arithmetic.

He was standing in the middle of the square at dusk on the twentieth with his hands in his pockets, looking at the counting house, and the church at the far end, and the streets that came into the square at its four corners, and trying to see two thousand people in it. Ottoline came out of her shop under the stopped clock with her shawl round her and her magnifying glass on its cord, and stood beside him, and looked where he was looking.

"Forty yards," she said.

He looked at her.

"A man's voice. Shouting. In still air." She did not look at him. "Forty yards, to be understood. Sixty to be heard and not understood. Less with a crowd breathing. A crowd's a great deal of noise even standing still." She nodded at the counting house steps, and then at the church. "It's a hundred and ninety from those steps to that door. I've paced it. I pace everything." She was quiet. "That white-haired boy from Tollet speaks in a whisper. I heard him through Sethe's window this morning, asking for his mother. You'd not hear him at four."

He stood in the square and looked at the hundred and ninety yards.

"A bell carries three miles," said Ottoline. "On a still evening. The church bell there, you can hear it at the vats." She pulled her shawl round her. "A bell's not asking anybody anything. It's just loud." She looked at him sideways. "There's a man down by the weir who casts bells. Badly, his father says. He made Ambry's type moulds in the spring." She turned to go back to her shop. "He's got a bell in his yard he cast at Easter for the chapel at Low Bank, and it came out of the mould with a crack in the lip and has never rung true. It's been sitting in his yard since, in the nettles. I'd know. It's the only thing in Wayford I can hear from my shop that's flat."



The bell founder's yard was a mess of nettles and broken moulds and slag, down by the weir under the willows, with a furnace like a beehive at one end and a great pair of leather bellows beside it that two boys worked by standing on boards; and in the nettles, on its side, a bell. A small one, as bells went: as tall as a man's chest, green already at the lip, with a crack running up from the rim a hand's length into the bronze, black, like a hair on a plate.

The founder was a big, anxious, sooty man named in no book Emeric had read, who had made Ambry's hand moulds all spring in a fever of pride and now stood in his own yard turning his cap round in his hands while a prince walked round his worst bell three times.

"It don't ring, Highness," he said. "I'd not sell you it. It don't ring."

"I don't want it to ring." Emeric crouched and put his hand inside the mouth of it. It was cold. "I want it to shout."

The founder looked at him.

It took most of an evening to explain, on a board in the founder's shed, with chalk; and it was simpler than it looked, which was why he trusted it. A voice was air, shaken. A loud voice was a great deal of air, shaken hard. A man's chest could only shake so much air, and then the air spread out in the open and thinned, the way rings spread out on a pond, and at forty yards there was nothing left. So you did not ask the man's chest to shake the air. You gave the air from somewhere else: a great deal of it, steady, under pressure, from a pair of bellows into a weighted leather bag, the way a church organ was fed. You ran that air through a little box with a valve in it. And you let the man's voice work the valve.

He drew it. A cup to speak into, at the end of a tube. At the bottom of the tube, a thin disc, stretched like a drum skin, that the voice would shake, as a voice shook the iron disc in a telephone's mouthpiece. On the back of the disc, a little grate of brass, like a comb; and against it, almost touching, a second grate, fixed. When the disc shook, the slots between the two grates opened and shut, a hair's breadth, in time with the voice. And the steady air from the bag came through the slots and was cut, by them, into the exact shape of the voice. Not the man's breath. The bellows' breath, hard and big and steady, chopped into words by a disc the size of a coin.

And then the bell. The crown cut out of it, so that it was a mouth and nothing else; and between the box and the mouth, a cone of brass, long, a little wider every inch of the way, so that the chopped air came out into the world gradually and did not spill, the way a voice does not spill out of a man's cupped hands.

The founder looked at the chalk and said nothing at all, the way a man stands in front of a horse he has been told will pull a plough backward.

"The bellows I've got," he said slowly. "And the bag, I can make the bag. Two hides. And a cone." He looked at the drawing. "I can cast you a cone. Three pieces. Three days." He scratched his head with his cap. "And you'll cut the head off my Low Bank bell."

"Yes."

The founder thought about that. Then he laughed, unexpectedly, a big sooty bark.

"The chapel'll have a new one by Michaelmas," he said, "if you're paying for it, Highness. And that one'll ring." He turned his cap round once more. "What's the little box made of? The disc and the combs."

"Something better than I can make in three days," said Emeric. "I'm sending for somebody who can."

He sent for Tess that night by the ship's evening run: a page in his own hand to the works, asking for her and her bench box and a sheet of the finest brass she had and whatever she used for the discs in the telephones, and saying what it was for. She came down on the next run with all of it, her chair on the tackle and her bench box in her lap; and Osric, who had been in Wayford with the column all week and happened, for no reason he gave anybody, to be on the road below the chain when the chair came down, offered to work the bellows.

That was the twenty-first. He did not tell Tess the other thing until they were in the founder's shed with the drawing in front of them and her bench box open on the trestle. Then he told her.

"No stones," he said. "Nothing on it with a stone in. Not an air heart, not a pump stone, not a light. It runs on the bellows and on men's arms or it doesn't run." He looked at the drawing. "There'll be people on that platform who scream at a lamp."

Tess looked up at him from her chair. She had been in a dynamo house once, between a man with a sword and a machine she would not let him break, and had been carried out of it on a door, and had lived since in a chair he had packed with every stone and safeguard he could think of, because he could not stop himself; she knew better than anybody in Kaldo what a thing could be to the person it had been done to.

"Then it runs on the bellows," she said, and pulled the drawing toward her.



Vantic was in the small parlour off Sethe's hall, where she had been for three days, writing.

He had felt her there every time he passed the door. Not easily. Outside the dead air his sense had come back whole, and with it every note in Wayford, and hers among them; and hers, which he had known for years as small and dry and kept, a woman's mana folded small and put away, was so thin now that he had to go looking for it. Thinner than anybody's in Sethe's house who was not on a bed in the long hall. She had poured all of it into a stone that was full, to make it break; and it was coming back to her as it came back to everybody, slowly, a night at a time, and it had a long way to come.

She was writing anyway. Her report for the House, in her square small hand, at a table by the window with her coat over the back of the chair; not the House's dark coat, which was in the long hall on a bed, wrapped round a child, but a grey one borrowed from the posting house that did not fit.

She did not look up when he came in.

"Your sword master," she said, writing, "has told forty men and a ship's crew that the House's factor came down from the ship on the night of the raid on the suit line. He has told them so in that voice of his, in the yard, the morning after, all at once, as one tells a garrison the hour of a parade." She dipped her pen. "Nobody has contradicted him. Fletcher came to me yesterday and said that he had seen me go down the line himself and thought it very brave in a factor, and that the line had been put away now and he would thank me not to go down it again." She wrote. "Vesk has not said anything. Vesk never says anything. He brought me a cup of something hot this morning and pointed at it with his chin."

"Did you?" said Emeric. "Go down the line."

"I went down on nothing at all." She wrote a figure, and checked it, and went on. "Most of the way. Then, at about the height of a house, your weight took me, and I came down the rest rather faster, and broke a good deal of my knee on Placit's cobbles." She turned the page. "The House will have my report of the strongroom by the next coach. It will describe the strongroom. It will not describe a stair."

He stood by the door.

"Something came into my head," said Vantic. "In the yard. Before I broke the stone." She did not stop writing; but her pen went slower. "Not the weight. The other thing. As if a door had opened somewhere behind my eyes, into a room I had not known was in there, and something had put its hand on the latch from the other side." She was quiet. "Everybody in that yard had it. I could see them. Hands over their eyes." She dipped her pen. "Was it you?"

"I think so."

"Don't do it again."

"I didn't do it the first time," he said.

She looked up, then. For the first time since he had come in. At his face, across the little parlour, for as long as it took to read a line. He did not know what she saw. He knew what he was thinking, which was that he had been thinking it for three days: that he had dragged Marrow into the room in the strongroom against the man's will and they had both bled for it, from the nose and the ears, and Marrow had been a torn thing on the floor afterward and Emeric had come away with a stranger's kitchen in his head that he still could not get out of it. Forced, it costs one of them, or both. And then, an hour later, in the cellar, his door had come open on its own and gone out along his own mana to every note it touched, a house full of them, and taken hold of every one, and he had felt every one of them feel it. And afterward every one of them had had the same nail behind the same eyes, and so had he. All of them. Both sides.

He did not know if it was that. If it was the same thing, done to forty people at once, by nobody; and the nail was the price, paid on both sides of forty doors. Or if it had only been anger, as he had told himself in the yard, and anger that large simply hurt. He did not know which he would rather.

"Then learn how not to," said Vantic, and went back to her page.

He stayed by the door a little longer. On the table beside her ink there was a cup, with the dregs of something hot in it, and beside the cup a small grey stone. One of Kaldo's. Charged; he could feel it from the door, small and bright. Somebody had brought it to her, to draw from, to fill herself back up faster than the nights would do it.

She had not touched it.

"It's from the ship's stores," he said. "Orrin charged it. Nobody was strapped to anything."

"I know," said Vantic. She did not look at it. "It fills back in. Mine always has." She wrote. "I'd rather wait."



Imani came up from the ward at midnight. She did not knock; she had not knocked on a door of his in a year. He felt her on the stair, and then on the landing, and then she was in Sethe's old room with her hair down and her ward dress unbuttoned at the throat and her shoes in her hand, and she put the shoes down inside the door and crossed the room and lay down on Sethe's bed, on her back, across the foot of it, across Ferra's ankles, with her arm over her eyes.

"Ow," said Ferra, from the pillows, with her book.

"Hush," said Imani. "I've been on my feet nineteen hours. Your ankles are the softest thing in Wayford."

Ferra moved her book to make room for Imani's elbow, and went on reading.

Emeric was in the chair by the window, where he had been since ten, with the shutters open on the square. It was dark down there now. The posting house had one lamp lit over its door. Under it, on the door, a sheet of paper; he could see it from here, white. Ambry's boy had been out with a pot of paste for an hour, and there were sheets on every door round the square, and on the church door, and on the counting house, and the boy had gone off down the vat road with his pot to do the rest.

"How are they?" he said.

"Alive," said Imani, under her arm. "Tansy ate an egg. Robin ate two and asked for a third and I said no and he looked at me as if I'd told him the sea was dry. Hew remembered his village. He woke up at ten and said it out loud, Coldwell, and then cried for an hour because he'd remembered it." She was quiet. "Wat looked at me. Four times. I counted."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Placit asked to see me," said Emeric. "The first night. As one gentleman to another." He looked at the paper on the posting house door. "I went. I stood outside the door, and I looked at him through the grate. Seventy years old. Drunk on other people for twenty of them. And he stood up off the floor and called me Highness, and said there'd been a misunderstanding, between men of our..." He stopped. "Of our sort. He didn't get to say it. But that's what he was going to say."

"He'd have said kind," said Ferra, not looking up. "Men of our kind. It's the phrase. Sethe used it in three letters."

"Our kind." He was looking at the square. "He's right. That's the thing I can't get out from under. He's a lord with a charter from a king and a seat at a table. So am I. So's my father. So's my brother. So's every man who'll read that notice in the north and think, there but for the grace of God, and lock his cellar." He was quiet. "I've been counting houses. Since the mounting block. Baronies. Counties. Duchies. I can't stop. A hundred and more halls, and a cellar under every one of them, and a law that lets a man hold another man's children for what he owes, and a Church that's made sure for a thousand years the children have nothing in their hands. And I don't know what's under any of them. I don't know what's under Corbin's. I don't think anything is. I didn't think anything was under Placit's."

Imani took her arm off her eyes and looked at the ceiling.

"You can't go and look under every house in the kingdom," she said.

"No."

"Then what?"

He did not answer at once. He was not sure he could. He had been circling it for three days, the way you circle a thing in a dark room with your hands out, and he had felt the edge of it on the mounting block and again outside Placit's grate and again tonight on the third page of his own charge, before Ferra turned it face down; and he had not said it, because he had not known what it was until it was in his mouth.

"What if there weren't any," he said.

Ferra's pencil stopped.

"Any what?" said Imani.

"Lords."

The room was very quiet. Down in the square, a dog barked once at the paste boy coming back, and stopped.

"You're one," said Ferra.

"I know."

"So's your father," said Imani, to the ceiling.

"I know."

Ferra put her pencil down on her book. She did not close the book. She looked at him across the dark room, over Imani's hair, with her face quite still, the way she looked at a column she had added twice and got two answers.

"Then who keeps the roads?" she said.

"Whoever's best at roads."

"And who says who's best?"

"I don't know yet." He looked at the paper on the posting house door, pale in the lamplight. "Not the one whose grandfather was. Not the one with the charter." He was quiet. "People who can read it. To start with. You can't choose anybody, if you can't read what they've done."

Ferra looked at him a while longer. Then she picked up her pencil again.

"The census asked," she said. "In the four baronies. Reads, yes. Reads, name only. Reads, no. Three boxes on every sheet. Every soul." She turned a page in her book, a long way back, and found something, and looked at it. "Nobody's ever added that column. Nobody asked me to."

"Add it."

"Ottoline's machine at the census hall can run it in a day." She wrote something. "She's across the square. I'll ask her in the morning."

Imani had turned her head on the blanket and was looking at him; not at his face, he thought, but at something about how he was sitting, the way she looked at a patient who had told her he felt fine.

"You'll need a very long time," she said.

"Yes."

"Longer than you've got, maybe."

"Maybe."

She looked at him a moment more. Then she reached out along the bed without getting up, and found his hand where it hung over the arm of the chair, and pulled it, until he got up and came, and lay down on Sethe's bed in his clothes between the two of them, Imani's head on his shoulder and Ferra's feet against his legs and Ferra's book on his chest, because she had not stopped writing in it and would not.

"Not tonight, though," said Imani, with her eyes shut. "Tonight you lie still and I sleep. Tomorrow you can take the kingdom apart."

She was asleep before he could answer. Ferra was not. He lay in the dark and listened to her pencil go on, small and steady, on the page on his chest, until somewhere toward two it stopped, and her hand stayed on the book.



The town read the notice in the morning.

It read it the way he had known it would: one in ten, aloud, to the other nine. He stood at Sethe's window at seven and watched it happen at every door round the square, a man or a woman in front of the sheet with their finger moving along the line and a knot of people behind them, listening, very still; and at every door, when the reader got to the end of the one line in Ambry's biggest letters, the knot of people did the same thing, all at once. They looked up at the Staple on its hill.

At the posting house door an old woman with a basket stood a long while in front of the sheet without anybody to read it to her. She could not read it. He could see that from the window, by the way she stood. She looked at the first picture, the man and the seal and the black cross, for some time. Then the cow. Then the open door.

Then she put down her basket on the step, and reached up, and put her finger on the row of little strokes along the top of the last picture, over the sun and the five short lines; and counted them, one by one, moving her lips, all the way to the end.

She took her finger away. She picked up her basket. She stood there with it, looking at the sheet; and then she turned round and looked up at the window of Sethe's hall, straight at him, as if she had known all along exactly where he would be standing.

Twenty-six.
