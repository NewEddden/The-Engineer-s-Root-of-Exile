Chapter 43 - The Board

The account had gone north in Sethe's coat, and Emeric had spent four of the nights since standing on the east wall behind the church, doing arithmetic he did not enjoy.

It was the one stretch of the ring the keep could not see. Sixty yards of wall with the church at its back and the deepest water in the barony under it, where the branch had cut a channel down along the rock and had gone on cutting it for longer than the town had been a town. In daylight men fished off the ledge at the foot of it. At night there was nothing down there but the sound of water going past, which was the sound of a crowd two streets away.

He had come up to think about the next war and had found himself thinking about the next attempt, which was not the same thing.

He worked it the way Ward had made him work Renner, which was to assume the other man was not stupid and then follow where that went. Sethe had not been beaten. Sethe had been billed, in a yard, in front of his own officers, and had ridden home with the paper in his coat. A man who has been handed an invoice does not come back the following spring with two hundred men and walk them at a wall that ate the last two hundred. He comes back with something cheaper.

Which left everything that did not need an army.

A fire in the seed store on a dry night. A knife on the Left Bridge at the change of the watch, when the two files passed each other and both assumed the other had looked. A rope over the parapet in the hour before dawn, which was the hour Emeric himself had proved you could do things in, three years ago, with a cart and a bribed clerk. A boat under the east wall, here, below him, where the keep could not see and a man could stand on the ledge with his hands on the stone and not be looked at by anybody in Kaldo.

Every one of those would have worked that night. He stood there and checked it properly, which took some time, because checking properly meant trying to break his own answer and not being pleased when it held.

The watch was forty line men and four knights, and the watch walked. It walked well. It saw what a man with a lantern sees, which is thirty feet of dark and his own breath, and behind that a wall it could not see over and a river it could not see into. The island had been chosen a long time ago against columns and siege trains and the kind of enemy who announces himself by arriving. Against a man in a boat it was a very expensive fence.

So the next one would be quiet.

He went down off the wall and slept badly and got up and wrote the war for the crown.

It took most of two days and it went into ruled columns, because that was the only form in which he trusted himself to report anything to the capital. The field. The sixty-one, by name and division. The wounded, by division, with Imani's counts beside his own. The prisoners, the terms, the ransom, the settled account. He sealed it and it went out of the Western Gate in the ordinary packet on quarter day, with the tally sheets and the census correction, crown post, regular as rent.

Then he took a flat stone off his desk and turned it over in his fingers and started designing a fence that did not need a man standing at every foot of it.

He had written the shape of it two winters ago and had not built it. One stone that decides, and a hundred that do not. He had built half of it under the farm already, which was one of the reasons he trusted it.

The rule underneath was older and simpler and had come out of the same winter. You never build a thing that watches the world. There is no such stone and there never will be. What you build is a stone that is already doing something, and then you read how hard it is finding it. A node on the wall would hold one small steady push against the air in front of it, a working so shallow it barely deserved the name, and it would cost what it cost, and the cost would not change from one hour to the next. Put a body in front of it, something with weight in it and blood in it, and the push costs more. The stone strains. The strain is the message.

It did not look. That was the part he could never get anybody to hold on to afterwards, not Ward and not Ferra and certainly not the line men, who decided within a month that the wall could see and were never talked out of it. The sense rune did not have a direction the way an eye has a direction. It had a direction the way a held-out hand has one. It pushed out along a flattened cone from the face of the stone, wide as a doorway at ten feet, wide as a cart at thirty, and nothing at all past forty, and it reported what leaned on it. Behind the stone there was no cone and there never would be, which meant a node told you nothing whatever about the inside of your own wall, and Emeric spent an evening with a slate laying the arcs so that they overlapped by a yard at the near edge and left no daylight between them at the far one.

Then there was the harder half, which was getting the report from a stone in a wall to a room where a man was sitting.

What he wanted was a wire. He knew the shape of the thing he wanted the way you know a word you cannot produce: a thin continuous run of something that carried the report along itself, from there to here, without a man in the middle of it. He did not have one. There was no rubber in Randillia and no reason to think anybody in four hundred years had wanted any. He had copper, in sheet and in pot, and copper carried heat and would probably carry other things, and he spent one evening at the forge with a strip of it and a charged chip proving that a flat strip carried nothing at all. He thought a hollow tube might. A tube is a pipe, and a pipe is a shape the world has opinions about. But drawing tube wanted a drawplate and a die and a man who had made both before, and Kaldo had none of the three, and he was not going to have them this year.

He wrote copper, drawn, hollow at the bottom of a page under the heading of things to try when there was a drawplate, and stopped thinking about it, which took him two days.

What he did have was the wall.

The wall was rubble core faced with local quarry stone, coursed, raked and pointed, and it had been raked and pointed by somebody in every generation since it went up. That was the whole answer and it had been sitting in front of him the entire time. You do not need a pipe if you are willing to cut the channel into the thing itself.

So the line went in the joint. A groove cut into the bed joint along the course, half an inch deep and half an inch wide for the thick run that carried the push out from the reserve, and a scratch a third that size, shallow, for the thin one that carried the report back. Both bedded in mortar with charged stone ground into it, sand-fine, more grit than powder. Then pointed over the top by a man with a trowel. Done well it was a joint. Done badly it was also a joint, only uglier, and the barony wall had four centuries of both on it already.

The cover was free and he did not have to invent it. The wall had just had ladders on it. Masons were going round the whole ring anyway, raking out courses that had been shaken loose, and nobody in Kaldo was going to look twice at men repointing a wall that had been hit with a siege the same autumn. The masons did the raking. Emeric did the eight inches in the middle that mattered, at night, alone, and then the masons pointed over him in the morning and grumbled about the man who kept leaving a course half finished.

He tested the first forty feet behind the church and then made Bram stand in front of it in daylight and tell him what he saw.

"Pointing," Bram said.

"Come closer."

Bram came to two feet and said pointing done by somebody who cared, which was not a compliment. At six inches, with the sun on it at the right hour, he said there was something in the mortar catching like mica.

That was the answer and Emeric was satisfied with it. Under direct light, at six inches, by a man who already knew to look, it was a fleck. At rest it was nothing. In the dark it was nothing. At any distance a man in a boat would ever be from it, and at any distance a man on the far bank with a glass would ever be from it, it was a wall.

Nowhere in the whole of it did anything shine. That was not economy. A thing that glows tells anybody standing in front of it where it is, that it is working, what it is spending, and how many of it you were able to afford. Emeric had seen exactly one piece of crown work in his life that lit itself for no reason, in a hall in the capital, and the man who commissioned it had been trying to impress his own guests.

The gaps were the problem, and there is no wall in the world without gaps. A doorway. An arrow slit. The arch of the water gate. Four places on the north face where the facing had come off in the plague years and been replaced with something that was not the same stone and did not take a groove without crumbling. You cannot run a joint across an opening.

For those he used a pair of stones, one each side, cut with matched runes and set to face each other square, and the report jumped the gap without anything physical crossing it. Six feet was reliable. Nine was possible if the facing stones were true to each other and nobody had stacked firewood in between, which at the north postern somebody did, twice, before Ward put a mark on the flags and an opinion behind it.

The line men called them skips, which was as good a word as any. Emeric wrote the position of every one of the eleven in the book and underlined it, because a skip was the one part of the run that could be broken by a man who never touched the wall.

The nodes themselves went into slots that were already there. The outward face of the old wall had a cut seat in every third or fourth course, chest height and hand-sized, squared and finished, going all the way round the ring. Whoever raised the thing had expected to have mages in it. Nobody in living memory had put anything into one, and the seats had spent a couple of hundred years holding rainwater, bird mess and, in three cases, somebody's chalked initials.

Into each one went a dressed block cut to fit: the sense rune on the outward face, a reserve stone housed behind it where the weather could not reach, a release rune set to trickle so the reserve bled out slowly instead of all at once, and eight inches of short run back through the block into the backbone in the joint. Twelve of them. Each carried its own reserve because one source feeding twelve nodes is one chisel away from a dark ring, and Emeric had spent a war watching things fail at their narrowest point.

None of that was the part that could be seen. The part that could be seen was a board.

Bram made the case out of oak and did not ask what it was for until it was finished, at which point he asked, and Emeric told him it was a case for a map, and Bram said that he could see that.

Nevin cut the map. It came out of a single slab off the quarry road, a little under four feet across, and what he cut into it was Kaldo: the ring of the wall standing proud, the two branches sunk either side as grooves, the keep, the church, the two market squares, the stub of the Left Bridge going off the western edge and stopping where the slab stopped. The houses were blocks. The keep was a bigger block with four notches in the top of it. Nevin apologised for the houses on three separate occasions and Emeric told him each time that it did not have to be pretty, it had to be in the right place, and then made him move the church twice with a rule and a length of string until it was.

Then he cut the slivers. Twelve of them, hair-thin, seated flush into the top of the model wall at the exact point on the ring where its node sat, the same charge-and-glow work he had done in the dark for eleven years and could do without looking at his hands. He was pleased to be asked. He told Emeric twice that mine lamps were harder, because a mine lamp has to survive being dropped, and Emeric agreed with him twice.

The control runes went into the back face of the slab, in the thickness, where a man would have to take the board out of its case and turn it over to reach them. The deciding stone was one of Corbin's, out of the handful his brother had given him at the banquet as a joke, and it went behind the backing on a shelf nobody had a reason to look at. Bound, and slow to bind, and done alone at night the way the pump-house stone had been done. Once it was his, it held his work. It also meant that it was his, in the way a dog is somebody's, and would not answer anyone else. He knew that before he started. He wrote it down as a cost and went on.

At rest it was a grey stone town in an oak case and it said nothing about itself whatever. Ward stood in front of it the first night it worked and did not speak for some while, and then said it was the first map he had ever been given that was not out of date.

That was the whole of the difference and it took Emeric some time to understand that it was the part other people cared about. Nobody had to be told which stretch was which. Nobody had to hold twelve numbers in his head at the third bell with his boots off. The light came up on the model exactly where the man was standing on the real wall, and a line man who had never read a word in his life could put his finger on it and say there, and be right.

The numbers were for Ferra's book. They walked the ring to set them on a grey afternoon with the wind coming off the lake: Emeric, Ward and Rylan, with chalk and a slate and a bag of blanks. Twelve runs, counted sunwise from the Western Gate, because a column wants a number in it. One was the gate itself and the ground in front of it. Four was the west face over the shallow water where the reeds came in. Nine was the blind sixty yards behind the church.

The stair was in the middle of nine. It went down through the thickness of the wall in a tight flight, wide enough for one man who did not mind his shoulders, and ended at a barred water gate and the boatman's ledge below, where nobody had tied up since the port silted and the trade went to the coast. The bar lifted from the inside.

Ward lifted it, looked at the hinges, set it back and pushed it down on its pegs.

"Sound," he said. "One at a time, and he arrives wet, and I have two men at the top of the stair who heard him coming up it. It is the least of my problems."

"It is the only door in the wall that opens onto water."

"It is a door onto a ledge. There is nowhere to go from the ledge except into the branch or up the stair."

"Then it costs us one stone," Emeric said, and set the ninth node in the inner arch at eye height, where its cone would lie out across the ledge and the water under it, and where mending it would mean going down the stair with a lantern.

Rylan had his hand flat on the wall by the gate the whole time, the way a man touches a house he grew up two streets from.

"Boys used to go off that ledge," he said. "Before the bar. Straight down and out, because it is deep as anything under there, and you come up twenty yards along if the water is moving."

"You did it."

"Once." Rylan set the lantern down. "My mother found out."

The building of it was a small job, which was the best thing about it. The ends of the system were free: river stones the size of an egg, and a boy with a basket, paid a half-day for a full one because Ferra had views about children on payroll. Nevin's work was done in a fortnight. The case was oak out of the yard. The mortar was mortar.

What it cost came out of Emeric, at night, one node and one skip at a time, and it made him poor company for eight days. Bram asked twice whether he was ill. Ferra did not ask and simply stopped putting anything in front of him after the second bell, which was worse.

The spares went to Vesk, along with the rest of the inventory that wanted charging, because that was work the terms allowed and work he did well. He charged them. He also held one of them up to the window in the mage house for rather longer than charging needs, turning it in the light, looking for the tool marks that were not on it. He said nothing about it. Emeric watched him do it from the doorway, and said nothing about it either, and made a note in his own head that the count of people who had seen his work and drawn no conclusion out loud was now one higher than he liked.

The swap schedule he set off his own capacity figure, the number he had derived in a cellar full of another man's crystals in his second winter and had never yet had a reason to doubt. Twenty-six days to a node, and he rounded it to three weeks.

They tested it over four nights, and the tests were the best part of the whole autumn.

A heron came down on the parapet at seven and lit nothing, which was correct, and mildly insulting. A dog crossing the gate ground lit one, which was correct and not useful, and became an argument about where the cone ought to sit that Ward won by pointing out that a dog on the gate ground at two in the morning is worth a man looking anyway.

On the second night they found out that the board counted.

Emeric had meant to test it properly and Declan got there first by accident, marching eight men out along the gate ground to put them somewhere while a cart turned. One went from a thread of light no longer than a fingernail paring to a bar of it half the length of the model run, bright enough to put a shine on the oak. The man in the chair got up out of the chair.

They spent the rest of that night and most of the next finding out how far it could be trusted, which was not very far and was still worth having. One man was a thread. Two or three thickened it. Six or more and the thing lit up like a seam. It could not tell you eight from eleven and it never would, and Emeric said so out loud several times because he could feel the line men deciding otherwise. What it could tell you was the only question that mattered at the third bell, which was whether to send a runner or wake a garrison.

Speed came off the board rather than out of any stone, and it came off it because Ferra had made him write the distances on the inside of the case lid. A node to the next node was a known number of yards. A light going out at one and coming up at two was a man who had covered that number of yards in the time between. All the gatehouse needed was the time, and the gatehouse had no clock, so the gatehouse counted.

Declan drilled them on it the way he drilled everything, which was relentlessly and without any apparent interest in whether they enjoyed it. A man walking the outside of the wall, sixty counts between lights. A man at a fast walk, forty. A man running in the dark over broken ground, a little under thirty, and not for long, because nobody runs the whole ring. A boat on the branch going down with the water, twenty or under, and there is nothing on land that does twenty.

So the card would end up saying, in the end, what the chair was actually for. Which stretch. How much of it. How fast. Anybody could learn to read the first. The second took a week. The third took a man who could count steadily while something was happening, and Declan turned out to be the only one of them who had assumed that was a skill and taught it accordingly.

Rylan swam the east branch at four on the third night, in water cold enough to make a case against the whole idea, so they could see what a man in the river looked like from inside the gatehouse. Four lit, thin, and then held, and then the thread slid four inches along the model wall and went out and five came up, and the count between them was twenty-two. He came out swearing steadily and without much invention, and stood by the brazier with a blanket round him and asked what it had looked like.

"You looked like a boat," Emeric said.

"That is worse than a lamp."

An otter lit nine twice in one night. The runner went down the stair with a lantern both times and came back up the second time and reported, formally, to the prince of the blood, that it was the same otter.

Between tests they sat in the gatehouse with the brazier and talked, and Rylan talked about home for the first time since the ward.

Neither of them said anything about why that was new. Rylan had brought him food at the eleventh hour of a very bad day and had told him to sit down, and that had been the end of a cold year, and the two of them had agreed without a word ever being spoken that it would stay unmentioned.

"Finn coughed from the first frost to the thaw," Rylan said. "The whole winter. You hear it through the wall. Imani told Nessa there is nothing in a tea that beats dry air, and that if we can put him somewhere off the water for a month when it turns, we should."

"Can you?"

"Her sister is out at Milford. Una. Married a drover and gets herself no lake and no fog and cattle in every direction." He turned his cup round on the bench. "Nessa wants him out there when it goes cold. He will hate it for four days and then he will be shovelling for the man and not want to come home."

Nine lit again at some point after that, and Rylan went and looked, and came back and said otter, and sat down, and it was then that he said the thing Emeric wrote down.

"It is a good machine, my lord. I mean that." He put his hands out to the brazier. "But the wall can light every lamp you like. Somebody still has to get out of the chair and go."

Emeric turned that over and found that it was not a complaint about the machine. It was a staffing problem, correctly stated by a man who had done eleven years of nights.

"So what do you do about it?"

"Two runners on the shift," Rylan said. "And the man in the chair never goes himself. Not once, not for a quick look, not if it is the seventh otter that night. Because the minute he goes, there is nobody reading the board, and then you have paid for a board so that nobody can watch it."

It went on the roster that night in those words, more or less, and within a month the line men were calling it Rylan's rule, and using it as a reason not to move, which Ward said was the natural life cycle of any rule ever written.

The one thing on the whole ring that was not hidden went up above the Western Gate in the last week, and Ward liked it better than everything else put together.

It was a dressed-stone housing bracketed to the arch over the gate ground, visible from the bridge, with two apertures cut through the face of it and a linkage down the inside of the jamb that anybody could see was a linkage. Emeric had not tried to conceal any of it. There is no sense hiding a thing whose entire purpose is to be the loudest object in the barony for four seconds.

Inside it were three parts and they all worked the same way, which was badly, once, and all at the same time. A sound crystal with its release set to dump instead of trickle, through the first aperture. A light crystal, the same, through the second. And a kinetic rune on a hand-tooled linkage with a gravity crystal in the assist bracket, which did nothing clever at all: it dropped the outer bar across the gate and drove two pins home, in about the time it takes to say so.

Ward went up and looked at the linkage for a long while, because the linkage was the part he could understand without being told anything, and then asked the question.

"What does it do to a man standing under it?"

"It does not kill him. It takes his ears and his eyes for about as long as it takes two men to come down off the wall head, and it shuts the gate in front of him while he is deciding what happened."

"And it fires how?"

"A man pulls it."

Ward turned round. "Not the board?"

"Never the board." He had expected the question and had the answer ready and did not enjoy giving it, because he knew what came next. "The board reports. It does not decide. A machine that hurts people on the word of another machine will one day do it to an otter, and then to a fisherman, and then to a boy who went off the ledge on a dare, and after the boy nobody in this town will let me build anything again, and they will be right."

So it ran off its own reserve and its own linkage and had nothing behind the backing of any board at all, and that made it the one piece of the whole system that did not care in the slightest whether Emeric was alive. Ward understood that inside about two seconds and said nothing about it and was visibly pleased for the rest of the afternoon.

The cost was that it was one shot.

It emptied both crystals in a single go and wanted the better part of a week in the mage house afterwards, and the linkage had to be reset by hand by a man with a bar, on a ladder, in whatever weather there was. Which meant the question of the second one.

Ward wanted it at nine. He wanted it in the arch of the water gate, facing the ledge, and he made the argument on the wall head with his arms folded and made it well, which was that it was the only door in the ring that opened on water, and that the countermeasure and the board had exactly the same blind spot, which was a man who had already got in.

Emeric priced it. Two crystals of that size were not in the barony, and the pair that were, were in the mage house doing something else. The linkage wanted Bram for nine days at a time of year when Bram was owed to the Works. And he could not justify it on the case, because the case was that men came up the branch at night in a boat, which had not happened in living memory, against a stair that held one man at a time and came up into a gatehouse.

He wrote it in the book, under the heading of things wanted and not bought, in his own hand, with the figure beside it. Then he went on to the next sheet.

The walk did not change. Emeric was careful about that and wrote the reason in the book so that a later version of himself would have to argue with it. Nothing he built was allowed to become the reason a man stopped doing the thing the building was supposed to help with. The board could tell the watch where to look. The board could never be why nobody was looking. If the stone behind the backing died in the night, the watch was to be exactly what it had been the month before, and nobody outside the gatehouse needed to know the difference.

Ferra came to the gatehouse on the fifth morning with her book under her arm and a look he had learned to recognise.

"It works," she said. "So now it gets a procedure and it gets a column, because a thing that wakes people at night and is not written down is a rumour with a lamp on it."

They wrote the card standing up, which took an hour, most of it spent on eleven words.

Dark and stays dark: the node is starved. Take a charged one from the box, swap it, log it.

Flickers: a true fault. Do not touch it. Wake the prince, wherever he is. Only he can mend it, at the stone.

Two or more dark together, side by side: that is not the stones. That is the wall. Send two men and a lantern to the joint between the last lit and the first dark.

The third line was his and he added it two days later, alone, and did not explain it to anybody. A run in a joint goes dark from the break outward. Everything downstream of a cut goes at once, and where it stops going is where the chisel was. It was the one failure in the whole design that told you something useful about itself, and it was also the only place in the system where a man with a cold chisel and an hour could take four stretches of the ring off the board without ever going near a stone Emeric had bound.

"You are certain about the second line," Ferra said.

"It is not pride."

"I did not say it was."

He did not explain the rest of it to her, because the rest of it was the trove and the trove was his, but the shape was simple enough. A stone had to be learned by hand before it could be spoken to at a distance. A stone that had gone wrong had to be learned again, and there was one pair of hands in the barony that could do the learning.

She hung the card herself, level, with two nails.

Then she ruled three new columns into the back of her book. The first was the swap log: date, stretch, and the hand that did it. The second was the lamp log: what lit, and what the runner found when he got there. The third was the count, because she had listened to Declan for one evening and had come away with the view that a number nobody writes down is a number nobody has.

"Not the otters," Emeric said.

"The otters especially."

"It is an otter."

"It is an otter on the east run, and in a year you will want to know how often the east run lights for nothing, and neither of us will remember, and you will guess, and then you will build something off your guess." She did not look up. "The otters go in."

The otters went in.

Nevin called them slivers, which was what they were, and nobody else ever did. The line men called them lamps from the first week, because a light in a wall is a lamp and because nobody was going to say sliver to another man at three in the morning, and the word that went into the roster and the log and the card and eventually into Ferra's ledger headings was lamps.

Rylan read the card twice that afternoon, the second time with one finger under the line, and made no remark about that, and Emeric made none either. The men who sat the chair and could not yet read it had it off by heart before they were allowed to sit, which took an evening each and was Declan's idea.

What Emeric wanted, and had wanted since the case went up, was to hand the thing to Ward.

It was obvious. Ward ran the watch. The board was the watch's eyes. The man who commanded at the gate should not have to send a boy up the hill to wake the baron because a lamp was doing something a card had three words for.

He tried it the only way that was open to him, on a night when the gatehouse was empty and the door was barred, with the grey stone in his hand and Ward's arm under his fingers.

Inside, the ring was not a board. It was the island seen from somewhere above it with all twelve cones laid out like still water, and every node a weight sitting in its own patch of the world, and the runs between them drawn in the joints like veins on the back of a hand, and Ward, who had been in there twice before and had not asked to go back either time, stood in it and looked at the whole thing for a while without saying anything.

Then he worked it. He put his attention on nine and the cone came up under it. He went down the ring, one to twelve, then back. He found stretch six, where the cone ran out four feet short of the corner, and said so, and he was right, and Emeric fixed it in the morning.

A quarter of an hour, perhaps. Then Emeric let the delve go, and they were two men standing in a cold gatehouse, and Ward had his hand flat on a slab of grey stone in an oak case that was not doing anything at all for him and never would.

Ward looked at his own hand for a moment.

"So it is you," he said.

"It is me."

"And when you are on the coast for ten days?"

"The watch walks, and anything that flickers waits."

Ward did not argue with it. He went to the door, and stopped there, and said the thing he had come to the door in order to be able to say without looking at anybody. "I have four men who can take my place inside an hour. That is not a compliment to them. It is the only reason I sleep."

Ferra said it more plainly the next day, and she had said it before, in the pump-house, standing over a bound stone nobody else had seen.

"This is the main again."

"It is."

"Then I will say it once, and then I will put it in the column and stop." She closed the book on her finger. "If it only works while you are alive and in the room, then the thing is not a machine. The thing is you. And I cannot put you in an inventory, I cannot buy a second one when the first is on the coast, and I cannot mend you."

He spent three days trying to prove her wrong.

He did it quietly and only among people who already knew some version of what he could do, because the alternative was widening the circle, and the circle was the one number in the barony he refused to let grow by accident.

Bram sat with the grey stone in his palm for an evening at the forge, patient as a post, and at the end of it it was a stone and he was a tired man who wanted his supper. Ferra held it for a quarter of an hour, and it stayed cold, and she gave it back and said, without any weight, that she had not expected anything and was still disappointed. Ward would not take it at all, and then took it, once, for about as long as it takes to check a coin, and handed it back and went out.

He went through everything Armand had ever taught him about stones, in his memory, line by line, looking for one sentence about a room inside one, and there was nothing, because Armand had never been in one. He considered Vesk for about a minute and a half. Then he stopped considering him.

On the third night he wrote it in the book under the roster and the fault card and the swap schedule, in the same hand as everything else.

Second key: none. Deferred.

He read it back. The farm ran on him. Now the ring ran on him too, and he had known that when he ordered the oak. Nobody had made him build it and nobody had made him build it this way, and the honest version of the entry was that he had been given the same problem twice and had answered it the same way twice, and the second answer had been quicker than the first.

He did not cross it out and he did not feel better about writing it down.

What he did instead, the following morning, was build something stupid.

It cost nine shillings and most of it was cord. Waxed line strung at ankle height across the gate ground and across the reed shallows on the west face, pegged low, run back through a drilled joint to a weighted clapper hung in the stair head where the man on the wall walk would hear it and nobody in the town would. Pull the line and the weight falls and the clapper goes. That was the whole of the machine. A child could have built it and a child could mend it, and that was the point of it.

It was worse than the board in every way he could list, and he listed them in the book because he did not want a later version of himself pretending otherwise. It told you that something had happened and nothing else. It could not count, it could not time, it could not say which end of the shallows. It rang for cattle. It rang for wind if the pegs were slack. It could be found by a man on his hands and knees in the dark and cut in a second, and a man who came twice would find it the second time. It wanted walking every third day and restringing after every hard frost.

He kept it anyway, because a bad thing that works when he is dead is worth more than a good thing that does not.

He could not put one at nine and he wrote down why, in the same entry, in the same flat hand. There is no ground to peg to on a ledge the branch comes over four times a winter. A cord under water is a cord that rings all night and is therefore a cord nobody answers by February. He looked at the problem from three directions over two evenings and did not solve it.

So the blind sixty yards behind the church had a node in the arch, no countermeasure in the housing he had not built, and no cord. It had the stair, and the bar, and the watch, and one lamp on a stone map in a room up at the western end of the town.

The board went on the roster proper at the end of the week. A line man in the chair, Rylan and a second runner on the shift, the card on the wall at eye height, the distances on the inside of the case lid, the spare box under it with eleven charged nodes in straw.

At some point after the third bell, nine lit.

The man in the chair counted, out loud, the way Declan had made him, and got to sixty and past it and nothing else came up, and he sent the runner and stayed where he was. Rylan went with the runner because there was nothing else to do, and the pair of them came back up out of the stair with their lantern swinging and the grin of men who have been outside in the cold and are about to be inside and warm. Rylan reported it at the door, formally, the way he had reported the first one.

"Otter," he said.

Ferra's log took its second line of the season in the man's own hand. Ward stood behind the chair for the better part of an hour afterwards, watching the board instead of the wall, saying nothing, with his arms folded. Then he went to bed.

Twelve lamps, a card with eleven words on it that mattered, nine shillings of cord that could not be strung where it was wanted, a ring of stones in a wall that answered one man, and, in the book below the board, in the same hand as the roster, the word he had written instead of a second key.
