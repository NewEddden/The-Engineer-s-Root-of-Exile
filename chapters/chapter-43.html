Chapter 43 - Service

The first service payment on the Ducatis loan fell due on the thirty-first of October and was made on the thirty-first of October, in the long room, at nine in the morning, and it took about four minutes.

Ferra read the figure. Vantic wrote it, and countersigned, and put her copy in the case, and Emeric signed the receipt, and that was the whole of it. There was no ceremony because Ferra had told him a fortnight earlier that there was not going to be one.

"It is not an achievement," she had said. "It is a Tuesday. If you make it an occasion you will be making an occasion of the fact that we are still in debt, and I would rather this room got used to paying and stopped noticing it."

What he noticed instead was that Vantic did not clear her throat.

She had done it every single time she spoke in that room since the nineteenth of October a year ago. Emeric had stopped hearing it somewhere around the middle of last December, in the way a man stops hearing a mill, and had heard it again this morning by its absence, which was a much louder sound. She said the number and the date and the covenant reference straight out, flat, from the top of the sheet.

He did not say anything about it. He thought about saying something about it for most of the morning and did not, and put it in the list that night instead, and was fairly sure that was the right call and entirely sure he would never know.

Ferra put the ore line in front of him on the fourth of November because he had asked to see the whole of it in one place, both sides, with nothing rounded.

"Duvrenne, last winter," she said. "Twice the price at the far end. Four times the carriage. Paid before it moved, so we carried the money out for eleven days and the ore for eleven days and owned a quantity of stone that was on a boat in December, which cost us an insurer, which cost us again. Say a load landed on our own quay at rather over four times what the stone in it was worth on a Duvrenne wharf, and I am being generous to Duvrenne."

"And the river."

"Four men, a rope, an hour, and the current does the work for nothing because water goes downhill whether we pay it or not." She turned the sheet. "Carriage on a Grayrock load, landed, against the same weight of stone: not nothing, because men and ropes and hours are not nothing. Near enough to nothing that I have had to change the column width."

"Say it as a fraction and then say what takes it back."

"As a fraction it is the difference between a thing you ration and a thing you use." Ferra did not enjoy saying it, which was how he knew it was large. "What takes it back is the cart road. Everything we send up to Grayrock so that four factors at a junction cannot starve a hill we now depend on. That is a fifth off the advantage in a normal year and I still will not guess at February. And the Duvrenne standing load, which is on its own line under the wells, at four times the carriage, forever, which you asked for and which I costed and which I have not changed my opinion about."

"And after all three of those."

Ferra put the last sheet down and did not slide it across, which was how she delivered a thing she thought would be misread.

He read it twice.

"Say it out loud," he said.

"The line runs the other way." She said it evenly and then had to stop for a second, which he had seen her do exactly twice in fourteen months. "I have kept the books of this barony for eleven years and I have written that number in red in every one of them. The whole of my professional life in this room has been the arithmetic of how long a thing can go on being smaller. I have a page in front of me that services a debt out of income, feeds two thousand three hundred and forty people, buys its own stone, carts a supply up a hill for a counterparty out of ordinary prudence, and comes out on the right side."

"That's the first time you've said any of that without the word yet on the end of it."

"Yet," Ferra said. "It is November and the number is a season old and the coalition has gone harder and there is a man at a junction counting our carts, and I will have the word yet on the end of every sentence in this book until I am dead. But the line runs the other way, and I am going to be pleased about that until about Thursday."

Imani came up on the seventh with a list, and the list was shorter than the one she had brought in October a year ago, and that was the thing that made him angry.

"You've cut it."

"I have cut it to what can be had," Imani said. "You will find that is not the same as cutting it."

She went through it in order because she had learned in this room that order was how you got heard. Forty beds where there were nine, which was frames and ticking and rope and could be bought in a market. Linen in quantity, and a second copper for boiling it, and a stove in the north end of the nave, which was the coldest two hundred feet of stone in the barony. Two women trained properly and paid out of the barony's four parts and not out of gratitude, and a third the year after. Stores held against a bad season instead of bought during one.

"All of that is permitted," she said. "I asked Master Vantic before I asked you, which I hope you will not take badly."

"I'd take it badly if you hadn't."

"Good. Then here is the one that is not permitted, and I am going to say it once and not argue about it, because I have watched you argue with a covenant twice and it is not a productive use of a doctor." She put the second sheet down. "A roof. Ours. Forty by ninety, stone footings, two wards and a room with a door that shuts, sited where the water is clean and downhill of nothing. Not the nave."

"Doran has never once complained about the nave."

"Doran will never once complain about the nave. That is precisely the difficulty." Imani did not raise her voice. "In August, that floor had two hundred and twelve men on it and nine of them were on beds and the rest were on straw, and Doran stacked his own pews against the wall the day the levy was called without telling anybody, and I will be grateful to that man until I die. And in February he will need the nave, because in February people die in this barony the way they always have, and the dead have to go somewhere, and I will be standing in the porch deciding which of those two things the building is doing that week. That is not a complaint about a priest. It is the arithmetic of one room."

"How many does it cost you? Honestly."

"I do not know and I will not invent a number to move you," she said. "It costs me the ones who do not come, because they have heard the nave is full, and I never see those and neither do you and neither does the ledger."

He took it at Vantic in writing on the ninth, which was the proper way, and she answered it on the eleventh in the long room, and he had known by the second sentence of her answer.

"No," Vantic said.

"Give me the reading. You found one in November for the works."

"I found one in November because there was one. That is the entire difference and I am going to spend five minutes on it because you have earned five minutes and because I do not want you asking me a third time in March." She had the covenant open and did not look at it. "In November you asked to put things inside a building you already had the use of. Repair, replace, maintain. My house has written those three words for ninety years and there is a shelf of opinions behind them, and I wrote to my house with a reading that any factor in this kingdom would recognise and would have written himself, and I signed my name under it and my name was not doing much work."

"And this."

"This is a building that does not exist, on ground that is not built on, for a purpose the barony does not currently carry out under its own roof." Vantic closed the book. "There is no reading. There is only a sentence I could construct, and I could construct one, I am reasonably good at this. And a reading I can defend and a sentence I can sell are two different objects, and the second one is worth exactly as much as my house's willingness to be sold to, which is a thing I would be spending rather than a thing I would be using."

"Then spend it."

"On what." She said it without any edge. "Say the sentence to me and mean it. On a building. In the second November of a loan with four and a half years to run, in a barony that has been at war once and has just made an exclusive supply arrangement with the barony that attacked it, and whose factor writes home every quarter and is believed."

"On forty beds under a roof that is ours, so that a woman who has already saved more of this town than I have does not spend February choosing between the wounded and the dead."

"That is a good sentence," Vantic said, "and it is the truest thing anyone has said to me in this room, and it will not survive the journey. It arrives in a counting house in the capital as: the borrower wishes to commence a building. That is all of it that gets there. And what I have to spend to make it arrive as anything else is my own credit, and I have a finite amount of that, and I have spent one of it already, and the next time you need it, you are not going to need it for a roof."

"How do you know what I'm going to need it for?"

"I do not," she said. "I know that in fourteen months every serious thing that has happened to this barony has arrived from outside it and without notice, and I know what my house's answer costs, and I know that a factor who asks twice in a year is a factor who is being managed by her borrower. I would rather be a person who says no to you in November than a person whose yes has stopped meaning anything by the time it matters."

He sat with that.

"I'm going to argue with you about this," he said, "and I want to say first that I think you're right, so that when I lose I'm not pretending I was surprised."

"Then do not argue," Vantic said. "That is not a rule of my house, it is my opinion, and I will give you my opinion once because you have not asked for it in fourteen months. You will win nothing today and you will spend two weeks of your own attention on it. The covenant does not care about your reasons. It is not a man. There is no version of this where you find the right words."

"Then what is there a version of?"

"There is the balance," she said, and cleared her throat, and it was the first time in a fortnight, and Emeric heard it very clearly indeed. "The covenant is not a judgment on you and it is not a punishment. It is a clause. It lapses when the balance comes current. That is the whole of the mechanism and there is nothing else in it."

He asked Ferra the question that afternoon and she had the answer ready, which meant she had done it before he asked, which meant she had done it some time ago.

"At this year's surplus, with the ore line as it now stands and no new revenue, and servicing on the current schedule." Ferra had the sheet turned face down and turned it over. "Seven years. Call it six if there are two good harvests and nothing goes wrong, and I have not seen two good harvests and nothing going wrong in eleven years of keeping this book."

"Seven."

"Seven. And in the seventh year Imani will be forty-one and the nave will have been the ward for seven Februaries." She put the sheet down. "You wanted the number. That is the number and I am not going to soften it, and I will tell you the other half, which is that it does not come down by being careful. Careful got us to the right side of the line this month. It will not get us to the end of a loan in less than seven years. Only a great deal more revenue does that, and there is not a great deal more revenue in two thousand acres and a hill."

He started the list that night and it took him three evenings, and he wrote it in the back of the book on its own pages, ruled, dated, with a column at the right for the reason it was not being done.

Imani's roof was at the top of it, and he wrote the seventh of November against it, and then he wrote the second line, which was the second wheel, and the third, which was the kilns, and the fourth, which was a proper school because Ferra had said the word twice in a year and had not asked, and the fifth and sixth and ninth, and he stopped at fourteen because the page ended and because he had understood something around the eleventh that he wrote down at the bottom in a different hand.

Paid on the thirty-first out of income and not out of anything else, and it took four minutes and Ferra would not let it be an occasion and was right. Vantic did not clear her throat. Fourteen months of it and it stopped this morning and I have decided not to mention it, on the grounds that a man who names a thing like that has just made it a thing that can be taken away again.

The line runs the other way. Ferra said it out loud and had to stop in the middle, and I am going to remember the stopping for longer than the number.

Asked for the roof. Told no, correctly, by someone who could have found me a sentence and would not, and who told me to my face that she was keeping her credit for a thing I have not met yet. I do not have a counter-argument and I have written that here rather than keeping it in my head where it would improve overnight.

Seven years. Six if nothing goes wrong. Imani spends every February of those seven in a borrowed nave deciding what the building is for that week, and she will not complain, and she cut her own list before she brought it to me so that I would not have to say no to the whole of it. That is what the covenant costs, and it does not cost me anything at all, which is the part I keep walking away from.

Fourteen lines on the deferred page and every one of them is waiting on the same clause. I have been treating that clause for a year as a nuisance in a contract, something to be got round with a clever reading twice a season. It is not a nuisance. It is the ceiling of everything this place can be, it comes down in seven years or it comes down when the revenue changes, and Ferra says the revenue does not change out of two thousand acres and a hill.

So I am not arguing with a covenant any more. I am waiting one out, and I have written the date at the top of the page, and I would like it noted that I do not yet know what I am waiting for.
