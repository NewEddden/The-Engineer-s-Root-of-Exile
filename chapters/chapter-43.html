Chapter 43 - The Board

The account had gone north in Sethe's coat, and Emeric had spent four of the nights since standing on the east wall behind the church, doing arithmetic he did not enjoy.

It was the one stretch of the ring the keep could not see. Sixty yards of wall with the church at its back and the deepest water in the barony under it, where the branch had cut a channel down along the rock and had gone on cutting it for longer than the town had been a town. In daylight men fished off the ledge at the foot of it. At night there was nothing down there but the sound of water going past, which was the sound of a crowd two streets away.

He had come up to think about the next war and had found himself thinking about the next attempt, which was not the same thing.

He worked it the way Ward had made him work Renner, which was to assume the other man was not stupid and then follow where that went. Sethe had not been beaten. Sethe had been billed, in a yard, in front of his own officers, and had ridden home with the paper in his coat. A man who has been handed an invoice does not come back the following spring with two hundred men and walk them at a wall that ate the last two hundred. He comes back with something cheaper.

Which left everything that did not need an army.

A fire in the seed store on a dry night. A knife on the Left Bridge at the change of the watch, when the two files passed each other and both assumed the other had looked. A rope over the parapet in the hour before dawn, which was the hour Emeric himself had proved you could do things in, three years ago, with a cart and a bribed clerk. A boat under the east wall, here, below him, where the keep could not see and a man could stand on the ledge with his hands on the stone and not be looked at by anybody in Kaldo.

Every one of those would have worked that night. He stood there and checked it properly, which took some time, because checking properly meant trying to break his own answer and not being pleased when it held.

The watch was forty line men and four knights, and the watch walked. It walked well. It saw what a man with a lantern sees, which is thirty feet of dark and his own breath, and behind that a wall it could not see over and a river it could not see into. The island had been chosen a long time ago against columns and siege trains and the kind of enemy who announces himself by arriving. Against a man in a boat it was a very expensive fence.

So the next one would be quiet.

He went down off the wall and slept badly and got up and wrote the war for the crown.

It took most of two days and it went into ruled columns, because that was the only form in which he trusted himself to report anything to the capital. The field. The sixty-one, by name and division. The wounded, by division, with Imani's counts beside his own. The prisoners, the terms, the ransom, the settled account. He sealed it and it went out of the Western Gate in the ordinary packet on quarter day, with the tally sheets and the census correction, crown post, regular as rent.

Then he took a flat stone off his desk and turned it over in his fingers and started designing a fence that did not need a man standing at every foot of it.

He had written the shape of it two winters ago and had not built it. One stone that decides, and a hundred that do not. He had built half of it under the farm already, which was one of the reasons he trusted it.

The rule underneath was older and simpler and had come out of the same winter. You never build a thing that watches the world. There is no such stone and there never will be. What you build is a stone that is already doing something, and then you read how hard it is finding it. A node on the wall would hold one small steady push against the air in front of it, a working so shallow it barely deserved the name, and it would cost what it cost, and the cost would not change from one hour to the next. Put a body in front of it, something with weight in it and blood in it, and the push costs more. The stone strains. The strain is the message.

None of that was the part that could be seen. The part that could be seen was a board.

Bram made the frame out of oak and did not ask what it was for until it was finished, at which point he asked, and Emeric told him it was a board for lamps, and Bram said that he could see that.

Nevin cut the lamps. Twelve of them, set into a painted ring of the island, one to each run of wall, the same charge-and-glow work he had cut in the dark for eleven years and could do without looking at his hands. He was pleased to be asked. He told Emeric twice that mine lamps were harder, because a mine lamp has to survive being dropped, and Emeric agreed with him twice.

The deciding stone was one of Corbin's, out of the handful his brother had given him at the banquet as a joke, and it went behind the backing of the board on a shelf nobody had a reason to look at. Bound, and slow to bind, and done alone at night the way the pump-house stone had been done. Once it was his, it held his work. It also meant that it was his, in the way a dog is somebody's, and would not answer anyone else. He knew that before he started. He wrote it down as a cost and went on.

They walked the ring to number it on a grey afternoon with the wind coming off the lake: Emeric, Ward and Rylan, with chalk and a slate and a bag of blanks.

Twelve runs, counted sunwise from the Western Gate, because that was where the board was going and a man reads outward from where he stands. One was the gate itself and the ground in front of it. Four was the west face over the shallow water where the reeds came in. Nine was the blind sixty yards behind the church.

The stair was in the middle of it. It went down through the thickness of the wall in a tight flight, wide enough for one man who did not mind his shoulders, and ended at a barred water gate and the boatman's ledge below, where nobody had tied up since the port silted and the trade went to the coast. The bar lifted from the inside.

Ward lifted it, looked at the hinges, set it back and pushed it down on its pegs.

"Sound," he said. "One at a time, and he arrives wet, and I have two men at the top of the stair who heard him coming up it. It is the least of my problems."

"It is the only door in the wall that opens onto water."

"It is a door onto a ledge. There is nowhere to go from the ledge except into the branch or up the stair."

"Then it costs us one stone," Emeric said, and set the ninth node in the inner arch, where its field would lie across the ledge and the water under it, and where mending it would mean going down the stair with a lantern.

Rylan had his hand flat on the wall by the gate the whole time, the way a man touches a house he grew up two streets from.

"Boys used to go off that ledge," he said. "Before the bar. Straight down and out, because it is deep as anything under there, and you come up twenty yards along if the water is moving."

"You did it."

"Once." Rylan set the lantern down. "My mother found out."

The building of it was a small job, which was the best thing about it. The ends of the system were free: river stones the size of an egg, and a boy with a basket, paid a half-day for a full one because Ferra had views about children on payroll. Nevin's lamps were cut in a week. The frame was oak out of the yard.

What it cost came out of Emeric, at night, one node at a time, and it made him poor company for eight days. Bram asked twice whether he was ill. Ferra did not ask and simply stopped putting anything in front of him after the second bell, which was worse.

The spares went to Vesk, along with the rest of the inventory that wanted charging, because that was work the terms allowed and work he did well. He charged them. He also held one of them up to the window in the mage house for rather longer than charging needs, turning it in the light, looking for the tool marks that were not on it. He said nothing about it. Emeric watched him do it from the doorway, and said nothing about it either, and made a note in his own head that the count of people who had seen his work and drawn no conclusion out loud was now one higher than he liked.

The swap schedule he set off his own capacity figure, the number he had derived in a cellar full of another man's crystals in his second winter and had never yet had a reason to doubt. Twenty-six days to a node, and he rounded it to three weeks.

They tested it over four nights, and the tests were the best part of the whole autumn.

A heron came down on the parapet at stretch seven and lit nothing, which was correct, and mildly insulting. A dog crossing the gate ground lit one, which was correct and not useful, and became an argument about where the field ought to sit that Ward won by pointing out that a dog on the gate ground at two in the morning is worth a man looking anyway.

Rylan swam the east branch at four, in water cold enough to make a case against the whole idea, so they could see what a man in the river looked like from inside the gatehouse. Four lit. He came out swearing steadily and without much invention, and stood by the brazier with a blanket round him and asked what it had looked like, and Emeric said it had looked like a lamp, and Rylan said that was a great deal of trouble for a lamp.

An otter lit nine twice in one night. The runner went down the stair with a lantern both times and came back up the second time and reported, formally, to the prince of the blood, that it was the same otter.

Between tests they sat in the gatehouse with the brazier and talked, and Rylan talked about home for the first time since the ward.

Neither of them said anything about why that was new. Rylan had brought him food at the eleventh hour of a very bad day and had told him to sit down, and that had been the end of a cold year, and the two of them had agreed without a word ever being spoken that it would stay unmentioned.

"Finn coughed from the first frost to the thaw," Rylan said. "The whole winter. You hear it through the wall. Imani told Nessa there is nothing in a tea that beats dry air, and that if we can put him somewhere off the water for a month when it turns, we should."

"Can you?"

"Her sister is out at Milford. Una. Married a drover and gets herself no lake and no fog and cattle in every direction." He turned his cup round on the bench. "Nessa wants him out there when it goes cold. He will hate it for four days and then he will be shovelling for the man and not want to come home."

Nine lit again at some point after that, and Rylan went and looked, and came back and said otter, and sat down, and it was then that he said the thing Emeric wrote down.

"It is a good machine, my lord. I mean that." He put his hands out to the brazier. "But the wall can light every lamp you like. Somebody still has to get out of the chair and go."

Emeric turned that over and found that it was not a complaint about the machine. It was a staffing problem, correctly stated by a man who had done eleven years of nights.

"So what do you do about it?"

"Two runners on the shift," Rylan said. "And the man in the chair never goes himself. Not once, not for a quick look, not if it is the seventh otter that night. Because the minute he goes, there is nobody reading the board, and then you have paid for a board so that nobody can watch it."

It went on the roster that night in those words, more or less, and within a month the line men were calling it Rylan's rule, and using it as a reason not to move, which Ward said was the natural life cycle of any rule ever written.

The walk did not change. Emeric was careful about that and wrote the reason in the book so that a later version of himself would have to argue with it. Nothing he built was allowed to become the reason a man stopped doing the thing the building was supposed to help with. The board could tell the watch where to look. The board could never be why nobody was looking. If the stone behind the backing died in the night, the watch was to be exactly what it had been the month before, and nobody outside the gatehouse needed to know the difference.

Ferra came to the gatehouse on the fifth morning with her book under her arm and a look he had learned to recognise.

"It works," she said. "So now it gets a procedure and it gets a column, because a thing that wakes people at night and is not written down is a rumour with a lamp on it."

They wrote the card standing up, which took an hour, most of it spent on eleven words.

Dark and stays dark: the node is starved. Take a charged one from the box, swap it, log it.

Flickers: a true fault. Do not touch it. Wake the prince, wherever he is. Only he can mend it, at the stone.

"You are certain about the last line," Ferra said.

"It is not pride."

"I did not say it was."

He did not explain the rest of it to her, because the rest of it was the trove and the trove was his, but the shape was simple enough. A stone had to be learned by hand before it could be spoken to at a distance. A stone that had gone wrong had to be learned again, and there was one pair of hands in the barony that could do the learning.

She hung the card herself, level, with two nails.

Then she ruled two new columns into the back of her book. The first was the swap log: date, stretch, and the hand that did it. The second was the lamp log: what lit, and what the runner found when he got there.

"Not the otters," Emeric said.

"The otters especially."

"It is an otter."

"It is an otter on the east run, and in a year you will want to know how often the east run lights for nothing, and neither of us will remember, and you will guess, and then you will build something off your guess." She did not look up. "The otters go in."

The otters went in.

Rylan read the card twice that afternoon, the second time with one finger under the line, and made no remark about that, and Emeric made none either. The men who sat the chair and could not yet read it had it off by heart before they were allowed to sit, which took an evening each and was Declan's idea.

What Emeric wanted, and had wanted since the frame went up, was to hand the thing to Ward.

It was obvious. Ward ran the watch. The board was the watch's eyes. The man who commanded at the gate should not have to send a boy up the hill to wake the baron because a lamp was doing something a card had three words for.

He tried it the only way that was open to him, on a night when the gatehouse was empty and the door was barred, with the grey stone in his hand and Ward's arm under his fingers.

Inside, the ring was not a board. It was the island seen from somewhere above it with all twelve fields laid out like still water, and every node a weight sitting in its own patch of the world, and Ward, who had been in there twice before and had not asked to go back either time, stood in it and looked at the whole thing for a while without saying anything.

Then he worked it. He put his attention on nine and the field came up under it. He went down the ring, one to twelve, then back. He found stretch six, where the field ran out four feet short of the corner, and said so, and he was right, and Emeric fixed it in the morning.

A quarter of an hour, perhaps. Then Emeric let the delve go, and they were two men standing in a cold gatehouse, and Ward had his hand flat on a board with twelve dark lamps in it that was not doing anything at all for him and never would.

Ward looked at his own hand for a moment.

"So it is you," he said.

"It is me."

"And when you are on the coast for ten days?"

"The watch walks, and anything that flickers waits."

Ward did not argue with it. He went to the door, and stopped there, and said the thing he had come to the door in order to be able to say without looking at anybody. "I have four men who can take my place inside an hour. That is not a compliment to them. It is the only reason I sleep."

Ferra said it more plainly the next day, and she had said it before, in the pump-house, standing over a bound stone nobody else had seen.

"This is the main again."

"It is."

"Then I will say it once, and then I will put it in the column and stop." She closed the book on her finger. "If it only works while you are alive and in the room, then the thing is not a machine. The thing is you. And I cannot put you in an inventory, I cannot buy a second one when the first is on the coast, and I cannot mend you."

He spent three days trying to prove her wrong.

He did it quietly and only among people who already knew some version of what he could do, because the alternative was widening the circle, and the circle was the one number in the barony he refused to let grow by accident.

Bram sat with the grey stone in his palm for an evening at the forge, patient as a post, and at the end of it it was a stone and he was a tired man who wanted his supper. Ferra held it for a quarter of an hour, and it stayed cold, and she gave it back and said, without any weight, that she had not expected anything and was still disappointed. Ward would not take it at all, and then took it, once, for about as long as it takes to check a coin, and handed it back and went out.

He went through everything Armand had ever taught him about stones, in his memory, line by line, looking for one sentence about a room inside one, and there was nothing, because Armand had never been in one. He considered Vesk for about a minute and a half. Then he stopped considering him.

On the third night he wrote it in the book under the roster and the fault card and the swap schedule, in the same hand as everything else.

Second key: none. Deferred.

He read it back. The farm ran on him. Now the ring ran on him too, and he had known that when he ordered the oak. Nobody had made him build it and nobody had made him build it this way, and the honest version of the entry was that he had been given the same problem twice and had answered it the same way twice, and the second answer had been quicker than the first.

He did not cross it out and he did not feel better about writing it down.

The board went on the roster proper at the end of the week. A line man in the chair, Rylan and a second runner on the shift, the card on the wall at eye height, the spare box under it with eleven charged nodes in straw.

At some point after the third bell, nine lit.

The runner went, and Rylan went with him because there was nothing else to do, and the pair of them came back up out of the stair with their lantern swinging and the grin of men who have been outside in the cold and are about to be inside and warm. Rylan reported it at the door, formally, the way he had reported the first one.

"Otter," he said.

Ferra's log took its second line of the season in the man's own hand. Ward stood behind the chair for the better part of an hour afterwards, watching the board instead of the wall, saying nothing, with his arms folded. Then he went to bed.

Twelve lamps, a card with eleven words on it that mattered, a ring of stones in a wall that answered one man, and, in the book below the board, in the same hand as the roster, the word he had written instead of a second key.
