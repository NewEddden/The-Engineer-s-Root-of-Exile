Chapter 43 - The Exchange

The autumn had nothing in it.

There was no harvest, because the field was ash for a mile and a half along the river road, and the ash had been rained into the ground by the middle of the month. There was no soap trade, because the halls that had bought Kaldo's soap were still deciding what they were now, and a hall that is deciding what it is does not place orders. And there was a payroll, written on a board in the market square, which Emeric had committed to on a set of steps in front of most of the town three years before, and which did not stop being owed because the work it paid for had burned.

Ferra put it in one line, which was her habit with anything that could not be improved by being longer.

"Idle paid men are worse than working paid men in every way I can measure," she said. "I can carry the wage through one season. I cannot carry it through two. So whatever they do next has to be worth doing, and it cannot cost anything."

"Anything."

"Very little. I will tell you what very little is when you tell me what you want."

He did not know what he wanted. That was the honest state of it for four days.

He had a list, and the list was long, and every line on it wanted money or ore or both, and the barony had Wayford's money and nothing else, and Wayford's money bought months. The foundry could run on the ore already in the yard. The garrison could drill. Farris could walk the burned divisions every morning with a slate and write down what the fire had done, and did, and came back each day looking a little older. None of that used the Works crews, who turned up at the yard at first light because they were paid to, and stood about in the cold because there was nothing to hand them, and were beginning to look at each other in the way men look at each other before somebody says something.

He found it on the fifth evening, walking the island at dusk, because walking was the only thing that had ever worked when he was stuck.

The old exchange stood at the head of the port where it had always stood, and he stopped in front of it the way a man stops in front of a thing he has passed a hundred times and has just seen.

Two storeys. One long floor inside, built for a trading crowd. The high windows along the upper wall, most of them empty, a few still holding panes of the old glass, green as pond water, with the street bent in them. The guild that raised it had dissolved before the plague and the building had stood empty since, which in Kaldo was not remarkable. What was remarkable was that it still stood at all. The roof had gone over one bay at the east end. The rest was sound.

He knew what he wanted before he had finished looking at it.

He brought Ferra and Bram the next morning and walked them through it without saying what it was for, and asked what it would take.

Bram walked the floor twice, stamped on it in four places, went up the stair and came down it, looked at the open bay for a long while with his head back, and named a figure. Ferra named a slightly larger one, because Bram had left out the stoves.

It was almost embarrassingly small. The shell was already there. What it needed was a roof over the open bay, boards where the boards had gone, shutters, stoves and flues, and benches. Kaldo had crews that had put up a foundry and a pump-house in a single season, a common measure that meant a joist cut at one end of the island fitted a beam cut at the other, and a brickworks that had been running for two years. A rough version would be usable in half a season. The whole of it in one.

"What is it for," said Bram.

"A school."

Bram looked at the floor again as if it had said something to him. Ferra wrote the word at the top of her page, above the figure, and underlined it once.

He had priced a school before. In the second year, from the foundations up, in brick, and had cut it in the same month he cut everything else, and had never told anybody it had been on the list. This was a quarter of that figure. It was a quarter of that figure because a guild had wanted good light to count coin by, a long time ago, and had built accordingly, and had then died and left the building behind like a coat on a hook.

He said the other thing to Ferra plainly, because it was true, and because she would have worked it out by noon.

"I am building it now because the year is empty," he said. "If the harvest had come in, I would have found a reason to build something else first."

"I know," said Ferra, and went to price the stoves.

He told the town from the same steps, in about as many words as it took. A school, in the old exchange, for the children of the barony. A roof on by midwinter. Lessons before the roof was finished, where the roof was sound. He did not make a speech. He had made the promise on those steps without a date, deliberately, because a missed date in that square would have cost more than the promise was worth. He did not remind anybody of that. Nobody needed reminding.

Nobody in Kaldo had forgotten it. Several people mentioned it to him that first week, in the way people in Kaldo mentioned things, which was sideways and at the end of a conversation about something else. A woman at the north well, who said her youngest could count to twenty and would he see to the rest. A carter who said it was about time, and meant it kindly. One of the older quarrymen, who said nothing at all about the school and asked a long question about whether the benches would have backs.

And Hollis.

Hollis found him at the yard gate on the third day with his hat in his good hand, the way he had once come to say he would bring his acres in. The other arm hung a little wrong and had done since the morning on the flank. He used it for what it would still do and did not discuss the rest.

"I heard about the exchange," Hollis said.

"Yes."

"I'll say this the once and leave it." He turned the hat a quarter turn. "I thought the school was a thing you said on the steps because you needed us that morning. I didn't hold it against you. A man says things in a hard moment, and I'd have said worse in your place. I'd stopped expecting it, is all. I wanted you to know I'd stopped, so you'd know what it means that I'm standing here."

Then he put the hat on and went back to the quarry gang, where he was still one of the better men on it with one arm and part of another.

Emeric stood at the gate for some time.

He had been braced for three years against the hostile version. The man who would stand up in a crowd and say you promised. He had an answer ready for that man and had kept it sharp. He had no answer at all for a man who had forgiven him in advance for a lie he had not yet finished telling, and who had walked to the gate to say so with his hat in his hand, and who had meant every word of it kindly.

It sat worse than anything anybody had ever shouted at him in that square, and it sat there for a week.

The faculty assembled itself out of people who already existed, which was the only kind of faculty Kaldo could afford.

Ferra took numerals, because she had taught them in the parish hall three evenings a week in the first winter and had been better at it than he was, by a distance neither of them had enjoyed discussing. Doran took letters, because Doran had the only books on the island, and because he had spent two evenings a week for more than a year teaching the garrison theirs off slates and a copied roll, and was curious, he said, whether children fidgeted less than soldiers. He did not expect so. He brought the books down from the church in a crate himself and would not let anybody else carry it.

Imani took the body. Why the water was boiled, and why hands were washed, and what happened to people who did neither. She told Emeric in advance that she intended to be blunt with the children about that last part.

"How blunt."

"I'm going to tell them what a baby looks like on the third day when the water wasn't boiled," she said. "Once. Clearly. And then I'm going to show them how to boil it."

"Good."

She looked at him as though he had said something obvious, which he had, and went back to the ward.

Bram took a craft track. He said he did not want it. He said so on the Monday, and on the Wednesday, and at some length on the Friday, and was at the exchange before anybody else on every one of those mornings, measuring the benches with his own rule against the common one and finding, each time, with visible disappointment, that they agreed.

Orrin, Emeric asked in the yard on a Thursday, and Orrin said yes before the sentence was finished.

"I haven't told you what it is."

"You were going to say schoolmaster," said Orrin. "You had the face for it. Yes."

He was exactly right for it, and Emeric had known that for weeks and had spent the weeks checking whether he knew it or only wanted it. Orrin talked. He loved a room. He carried the shape of the whole field in his head the way a man who has walked a country carries its roads, and he could teach letters and numbers to anybody who would sit still, and he could teach the thing nobody in Kaldo had ever been taught at all, which was what mana was and what a body did with it.

That was the part that would matter in ten years. A barony that could train its children's control could, in time, train its own carvers and its own stone-chargers and everything after them, instead of relying on one prince with one pair of hands working at a desk late at night. Ferra had been raising that problem for two years in a tone that had been getting steadily flatter. This was the first real answer to it, and it had walked into Kaldo as a prisoner.

Emeric went to watch the first lesson.

It was in the east end of the exchange, under the half of the roof that was sound, before there were benches, with the children sitting on the boards with their coats on and their breath showing. There were eleven of them. Orrin had spent the first week testing every child who came through the door, with a lamp stone and a cup of water and a great deal of talk, and had kept the ones who had anything to work with, and had told the others they would be taken later, and had made later sound like a promise rather than a refusal. Emeric noted that, and knew he could not have done it.

Orrin did not start with mana. He started with a pan.

A pan of water on the one stove that worked, and a stone out of the cold yard, and a question. He put the cold stone in the warm water and asked what would happen, and let them argue about it for far longer than Emeric would have, and let the wrong answers stand in the room without touching them, and then had a girl of about nine put her hand in the pan and tell everybody what she felt.

"It's gone colder."

"Has it. Where did the warm go?"

"Into the stone."

"Why the stone?"

She did not know. Nobody knew. Orrin walked up and down the boards with his hands behind his back and let them not know for a while, visibly enjoying it, and then he told them that the warm had gone where there was less of it, the way people in a crowd move toward room, and that it would keep going until the stone and the water were the same, and then it would stop. He said this was the most important thing anybody in the room would learn that winter. He said it was not about water.

Then he gave each of them a lamp stone, dull and cold out of the box, and told them to hold it in both hands and find the place where they were warmer than it was.

A boy at the back found it first, and Emeric saw it happen. The stone did not light. It did not come close to lighting. But the boy's face changed in the way a face changes when a thing it has been told turns out to be true, and he looked up at Orrin with an expression Emeric had last seen on Imani, in a small room behind a church, in the second before she leaned in.

"There," said Orrin. "That. Remember where that was. It will be there tomorrow, and the day after, and it will be bigger."

The children left arguing about it. Two of them were still arguing about it at the gate.

Emeric walked back to the keep and wrote nothing down, because nothing that had happened needed a column. He was aware all the way back of having watched a man do easily the thing he did worst, which was to let people stay wrong long enough to want the answer.

Vesk would not teach the eleven. He said so without heat.

He took two instead, two of the older ones Orrin had sent on, and he took them into the foundry yard in the afternoons and kept them there, and what he did with them was not teaching so much as apprenticing. They carried for him. They watched him. They did the same small thing forty times, and then forty times again, and then were asked what had been different about the thirty-first.

Emeric asked him once why two and not forty.

Vesk looked at him for a while with the particular care he gave everything, and did not answer, and Emeric did not ask again. Whatever the reason was, it was in the notebook, and the notebook was his.

The windows were the problem, and Emeric had known they would be from the moment he stood in the street and looked up.

The openings had been cut for light along the whole upper wall, and he wanted light coming through them. Not for the look of the thing. A room where children read in gloom teaches them that reading is unpleasant, and they learn that faster than they learn their letters, and they keep it longer. Shutters kept out the weather and kept in the dark. Oiled cloth was better and was not good. What the openings wanted was glass.

Glass existed in Randillia. There was a great deal of it in the palace, in the long gallery, and he had spent his childhood looking at the gardens through it and watching the hedges swim. It was green. It was full of small bubbles the glaziers called seed. It bent whatever was behind it. It was expensive because it was difficult, and it was difficult because nobody who made it had ever worked out which part was the difficult part.

Emeric knew all four parts. He had known them in another life the way a man knows the names of his neighbour's children without ever having been introduced to them.

He set them out at the foundry bench for Bram and Colm, with Ferra at the end of the bench writing, one to a line, in the flat voice he kept for things he was sure of.

Iron in the sand. Iron is what makes the green, and the river sand here was so heavy with it that it came out of the bank the colour of rust.

Not hot enough for long enough. The seed is air that never had time to rise out of the melt before the glassmaker lost patience and worked it.

Nothing to cancel the colour. There is a thing that takes the last of the green out, and it has to be put in on purpose, and nobody here puts it in.

Cooled too fast. Glass that cools in whatever time the room happens to take keeps the cooling locked inside it, and a pane holding that much of its own history cracks at nothing, on a cold night, with nobody near it.

Colm listened to all four and asked the only question that mattered, which was which of them they could actually do.

"The first depends on the sand," Emeric said. "The river sand is no use to us at all. I want sea sand. Pale, washed, low in iron. There is a bar of it at the bottom of the barony, where the long water meets the sea. It is on the tithe map. I have never been down there."

He said it out loud, in a room with four people in it, and not one of them heard anything wrong with the sentence. Neither did he.

Saban was in Kaldo that week, between runs, and Emeric sent for him and asked him to go down the long water and bring back as much pale sand off the bar as a boat would carry.

Saban asked once what it was for.

"Sand," said Emeric.

Saban looked at him for about as long as it took to decide this was a trade secret. He priced it in his head as bulk freight at a bad time of year, said he would need a boat and a boatman and most of a fortnight, and went. He had been doing business with this man for two years and the man had never once asked him for anything pointless, and it did not seem to Saban a sound time to start doubting him over sand.

The roof went on over the open bay while he was gone.

It went on in nine days, with a crew that had roofed a foundry and knew exactly how much it hated doing it in wind, and on the tenth day there were children inside the building who had not been sent there by anybody, sitting on the new boards and waiting to be told something.

Nobody decided what to call the place. The guild's name was still cut over the door, half of it gone. Emeric had been saying the exchange for a month, and Ferra had been writing old exchange, works in the ledger, and Doran had called it the building in two sermons. Before the boat was back the town was calling it the school, all of it, the carters and the quarry gang and the women at the north well, and nobody had agreed to that, and nobody was going to be able to stop it.

Emeric noticed on a grey morning two days before the boat was due, when a carter new to the island asked him the way to the school, and he gave it, and then stood in the street for some time after the cart had gone.

He had sent a man with a boat to a part of his own barony he had never seen, to fetch sand, and he had not thought twice about it.
