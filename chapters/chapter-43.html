Chapter 43 - Furniture

The gate sent a rider at the middle of a hot afternoon in the last week of August, and then, after a pause long enough to mean that somebody had gone down to look properly, a rider again, slower, because the gate crew were not certain the board had anything for what they were looking at.

Rylan went down. He came back up the hill an hour later walking beside a hired cart, which was coming up the high street at the pace of the horse in front of it, and the horse in front of it was old.

There were three crates in the back of the cart, roped down under sacking, and a long low bench of some dark wood lashed on top of them, legs in the air. There was a carter on the box. And beside the carter, upright, with his hands folded in his lap and a hat with a wide brim pulled down against the sun, sat a thin old man in a coat of plain grey wool.

Emeric was at the top of the gate yard by the time the cart came under the arch. He had known who it was from the window, from the set of the shoulders, before he could see the face.

"He gave his name at the South Gate," Rylan said, coming up beside him. "Then the gate asked his business, the way Ward makes them ask everybody's, and he said it was regarding nothing." Rylan looked at the cart with deep interest. "The gate crew didn't have anything on the board for that."

The carter got down. Armand did not, at once. He sat on the box and looked round the gate yard: at the petition line, which had turned to watch; at the keep going up behind; at the wire coming down off its bracket on the south wall and away over the yard on its first pole, with one of the potter's glazed knobs on top. He looked at the wire for longer than at anything else. Then he climbed down, carefully, backward, and turned round and found Emeric in front of him.

"Your Highness."

There was nobody near enough to hear, this time, except Rylan, and Armand did not look round to see.

"Emeric," he said.

"You came."

"I said I would come when what needed settling was settled." Armand took his hat off and turned it in his hands, once, round by the brim. "It is settled."

He did not say what it had been, or how it had been settled, and the plain grey coat said nothing either. Emeric had never seen him in anything but tower cloth, with the tower's marks on it. This coat had none. He did not ask about that either.



The rooms were at the top of the keep, in the east tower, because they were the best rooms in it that nobody was using and because they had a window that looked down the river. Hale had them aired inside the hour, with a kind of grim pleasure at being asked to make a room ready for a guest of standing, which had not happened in that house in a long time.

The stair to them was a hundred and some steps, turning, worn hollow in the middle.

Armand went up it beside Emeric, slowly, talking about the road. The road had been dull. The inns had been worse than he remembered, or he had been more comfortable than he remembered, he could not decide which. Wayford had a new bridge toll and a magistrate's clerk who had asked him his business twice and written down both answers.

About halfway up he stopped talking.

A few steps further he stopped walking as well, on the landing by the arrow slit, and put one hand on the wall, and stood there in the light from the slit, looking out of it at the river, which had apparently caught his eye. His breath was coming short and quick through his nose. The two first fingers of his other hand had gone to the inside of his own wrist, the way they had rested there in the courtyard of the tower last summer, folded, quiet, as if they belonged there.

Emeric had stood in Imani's ward ten months ago and shown a room full of children where to put two fingers to find a heart. He knew what he was looking at.

He did not say so. He stood on the step below and looked out of the slit as well, at the river, and waited.

"The tower," Armand said, without turning round, "has a stair that is honest about what it is. Every step the same height. This one was built by men who were in a hurry and did not expect to be old." He took his fingers away from his wrist and put the hand in the pocket of the grey coat. "I shall learn it."

They went on up. Armand talked about the road again, the rest of the way, and did not stop.



The crates came up after him in the arms of the gate crew and were stacked against the wall of the larger room, and the dark bench was set down under the window, and Armand thanked each man by name, having asked each man his name on the stair, and gave each of them a copper, which they tried not to take and took.

He would not have them opened. He would open them himself, he said, slowly, over a week, and know where everything was put.

Emeric sat with him that evening by the window, with the river going dark below and a jug of the inn's small beer between them, because the keep's own was worse.

"What are you now?" he said. "In the tower."

"In the tower, I am a portrait." Armand drank. "When a man of a certain length of service leaves the tower, the Circle gives him a title on his way out of the door. A long one, with my name in the middle of it. It means that I may sit in any room of the tower I like, at any meeting, for as long as I live, and that nobody in that room is obliged to listen to a word I say." He set the cup down. "It is furniture. Very good furniture. They polish it."

"And here?"

"Here I am an old man with three crates and a bench." He looked at the bench under the window, in the last of the light, with something close to affection. "And a notebook I gave away."

Emeric had the notebook in his coat. He had carried it in the same pocket for a year and a half. He took it out and put it on the table between them, and Armand looked at it, at its cracked spine and the back pages swollen with Kaldo, and did not touch it.

"You filled it," he said.

"Most of it with sewage."

"The best science usually starts there." He looked at the notebook a little longer. "In the courtyard you told me I had been looking correctly and stopped too soon. You said I should put my hands on it."

"Yes."

"Not tonight," Armand said. "I have been a fortnight on a road. I should like to put my hands on a bed." He got up, using the edge of the table to do it, and stood looking down at the river. "Show me the town tomorrow. I would like to see what you have been doing with my sewage."



He saw a great deal of it.

He walked slowly and stopped often, and asked questions that were short and never stupid, and argued with the answers the way he had argued with a boy of seven across a table in the tower, by asking another question that showed where the first answer was thin. He looked at the tap at the top of the lower ward, and asked where its water came from, and was driven out through the South Gate and along the arches to see.

The span had been standing since July. The mason had knocked out the wedges on a still morning in the second week of the month with twenty men watching and the carpenter's frame creaking under the new stone, and the frame had settled a finger's width away from the arch, and the arch had not moved. The trough was gone. The channel ran over the gap on stone now, and the mason had gone back to making gravestones, which he said paid better and argued less.

Armand stood under the span and looked up at it, at the voussoirs and the keystone and the new pale mortar between them.

"No stone," he said. "Not one. Nothing loaded, nothing bound."

"No."

"The tower would have floated a span like this into place in an afternoon. Six men on the rail and a crystal each." He turned his head and looked along the arches, going away across the fields, the old ones, built by somebody's grandfathers with ropes and frames. "And held it there for as long as somebody paid them to."

He saw the soap yard, where Abloh looked at him once, all over, with the same expression he gave a bolt of cloth, and went back to his kettles. He saw the dyehouse, and the jars on the bench, and the pinned needle, and stood over the needle without saying anything at all, and then asked one question.

"What is moving it?"

"Current. Out of the jars."

"Which is?"

"I'll show you properly," Emeric said. "Not in a sentence."

Armand looked at him, and then back at the needle, and did not say whether he thought it clever or merely wrong.

He saw the school, which was shut for the harvest, as the square had said it always would be. Sela was there anyway, alone, with the benches pushed back against the walls and the slates in stacks, scrubbing the floor on her knees. She sat back on her heels when they came in. Armand stood in the middle of the long room and looked up at the trusses and the high windows and the alphabet sheets pinned along the wall, and then he asked her, with great courtesy, whether the school would have room in its rotation, when it opened again, for an old man who knew a little about stones. Sela looked at the grey coat and the hat and the courtesy, and said a morning a month, and he would bring his own chair, because there were none to spare, and he would not frighten anybody.

"I will try not to," Armand said.

"Don't try," said Sela. "Don't."

A boy followed them from the exchange as far as the canal ring, at a distance he plainly thought was invisible. Emeric knew him: Arthur, Sela's youngest, who was up first every time in Ward's yard. At the ring Armand stopped and turned round, and the boy stopped too, too late to look as though he had not been following.

"Are you a mage?" Arthur said.

Armand considered him, the whole of him, from his bare feet to his hair.

"I was," he said. "Now I am mostly old."

Arthur thought about that, and seemed to decide it was a fair answer, and went home.

Ward met them at the gate yard on the way back. He had not come down the hill with them, and he had not been asked to, and he was standing at the top of the yard when they came in under the arch, exactly where a man might happen to be. He and Armand looked at each other across the cobbles. Emeric had seen them in the same room perhaps a dozen times in all his years at court, and never once seen either of them speak first.

"Master Armand," Ward said.

"Ward." Armand looked at him. "You are still alive."

"Yes."

That was the whole of it. Ward turned and went back to the yard, and Armand watched him go with an expression Emeric could not read at all.

He saw Bram's forge from the doorway, because Bram was in the middle of a heat and did not stop it for anybody. And he saw Bram's kitchen, behind the forge, where Ferra sat at her table under the window with the barony's books in two stacks and the lamp not yet lit.

She stood up when Emeric brought him in, with the window light behind her red hair. She looked at the old man in grey and at the prince, and she did not offer her name, and Emeric did not offer it for her.

Armand looked at the stacks of ledgers and the open book in front of her, and at her hands, which had ink on the side of the right one, and asked whether he might see what she was working on.

"No," said Ferra.

Armand inclined his head, and asked her nothing else, and they went.

And he saw the ward, from its side step, since that was as far as anybody went without Imani's yes. She came out to the step with a basin in her hands and her hair tied up for work, and looked at Armand once, up and down, the way she looked at everyone who came to her step, and then again, longer, at his face and his mouth and the hand he kept in his coat pocket.

She did not say anything to him about it. She said good afternoon, and asked whether he would want anything of the ward while he was in Kaldo, and he said that he hoped very much he would not, and she said that was what everybody said.

When he had gone a little way up the lane ahead of them, she said to Emeric, without lowering her voice much, "Your old man. Keep him off stairs."

"He lives at the top of the east tower."

Imani looked at him as though he had told her the old man slept in the river.

"Then move him," she said, and went back in.



They ate that evening in Armand's rooms, because Armand did not want to come down the stair again, and did not say so.

He had opened one crate. The bench under the window had things on it now: a set of brass weights in a box lined with green cloth, a glass, a little cup of polished stone, and a wooden case with a lid, closed. He did not explain any of them. He ate very little and asked a great many questions about the river and the price of ore, and listened to the answers with his head on one side, and when Emeric had finished telling him about Grayrock he sat back in his chair and looked at him over the top of his cup.

"Which one?" he said.

Emeric looked up.

"Which one what?"

"Everything else you showed me today, you showed me." Armand turned the cup in his fingers. "The arch. The jars. The soap. You stood beside me and pointed and talked. Two women you took me to, and at those two doors you did not look at me at all. You watched them look at me." He drank. "So. Which one?"

"I don't know what you mean."

Armand looked at him a little longer.

"Then it's both," he said, and reached for the bread.
