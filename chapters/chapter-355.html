Chapter 355 - The Keel on the Sentinel

The billets came over on the eighth of November in nine crates stencilled LAMP GLASS, WITH CARE, and four of the Hod's crew carried each one down the ramp as if it were.

"They're steel," said Tess.

"They're lamp glass," said Saban. He had come across on the run for no reason he would give, and stood at the foot of the ramp with his hat on, watching the crates go by. "Ask anybody."

Nobody on the island was going to ask anybody. That was the point of the island.

They had built the berth over the Cradle in October, before she came out: a roof of sawn timber and tarred sailcloth on posts, over a third of the slab's deck, so that from the air the east bay showed a long shed on a stone pier and nothing else. Nothing flew over the island but Kaldo's own ships. Saban had wanted the roof anyway. Under it, on the deck, the keel blocks stood in a line down the middle, oak, each one cut to the curve of a hull that didn't exist yet, and at the far end Fletcher's men had the first ring of the pressure shell on a sledge, ready to be walked onto them.

It was a short fat tube of steel plate, taller than a man, rolled at the works and welded down its seam. Nickel and chromium in the steel, Bram's melt, out of the Forge at Kaldo in pieces small enough to pass for roofing.

"That's not the keel," said Wren, who had come down from the mill to see it. "A keel's a long thing. Along the bottom."

"On a boat made of wood," said Tess.

"What's this one made of?"

"Rings."

Fletcher's men walked the ring onto the blocks on rollers, with a lot of shouting, and Fletcher stood at the side with his chalk in his hand and said nothing until it sat, and then went round it on his knees with a straightedge and said, "Aye," and wrote on it. That was the keel laid. Emeric had come out for it. He stood a little way back in his coat with Saban, and when it was done he went and put his hand flat on the plate and took it off again, and that was all.

"What's she called?" said one of Fletcher's men. "For the book."

"Nothing goes on her," said Saban, before Emeric could answer. "No name. No flag. She's a survey vessel."

"Aye, but what do we call her?"

Emeric thought about it. "Challenger," he said. "It's an old name. There was a ship that went and sounded the deep, once, in a book I read. Nobody paints it."

By the end of the week the whole yard was saying the Challenger, and nobody knew why, and nobody had painted it.



Tess had the order of the work pinned on the board in the berth's drawing room, a lean-to at the shore end with a stove and two benches. She had written it out herself, because Emeric's version had been in the wrong order.

The pressure hull first: the rings of welded alloy steel, end to end, with the ring frames inside them, the way a cooper puts hoops on a barrel. The outer fairing over that, for the shape. Then the openings: the ports down the flank, each a pair of glass plugs one behind the other in a stepped bore, with a little gauge between them, so that if the outer plug went the gauge would climb and the inner one would still hold. The nose. The diesel engine, for running on the surface and charging the cells. The storage battery and the electric motor, for running under. The propeller. The air. The ballast tanks and the trim tanks, the emergency blow, the latch, the drop weights. The gauges.

And at the bottom, under a line she had ruled across the sheet with Bram's steel rule: Then the stones.

"You've put the stones last," Emeric said, the first morning, reading it over her shoulder.

"She has to swim without them," said Tess. "If she can't swim with every stone in her cold, she's not a boat. She's a coffin with lamps in." She didn't look round. "Bram says that."

"Bram says that about everything."

"He's right about everything," said Tess. "It's very annoying."

He didn't argue, which was one of the things she liked about him. He was gone on the Hod before noon. He had the rest of it to run. Tess had a boat.

The crews were sworn staff, the island's own, a hundred and some of them on the Challenger by the end of November and more coming. They were good. They had built the works and the Dust House and the pier sheds, and they were used to being told a thing was for a purpose and not asking what the purpose was for. They had been told a survey vessel and nothing more. Tess heard within a fortnight that she was for sounding the reef, for looking for the castaways' wrecks on the Pull's track, for the pearl beds off the Isle, and for finding the old quay at the bottom of the bay. She heard each of these from a different crew, and each crew was quite sure.

"You've been busy," she said to Saban, on the run back.

"I've been in the canteen," said Saban. "Their soup's very good."



The nose was hers.

Nobody had said so. Bram had looked at the drawing in October, the dome of glass ten feet across that was to sit in the Challenger's bow in its steel seat like an eye in a socket, three of them stacked one inside the next, and said that he did steel and she knew where the kiln was, and gone back to his billets. Ivo had sent his annealing schedule out on the supply run in his own hand, four pages, very neat. Ivo's telescope blanks were a foot across. Tess had read his pages twice, in bed, and then a third time, and on the third time had started writing in the margins.

They built the kiln on the works shore beside the Dust House, out of firebrick the island's brickworks had been saving for something, a squat round house with a domed crown and a door you could drive a cart through. The kiln crew was eight from the brickworks and the glass shed, and their foreman was a square, grey woman who had laid more firebrick than anybody alive and told Tess so the first day, in the tone of somebody getting it out of the way. Wren asked to go on nights. She was tired of the mill, and she had held a wrench for Tess on the flywheel and lain under the bench when it walked, and Tess said yes.

The first blank went in on the twentieth of November.

The melt came out of the glass shed's tank in ladles, white, too bright to look at, and went into the mould in a long slow pour that took most of a morning: a dish of fireclay and sand on an iron bed, with a core over it to give the dome its inside face. The outer ply, the thickest of the three, the one that would have the sea against it. When the last ladle was in and the core lowered and the door bricked up, the kiln crew stood round in the heat with their faces red and their shirts black, and nobody said anything, and the foreman spat on the brick by the door and watched it hiss.

After that it was waiting. Glass that has just set is full of strain, the outside cooled and shrunk round an inside still hot and big, and if you let it cool as it likes, it tears itself. So you hold it hot, at the heat where the glass will give and let the strain go out of it, and then you bring it down slowly, a few degrees a day, so that the outside and the inside go down together. Tess's margins said Ivo's rate was for a foot of glass, and this was ten feet across and as thick as her forearm was long, and she had halved it. She thought she had halved it enough.

She was in the kiln shed most nights. There was a cot in the corner and a stove and a bench with the night book, and every hour somebody read the witness cones through the spy-hole and wrote the colour down. Wren learned the colours in two nights and argued about them on the third.

"That's orange."

"It's cherry."

"It's orange with ambitions," said Wren.

On the ninth of December, the nineteenth night, a little after two, the kiln went off like a pistol in a cellar.

Tess was awake before she knew what had woken her. Wren was standing in the middle of the shed with the lamp in one hand and the night book in the other, absolutely still. Then the kiln ticked. It was a small, dry, patient sound, like a clock in another room, and it went on ticking, and every few ticks there was a sharper one.

"Don't open it," said Tess.

"I wasn't going to."

"I know. I'm saying it for me."

The foreman came up from the brickworks' huts in her nightshirt and boots with a coat over both, and stood at the spy-hole and looked, and came away, and sat down on the bench beside Tess's cot without asking.

"That's gone," she said.

"Yes."

"You'll want to know why before we open her."

"I'll want to know why before I go to sleep," said Tess, "and I'm not going to sleep."

They couldn't open it for six days. Cold air on a cracked blank only cracks it more, and Tess wanted the pieces whole, to read them. When they unbricked the door at last and drew the bed out on its rails, the dome was there and still in one piece, more or less, with a crack running up from the rim on the bed side a third of the way to the crown, and two more branching off it, fine as hairs.

Tess had them help her up out of the chair. She put her stick on the bed rail and leaned over the rim and looked at where the crack began, and then got the foreman to hold the lamp lower, and looked again.

"The floor," she said.

"There's nothing wrong with my floor."

"It's a good floor. It's cold." Tess put her finger on the rim, where it had sat on the bed. "It started here, where it touched. The crown of the kiln was where Ivo's schedule said. The floor wasn't. The bottom of the glass went down faster than the top and pulled."

The foreman looked at the rim a long time.

"You never said you wanted the floor hot," she said at last.

"I didn't know I wanted it."

"Well." The foreman put the lamp down. "Now you do."

Tess didn't trust the witness cones after that. They told you the heat at the spy-hole, which was at the height of a woman's eyes and nowhere near the floor. In the Motion Shop she had a little trick she used for the bearing trials: two wires of different metals twisted together at the end, which made a tiny current when the joint was hot, more current the hotter, enough to move the needle of a galvanometer. A thermocouple, Emeric called it. She'd had one through the crown of Orrin's tile kiln years ago, and it had never once occurred to her that a floor would want its own. She made eight in a day out of what the island's stores had, and the kiln crew bricked them through the wall in pairs: crown and floor, front and back, so you could read the kiln from four sides at once on a board of needles by the cot.

The first time they fired the empty kiln with the needles in, crown against floor, the floor read so much colder than the crown that Wren tapped the dial to see if it had stuck.

The foreman fixed the floor. She did it in four days, without being asked twice: a bed of dry sand under the iron, a second firebox beneath the floor with its own flue, and a damper Tess could set from the cot. After that the two needles ran together within a hair, and the foreman came and looked at them every morning before her breakfast, as a woman looks at a child that has finally learned to sleep.

That was the floor. The thickness was worse.

Tess did the sum in the night book, in the margin of the night it cracked. Strain in a thick blank takes longer to come out than in a thin one, much longer: twice as thick, and the cooling wants about four times as long. The drawing had the middle ply as thick as the outer, because the middle had to hold the whole sea by itself if the outer went, and somebody had drawn it thick to be safe. At the honest rate, with the floor right, a ply that thick would sit in the kiln into February. Two of them would sit in it until spring.

"Thicker's stronger," said Wren. "Isn't it?"

"Not if you can't cool it."

She didn't make the argument. She put it in the test chamber. There was a force pump and a steel pot with a glass face in the berth's shore shed, for the port plugs, and Tess had the glass shed cast her coupons for it, round plates the size of a dinner plate: two thick, cooled at Ivo's rate, which was what the first blank had had; two half as thick, cooled properly, with the needles. She pumped them one at a time with Wren on the handle and the gauge in front of her and the whole kiln crew standing outside the door, because they'd heard.

The first thick coupon went at a number Tess wrote down and didn't say. The second went lower. The thin ones went past both, and Wren's arms gave out on the handle before the second thin one did, and a brickworks lad took over, and it held past where Tess had told him to stop.

"Well, that's rude," said Wren, rubbing her shoulders.

So the plies were redrawn. The outer stayed as it was drawn, because it would meet the sea and everything in it, and because Tess wanted a margin on that one and nowhere else. The middle was cut to what the chamber said would hold the full working pressure, alone, with the standing margin, and not a finger more. The inner was only a guard against splinters and scratches, and went thinner still. She took the drawing out to Emeric on the next run. He looked at it for a while, and at the coupons she'd brought in a box, the broken ones in their halves.

"I drew it thick," he said.

"You drew it safe," said Tess. "It wasn't."

"Do it," he said, and signed the corner.

The foreman built a second kiln beside the first, smaller, from the same brick, in eleven days. The outer ply went back into the big one on the twenty-third of December with the needles in and the floor hot, on a schedule that would bring it out about the first week of February. The middle went into the small one on the twenty-sixth.

Ferra came across on the run in the middle of all that and looked at the cracked blank on its bed outside the kiln shed, where it would sit until they had the heart to break it up for cullet, and wrote it into the book.

"Not under breakage," said Tess.

"Under what, then?"

"It wasn't breakage. It was finding out about the floor."

Ferra wrote something, and turned the book round. The column heading said Learning. The figure under it was the largest on the page.

"It's a column," she said. "It's the dear one."



Fletcher had never built a boat out of rings.

He'd named eleven boats and built more than that, out of oak and elm, and a few out of steel plate on frames, which was near enough the same thing if you didn't look hard, and then the ships that went up, which were their own trouble and none of it in the water. A boat was a keel and a backbone and ribs off it, and planks on the ribs, and the sea pushing on the planks and the ribs taking it. Any carpenter could see how that went.

This one the sea would push on from everywhere at once, and harder the deeper she went, and the shape that took that was a tube with hoops in it, and the tube was the backbone and the ribs and the planks all together. Bram said barrel. Fletcher had looked at the first ring on its blocks in November and thought a barrel the most sensible thing in the world, and wondered why he'd built the other shape all his working life.

By the middle of December there were six rings on the blocks, welded end to end, and the ring frames going in.

The frames came out of the island's own forge. They were forged from the grey billets in the lamp-glass crates, Bram's steel, the bucket steel, and they were as hard and as stiff as anything Fletcher had put a file to. They came into the berth as bare hoops, a span deep, and Bram's men took them into the hull and hung them, and shrank them onto their seats with heat on the outside, and bolted them through. Bolted. Not welded. Bram had said it the first day and painted it on the board in the berth door in letters a foot high, and Fletcher had thought him fussing, because Bram always fussed about his steel the way other men fussed about their daughters.

On the sixteenth Fletcher came up the berth after his dinner and found Bram on his knees in the fourth ring with a file in his hand and his face gone the colour of the plate.

There was a lug on the frame. A little iron lifting eye, the kind you tack on anywhere for a sling, and somebody had tacked it on to this one, on the bucket steel, with a torch, to save rigging a strop round the hoop to get it up into its seat. It was a good weld. Fletcher could see it was a good weld. Bram put the file to the frame an inch from it and drew it, and the file skated, the way it does on hard steel. He put the file to the frame beside the weld, where the heat had been, and drew it again, and it bit, and a curl of bright grey came off the edge as easily as off a horseshoe.

"There," said Bram.

"What's there?"

"Nothing's there. That's the trouble." Bram sat back on his heels. "What makes it hard is the dust milled into it, all through, spread out fine. You melt it, even a little, the dust runs together in the melt like fat in gravy, and when it sets it's just iron. Soft iron, in a ring that's meant to be the hardest thing on her." He looked at the lug. "Who?"

The welder was a Grayrock man, a good one, who had joined the crews in the spring and had never been told why the grey steel was different from any other, because nobody was told why anything was anything on this island. He stood at the ring's mouth with his cap in his hands and said that the board said not to weld the frames, and he'd thought it meant the joins, and Fletcher believed him.

The frame came out. It took a day to get out, and Bram wrote a note to the Forge for another billet, which meant another night run and another crate of lamp glass, and Ferra would have it in her book by the end of the week under whatever she called it.

Fletcher stood at the board in the door that evening for a long while.

The trouble wasn't the welder. The trouble was that the grey steel looked like steel. It looked like every other piece of steel in the berth, and there were a hundred men in the berth, and half of them carried a torch. Fletcher went to the paint store and came back with a pot of the blue they used for the Hod's bilge marks, and that night he and two of his own men went through the whole hull with a lamp and put a stripe of blue on every piece of bucket steel in her: every frame, every collar, every bolt head in the seat ring that was waiting for the nose. In the morning he chalked a new line on the board under Bram's, smaller.

Nothing hot on the blue.

Bram read it on his way in, and grunted, and went and fetched the paint pot, and stood in the door and painted a blue stripe under Fletcher's line, so they'd know what blue meant.

The other trouble was the plating.

In November Tess had sent out four trial panels from her plating bath, steel plates the size of a door, with a skin of nickel on each that had the water dust in it, keyed slick. They were for the outside of the fairing, last of all, to make her slide through the water easier. Fletcher had hung the four of them off the Cradle's side in the bay on chains, to see what a month of salt did. Bram pulled them up on the eighteenth.

Three were fine. The fourth had a pinhole in its skin no bigger than a pin's head, and under the pinhole, when Bram took a chisel to it, the steel had been eaten out in a pit as big as a thumb's end.

"Plating's fine," said Bram, turning it over. "It's the steel under it gone."

Fletcher knew that one. Every shipwright on the coast knew it, even if nobody could tell you why. You put copper sheathing on a hull and nailed it with iron, and in a year the iron nails were gone to rust and the copper bright as the day it went on. Two metals didn't keep house together in salt water. One of them always ate the other.

"You want something worse next to it," he said.

Bram looked at him.

"Something the sea likes better. Let the sea eat that." Fletcher shrugged. "My father tacked a lump of something soft onto the rudder irons. I never knew what. He said the sea would take its rent from somebody and it might as well be a lump."

Bram stood there with the panel. Then he said, "Zinc," and went off up the shore to find Tess, walking fast, and Fletcher heard him say it again to himself a little way off, pleased, in the voice he used for steel.

They hung zinc on the panels the next week, a block on each, bolted, and painted the gaps between them, and in January Tess had the panels insulated from the fairing on gaskets so that no plate of hers would touch the hull's bare steel at all. Fletcher wrote it in his yard book under the panels' numbers. Then, because he was a careful man, he went down the Cradle's side and doubled every mooring on the yard's three punts.

The yard had lost one in November. It had been tied to the Cradle's rim by a lad who could tie a knot and didn't, and the tide had taken it out of the bay mouth faster than a man could row after it, and the Pull had it by noon. Nothing that slipped her moorings on this island ever came back.

The covered tank at the far end of the deck had its lid on, as it always did, and a man of his on it at night, as there always was, and Fletcher didn't look at it on his way up from the punts. He knew what was in it, and didn't look, the way you don't look in a woman's window.



The air was the part Tess liked best, because it was the part where every answer was a plain one and you could get every one of them wrong.

A sealed boat's air goes bad two ways: the good part gets used up, and the breath the crew give back builds up, and either will kill a man in his berth without waking him. So you put the good part back from flasks of liquid oxygen, and take the bad out with lime, in canisters, in a trunk the fans blow through. A cold stone in the trunk pulls the wet out, because a sealed hull sweats, and a sweating hull rots its own wiring. And behind all that, for when the flasks run low, there's electrolysis: put a current through water and it gives up oxygen at one plate and hydrogen at the other, and you keep the oxygen, and you put the hydrogen over the side because it burns like nothing else.

Fresh water. Stored. Carried in a tank of its own.

Tess had written that in red on the air plant's drawing, and underlined it, and the air plant's crew had nodded, and on the fourth of January one of them filled the trial cell from the bay.

He did it for the most reasonable reason in the world. The fresh water came over the hill from the river in a cart, and the bay was right there, under the berth, and there was a great deal of it.

Tess was at the shed door in her chair when the smell came. It was sharp, and greenish, and it went straight into the back of the nose and down the throat like a hook, and three men came out of the air plant shed coughing, and the fourth, the one who'd filled it, came out last with his eyes streaming and the cell's lead in his hand, which was the right thing to have done, so she didn't shout at him until later.

Imani was on the island that week. She came at a run with her bag and looked at four throats and listened to four chests and said that they'd live, and that they'd cough till Thursday, and that if any of them did it again she'd tell their mothers.

"Salt," Tess said to him, when he could hear it. She had him sitting on an upturned bucket outside with a cup of milk Imani had made him drink. "The sea's got salt in it. Put a current through salt water and you don't get oxygen at that plate. You get that." She jerked her head at the shed, where the door was propped and the smell was still coming out of it in a thin, nasty stream. "In a shed with the door open, you cough. In a boat with the hatch shut, at the bottom of the sea, you all die, and nobody knows why."

He looked at the shed. "Fresh water," he said.

"Fresh water."

He painted it on the cell himself, in letters as big as Bram's, and on the fresh water tank, and on the bay side of the shed's wall where you'd see it from the water. Then he painted NOT THE SEA under it, which Tess thought was overdoing it, and left.



The tanks were Bram's, and Fletcher's, and they went in between the pressure hull and the fairing, where the space was.

A boat that only goes down while something holds her down is a boat that stays down when the something stops. So the Challenger was built to float. Fill the ballast tanks with sea and she was heavy enough to sink; blow the sea out of them with compressed air from the flasks and she rose. The trim tanks, small, fore and aft, moved water from one end to the other so she sat level. If everything went at once, the emergency blow dumped every flask into every tank. And if even that went, there were the drop weights.

The drop weights were iron, slung under the keel in a long rack, and they were held by the latch. The latch was the same as the one on every one of Kaldo's ships: an air stone keeps a bellows full, the full bellows holds a catch shut, and if the stone stops for any reason at all, the bellows goes slack, the catch lets go, the weights fall, and a boat that's lighter by a few tons comes up. Nobody has to do anything. Nobody has to be awake.

They tested it on the Cradle's deck on the ninth of January, with the rack a foot off a bed of sandbags and every hand who could find a reason standing round. Bram lifted the air stone out of its socket on its cord. The bellows sighed. It went soft, and lay down on itself like a tired dog.

The catch didn't move.

"Ah," said Bram.

It was salt. The catch's pin was iron, and the island's air was full of the sea, and in a week of standing in the berth the pin had put on a coat of rust that was holding it fast in its bronze bush. Nobody had thought of it, because on a ship in the air it never stood still long enough. Bram got it out with a drift and a hammer. Tess had the glass shed's lathe turn a new pin in bronze, and a bush to fit, and they greased it, and Bram lifted the stone out again the next morning.

The bellows sighed. The catch went over with a clean little click, and some tons of iron fell a foot into the sandbags with a sound you felt in your teeth, and somebody at the back cheered and was told to stop.

"Again," said Fletcher, writing.

They did it six times. It went every time. Tess wrote all six in her book and went to see about the berths.

The cabins were in, in the middle three rings, behind the control room: bunks in pairs, one over the other, twelve in all, each with a curtain and a leather breathing mask on a hook by the pillow, and a locker underneath. Imani had come down to look at the sick bay, which was one bunk and a cupboard, and had said what she thought of it. Ferra had come down because Imani had.

Imani measured a bunk with Tess's steel rule. She did it carefully, end to end and side to side, and read the figure, and looked at Emeric, who had come in behind them on the day's run and was standing in the cabin door with his head bent under the frame.

"Single," she said.

"It's a boat," said Emeric.

"I can see it's a boat." She measured it again, as though it might have grown. "Who drew it?"

"I did."

"Single," said Imani again, to Ferra, as if that settled something about him that she'd always suspected.

"We'll take it up with the drawing office," said Ferra.

"I am the drawing office."

"Then you'll be easy to find," said Ferra, and the two of them went out past him through the door, one on each side, and Imani put the steel rule in his coat pocket on the way past without looking at him, and he stood there with it.

Tess looked at the bunk. It was a perfectly good bunk.

"Married people," she said, to nobody, and wheeled herself back up the plank to see whether the motor was in.



The magic went on last, the way she'd written it.

She liked that part less than she'd expected to. It was easy. Every piece of it went onto something that already worked, and you could take it off again and the boat would still go.

The four ducts went on at the stern in the third week of January, round the propeller's shaft, each one an iron housing a little longer than a man with a water stone inside, and a mouth at either end. Water in at the front, pushed out at the back, no blade to sing or chew itself up. Bram called them pump-jets because Emeric did, and the hands called them the pipes. They were the sea scooter's trick made big, and when Emeric came out to see them run on the Cradle's deck with their mouths in a trough of water, they put a wave over the trough's end and soaked Saban to the knee.

"That's for the canteen," said Tess.

The shield went on the bow and round the nose's seat ring: a lattice of shard-stone facets, each cut and delved, each bolted in its own iron cup, all of them corded back to a register stone in the control room, so that the boat would stop what she didn't know and let through what she did. There were fewer facets than Bram wanted. Shard stone came out of one seam in the world, under the Isle's west cliffs, and every one of these had been fought over with Ferra by name.

The cabin's density kit went in after: the iron skin lining the cabin rings, the mana scrubber, the head tank, the cold trap, Imani's mark on the dial where it ought to sit. That one Imani checked herself, on her knees in the control room with her own sight, and moved the mark half a line, and said she'd move it back if anybody argued, and nobody did.

The slip panels went on last of all. Each had its own keystone, set in the panel's corner under a brass cap, because a weld seam breaks the web of dust in a plate and a skin across the whole hull would have been a skin with a hundred breaks in it. Each sat on its gaskets with its block of zinc. One of the plating crew asked Tess, in the shed, whether she'd turn faster with them.

"No," said Tess.

"Then what are they for?"

"She'll slide. Less drag, end to end. Less weed on her." She looked at him. "She turns on her planes and her pipes. Take every panel off her and she turns just the same. She's only slower going straight."

"So they're for going straight," he said, disappointed.

"Most of the sea is straight," said Tess.



Zale brought the guest down to the berth on the last Sunday in January, when the crews were off and the berth was empty.

They came on foot, she in a mill shirt and the long grey cloak with the hood up, he with his hands in his pockets and his cap down. They were talking. Tess, at the berth's door, heard a word of Kaldo now and then and a great deal that wasn't, and once the short sound through the guest's nose that Zale said was a laugh.

Zale had come to say a thing, and he said it to Tess, in the drawing room, with the guest standing at the door looking down the berth.

"The Catch is full," he said. "Or as near as matters. Emeric read it off the reef on Tuesday. She'll go out past it, and come back past it. There's no other way off this island that doesn't. And it pulls the heaviest thing on its line. Boat, man, whale, whatever's densest. That's how the castaways got here." He nodded down the berth at the hull. "When she's got her stones in, charged, and her flasks of mana, and Emeric aboard, she's the biggest fish it's ever felt."

"So she doesn't go near it," said Tess.

"So she goes when it's empty. It fires on something big, sooner or later. A whale, most years, in the winter, off the reef. Then it's six weeks filling. She goes out in those six weeks, and she's back in them, and she keeps wide of the line with her stones down on the way in." He looked at Tess. "Not before. Whatever she's ready for."

"She's not ready," said Tess. "The outer ply's in the kiln till February."

"Then there's no quarrel," said Zale.

The guest had come in from the door while they talked. She stood by the board with the order of work on it, which she couldn't read, and looked at it anyway, at the ruled line near the bottom. Then she went out and down the berth, slowly, and Tess went after her in the chair because somebody had to.

The middle ply was on its stand by the bow, out of the small kiln since the nineteenth, waiting for the other two, under a canvas cover with a frame inside it so nothing touched its faces. The inner was in the small kiln now. The guest stopped at the stand and looked at Tess, and Tess, after a little while, reached up and pulled the canvas back off the dome.

It was clear. It was the clearest thing Tess had ever made, a bowl of nothing ten feet across, with the berth's lamps on its inside face and the dark hull behind it bent all round its rim, and you could see straight through it to the steel seat ring with the blue paint on its bolt heads and Bram's grey frames beyond.

The guest put her hand on it. Flat, the way Zale said she put it on things to know them. She held it there, and moved it a little along the curve, and took it away and looked at her palm, and put it back.

"Boat," she said.

"That's the eye," said Tess. "The front. Where you'll look out."

The guest looked at her, and at the dome, and back down the berth at Zale, who had come out and was standing at the drawing room door, and said something to him in her own language, short, with the stop in the middle.

"She says it's very thin," said Zale.

"It's what the chamber says."

He told her that. Tess didn't know how; she didn't think there was a word for chamber in it. The guest listened, and looked at the glass a long time, and then gave the little click at the back of her throat and went back up the berth to Zale. They went off up the Cradle's deck together, still talking, about the reef, Tess thought, from the way his hand went out over the water and drew the line of it.

Tess put the canvas back herself, which took a while from the chair. Then she went up to the kiln shed.

Wren was on. The board of needles by the cot stood together, crown and floor, front and back, within a hair, and the big kiln ticked very faintly in the dark as it came down its few degrees a day, the way a stove does when it's going out and doesn't mind. Bram's letter from Kaldo was on the bench, under the night book. It was mostly about a billet. At the bottom, in the same hand, it said that the City Shell's grown core had put on another finger's width since the new year, and that Armand said it was growing like a child, and Bram said that wasn't a measurement.

"Anything?" said Tess.

"It's cherry," said Wren. "Since about six." She showed her the book. "Properly cherry. No ambitions at all."

Tess wrote the hour under it. Then she wrote the date the outer ply would come out, which was the second of February if nothing cracked, and drew a box round it, and lay down on the cot in her coat with the needles where she could see them, and went to sleep.
