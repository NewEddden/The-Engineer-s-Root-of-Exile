Chapter 78 - No Audience

He did not go to the palace.

It was the first thing he had decided on the road north, and he had decided it again every morning since, in the yard of every posting house, getting up out of the straw. The exile was real. His father had stood close enough to touch him in an office two and a half years ago and said so in pieces, with pauses between them: real in the rolls, real in the treasury, real in every place a thing had to be real to hold, and Emeric was to do nothing anywhere at any time to make any man think otherwise. A third son seen walking in at the palace gate in December, with his boots still muddy from the south road, would be in every book in the city by dark.

He knew all that. He had known it in August, and had sat in an inn in this same ring for two nights while the judiciary wrote out its forms, and had not been sent for, and had known why, and had ridden home.

He had not had the war then.

"I want to ask him one thing," he said to Ward, on the morning after Keller, in the room with two beds and the window onto the yard. "Not at the gate. Anywhere. A room in a house he chooses. An hour. Whatever he can spare that nobody will write down." He stopped. "Can you carry that?"

Ward was sitting on the other bed, pulling his boots on. He finished the left one. Then he sat with his elbows on his knees and looked at the floor between his feet for long enough that Emeric thought he was going to say no.

"I can carry it," Ward said.

He went out at nine. Emeric heard him in the yard, and heard the gate, and then nothing.



He was gone four hours.

Emeric spent them as badly as he had spent any four hours in his life. He read the satchel through again, which there was no reason to do. He went down to the long room and ate something without noticing what it was, and came back up. He stood at the window and watched the yard, where a carter was trying to back a wagon into a space that would not take it, and was going to go on trying until the space changed its mind. He took the strip off his forearm and looked at the cut, which was closing, and tied a fresh one on. He did the arithmetic, because he could not stop himself, and the arithmetic was this: from the outer ring to the palace was an hour on foot if you knew the streets, and Ward knew them. To be told no at a door took the time it took to say no. An hour there and an hour back, and a no at the door, was two hours and a little.

At one o'clock it had been four.

Ward came in with snow on his shoulders, and shut the door, and stood with his back to it, and did not take his cloak off.

"No," he said.

Emeric had known it would be no. He had known it in the yard at nine. It went into him anyway, low down, the way a known thing does.

"Did he say why?"

"Three things." Ward said them the way he gave a report at the guard table, flat, in order, each one a line and no line longer than it had to be. "The exile is real until he says it is not. He has not said it. A son seen with the king is in every book in this city by dark, whichever room it is in, because there is no room in this city that is not somebody's." He paused. "Your case is to be won in Keller's court, on paper, with nothing of his in it. Not a word. Not a letter. Not an hour in a room. If his hand is on it anywhere, it is a father's favor, and a father's favor will not hold, and it will be the first thing they say."

That was true. Emeric had spent the walk back from Keller's chamber working out exactly that, and had not wanted to arrive at it, and his father had arrived at it first, somewhere in that palace, in four hours, and sent it back.

"And the third?"

Ward was quiet longer than the other two had needed.

"He said the city is not safe for you."

Emeric waited. Ward did not go on.

"Not safe how?"

"He did not say." Ward looked at the window. "I do not think he knows. I think he has thought it for years and never found out why." He said it with no weight at all, the way he said everything, and Emeric heard under it that Ward had thought the same thing for just as long, and found out just as little.

He sat on the bed. The satchel was beside him, and he put his hand on it without meaning to.

"Was that all?"

Ward took his cloak off, finally, and shook the snow off it onto the floor, and hung it on the peg by the door.

"He asked whether you are eating," he said.

Emeric looked at him.

It was not a thing a king said. It was not a thing that went in a reply carried by a chamberlain to a garrison knight at a side door. It was the kind of thing a man said across a table, not looking at you, while he said three other things that mattered more. He had heard his father say it once before, when he was ten, and could not remember the rest of that conversation at all, only the question, and the back of his father's head.

"Did he say that to you?" Emeric said. "Himself?"

"It was his answer," said Ward, and sat down on the other bed and began to take his boots off again, as if the day were over, which for Ward it apparently was.

Emeric did not ask anything else. He thought about the four hours, and the snow on Ward's shoulders, and a letter at a guard table in a small square hand on paper that was not Kaldo's, and he put all three of them down in the place where he had been putting things of Ward's for two years, and left them there.

"I'm eating," he said.

"I will tell him," said Ward, to his boot. Then, a little later, without looking up: "Waylon is in the city. At the palace. Since the start of the month."

Emeric thought about that, and about a groom in an inn yard two summers ago telling another groom that the third prince's brother had been at the judiciary asking which day the hearing fell on.

He did not send. Waylon did not come. Neither of them, as far as Emeric ever knew, was surprised.



Armand's colleague lived in the second ring, in a narrow house in a street of narrow houses that all belonged to the Circle, near enough to the tower that its shadow lay across the roofs at noon and far enough from the cathedral that you could not see it from the door. Emeric had made sure of that from the inn's window before he went, and then had made sure again from the end of the street.

The man who opened the door was old. Not Armand's kind of old, worn thin and bright and short of breath. This was the other kind, heavy and slow and grey all through, with a face that had stopped expecting anything of a door some years ago. He took the letter in his own hand, as Armand had said he must, and looked at the seal, and at the name on it, and at Emeric.

He read it on the step, twice. Snow came down on the paper and he did not brush it off.

"Armand," he said, as if testing whether the name still worked. "I heard he'd gone south."

"He has."

"To die, they said." The old man folded the letter. "He looks well?"

"He looks like Armand."

That got something that was nearly a smile, very far down. The old man put the letter inside his coat.

"He's asked me to show you what he did," he said, "when he was young. He says you'll know what he means when you've seen it, and that I'm not to explain it first, because he never could." He looked at Emeric for a while. "Can you carry two buckets of water up a stair without spilling them?"

"Yes."

"Then come back tonight at the second bell. Wear something you don't mind ruining. Bring nobody inside." He looked past Emeric's shoulder at Ward, who was standing at the end of the street not looking at anything. "He can come as far as the door."



The mages' door was at the back of the Circle's lower building, in a yard full of snow and barrels, under a lamp that hissed. Ward walked him there through the dark streets and stopped at the edge of the lamp's circle and did not come into it. He had his sleeves pulled down over his wrists, all the way, and his hands in his armpits, and he stood with his back to the wall where the snow could not reach him and did not say anything at all.

The old man gave Emeric two leather buckets and a canvas apron and took him in.

Down. A stair, and a corridor, and another stair, stone, worn hollow in the middle of every step by hundreds of years of feet going down and fewer feet coming up. The buckets were full and heavy. The corridor smelled of tallow and wet stone and something under both that he could not name until he was nearly at the bottom, and then could. It was the smell of the ward at Kaldo in the first week after the war. It was the smell of bodies working harder than bodies can.

The room had no windows. It had a door, and a drain in the middle of the floor, and lamps on the walls, and nineteen men lying on the stone in their underclothes in three rows, on nothing.

They were young, most of them. Soldiers, by their hands and their haircuts. They were lying with their arms at their sides and their eyes open, looking at the ceiling, very still, the way men lie still when they have been told to and are frightened of what happens if they don't. At the head of the room, where an altar might have been in another kind of building, a man in a bishop's white sat in a carved chair with a book open on his knees, and did not look up when the door opened.

Eight mages stood along the walls. Not all at once; that was the first thing Emeric understood. Four of them stood at the heads of the rows with their hands out, palms down, a hand's breadth above a man's chest, and four stood back against the wall behind them, waiting, grey, the way the men in the floating gallery waited behind the men who gripped the stones. The old man put Emeric against the wall by the door with the buckets, and took a ladle, and went and stood behind the first of the four.

Then it started.

Emeric did not see anything. There was nothing to see, the first time. The four mages at the heads of the rows leaned, very slightly, all together, and their faces went still, and nothing happened in the air at all.

He felt it in his feet.

It came up through the stone. It was the sense he had spent three months getting back his first winter, the one that told him where a buried thing lay and which way a crack ran, and it had never in his life told him anything like this. It was a wave. Mana, pure, uninstructed, forced down out of the four of them into the men on the floor all at once and much too hard, the way water is forced through a pipe that is too small for it, and it went through the floor and through his boots and up the bones of his legs and he had to put his hand flat on the wall behind him so as not to sit down.

On the floor, nineteen men arched their backs.

Not all of them made a sound. Most of them did. It was not a scream. It was lower than that and it went on longer, and it stopped when the wave stopped, and the four at the heads of the rows straightened and stepped back, and the four from the wall stepped forward and took their places, and the four who had stepped back came along the wall past Emeric to the buckets, grey, shaking, wet through, and drank from his ladle, one after another, without looking at him. One of them spilled most of it down his chin. The next wave came up through the floor before the last of them had finished drinking.

It went on like that.

The old man came back to the wall between the third wave and the fourth to drink, and stood beside Emeric with the ladle in his hand, and spoke without turning his head, in a voice that would not carry past the two of them.

"Nine hours, this one," he said. "Some nights fourteen. It depends how many. It depends how long they last."

Emeric said nothing. He did not trust anything he would have said.

"Every man who goes to the top of the tower takes his turn in this room," the old man said. "It was done before me and it'll be done after me. I've done mine. I'm doing it again. Armand did his. Twenty years ago, near enough. He was the best of us. He came down every night with a book under his arm, and he wrote down every man who lay on that floor, his name and his age and how big he was and how long he lasted and what we did to him and when, because he thought if he wrote it all down he'd find the way to do it so fewer of them died." He drank. "Hundreds of them, by the end. I've seen the books. Nobody else ever read them."

"Did it help?"

"No." The old man put the ladle back in the bucket. "It helped nobody. They died at the same rate with his book as without it. He knew that by the end. He went on writing it down anyway, every night, till his rotation was done, and then he took a post teaching a boy at the palace, and never came back down these stairs." He looked at Emeric then, for the first time since they had come into the room. "You'd be the boy."

The fourth wave came up through the floor. In the second row a man stopped arching his back halfway through it, and lay down flat, and did not move again.

Nobody went to him. The wave ended. The mages changed over. Emeric watched the man in the second row for the whole of the next wave, waiting for him to breathe, and he did not, and at the end of it nobody went to him either.

It was not quick. That was what Emeric had not understood, and understood now, standing against the wall with the ladle. He had thought, if he had thought about it at all, that it would be like a fire or a fall: a thing that happened, and was over. It was not like that. It was work. It was eight tired men doing a job by lamplight in shifts, and nineteen men on a stone floor being done to, wave after wave, through the whole of a winter night, until each of them either broke or did not, and the ones who broke did it slowly, one at a time, at no particular hour, with nobody watching but him.

He counted. He could not stop himself. By the bell that he thought was midnight there were three lying flat. By the one after that, seven. Three were carried out between waves by men who came in from the corridor with a hurdle, still breathing, and did not come back. The bishop turned a page of his book.

Emeric carried water. He filled the buckets at a cistern in the corridor, twice, and came back, and held out the ladle to men who did not see him, and felt every wave in his feet, and did not sit down.



Toward dawn the waves came further apart, and then stopped.

The eight mages stood back against the walls, all eight of them at once now, grey to the lips. On the floor ten men lay flat and six were still breathing, and of the six, four were not going to get up, and Emeric could see which four from the color of them and did not need anyone to tell him.

The bishop closed his book and stood up.

He walked down the rows. He was not an old man and not a young one, and he had a pleasant face, and he walked slowly, as a man walks through a churchyard. At each man lying flat he stopped, and looked down, and said the man's name aloud, the full name, from the book, and then said, in the same pleasant voice, that this man had been weighed under the Light and found wanting, and that the Light had not chosen him, and that his failure was his own. Then he went on to the next. Ten times. He did not hurry any of them, and he did not slow down for any of them either.

Two men got up off the floor at the end of it.

They got up slowly, on their hands first, the way a man gets up out of a river, and stood swaying, and one of them looked down at his own arms. In the lamplight, from the wrist to the elbow, the veins stood up under the skin in raised silver lines, ring over ring, as if someone had drawn them on with a pen dipped in metal. The bishop came back up the rows to them and put his hand on each of their heads in turn and said that the Light had chosen them. The one who had looked at his arms began to cry, and the bishop told him that was proper, and blessed him again.

The old man took the buckets out of Emeric's hands.

"That's it," he said. "That's what he did. Go home."

Emeric did not need persuading of anything. He had not needed it at the top of the stairs, and he did not need it now. He would never send a man down those stairs. He had not considered it once in two and a half years, and if he had lived another two hundred he would not have considered it. The room had not taught him that. He had brought it in with him.

What he carried up the stairs instead, past the old man and out into the yard and the snow, was a young mage with a book under his arm, coming down those steps every night of his rotation with the names of men who were going to die, and writing them down, because it was the only thing in that room he could do.

Ward was where he had left him, against the wall, out of the lamp's circle, with his sleeves down. He looked at Emeric's face and did not ask anything. He took off his own cloak and put it round Emeric's shoulders, over the wet canvas apron, and they walked back to the outer ring through the empty streets in the first grey light, and neither of them said one word the whole way.



The posting house for the south road was beyond the outer ring's gate, and the way to it went down a street of shops that were opening as they passed.

Halfway down, there was a stationer's.

The boy inside was taking the shutters down. In the window, laid out on a strip of green baize, there were pens: quills cut and uncut, a few steel nibs on wooden holders, and in the middle of them, on a little stand of its own, a pen made all of one piece, dark and polished, with a fine gold band round it where the hand would go. It was the kind of pen a person bought for someone who wrote all day and had never in her life owned anything that was only for her.

Emeric stopped and looked at it.

He looked at it for long enough that Ward stopped too, a few paces on, with both horses, and waited.

Then he went on. He did not go in. He had a satchel full of a case that was not a case yet and two years of reasons not to spend a coin on anything that was not a column, and he had come out of that room an hour ago, and it was not a morning for buying anything. But he knew the shop, and the street, and where it was, and he knew he would know them again.

They rode south in a light snow, on post horses, with seven days of road in front of them and the shortest day at the end of it.
