Chapter 78 - The First Turning

The bed of the first lathe had been lying in the yard since the snow.

It had come out of the sand in the second week of the new year, a long iron casting shaped like a trough turned upside down, with two raised rails along its back and a thick foot at either end, and Bram had carried it out of the foundry on a sledge with three men and laid it on timbers against the south wall and left it there. Emeric had not asked him to. When he came across the water in the first real thaw and found it rusting quietly under a skin of meltwater, he asked why.

"A casting moves when you open it," Bram said. "Skin's harder than the heart. Cut the skin off one side and the rest of it pulls. So you leave it out, and it settles, and then you cut it." He looked at the bed without affection. "You'd have told me to in a month. It'd have been a month late."

That was the first correction, and it was made before a single tool had touched the metal, and Emeric went away and thought about it for the rest of the day.

The finishing shop stood on the upstream side of the foundry, under the same roof line, with its own belt run off the wheel. It had gone up in the autumn and been full since the day it was glazed. Bram cleared a bay at the far end of it and built a bench there out of oak planks four fingers thick, and when Emeric asked why so heavy, Bram said that he did not know what was going to happen on it and would rather the bench survived.

He had never seen a lathe of the kind Emeric meant. He had seen the other kind all his life. Every turner in the kingdom had one: two posts, a cord round the work, a springy pole overhead and a treadle underfoot, so that the piece spun one way on the downstroke and back the other on the up, and the turner cut only on the downstroke, holding his chisel against a bar with both hands and his whole body behind it. A good turner could make a chair leg that looked round. A good turner could make two that looked like each other.

"Looked," Emeric said.

"Looked," Bram agreed. "Nobody measures a chair leg."

Emeric drew the thing on the bench with a nail. The bed, and on its back the two rails he called ways. At the left end a headstock with a spindle running through two bearings, driven by the belt. At the right end a tailstock that slid along the ways and locked, to hold the far end of the work. And between them, riding on the ways, a carriage, and on the carriage a small slide that moved across the bed on a screw, and on that slide a clamp that held the cutting edge.

"The man doesn't hold the tool," he said. "The machine holds it. The man turns a handle, and the edge goes in a hair, and the work goes round against it. If the spindle runs true and the ways are straight, whatever comes off is round because the machine is round, and it doesn't matter whether the man is any good."

Bram looked at the drawing for a long time.

"So the skill's in the machine," he said.

"The skill's in making the machine."

"And after that."

"After that, the man has to be careful. He doesn't have to be good."

Bram had been a good man with a file for thirty years. There was a particular stillness that came over him when he understood a thing all the way to the end, and it came over him now, and Emeric watched it arrive and did not say anything, because there was nothing useful to say. Bram was working out what was being taken from him. Then, a little more slowly, he was working out what was being given, which was that the thing he had spent thirty years learning to do by hand could now be done by the boy off the quarry gang, and that the boy would do it the same every time, and that Bram himself would be free to do whatever came after.

"Well," Bram said eventually. "Somebody has to make the machine."

They made the first one at night, because Emeric could not afford to be seen doing it.

The difficulty was the one he had been turning over since before the covenant lapsed, and it was very simple. A lathe that cuts true needs ways that are straight and a spindle that is round. To make a straight way you need a machine that moves in a straight line. To make a round spindle you need a lathe. Every tool in the world was cut on a tool, and the first one had to be cut on nothing.

So the first one would be cut on him.

He came down to the shop after dark, with the belt thrown off and the shutters closed and a single lamp, and Bram came with him, and Ferra came as far as the door and stood a marked candle on the shelf inside it and went away again. She had logged every session in the delve for eight years, and for most of them she had logged the awake sessions under a different mark from the rest, because their cost was not the same and she had noticed that before he had.

He sat on a stool at the end of the bench with his mother's stone in his left hand, and reached for Bram, and felt the old man's stone answer against the skin of his chest where he wore it now, and Bram came into the overlay without a flicker, the way he always did.

The overlay was not the room above Kaldo. It was the shop, exactly as it was, with the lamp and the bench and the bed of the lathe lying on its timbers, and laid over it, standing in the same air, was the lathe as it would be when it was finished. Emeric had built that lathe in the delve over the whole of the winter, a line at a time, until every dimension of it was a fact. It stood there now, pale and sharp-edged, with its ways running straight as a ruled line through the dark iron of the real ones, and where the two did not agree, the disagreement was plain to see.

"There," Bram said, and pointed, and his finger went through the true way and into the real one. "It dips. A hair, but it dips."

"I see it."

He took hold of the second of his three drills, the blade of moving air, and brought it down, and down, until it was not a blade at all but an edge thinner than paper, and laid that edge against the iron.

It was slow. It was slower than anything he had ever done with his hands. He drew the edge along the way, and a curl of iron no thicker than a hair came up off it, and he drew it again, and in the overlay the real way crept towards the true one a shaving at a time. Outside, the lamp burned down by the width of a fingernail. Inside, he worked for what felt like the better part of a day, and did one rail.

Bram watched. After a while he put out his hand towards the iron.

"I'll hold it steady for you."

"No."

"It's shifting. I can feel it through the bench. Let me put a hand on it."

"No." Emeric did not take his eyes off the edge. "If this goes wrong, I'll feel it go a breath before it does. You won't. You'll feel it when it's already happened." He drew the edge again. "I'm not putting your hands where only I can see what's coming."

Bram took his hand back. He did not argue. He stood at the other end of the bench, which Emeric had half hoped he would, and watched the curls come off the iron.

It was on the fourth night, with both ways done and the headstock bored, that Bram found the thing Emeric had missed.

They were on the spindle. It was a bar of good wrought iron as thick as a wrist, set up between two rough centres on a wooden frame with a cord round it, and Bram was turning it by hand, slowly, while Emeric held the edge still against it and let the bar come round. It was going well. The real spindle was almost exactly the true one, all along its length, and Emeric was beginning to think about the end of it.

"It's warm," Bram said.

"It would be."

"Iron grows when it's warm." Bram took his hand off the crank and laid his palm flat on the bar, where the edge had just been. "Not so you'd see. But you're cutting it to your picture while it's hot, and your picture's cold. Let it sit till morning and it'll shrink off your picture all the way along."

Emeric sat with that. It was true. He knew it was true, and he knew it in the way he knew the fact about glass and wood fires, without being able to say where it had come from, and he had not thought of it once. He had been measuring warm iron against a cold drawing for four nights.

"The ways," he said.

"Were cool. You cut them slow." Bram shrugged. "The spindle you've been at for two hours, one place."

So they stopped, and let it cool, and in the morning the spindle was a hair under the drawing at the place where it had been hottest, and they cut it again, a little and then let it sit, a little and then let it sit, over three more nights, measuring it cold against the master rule each morning with a pair of calipers Bram filed specially for the purpose and would let nobody else touch. It was tedious beyond anything, and it was right.

The overload came on the seventh night, on the screw.

The cross-slide needed a screw to move it, and a screw was the hardest thing on the whole machine, because it was a spiral and every turn of it had to be the same as every other turn. He had built the true one in the delve as a single helix of perfect pitch, and in the overlay it stood through the plain iron bar like a coiled thread of light. He set the edge at the start of it and began to follow it round.

He was tired. He knew he was tired and he went on, because he was more than halfway along and did not want to set it up twice. The edge was cutting a groove and the groove was deepening, turn after turn, and somewhere towards the far end the iron changed under it, a hard spot in the bar, a knot in the metal that the edge did not so much cut as catch on.

He felt it a breath before it happened, exactly as he had told Bram he would. It did not help.

The edge caught and the bar tried to climb it, and he poured more into the blade to hold it, which was the wrong thing and he knew it was the wrong thing as he did it, and the shape of the blade went. What had been an edge thinner than paper came apart into a spray of moving air with far too much behind it, and it went sideways across the bench.

It took a strip off the oak the width of two fingers and the length of a forearm. It left the wood black and smoking along the whole track. It went straight through the place at the end of the bench where a man would stand to hold a bar steady with both hands.

Emeric came out of the overlay grey, with his head ringing like a struck pan and the taste of iron in his mouth. The lamp was still burning. The candle on Ferra's shelf had gone down by barely a notch.

Bram was standing where he had stood every night since the first, at the far end of the bench. He was looking at the track in the wood. He did not say anything. After a while he walked round and put his hand flat on the scorched oak, in the place where his hands would have been, and left it there until the wood had stopped smoking.

"That," Emeric said, when he could, "is why the second one gets cut by this one and not by me."

"Aye," Bram said.

The screw was ruined. He cut another, the next night, rested, with a new bar, and it came out true.

On the fifteenth day of spring the first lathe stood on the scorched bench with the belt on it, and Bram put a bar of iron between its centres and turned the handle on the cross-slide, and a long bright curl came off the bar and dropped to the floor, and the bar went on turning, and the curl went on coming, and when Bram stopped the belt and put the calipers on it the bar was the same thickness at one end as it was at the other.

Bram measured it four times in four places. Then he measured it again.

"Well," he said.

That was all. Emeric had not expected more and would not have known what to do with it.

It was not a good lathe. Emeric could see everything that was wrong with it. The ways were straight but not as straight as he wanted, the spindle ran true but had a little play in its bearings, and the screw, though it was a fine screw, was the only one in the world and he did not entirely trust it. It would cut parts that were good enough, and no better. That was all it was for.

"Now," he said, "we make one that's better than this."

"With this."

"With this. And with Neta and Jorrin."

Bram looked at him, and then looked at the lathe, and Emeric understood what he was being asked without either of them having to say it. What was about to go into those two apprentices' hands was not a trade. It was the thing that made trades, and once it was in a person's hands it did not come back out again, and he had already once watched a man in this barony turned into a door.

"Give me a day," Emeric said.

He went to Ferra in her room in the keep, where she had three clerks and a window and, since the first week of the winter, a locked box that she did not open in front of the clerks.

She heard him out. Then she said, "Together, it says," and sent a boy for Ward.

Ward came up with a leather satchel under his arm that he did not open, and put it on the table in front of Ferra, and went and stood by the window with his back to both of them, which was his way of being in a room and not in it. Ferra unbuckled the satchel. The pages inside were dense and even, and even the hand was not one he knew. She had written the whole file in a hand that was not her own. It meant nothing to him at all. Every line of them was in her cipher. He had never been given it and he had not asked.

She did not write anything down. She read a page with her lips closed, slowly, the way a person reads a letter in a language they learned late, and then she looked up and told him what it said.

"Neta. Thirty. On the soap rota at twenty-two, before that nothing on any roll. Mother died in the plague year. No father on record. One brother, Pim, who drives cattle for Paxton's factor at Milford and comes through at the spring fair. She owes nobody. Three people owe her, small sums, and she's never pressed any of them. She argues with Bram and wins about twice a year." Ferra did not smile. "Twice since the war a carter out of Wayford has stood her a drink at the spring fair and asked what the foundry's pouring. Both times she told him iron. Both times she told Ward the same day."

Ward did not turn round. "She did."

"Jorrin," Ferra said, and turned the page. "Twenty-five. Off the quarry gang. Mother living, keeps house on the long street. Father living. The father drinks, and has a slate at the tap-house on the quay that stood at eleven weeks at midwinter. Jorrin has been paying it down since the autumn. A little each week, in coin, in person, when his father isn't there to see it. The keeper's never told the father, and neither has he." She laid the page flat. "If anybody wanted Jorrin, that's where they'd take hold. Not him. The slate."

Emeric sat with that for longer than he meant to.

He had liked Jorrin for seven years, on his face, the way he liked most people on theirs. He had thought of him as the lad Bram took off the quarry gang for no reason anybody could see and who had turned out to have a gift for patience. He had not known about the slate. He would never have known about the slate. It was the kind of thing a man carried quietly for years so that nobody would know it, and now it had been written down by somebody he did not know, and read aloud to him by Bram's daughter out of her own head, and the young man it belonged to was down in the shop sweeping the scorched bench and had no idea that anybody in the keep was saying his name.

It was the first time he had been told about a person by a document. It was accurate, and it was useful, and he did not like it at all, and none of those three things cancelled any of the others.

"I could clear the slate," he said. "Quietly. Then there's nothing to take hold of."

"You could," Ferra said. She was buckling the satchel. "That's exactly what somebody else would do, if they wanted him. Pay what his father owes and let him know who paid it."

He thought about that, and found that she was right, and that he had not seen it, and that the file had just told him what the handle was and he had reached for it inside a minute.

"Leave it," he said.

"It's left." She handed the satchel back to Ward without looking at him, and Ward took it and went out, and neither of them had said a word to each other the whole time.

"Both of them," Emeric said. "Yes."

"I didn't say yes. I read you a page."

"I know," he said. "I'm the one saying it."

Neta and Jorrin cut the second lathe on the first, and it took them the rest of the spring.

Emeric showed them how, and then he mostly stood back, because the whole point was that he should not be necessary. The second lathe's spindle came off the first lathe's centres. Its screw was cut with the first lathe's screw driving the carriage, so that the new thread was a copy of the old one. Its bearings were bored on the first lathe's cross-slide. Everything that could be cut, was cut, by the machine, with an apprentice turning a handle.

The ways were the part that mattered, and for the ways he did not use the first lathe at all.

He had Bram pour three iron plates, each the size of a large book, and had the faces of all three roughed flat on the first lathe as best it could manage. Then he gave them to Neta, with a pot of lampblack ground in oil and a set of scrapers Bram had forged to his drawing, and showed her what to do. Smear a thin coat of the black on one plate. Lay a second plate face down on it and rub. Lift it. Wherever the black has come off onto the second plate, that is a high spot. Scrape the high spots down, a sliver at a time. Then do the same with the second and third, and then the third and first, round and round, never the same pair twice running.

Neta did it for two days before she asked why three.

"With two," Emeric said, "you can make one a hill and the other a hollow and they'll fit each other perfectly, and neither of them's flat. With three, the only way all three can fit each other is if all three are flat." He picked one up. It was heavy and dull and already a good deal better than the first lathe's ways. "Nothing tells them what flat is. They find it between them."

She looked at the plates for a while, and then at him.

"So they get better than what made them," she said.

"Yes."

"That's not right."

"It's right," Emeric said. "It just isn't fair."

She went back to scraping, and she did not stop for three weeks, and her hands did not shake. At the end of it the three plates would lie on each other and wring together so tightly that Jorrin could lift the bottom one by the top, and the ways of the second lathe were scraped to them, and they were straighter than the first lathe's by a margin Emeric could see in the overlay without looking hard.

He stood in the finishing shop on the day the second lathe first turned under power, with both apprentices and Bram and three men from the foundry floor who had drifted over to watch, and said the thing he had been waiting since the winter to say.

"Everything from this one on is ordinary." He put his hand on the second lathe's headstock. "There's no stone in it. There's nothing of me in it. If I went off the Left Bridge tomorrow, the two of you could make the third one, and the third one would be better than this, and the fourth better than that. Nobody needs to do what I did on that bench ever again." He looked at the scorch. "I'd rather nobody did."

The three men from the foundry floor looked at one another. Jorrin looked at the lathe. Neta, who had spent three weeks being told that iron could be made better than the thing that made it, looked at Emeric for a long moment with an expression he could not read, and then went back to work.

The gauges came next, because a lathe that turns true is no use if every man measures with his own rule.

The master foot hung in its case in the yard where it had hung since the evening classes, cut in iron by Bram, twelve divisions, never to be redefined, with copies of copies forbidden. On the second lathe, over a week, Jorrin turned a set of short iron rods against it: a foot, a half, a quarter, an inch. Each one was checked against the master and against nothing else. Emeric had him stamp each one with the master's mark and a number, and they went into a box lined with felt, and the box went into the finishing shop on a shelf of its own, and every calliper in the foundry was set from them.

And then, at last, the bearing.

He had shown it to Bram in the room above Kaldo, in the air, turning, opened up like a cut fruit. Now they made it in iron, and it was a good deal less beautiful and took a great deal longer.

Jorrin turned the two rings on the second lathe, an outer and an inner, each with a groove cut round its face for the balls to run in. The balls were the hard part. Bram forged a dozen iron beads by eye, as round as a good smith's eye could make them, which was not round enough. Emeric had Neta roll them between two iron plates with grooves cut in their faces, with emery and oil, round and round, for days, changing the direction every hour, until every bead was the same size as every other and all of them were rounder than anything anybody in the barony had ever held. Then Bram packed them in an iron box with charcoal and burned bone and put the box in the furnace for a day, and quenched them, so that they came out with a skin as hard as a file and a tough heart, the way he had hardened plough points for thirty years. Then Neta rolled them again.

Emeric put it together himself, on the bench, in the afternoon light, with a thin brass ring to hold the balls apart so that they did not rub on one another. It took him a long time, because his hands were not as good as Neta's. Then he gave it to Bram.

Bram held the outer ring in the flat of one hand. He put one finger of the other hand through the inner ring and turned it.

It turned. It went on turning after he took his finger away, round and round in his palm, without a sound, and without the smallest wobble, until at last it slowed and stopped of its own accord.

He turned it again. It did the same.

Emeric had been waiting all spring for Bram to ask the question again. He had been dreading it, and he had been, if he was honest, half hoping for it, because a question asked twice could at least be answered with something, even if it was the wrong thing. How long have you known.

Bram did not ask it. He held the bearing and watched it turn, and Emeric saw him decide not to ask, saw the question come up and be looked at and be put away again, and that was worse. The question had been a thing between them. The decision was a thing Bram was going to carry by himself.

Then the bearing turned true in Bram's hand one more time, and Bram's face did something that was not about the bearing at all, and Emeric looked at the second lathe until it was finished, and decided he had seen nothing.
