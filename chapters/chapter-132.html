Chapter 132 - The Cursed Shaft

Hask's crew had been driving the crosscut for five weeks when Emeric came back up the river.

Hask was Grayrock's oldest working miner, a short square man with a grey beard cut close and a face like the end of a pick handle, who had been underground since he was nine and had stood in the east working's gallery, at the age of twenty-two, over the first two men the curse had taken. He had not gone down it since. Nobody had. When Bryda sent for him in October and told him what the new holder wanted, he had listened to all of it standing in the hall with his cap in his hands, and then said that he would drive the holder a crosscut from the Low Drift to the foot of the cursed shaft, because that was rock and he understood rock, but that he would not go into the shaft, and nor would any man of his, and the holder could hang him for it if he liked.

Emeric had said that nobody would go into the shaft who did not choose to, and Hask had looked at him for some time and then gone away to choose his crew.

The Low Drift was the deepest live working on Grayrock's east hill, a long level gallery that had been worked for ore since before Renner's father and never closed, because the curse had never touched it. It ran along the hill a little above the sealed working and to one side of it, and the survey in Renner's box showed where the two came nearest: a hundred and forty feet of rock, slantwise, from a bend in the Low Drift to the foot of the old shaft, where the cursed galleries went off it into the dark.

A hundred and forty feet. Five weeks.

It should have been three. The survey called the rock slate. It was not slate. It was a hard grey stone with quartz through it in white veins that turned the edge of a pick in an hour and rang like a bell when it was struck, and Hask's crew had cursed it by every saint in the south and some from elsewhere, and driven through it a foot and a half a day, in shifts, by candle, with the spoil going back along the Low Drift in tubs on the old wooden rails Odo Pike had laid forty years before.

"Your surveyor never came this deep," Hask said, the morning Emeric came down the Low Drift to see. He did not say it with any particular feeling. "He drew slate because slate's what's on top. He drew what he thought was here."

"Paskin could have shaped it for you," said Emeric. "One of Armand's mages. A week."

"Aye. And then I'd have a hole I didn't cut through rock I didn't feel." Hask looked at the face of the crosscut in the candle light, grey and white and wet. "I'll know this rock now. Every foot. If it moves, I'll hear it before it does." He spat. "Your mage won't be down here when it moves."

Emeric did not argue. It was what he would have said.

They broke through on the second of December, at the end of the night shift. Hask's pick went through the last hand's width of the quartz rock and into nothing, and a breath of air came back through the hole into the crosscut, cold and stale and smelling of nothing at all, and the candle on the ledge beside Hask's head leaned away from it, and shrank, and went blue at the edges.

Hask came back along the crosscut with the candle in his hand, walking, not running, the way a man walks who has been underground since he was nine and knows what running does to a man's breath. He came out into the Low Drift where Emeric was waiting and held the candle up between them. It was burning again, small and yellow.

"That," said Hask. "That's what's down there."

"I know."

"That's the curse." He looked at the candle. "It came out of the hole and laid my light down like a hand. And there's no smell. And no wind. Just that." He looked at Emeric. "What is it?"



Emeric told him, and Ward, at the head of the old shaft, in the cold, on the morning of the third, with the boards off the shaft mouth and the shaft going down into the dark in front of them, square and black and lined with old timber.

"It's not cursed," he said. "It's spent."

Ward looked at the shaft.

"Air's made of more than one thing," said Emeric. "The part that keeps a fire burning and keeps a man alive is only a part of it. Down there, in a closed working, with old wood rotting on every prop in every gallery for twenty years, the rot's eaten that part out. What's left is the rest. It looks like air. It smells like nothing. And it's heavier than good air." He crouched at the edge of the shaft and held his hand out over the dark. "So it doesn't blow away. It lies in the bottom of the working like water in a well. Pooled. Still. Up to a level. And a man walks down into it and doesn't know he's in it, because it doesn't feel like anything, and he gets tired and sits down." He looked up at Ward. "That's the curse."

"And the candle," said Ward.

"A flame needs the same part of the air a man does. It needs it more. So it goes out first. Lower a candle down that shaft on a string and where it dies, that's the top of the pool." He stood up. "Every miner in the old books carried a light for that reason. The light's the gauge. Watch the light."

Hask was standing behind them with his crew. They had come up out of the Low Drift to see the boards come off, eight of them, with their caps in their hands, at the back of the shaft head, as close to the edge as they would come.

"Then how do you get it out?" said Hask.

"You burn it out."

He had them build a chimney. Boards, from the old shaft's own cover, nailed into a square trunk a foot across and as tall as two men, standing up on the shaft head beside the shaft mouth over the end of the crosscut's return, so that the crosscut and the shaft made a loop: down the shaft, along the old gallery, out through Hask's hole, back along the crosscut into the Low Drift, and up. And at the foot of the chimney, in the Low Drift, where the return came in, an iron fire basket full of Grayrock's coal, lit.

The fire drew. It pulled its air up the chimney from the crosscut behind it, and the crosscut pulled from Hask's hole, and the hole pulled from the old gallery, and the old gallery pulled from the shaft, and the shaft pulled from the sky. Good air went down the open shaft mouth. Spent air came out through the crosscut and was dragged through the fire and up the chimney and out into the December morning in a column of coal smoke, and gone.

Then, on a chain, down the shaft, a second fire basket. Lowered a yard at a time, behind the dead line, following it down as it sank.

"No mana," said Emeric, to Hask, at the shaft head, while the basket went down. "No stone. No mage. No prince. A fire and a chimney. Any man in this barony can build one, and every man in it should know how, before he goes into an old working." He watched the chain pay out. "That's all it is."

Hask watched the fire basket glowing in the dark of the shaft, a yard lower every hour, and the coal smoke going up out of the board chimney beside him into the cold.

"It's a stove," he said, slowly.

"It's a stove."

Hask turned round to his crew at the back of the shaft head. "It's a stove," he told them, as though they had not heard. And the crew looked at the chimney, and at the chain, and at each other, and Emeric saw something go out of their faces that had been in them since the boards came off, and stay out.



It ran four days. The basket went down the shaft a yard an hour by day and slower by night, and the column of coal smoke went up out of the chimney into the winter sky above Grayrock's east hill and the whole of Grayrock came out on the hill road to look at it. On the evening of the sixth the basket on its chain reached the foot of the shaft, ninety feet down, and sat there on the bottom in the mouth of the old gallery, burning, yellow, steady.

The shaft was clear.

Emeric went down the ladder in the morning with a lamp to the foot of the shaft, where the old gallery went off into the hill, and held the lamp out into the gallery mouth.

It burned. He took a step in. It burned. A second step. A third.

On the third step the flame leaned, and shrank, and went blue, and went out.

He stepped back. The lamp, back in the shaft, took light again from the fire basket's coals.

He stood at the foot of the shaft in the cold with the dead lamp in his hand and looked down the gallery.

It went downhill. That was the whole of the trouble, and he had known it from the survey and hoped it would not matter. The gallery went off the shaft foot and down, into the hill, at a slope a man could walk without noticing, and the fire's draught came down the shaft and turned along the bottom and went out through Hask's hole and never went into the gallery at all, because there was nothing at the gallery's far end to draw it. The spent air in the gallery lay there, below the line of the draught, pooled, in the dark, the length of the gallery, untouched.

To clear it they would have to screen the gallery: a curtain of canvas along its whole length, splitting it down the middle, so the draught went in on one side and came back on the other. And to hang a screen the length of the gallery, a man would have to go the length of the gallery to hang it.

And nobody knew how long the gallery was. Or whether it forked.



"Then nobody goes in," said Ward, at the shaft head, that evening.

"Somebody goes in."

"Then not you."

"Then who?" Emeric looked at him. "Hask? He'll go if I ask him. He'd go and he'd hang a screen in the dark with his breath held and he'd get forty feet and go to sleep, and I'd have to go in after him anyway, because I'd be the only man on this hill who could tell where the air was bad and where it wasn't." He kept his voice down. The crew was at the hut by the hill road, at their supper. "I won't send a man into a danger only I can read. That's the rule. I've had it since the first well. I'm not changing it in a hole in Grayrock."

"You are the barony," said Ward. "You are four baronies. You are every stone in the wall and the board and the forge you mean to build, and the only man in Kaldo who knows what is in the room." He did not raise his voice either. "The table said: nothing wakes one man. You are the one man everything wakes."

Emeric did not answer that.

He did not answer it because Ward was right, and because Armand held the stones' second key now, and because the first act of the Standing Order had been for exactly this, and he had sat at the table and lost the vote and been glad to lose it. And still, at the foot of the shaft, with the gallery going down into the dark in front of him, there was nobody on the hill but him who could do it, and he knew it, and Ward knew it, and Ward had said the thing that needed saying by somebody because it was his work to say it, and had not expected to win.

Ward looked at him a while longer.

"Then I hold the rope," Ward said.



Ward set the terms. He set them at the shaft head at first light on the seventh, in the cold, with a coil of new rope over his shoulder that Hask's crew had brought up from the quay and Hask had gone over himself, every fathom, for chafe.

The rope round Emeric's chest, under his arms, tied by Ward, not by Emeric. Ward at the gallery mouth, at the foot of the shaft, in the good air, with the other end of it round his own waist and his hands on it. Two tugs, and Ward hauled. No tug at all for a count Ward would keep, and Ward hauled. Emeric did not argue with any of it. He had expected to, and found when the time came that he did not want to, and knew why, and did not say: that he did not want to go down that gallery alone.

"And why not a stone," said Ward, at the gallery mouth, with the rope in his hands, and the fire basket burning behind them at the shaft foot. "You have a chest of them. You have a woman at the House who would sell you more. Why not a stone that blows the bad air out of the hole before you walk into it?"

"Because a wind stone only moves air," said Emeric. "It doesn't clean it. It'd stir the bad air up off the floor and mix it with the good and make the whole gallery bad from top to bottom." He checked the knot on his chest. "And because the gallery has one mouth. Blow into a hole with one mouth and the air goes in and comes straight back out past the stone. It never reaches the end." He looked down the dark of the gallery. "There's a stone I could make that would scrub the air. Take the bad out of it and leave the good. I've thought about it. I've never made it. I've never tried it." He looked at Ward. "And when a stone fails, it fails like the air down there fails. Silently. You don't know it's stopped until you're asleep." He put his hand on the rope. "This doesn't fail like that. If it parts, you'll feel it part."

Ward looked at him across the rope.

"You have another thing," Ward said. "Besides the stones."

"Yes."

"You will use it."

"Yes."

"Then why the rope?"

"For when it stops," said Emeric.

Ward did not say anything else. He took a turn of the rope round his own forearm, over the black Kaldo iron of his bracer, and set his feet.



He let the sense out first.

He did it from the gallery mouth, standing in the good air beside Ward, with his eyes shut: the way Armand had taught him by the fire, the way he had kept it open thin round himself all day every day since the spring. He let it go further than he ever had in daylight. Down the gallery. Into the dark. Not seeing it. Knowing it, the way a dark room comes back to a man who claps his hands.

The gallery came back to him.

The floor going down, steadily, on the slant. The old props on either side, rotten, soft, like wet bread under his sense, every one of them, and the roof above them sagging between the worst. Forty paces down, a little more, a fork: the gallery split, left and right, the left going on downhill and narrowing, the right going level and stopping short at a wall of loose fall twenty paces on. And on the floor of the left branch, ten paces past the fork, the rails. Odo Pike's rails. Two lines of rotted oak, with a tub still standing on them, tipped on its side.

And below it all, below the floor of the gallery, below the rock under the floor, a long way down, the thing.

It was not like anything he had felt. Not a stone, not a note, not a warmth. It was a weight. A heaviness in the hill that went down and down and along and along, the length of the hill and more, under everything, so deep and so large that he could not feel its edges. And as he felt it, it felt him. He knew it did. The deep quiet thing in him that Armand said sounded like a stone falling down a well turned over, all at once, toward it, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice saying his name.

He opened his eyes.

"Forty paces," he said. "A fork. Left goes on, and down. Right's blocked twenty paces in." He did not know his voice had gone strange until he heard it. "And it's under all of it. All of it, Ward."

Ward's hands did not move on the rope.

"Go," he said.

He took his breath. Not a great gulp. A good breath, slow, of the clean air the fire had drawn down the shaft, the way a man breathes before he dives off a rock into a river he knows. And with it, as he let it fill him, he did the other thing: the thing Linnet had taught him in bay three, that the air was a crowd and not a wind, and you did not push a crowd, you made a way for it.

He made a way for it round his head.

A crowd of the good air, the fire's air, the shaft's, gathered and held round his face and his shoulders like a hood, close, the size of a bucket, and told to stay. Not pushed. Held. He could feel it there, cool on his skin, the way he could feel the warmth of a fire on one side of his face. It cost him. Air cost him three times what fire did, and holding a crowd still was harder than moving one. He could feel the cost going out of him in a steady draw, like a lamp stone on a long night.

He walked into the gallery with the hood of good air round his head, and the rope going out behind him through Ward's hands, and no lamp, because he did not need one.



He did not need to see. He knew where everything was. The floor going down under his boots. The rotten props on either side, soft, every one of them, and the roof between them, and the one place twenty paces in where the roof had come down a little and he had to stoop. He felt the bad air round him: it was nothing, it was the absence of the thing the hood was made of, and it pressed in at the edges of the hood, all round, like cold water round a swimmer's face, and did not get in.

He breathed. He breathed the hood. It held.

The fork at forty-one paces. He took the left. The floor went down more steeply, and narrowed, and the rails were there under his feet, soft as moss, and the tub on its side, and he stepped round it. Ten paces. Twenty. The hood was thinner. He could feel it thinning, the good air in it going, breathed, used, the way the air goes out of a room with a fire in it and the door shut. He made a way for more. There was no more. There was nothing round him but the spent air, and he could not make a crowd out of nothing.

Ten breaths left in it. Perhaps twelve.

He knew where the end of the gallery was. He had felt it from the mouth: forty paces past the fork, a face of rock where the old miners had stopped, and on the floor at the foot of the face, lying in the rubble where somebody's pick had prised it out twenty years ago and left it because he was already too tired to bend down, a stone.

He went to it. He did not hurry. A man who hurries in bad air breathes faster, and he had ten breaths.

It was the size of his fist. He knew it before he touched it. It sat on the floor of the gallery among the broken quartz and pressed upward, very slightly, against nothing; and when he crouched and closed his hand on it, it pressed against his fingers, up, and the thing in him that had turned over at the mouth of the gallery turned toward it and was quiet.

He held it. Six breaths.

He turned round.

Turning back was the hardest thing he had ever done. It was not the bad air, or the dark, or the rope. It was the thing under the floor. He could feel it below him, all of it, the length of the hill, so close now, under his boots, under the rubble, under a few yards of rock, and the thing in him wanted to stay. Wanted to go down to it. Wanted it the way a man in a desert wants the sound of water. He stood at the face of the old gallery with the stone in his fist and four breaths in the hood and felt it pull at him, from below, from inside, and for the length of one of those four breaths he did not move.

Then he tugged the rope. Twice. Hard.

And walked.



He felt the water halfway back to the fork.

Not heard. Felt. The same way he felt the weight under the floor: a heaviness, in the rock to his right, behind the right-hand wall of the left branch, where the survey showed nothing. A great deal of heaviness, very close, lying in the rock, and pressing. And moving. As he passed it, it moved. A shift, all at once, through the whole of it, the way a full cart shifts on a slope when the brake is taken off and has not yet begun to roll.

An old working. Flooded. Lying in the hill behind the gallery wall, full to its roof with twenty years of water, behind a skin of rock that Hask's crew had been ringing with their picks for five weeks on the other side of the hill and four days of fire had dried and heated and cracked.

And it was coming.

He did not stop. He did not run. He walked faster, two breaths in the hood, the rope coming in ahead of him through Ward's hands at the gallery mouth, steady, the way Ward hauled, and he felt the pressure behind the wall to his right build, and build, and the rock between him and it go thin, and he was at the fork, and past it, and the gallery was going up under his boots toward the shaft and the good air and Ward, and he had one breath in the hood, and he let the hood go, because there was nothing left in it to hold, and walked the last twenty paces on held breath with the rope going taut ahead of him.

He came out of the gallery mouth into the shaft foot and the fire basket's light and Ward's hands on him, and breathed.

"Up," he said. "Now. The water's coming."

Ward did not ask what water. Ward put him on the ladder ahead of him and came up behind him with the rope still round them both, and they went up the shaft ladder as fast as two men can go up ninety feet of ladder in the dark, and Emeric felt the water go behind them when they were sixty feet up.

He felt it go through the wall of the left branch the way a fist goes through a wet paper window. All of it. At once.

The sound came up the shaft after them. A long low roar, like a weir in flood, from the bottom of the hill, and then the air: a great breath of it, pushed up out of the gallery ahead of the water, out of the shaft foot, up the shaft past them, hot and cold at once and stinking of twenty years of rot, so hard it took Emeric's hair and lifted it and he had to hold the ladder with both arms. The fire basket at the shaft foot below them went out. Then the water came into the shaft foot with a sound like a building falling down, and filled it, and began to come up.

Not fast. It was a big hill and a long gallery and the old working had a great deal of room to fill. But it came. Ward, below him on the ladder, looked down at it once, black and churning in the dark at the bottom of the shaft, and then up at Emeric, and said nothing, and climbed.



The shaft head was in uproar.

The rush of air had come up out of the shaft mouth like a gale and thrown the board chimney over and torn the canvas off the crew's hut on the hill road. And the winding frame over the shaft, the old frame of timber legs and a cross beam that had stood over the east working since before Renner's father and held the rope and the bucket for lifting spoil, had been rocked on its legs by the blast. Its old timbers were as rotten as the props in the gallery. Emeric felt them go before he saw them, as his head came up out of the shaft mouth into the daylight: two of the four legs, on the downhill side, cracked through at the foot, both, and the whole frame leaning, slowly, over, toward the downhill side, where the winding boy stood.

The winding boy was Hask's grandson. Twelve. He had been at the windlass, because the windlass was his, and he was standing with his hands still on its handles staring at the shaft mouth with his mouth open, and the frame was coming over on top of him, the cross beam the thickness of a man's body and twice his length, slowly, the way a tree comes down, and the boy had not looked up.

Emeric was half out of the shaft mouth with Ward's rope round his chest and his legs still on the ladder and the stone in his fist, and he was too far, and he knew it, and he reached anyway.

He did not get there.

Somebody else did.

The frame stopped.

It stopped in the air, leaning, the cross beam an arm's length above the boy's head, as if it had come down on something that was not there. A pillow of nothing. It stopped and hung, and creaked, all its old timbers groaning, and did not come down any further.

Vantic was standing twenty paces away on the hill road, in front of the miners' hut, where she had been standing all morning with her book because the mine was the House's security now and the House's factor had come up the river to watch what was done with it. She was not holding her book. It was on the ground at her feet, open, face down, in the mud. Her left glove was off. It was in her other hand. And her bare left hand was out in front of her, open, palm toward the frame, fingers spread, quite steady.

And in the air in front of her palm, fading as he saw it, so faint that in the grey December daylight he could not be sure he saw it at all, a shape. Lines. A sign, written on the air in a light the colour of a lamp stone's, a glyph, the way a mage's spell wrote itself before it took, the way his own fire did behind the shutters. Fading. Gone.

The boy at the windlass looked up at last, at the beam above his head, and screamed, and ran.

The frame came down. It came down slowly, as if somebody were letting it down, and lay across the windlass and the empty place where the boy had been, and settled in the mud with a long creak, and was still.

Vantic lowered her hand.

She stood on the hill road with her bare hand at her side. Then she put the glove back on. Finger by finger. Not quickly. And bent and picked up her book out of the mud, and wiped its cover on her coat, and stood holding it.

Nobody was looking at her. Everybody on the shaft head was looking at the boy, who had run into his grandfather's arms by the hut, or at the frame lying in the mud, or at the shaft mouth, where the roar from below was still coming up and the stink with it, and where the holder of Grayrock was climbing out of the cursed shaft alive, with Ward behind him, with a stone in his fist.

Emeric looked at Vantic.

She looked back at him. Across twenty paces of the shaft head, in the mud and the stink and the shouting. She looked at him for as long as it took to read a line. She did not look away. She did not look frightened. She looked like a woman who had written something on paper she could be faulted for, knowing it, and was waiting to see what would be done with it.

He did not do anything with it.

He looked away first. He turned to Ward, and to Hask, who was coming across the shaft head with his grandson behind him, and said something about the water, and how long it would take to fill the old working and stop, and what they would need to pump it, and did not look back at the hill road at all.

He did not say anything to anyone about it. Not that day. Not on the river going home. Not to Ward, who had been below him on the ladder and seen nothing, and not to Ferra, and not to the table.

And that evening, at Bryda's long table, when Vantic came in late to supper with her book under her arm and her gloves on and sat down at the far end, he saw her look at him once, down the length of the table, quickly, and away. And he saw her see that he had said nothing. And neither of them ever mentioned it.



The miners had seen him come out.

That was the thing he had not reckoned on, and could not undo. Hask had crossed the crosscut that morning against his own orders, the one thing he had said he would not do, because he had felt his rock move through his boots in the Low Drift and come to see. He had come up the Low Drift's ladder onto the shaft head as Emeric came out of the shaft mouth, and he had seen it: the holder of Grayrock coming up out of the cursed working with the roar of the water behind him and the stink of the curse coming up past him in a gale, alive, walking, with a grey stone in his fist that sat in his open palm when he opened it and pressed upward, very gently, as if it would have liked to rise.

By the evening the whole of Grayrock had it. The prince had gone down the cursed shaft where five men had died, and walked through the curse, and come out of it with the water of the whole hill after him, and brought up a stone that floated.

On the hill road on the morning they left, an old woman Emeric had never seen came out of a cottage door as he went by, and caught at his sleeve, and pressed it to her forehead, and said a word he did not catch, and let go.

He caught it the second time, from a miner's wife at the quay, and the third time from a carter, and by the time Somebody Came had cast off from the bend below the spur he had heard it a dozen times.

Blessed.

He hated it. He stood at the rail going down the river with the stone in his coat and Hask's grandson's face in his head and hated it more than anything that had been said to him in four years. It was a stove. He had told them it was a stove. He had built them a chimney out of their own boards and told them any man in the barony could do it.

"They'll not hear it," said Ward, beside him. "Not now. You came out." He looked at the river. "They will have to watch you be ordinary for a long time before they hear it."



Armand had not waited.

Emeric found that out at the water gate, coming home on the twelfth, before he was off the boat. Linnet was on the river stair, waiting, with her shawl over her head, and her face told him before she did.

He had left Renner's three samples with Armand when they went up the river, in their leather bag, because they were small and Armand had asked, and because the old man held the stones' second key now and it had seemed right. And Armand had held them in his hand for a day. And on the second day, alone, with nobody to take him and nobody at the door, he had gone into the room with one of them, by himself, from his chair by the fire, because he had to know what it was and could not wait for Emeric to come home and show him.

He had measured it. All of it. He had been gone, Linnet said, from the middle of the afternoon until she came in at dusk with his supper and found him in the chair with his eyes open and not seeing, and the stone in his hand, and his breath going in pieces.

He had come back on his own. Before she could fetch anybody. And had slept for a day and a night, and was sitting up now, in bed, which he had not been in by day since he came south, and was, Linnet said, very pleased with himself and very ill, and would not be told which.

Emeric went to him straight from the boat.

Armand was sitting up against the bolster with the blanket over his knees and a sheet of paper on the board across them, covered in his small cramped hand, and his face was grey, and his eyes were bright.

"Before you say it," Armand said. "Yes. Alone. Yes. It was stupid. Yes, it cost me a day and very nearly more." He put his hand flat on the paper. "And it was worth every hour of it, and if you scold me I'll have Linnet put you out."

Emeric sat down on the edge of the bed.

"What did you find?"

Armand looked at him. Then, without a word, he turned the paper round on the board so that it faced Emeric, and pushed it a little toward him.

It was the stone. Opened up, in Armand's careful drawing, the way Emeric opened stones on the bench in the room: its inside, the grain of it, the way the mana lay in it. And beside the drawing, figures. How much the stone held. How much it pushed, and against what. How the push ran, along the grain, one way. The first measurement of a gravity stone anybody had ever made from inside one, in the tower's own careful notation, by the tower's best crystal theorist, in his eightieth year, alone.

"Gravity stone is rare," Armand said. "You know that. A body of it under a barony is a thing nobody in the Circle has ever seen in four hundred years." He leaned back against the bolster. "There's only one thing rarer. A seam of shard stone. And nobody's found one of those in Randillia in my lifetime." He closed his eyes. "Go away now. I'm going to sleep. I've earned it."

Emeric sat on the edge of the bed with the paper in his hands.

That night, at his table, with the fist-sized stone from the bottom of the cursed gallery on the wood in front of him in the candle light, pressing upward, very gently, against nothing, he took out the notebook and turned to a fresh page.

He had a list. He had been making it in his head all the way down the river, of what a gravity stone was for, and what a body of them was for: the ship, the K Discovery's matrix, the mine's own haulage, Odo Pike's iron road, a hundred things.

He did not write any of them.

He wrote one word at the top of the page.

Tess.
