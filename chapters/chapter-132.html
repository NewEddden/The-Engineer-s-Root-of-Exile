Chapter 132 - Copperas

The works went up in the first weeks of the spring, on the flat below the mill where the river came round in a slow bend and the ground had been nobody's since the flood.

It went up to Vantic's figure. Emeric had kept the sheet she had spoken into Ferra's room nearly two years before, the afternoon she held a trial sheet to the window and told them what a real works would want, and he built it to that and nothing larger. Four vats in a row under one long roof. A beater on its own wheel, in its own house at the end of the row, with a race cut to it from the mill's leat and a gate on the race so that the mill and the beater could not both be starved at once. A drying loft over the whole length of it, three times the old one, open-sided under the eaves with louvres Colm's men fitted in pairs so the wind could be let through or kept out. A glue house set well apart downwind, because nobody who had smelled hide scraps boiling wanted them near anything else. And a finishing room at the far end, with the presses and the cutting tables and a long bench under the windows.

Thirty hands, near enough, by the time the roof was on. Ferra had every one of them in the book with a wage against the name, and the wage was a Copper and a half a day for the rag floor and two for the vats and three for anybody who could be trusted with the sizing, and she read the column out at the table and nobody objected, because it was more than the fields paid in the spring and less than the foundry.

The vat floor wanted training, and the only person in the south who could train it came from Wayford.

Anse Keeling came south on the grain carts at the start of the thaw, under the terms her family had taken in the autumn, with a bundle on her back and her father's second-best mould wrapped in sacking under her arm, because she said she was not going to learn anybody's vat with a mould she did not know. She was the eldest girl, the one who had asked Emeric in her father's shop whether she could learn the dipping too. She was about sixteen and very plain-spoken and she had been at a vat since she could stand on a box to reach it.

She watched Neta's two vatmen dip for about the length of a quire, standing at the end of the row with her arms folded. Then she said, "You're fighting it," which Emeric had heard before, from an old man with his arms wet to the elbow, and had passed on and seen come to nothing, because it is one thing to be told not to fight water and another to stop. Anse did not tell them again. She took the mould out of the nearer man's hands and dipped it and lifted it and shook it, once side to side and once front to back, so gently that it hardly looked like shaking, and turned the sheet onto the felt, and handed the mould back, and stood there while he did it wrong nine more times, and on the tenth he did not, and she said, "That," and walked to the next vat.

Neta went back to the mill.

She had been lent to the paper since the trial, because she had the hands for it and there had been nobody else, and she had done it for two summers without once saying she would rather be anywhere else. She went back to the mill the week Anse came. She said nothing about it. She walked up the path from the new works to the mill with her apron over her arm, and went in at the mill door, where Jorrin was bringing a bar out of the forge, and Emeric, who happened to be at the leat gate, watched her stop inside the door and breathe in the smell of the hot iron and the scale as though somebody had opened a window.

The converter went up in the mill yard at the same time, beside the new works, where it could be blown off the works' own shaft.

Bram built it smaller than he had drawn it, because Emeric had stood over the tallies and shown him where. The pot was the height of a man, riveted, pear-shaped, on two trunnions set where it could pour. It was lined with Colm's brick and a spare lining stood stacked beside it under a sail, because Ferra had costed a spare and Ferra was going to see it. The bellows were the size of a cart and came off the beater's own wheel through a clutch Jorrin cut, so that the paper and the steel took turns at the river. It burned coke out of a small oven Bram built against the yard wall, and the coke came from Grayrock's coal, down the river on the steamboat in sacks.

The first blows happened in the evenings, after the works had shut, with Bram and Jorrin and the three men Ferra had costed. Emeric was not there for them. He had told Bram what to look for, the colour of the flame at the mouth and the moment it dropped, and then left him to it, because it was Bram's, and Bram needed to find out on his own that a thing he had drawn on the backs of his own tallies could roar.

The iron salt came first, because the ink could not start without it.

They did it on the old spoil behind the foundry, the heaps of spent ore that had been sitting in the rain for nine years. Emeric had a shallow trench dug along the foot of the oldest heap and a row of iron trays set in it, and the sulphur went into the trays in lumps and was lit, and burned with a thin blue flame that could hardly be seen in daylight and a smell that could be smelled from the Left Bridge. The fumes went up through the heap. The rain did the rest, and when it did not rain, two men with buckets did. At the end of a week they dug the heap over and ran water through the loose stuff into a settling tank, and let the tank stand, and boiled the liquor down in an iron pan, and at the bottom of the pan, cooling, there were crystals.

Green. The same pale sea-glass green he had once found crusted in the cracks of the stone with a boy and a spade.

They came out by the barrelful.

Ferra had both figures side by side at the end of the second week. The old way, a boy at a Copper a day on his knees in the heap with a spade and his fingers, had come to a barrel in about a fortnight, and the barrel had cost sixteen Copper and the boy's knees. The new way came to a barrel in two days with two men, and a few pounds of sulphur that she had costed down to a fraction of a Copper, and the pan and the fuel, and it came out at a little over three Copper the barrel.

"A fifth," she said, and entered it.

There was a larger way. He knew it as clearly as he knew the small one. You could burn the sulphur into a chamber instead of a heap and catch what came off it with water, and what you caught was an acid that would eat iron and cloth and skin and was, in the world he had come from, the thing half of every other thing was made with. But the chamber had to be lined with something the acid would not eat, and the only thing he knew that it would not eat was lead, and Kaldo had none. He wrote it in the notebook, under Bram's line from the table, and left it there.

The straw failed the first time.

He had known it might, and it did. They cooked it in the glue house's biggest copper, chopped short, in water with the sulphur burned into it through a pipe and a measure of lime, for a whole day, and at the end of the day it was brown mush. Not fibre. Mush, the colour of weak tea, that fell apart in the hand and would not hold on a mould at all. The liquor had been too strong. It had not parted the straw. It had eaten it.

He stood over the copper with Anse, who had come to look because she had never in her life heard of paper made of straw and did not believe in it, and she put her hand in and brought up a fistful of the brown and let it run out between her fingers.

"That's not paper," she said.

"No."

"That's what's under a cow."

"Near enough." He took the notebook out and wrote the strength of the liquor down and a line under it. "Half as strong. And longer."

The second cook held.

It came out of the copper pale and stringy and wet, a great sodden mass of it, and when it went through the beater it came apart into something that looked, in the vat, very nearly like rag pulp, if a little yellow and a little short. Anse dipped the first sheet herself, without being asked. It held on the mould. It came off onto the felt whole. It went up to the loft and dried, and came down, and went through the glue bath, and came down again.

It was not as good as rag. Anybody could see that. It was a little rough and a little yellow, and when it was held to the light the fibres in it were short and crowded, and a sharp nib would catch in it if it was pushed. Pell would have looked at it for less time than it took to turn it over.

It was cheap. Ferra did the sum before the sheet was dry, from the cost of a cartload of straw out of the ricks and the sulphur and the lime and the day's firing, and the figure she arrived at was about a third of what a sheet of rag cost, and the rag was the thing that ran out and the straw was not.

Emeric held the sheet to the window in the finishing room.

He did not say anything. There was nobody in the room just then to say it to, and he would not have said it if there had been. It was the first sheet of paper in the world that had not been made out of somebody's old shirt. Every sheet before it, every one, in every country he had ever heard of, in this world, had been a coat or a shift or a sheet off a bed first, worn thin and thrown away and picked up off a midden by a child and sold by the pound. This one had been standing in a field last autumn.

He let that be his, for about as long as it took to look at it. Then he gave it to Ferra for the book.

The sheets were cut on Bram's master foot.

That had been the whole point of the master foot, when Bram had cut it on the surface plate and struck the division off it and sealed it in the keep: that a thing measured in Kaldo would be the same thing measured anywhere a copy of the foot had gone. The standard sheet was cut to it, on a table with a steel edge and a stop that Jorrin set to the division and pinned, so that every sheet off the table was the same sheet. A clerk in Milford would get the standard sheet. A clerk at Emberfell would get the standard sheet. A primer printed in the finishing room would fit a cover made in the bindery, and a drawing made on one sheet would lie exactly on another. It was a small thing. He had spent a great part of his other life among people who did not think it was small at all.

Ferra called it the standard sheet in the book from the first day, and so did everybody else, and nobody thought to call it anything else.

On the morning it happened, Colm came down from the glasshouse with a pane of glass.

He came into the finishing room at the start of the day, when the works was just opening and the finishing room was still cold, carrying it flat in both hands in front of him in a fold of felt, and he did not say anything. He held it up to the east window.

It had no green in it.

Emeric had been looking at green glass all his life in this world, in the long gallery at the palace and in the church windows and in the panes Colm had been making for three years, which had got clearer every year and had never stopped being green, faintly, at the edges, the colour of shallow water. This had none. It was clear. It was clear the way the air in front of it was clear. The window behind it was simply there, the yard and the mill wall and a cart going by, without any colour laid over it at all.

"I've been putting in a pinch," Colm said, "of that black grit. Off the Grayrock carts, the stuff that comes down in the spoil with the coal. Nobody wants it. I had a sack of it off the steamboat for nothing." He turned the pane a little in the light. "You said a thing had to be put in on purpose to take the green out. Three years ago. At the bench. You said nobody here puts it in." He looked at Emeric. "I've been trying things in the melt since. Salt. Bone ash. Ash off three different woods. That's the first one that did anything."

Emeric knew what the grit was.

He knew it as he knew the rest of it, whole and without any road back to where it had come from: the black stuff was the thing glassmakers in his other world had called the glassmaker's soap, for exactly this, because it washed the green out. It was a real find. Colm had made it on his own, over three years, with a sack of spoil nobody wanted and a sentence Emeric had said once and never followed up.

"That's good," Emeric said. "That's very good. Put it in the book with the measure, so you don't lose it."

"I've got the measure," said Colm, who had never in his life lost a measure, and went away pleased, carrying his pane.

Emeric did not think about the grit again that morning. He was not listening. There was a line of ink on the bench in front of him, and it was already dry.

It had been an ordinary line. One of the finishing room's apprentices, a boy of twelve called Pim who had come off the rag floor in the winter because he could write, had been set to try the new batch of ink before the day's copying began. That was the rule: every pot tried on a scrap before it went near a book. The pot had been boiled the evening before, the first batch made with the green crystals out of the pan, and left overnight to settle on the long bench under the windows.

On the long bench, beside the rack.

The rack held the printer's stones. It was a frame of oak with a row of small cups along it, and in the evenings Orrin came down from the school and charged the stones in it, a quarter of an hour apiece, and in the mornings they went into the printer. It had stood on that bench since the autumn, because the finishing room was where the printer was and the bench was where there was room.

Pim had dipped a pen in the pot and drawn a line across a scrap of the standard sheet, and lifted his hand, and the line was dry before his hand was off the paper.

He had not thought anything of it. He was twelve. He had drawn another line, and that was dry too, and he had said to nobody in particular that the new ink was good, and Emeric, who had been passing behind him with Colm's pane still in his head, had stopped.

A line of iron gall ink does not dry before a hand comes off the paper. It sits wet and pale on the sheet for the time it takes to count ten, and then goes grey and then, over a day, black. That was the whole of iron gall. He had made it for two summers. He knew what it did.

This line was black already.

He tested it properly.

He did it the way he did everything, the way he had taught Bram and Ferra and Jorrin to do everything, one change at a time and a line in the book for each change. He did not say why. He told Pim he wanted to try the new ink against the old, and set him to rinsing pots, and the boy, who was twelve, thought nothing of that either.

The old ink, the pen ink from the autumn, off the shelf at the far end of the room: wet on the sheet, a count of ten, grey. As it always had been.

The new ink, the same pot Pim had used: dry at once, and black.

So it was the new crystals, or it was the pot, or it was the night on the bench. He boiled two more pots of the new ink that afternoon, from the same crystals and the same galls and the same water, in the same copper. One he set on the long bench beside the rack. The other he carried to the far end of the room and set on the shelf beside the old ink, twelve paces off, and he left them both overnight and locked the room.

In the morning the pot beside the rack dried as it went on. The pot on the far shelf sat wet on the sheet for a count of ten and went grey.

He did it again with the crystals from the old spoil, the ones a boy had picked out with his fingers, boiled beside the rack. Those dried as they went on too. So it was not the crystals. It was the stones.

Then he put the sense to it.

He had not wanted to do that in the finishing room, with people in it. He waited until the midday bell, when the room emptied for bread, and then he drew a fresh line in the pot that had stood by the rack, a long one, slowly, across the whole width of a sheet, and while the nib was still on the paper he let the sense out and down, the way he let it out over a room when he wanted to know what was in it.

The room was full of the ordinary thin medium that was in every room. The rack was a warm place in it, the stones in their cups each a small steady weight. The old pen ink on its shelf was nothing at all.

The wet line had a pulse in it.

It was very faint. He would not have found it if he had not been looking for exactly that, in exactly that place. It was a faint, even, falling note, all along the line where the ink was still wet, and as he held the sense on it he felt it go down. Not flicker. Go down, steadily, the way the sound of a bell goes down, all along the line at once, and the line went black as it went, and when the last of it had gone the ink was dry and there was nothing in it at all. He put the sense back over the dry line to be sure. Nothing. A dry line of ink on a sheet of paper, as dead as the paper.

He dipped the pen again and wrote his own name.

Emeric.

And he sat at the bench in the empty finishing room with the sense held on it and listened to it go quiet. The E first, because it had been written first. Then the rest of it, letter by letter, in the order he had put them down, a faint falling note under each one, like somebody saying the name very softly and more softly and then not at all.

He worked it out sitting there, before the bell went and the room filled again, and once he had worked it out he did not let himself go back over it.

The ink took up the medium while it boiled. It was water and iron and galls, simmering for an hour beside a row of charged stones, and something in it drank what the stones gave off, the way a cloth set by a fire takes up the heat, and held it. When the ink went down onto paper, it let it go. It gave the medium back to the air as it dried. And the letting go was what dried it: the ink did not sit and wait for the air to take the water, it pushed the water out as it gave up what it held, all at once, and that was why the line went black before a hand was off the sheet.

Which meant that for as long as a line of it was wet, it was saying so, in the only voice there was to say it in, to anybody who could hear.

That was all. He did not go any further than that. He sat at the bench with his own name drying on the sheet in front of him, dead now, black, as ordinary as any name in any book, and did not go any further than that on purpose.

He told nobody for a day.

He carried it round with him the whole of the next day, through the table and the works and the yard, and did not say anything, and nobody asked him why he was quiet, because it was spring and everybody was quiet in the spring with the amount there was to do. Then, in the evening, he went up to Armand's rooms and told him, because if he was wrong it was Armand who ought to be the one to say so, and if he was right it was Armand who ought to know first.

Armand listened to all of it without interrupting. Then he said he would like two pots of it, one boiled by the rack and one not, and a sheet of the standard paper, and a day.

It took him two. He did it on his own bench, in his own rooms, with his own instruments, which were a great deal older than the sense and slower and did not depend on anybody's say-so. On the evening of the second day he came down the keep stair with his stick in one hand and a sheet in the other, and found Emeric in the long room, and put the sheet on the table in front of him.

It had two columns of figures on it in his crabbed small hand, and at the foot of the columns a line, and under the line a word.

Confirmed.

"It is real," Armand said. He sat down, slowly, as he did everything now. "It is small, and it is real, and it lasts exactly as long as the ink is wet." He looked at the sheet. "I have been working with the medium for forty years. I have boiled a great many things beside a great many stones. I would never have found this in a hundred more, unless I had been told exactly what to look for, and where, and on which bench." He said it sourly, as a man says a thing against himself that he would rather not. "Neither will anybody else."

Emeric looked at the sheet.

It was the thing he had wanted Armand to say and the thing he had been afraid of Armand saying, and they were the same thing, and he found that he could not tell, sitting there, which of them he was feeling.

"What will you call it?" said Armand.

He had not thought of it. He thought of it now, and the name came up at once, plainly, as though it had been waiting.

"Kaldo black," he said.

Armand nodded, and took the sheet back, and folded it, and put it inside his coat, and did not ask what a voice in an ink was good for. He was a clever old man and he had had two days.

Neither did Emeric. He sat on in the long room after Armand had gone up, with the fire going and the table empty, and kept that question exactly where it was, outside the door, and did not let it in.

He went down to the finishing room before he went to bed.

It was dark and locked and smelled of glue and cold paper. He lit the lamp by the long bench, and dipped a pen in the pot that had stood beside the rack, and drew one line across a scrap of the standard sheet, slowly, from edge to edge.

Then he put the pen down and sat with the sense on it and listened to it dry, all along its length, the faint falling note under the whole line at once, going down and down in the quiet room, until it stopped.
