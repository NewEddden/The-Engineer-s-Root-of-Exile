Chapter 273 - Under the Isle

The K Discovery set down on the sea a mile off the Isle's desert shore at a quarter past eleven on the Saturday night, with no moon, and her lamps out, and nobody on her deck saying anything at all.

She came down out of the dark the way she came down onto the Mere, slowly, on her stones, with the faint violet along her keel the only light about her; and Old Edrin held her a hand's breadth off the swell, and then let her down onto it, so softly that the water hardly moved, and she sat there on the black sea under the stars like a duck on a pond, rocking a little, with the long low shape of the Isle lying across the dark ahead of her and the smell of it coming off the land on the night air. Hot rock, and dust, and under them, faint and sour and unmistakable, like a lamp that has been turned down too far, the pitch lake.

To the south, round the headland, out of sight, the K Endeavor was in the cove.

Emeric could feel her. A long dense shape a few feet over the swell at the cove's mouth, hanging there on her own stones with her side pods open, and the small bright notes of her crew on her deck, and one note in the water under her, a man in heavy diving dress on the end of an air line, walking on the bottom in the dark toward the old tanks under the west cliffs with a hose over his shoulder. She would be there till four, filling. It was her first full load, the whole month's oil up out of the drowned tanks and into her pods, four hundred tons of it if the pumps held; and if anybody on the Kesrath tower's ridge looked out to sea tonight and saw anything at all, they would see her, in the cove on the far side of the island, and not the K Discovery sitting quiet off the east shore with her boat going down on its falls.

That was what the K Endeavor was for, tonight. That was all she was for.

The boat went down at twenty past. There were nine in it. Emeric and Zale and Ward and Rylan, the lead team. Three of them in their own suits with the water edit: the close black skin, rubber over the wool, with the plate cut down to what a swimmer could carry, at the chest and the belly and the shoulders, the forearms, the knees and the shins; the flight helms on their laps, round glass in front, and a stone lit at each breastbone and in each helm's cheek, turned down to a violet you had to be looking for. Their flasks on their sides. Zale in the borrowed rubber suit, with no plate on him at all and a mask round his neck, because a man who did not know what a suit's heart was could not be given one. Osric and Declan, who would haul. Arthur, who would stay on top at the sinkhole's mouth, kitted, with a full set of flasks and a reel, and go down the line after them if anybody was overdue. Imani, with her bag, and a bottle of the plant's oxygen in a canvas sling, who would stay on top with Arthur. And Ned, at the oars, who would take the boat back to the K Discovery when they were ashore and come back for them at the low water before dawn.

Nobody spoke. Ned rowed with his oars muffled in rag. The Isle came up out of the dark ahead of them, a long pale line of sand under a long dark line of land, and the surf on it, small, whispering; and the boat's keel touched sand, and they got out into the warm shallows and carried the bags up the beach between them, and Ned pushed off, and was gone.

They stood on the sand in the dark and listened.

Nothing. The surf behind them. A small wind off the land, hot, with the pitch in it. Far off to the west, high up, on the ridge that ran along the island's spine, a single point of yellow light, very small, not moving: the Kesrath tower's lamp.

Emeric opened his sense.

He let it go out from him along the sand and up the slope of the dunes and over, wide, the way he had been learning to let it go since the pod: not a thread any longer, but a long low wave, like the swell going up a beach. The island came back to him. Sand, warm, empty, faintly alive with small things in it: beetles, a snake asleep in a hole, a bird's nest in a thorn bush. Rock under the sand, dense, quiet. Water, under the rock, very deep down, salt, moving with the tide. And ahead, a mile inland, a great flat stillness that his sense slid over the way a hand slides over oil, that held almost no mana at all and smelled, even to the sense, of something very old and very dead: the pitch lake.

And on the ridge, a mile and a half to the west, round the lamp, the small bright notes of six men. Asleep, four of them. Two awake. One of them walking.

"Six on the tower," he said, very low. "Two awake. One on the ridge path, walking, with the lamp."

Ward nodded once in the dark.

They went inland.



The pitch lake was not a lake. Emeric had seen it from the air, from the K Endeavor's deck at a thousand feet on four night runs, as a great dark flat blot in the desert; he had thought of it as water. It was not water. They came over the last dune at a quarter to twelve and it lay in front of them under the stars, flat and black and dull, a quarter of a mile across, with no shine on it at all; and the smell came up off it so thick that Zale put his arm over his face. Here and there on its surface something moved, slowly: a bubble coming up through it, fat and lazy, as big as a man's head, swelling, and bursting with a soft wet sound, and the smell coming off it in a breath.

"The black water breathes," said Zale, through his sleeve.

They went round its east shore, keeping off it, on the sand. Bram's safety lamp was in Rylan's hand, lit and shuttered, a faint glow through the gauze. Twice, where the shore came close, Rylan opened the shutter a crack and held the lamp low, and the flame stood up tall and thin inside its gauze with a faint blue cap on it like a ghost's hat; and he shut it again and they moved away from the shore and went on.

The sinkhole was where Vey had drawn it.

It was a round hollow in the sand a hundred yards back from the lake's east shore, twenty yards across, with its sides sloping in, as if somebody had pressed a great thumb into the desert; and at the bottom of it, half buried, a slab of cut stone. Then another, below it, and another. Steps. Wide shallow steps, worn in their middles, going down into the sand at the hollow's bottom and under it.

The sand had eaten the stair. Vey had said so. They dug it out.

They dug for an hour, with the short spades from the dry bags, in the dark, all of them, in turns, with Imani holding the lamp low and shuttered so its glow fell only on the sand; and step by step the old stair came up out of the sand under their spades, going down, steeply, into the ground, between walls of cut stone that came up as they dug, until they were standing on the seventh step in a narrow cut deeper than they were tall, with the stars a long thin strip overhead and the sand piled at the cut's top. And at the bottom of the seventh step, where the eighth should have been, there was water.

Black. Still. Salt. Lapping, very faintly, at the stone.

"The sea's door," said Zale.

Emeric knelt on the step and put his hand in it.

It went down. His sense went down through it, into it, ahead of his hand, the way it had gone down into the Mere: a passage, cut through the rock, not natural, square, going down from the stair's foot and under the island at a long slant; and full to its roof of black salt water, moving, very slightly, toward him. The tide coming in. Then, far down, forty feet, it levelled, and ran on under the rock a long way, a quarter of a mile, he thought, before it began to rise again on the far side.

And on the far side, air.

"Low water's at ten past two," said Zale, beside him. He had his watch out, the chronometer he had brought, shuttered under his hand. "It's turning now. In an hour the current'll be out. We go in at the slack, when it stops. We've got from the slack to the turn to go through and come back. Then the door shuts." He put the watch away. "No third time."

"How long's the slack?"

"An hour. Maybe a bit more." Zale looked down at the black water. "That's in, and out again. Or in, and stay till the next one."

Nobody said anything.

High on the ridge to the west, the yellow point of the Kesrath tower's lamp had begun to move.



They lay still for an hour.

It was the longest hour Emeric could remember. The lamp came down off the ridge, slowly, swinging, on the path that ran along the slope of the island above the lake, toward the east; a man on foot with a lantern, walking a round he had walked a thousand times, not hurrying. They lay in the stair's cut, the eight of them, flat on the cut stone steps in the dark with their faces against their arms, and the shuttered safety lamp under Imani's coat, and did not move. Emeric held his sense on the walking man's note: small, bright, a little bored. He came within four hundred yards of the sinkhole. He stopped there, on the path above the lake, and set his lantern down on a rock, and stood with his back to them looking out at the lake for a long time; and Emeric, flat on the step with sand in his mouth, felt him do something he did not understand, and then did.

He was relieving himself. Against the rock. Looking out at the black water breathing under the stars.

Then he picked up his lantern and went back the way he had come.

Rylan, beside Emeric on the step, let his breath out very slowly into the sand.



They went in at the slack, at four minutes past two.

Emeric went first, with the reel. Ward behind him, then Zale, then Rylan last, each with a hand on the line; and on the stair above them in the dark, Osric and Declan in their suits with their own flasks, waiting for the line to be laid before they followed with the lift bags; and Arthur on the top step, kitted, with his helm under his arm and his hand on the line where it went down into the water, and Imani beside him with her bag and her watch.

He opened the door before he went under.

He did it on the step, with the water round his knees and his helm shut, the way he had done it for practice in the Mere; reaching for Ward first, whose note he knew better than his own, and finding the hair-width gap in himself that he held open to the slow place, and opening it a hair more, toward Ward, and through. He felt Ward take it, steadily, without any fuss, the way Ward took everything. Then Rylan, who had said yes on the jetty; a little slower, a little stiffer, and then open.

The overlay came up.

To Emeric it was only what he always saw, with his sense open wide: the passage ahead in the black water, drawn in faint lines like chalk on a slate, its walls and roof and floor going down at their long slant; the line in his hand, picked out bright; the four of them, marks; the way out behind them, one line home up the stair. Now he drew it twice more, through the door, over the eyes of the two men behind him, faint, the way a reflection lies on a window with the night behind it.

Can you see it? he said, through the door.

Yes, said Ward. As if Ward had been asked whether he could see the yard.

Rylan was quiet a moment. Then, very low, in the door, so low it hardly came through: Light. It's like light.

Behind them, between them, Zale's note: deep and slow and steady. Emeric felt it, in the water, as they went down; felt it come to the edge of the thread going between the three of them, the way a man's hand comes to the edge of a draught under a door. It stopped there. It did not go into it. It could not; it did not know how. It felt the thread the way a man in the dark feels a cold breath on the back of his neck, and does not know where it is coming from.

Emeric waited for it to ask.

Zale's note did not ask. It moved back, a little, away from the edge of the thread; and settled, deep and slow, on the line. And frogged on.

They went down.



The passage was cut. All of it. It went down from the stair's foot at a long even slant, square, a little wider than a man's arms spread, its walls smooth under a fur of fine pale silt that came off at the touch of a fin in long slow clouds; and Emeric went down it in the black with his lamp on his hand and the reel paying out line behind him and his sense going ahead of him like a hand along a wall in a dark house, and it did not end. Twenty feet. Thirty. Forty. The cold came up from under. The passage levelled.

And ran on.

It ran on under the island for a quarter of a mile, by the reel's count. It was the longest quarter of a mile Emeric had ever swum. The lamps showed them three yards of black water and the silt on the walls going past and the line going on ahead from Emeric's reel, and that was all; the overlay showed them the rest, the passage running on ahead in its faint chalk lines through the dark, straight, square, empty. There were things on the walls. Carvings, under the silt. Emeric felt them pass with his sense, cut into the stone at regular distances: the same shape, over and over, a ring with a mark in it. He did not stop for them.

Zale did not need the overlay to swim. He swam like a fish, now; like a frog, flat and level, his fins turning out and pushing and gliding, not a cloud behind him. Rylan had made a cave diver of him in an afternoon, and he hated it, and he was good at it.

Halfway along, Rylan's hand came up off the line and touched Emeric's ankle, twice. Stop.

Emeric stopped.

What? he said through the door.

My gauge, said Rylan. Third.

Emeric looked at his own. He had gone down with his flasks full; they all had. He had used, in a quarter of a mile at forty feet, a little less than a third of what he had. Rylan had used a third.

Ward?

Under, said Ward.

Then on.

They went on. Rule of thirds. A third going in, a third coming out, a third for trouble. Whoever hit his third first turned the whole team. Rylan had hit his; but his was a third of the way in, and they were near the far end now, Emeric could feel it, the passage rising ahead at its long slant toward the air on the far side. If they got there, they would breathe from the air, not the flasks. If the air was good.

The passage rose.

Thirty feet. Twenty. Ten. Emeric stopped at fifteen, by the depth gauge on his wrist, and hung there in the black water at the line, and counted three minutes on his watch while the others hung below him, the way he had made them practise in the Mere till Zale swore. Then up. The black water went grey over his lamp, and then silver, and then his head came out of it into air.

He hung in the water at the top of another stair, the twin of the one they had come down, rising out of the black water into a dark that his lamp did not reach the end of. He did not open his glass. He did not breathe.

Rylan came up beside him. Then Ward. Then Zale, last, blowing.

"Don't," said Emeric. "Don't breathe yet. Lamp."

Rylan had it. He held it up out of the water at the full stretch of his arm, the brass gauze with the little flame inside it, and opened the shutter.

The flame stood up.

It stood up tall and thin inside its gauze, much taller than it should have, and on top of it, all the way round its head, there was a cap of pale blue light, wavering; and as Rylan lifted it higher, toward the roof that none of them could see, the blue cap grew, and the flame stretched up into it, thin as a needle, blue, and fluttered.

"Down," said Emeric.

Rylan brought it down, toward the water. The flame shrank. The blue cap shrank, and went, and the flame stood yellow and small and ordinary for a moment, a hand's breadth over the water; and then, as Rylan moved it along the stair's edge toward the far side, where the dark was lower, it guttered, and went dim, and went small and red and nearly out.

He brought it back. It came back up, yellow.

"Bad," said Ward.

"Both kinds," said Emeric. "The light kind up in the roof. The lake's breath, the kind that burns; it's come up the rock and pooled up there under the ceiling. And something heavy in the low place, over there, that the flame won't live in." He looked at the dark. "The rotten egg kind, I think. From the pitch. It lies in the low places like water. You can't smell it when there's enough of it to kill you."

"So we don't breathe," said Rylan.

"We breathe off the flasks."

Rylan looked at his gauge. They all looked at their gauges. Nobody said anything. They had a third each to get home on and a third for trouble, and Rylan had less; and the cave was out there in the dark beyond the stair's top, the whole of it, with whatever was in it, and they were going to have to walk through it with their mouthpieces in, breathing off the trouble.

Emeric could not see gas. He could see every stone in the walls, and every grain of silt, and the mana in the rock, and the line, and the four of them; he could see the dark beyond the stair's top opening out into something very large, very large indeed, a great space of cut stone and standing shapes going away into the hill farther than his sense would reach. He could not see the air in it. He had told them so on the jetty. He did not have a sense for air. Nobody did.

He was working out the sum, how long, how far, when Zale began to whistle.

It was very low. It was hardly a whistle at all; a breath, between the teeth, through his lips, soft, a sailor's whistle, the kind of thing a man does without knowing he does it while he waits for a wind on a flat calm deck. Zale was standing on the stair's top step, in the water to his waist, with his mask pushed up on his forehead and his mouthpiece out and his lips pursed, and his face very calm, looking up into the dark under the roof.

And in front of his face, a hand's breadth off his lips, a glyph came up.

It was small. It was grey, almost white, the colour of a cloud seen from under; a ring no bigger than a coin, with a character in it that Emeric had seen a thousand times on Old Edrin's casting seat and never seen made like this, out of a breath, with no hand to point it. It sat in the air in front of Zale's lips and turned, slowly, once. And Zale went on whistling, softly, a long low note and a short one and the long one again, like a man calling a dog he is fond of.

The air moved.

Emeric felt it on his face. All of it, in the whole great dark space beyond the stair's top: a long slow drift, like the draught in a cathedral, going up. Up into the roof, along it, away, toward somewhere at the far end, high, that Emeric's sense could feel as a narrowing in the rock and the faint, faint warmth of the desert night coming down through it. A shaft. A chimney, cut, going up to the surface. And the heavy dark place on the stair's far side, the low place, stirred, and slid, and went down, thickly, the way water goes down a drain, into something below it that Emeric's sense felt as a hole in the floor. A pit. A side pit, cut, deep.

Zale stopped whistling.

The glyph faded off the air in front of his lips like breath off glass.

"Lamp," he said.

Rylan held it up.

The flame stood yellow and small and ordinary inside its gauze. He held it high, toward the roof. Yellow. He took it along the stair's top to the far side, where the dark had been low and heavy, and held it down. It stayed yellow.

He opened the gauze, very slightly, with his thumb, which nobody had told him he could do, and held it there.

Yellow.

Zale took his mask off his forehead and hung it round his neck, and breathed in, deeply, through his nose; and breathed out.

"Pitch," he said. "And old. Like a church." He looked round at the three of them, standing in the water on the stair with their glass still shut and their eyes very wide behind it. "You can open those. It's good to the far end. I've sent the light stuff up the hole at the top and the heavy stuff down the one in the floor, and they'll stay there a while. It'll come back in a few hours. It always does." He considered. "Maybe sooner. It's a big room. I'd not sleep in it."

Rylan unlatched his glass, slowly, and breathed.

Ward opened his. He did not breathe at once. He stood in the water on the stair and looked at Zale, a long look, from his wet beard to his eyes, in the lamplight.

Emeric opened his last. He looked at his gauge. He had a third and a little over. They all had. They had not breathed off the trouble at all.

"Every sailor whistles," said Zale, to nobody in particular, wringing the water out of his beard. "For a wind. On a flat calm. Every sailor in the world." He shrugged, very slightly, the big easy shrug. "Mine works."

Ward said, "The coaster."

Zale looked at him.

"On the Mere," said Ward. "At dawn. With no wind." He did not say anything else.

"Every sailor whistles," said Zale again, and went up the stair.



It was a city.

They did not understand that at first. They came up out of the water onto the stair's top, the four of them, with their lamps, into the great dark space that Emeric's sense had found; and the lamps showed them a floor of cut stone going away into the black, flat, swept, and walls of cut stone going up out of the lamps' reach. That was all, for the first few paces. Then Rylan turned his lamp up, full, and held it high, and they saw the street.

It went away from the stair's top, straight, between buildings. Houses. Cut out of the hill's own rock, not built; the rock left standing in the shape of a house, with a door in it and windows, and the next one beside it, and the next, in a row, going away into the dark down both sides of the street as far as the lamp would go and farther. Flat roofs. Steps up to the doors. A gutter down the street's middle, cut, dry. And the whole of it, every stone, every step and sill and lintel, swept clean, as if somebody had come through it with a broom the morning they left, and shut the doors behind them.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"How old?" said Rylan at last. His voice did not carry; the stone took it.

"Old," said Emeric.

"Older than the tower?"

Emeric did not answer. He did not know. He was looking at the first of the figures.

It stood at the stair's top, to the street's left, facing out: toward the water, toward the stair they had come up, toward anybody who might come up it. A head taller than he was, and nearly twice as broad. Grey stone, the same stone as the street. Faceless: there was a head, smooth, with a shallow ridge where a brow would be and nothing else. Arms down at its sides. Hands open, the fingers long and a little curled. And at the base of it, where it stood on the street, a hairline seam, so fine that the lamp only found it when Rylan held it low.

There was another to the right. Facing out.

Emeric put his hand on the left one's arm.

It was stone. Cold, dense, dead. And inside it, under the shell, his sense went in and found what he had half expected and still was not ready for: the shell was only a shell. Inside, there were rails. Fine ones, finer than anything Kaldo had ever drawn, running from every joint, the shoulder, the elbow, the wrist, every finger, the hip and knee and ankle, all of them going up through the body in a bundle like the nerves in an arm Imani had shown him in the ward, to two places. One in the head. One in the chest. Two hollows, each the shape of a long small egg.

Empty.

And at the joints, inside, packed close round the rails, stones. Not his kind of stone; not round, not egg-shaped. Rods, at the elbow, long and thin, lying along the arm's length. At the shoulder, a stack of thin flat plates, dozens of them, like the leaves of a book. At the hip, coiled. Gravity stones, every one of them, shaped. Dead now, empty, their charge long gone; but shaped, on purpose, by somebody who had known that a stone's shape mattered and how, when Kaldo had only ever known the egg and the neck.

He took his hand away.

"What is it?" said Ward.

"A statue," said Zale, behind them. "At a door. Like at a church."

Emeric did not say anything. He could not say anything, in front of Zale, that was true.

Not a statue, he said through the door, to Ward and Rylan. A machine. It walked. There are seats for two control stones in it, in the head and the chest, and they're empty. Somebody took them. Don't say anything.

Ward's note did not change at all. Rylan's jumped, once, and settled.

They went on down the street.



There were figures at every gate. At every wide door of every bigger building, two by two, facing out. Ward noticed it before anybody; he said it aloud, in the second street, quietly, to nobody. "They're all facing out." He stopped in front of a pair at the door of a long low hall with a row of pillars along its front and looked at them, and then back up the street at the pair at the stair, and then down it. "Every one. Facing the way you'd come in from."

"Guards," said Rylan.

"Guards face out," said Ward. "When they're guarding something." He looked at the long hall behind the pair, at its dark door. "Or when they're keeping something in, they face the other way." He was quiet a moment. "These are facing out."

Emeric felt the empty seats in every one they passed. Every one. Two empty hollows, head and chest, the shape of a stone; in every figure in the city, all down every street. Somebody had gone through this whole place, figure by figure, door by door, and taken the deciders out of them all. Carefully. The way you take the seeds out of a granary before you leave a farm, so that nobody who comes after can plant them.

He did not know who. He did not know when. He did not know whether the people who had taken them had been the people who made them, leaving, or the people who came after.

The street went down, gently, into the hill. They followed the line, laying it as they went, Emeric with the reel; and the street ended at a square, wide, with a dry fountain in it, and on the square's far side a great door, the widest they had seen, three times the height of a man, in a wall of cut stone that went up out of the lamps' reach.

There were two figures at it. Facing out.

And one of them was not empty.

Emeric felt it from across the square. He stopped walking. He stood in the middle of the dry square with the reel in his hand and the line going away behind him up the street to the stair, and his sense went across the square to the figure on the door's right, into it, through the shell, along the rails to the chest; and in the chest seat, sitting in it, grey, unlit, still, there was a stone.

He knew it at once. He would have known it anywhere. A long small egg, smooth, of the kind he had carried in a pouch through the gates of the capital at twenty-two, of the kind that had dropped out of a pod into Bram's basket in April. A Hollow Crystal. Sitting where it had been put, a very long time ago, in the chest of a walking machine at a door, in a swept city under the sea.

The head seat was empty. The chest seat was not.

He walked across the square to it.

"That one," he said aloud.

They looked at him.

"We take that one home," said Emeric. "That one. Not the others."

"It's a statue," said Zale, "the size of a cart. We came through a hole in the sea."

"It comes apart," said Emeric.

He put his hand on its chest.

There was a plate on its back. He had felt it from across the square: a panel, set in the stone between the shoulders, very fine-edged, the seam round it a hair's breadth. He went round behind the figure and felt for the panel's edge with his fingers and found a place where the seam was a little wider, a notch, and put his thumb in it, and pushed.

The plate came away in his hands.

It was as thick as his palm and heavy, and behind it, in the figure's back, in a hollow, there was a tank. A canister, of something like glass, clouded with age, as long as his forearm, empty and dry; and leading out of the top of it, into the rails, a small tube, a glass port with a fitting on its end like the fittings on Kaldo's own liquid-mana flasks, only smaller and finer. He looked at it for a long time.

It ran out, he said through the door. It didn't stop because somebody took its stone. It stopped because its tank ran dry. And the stone sat there and waited.

For what? said Ward.

Emeric did not answer.

He put his hand on the figure's shoulder, where it met the arm, and felt along the joint with his sense; and found what he was looking for, a ring of fine pins round the shoulder's cup, holding the arm in, each pin set in its hole with a catch. He pressed the first one. It gave. He pressed the second.

The arm came off.

It came off at the shoulder in his hands, cleanly, the whole arm, heavy as a man, and he staggered under it and Ward caught the other end; and they stood there in the dry square in the lamplight holding a stone arm between them, with the fine rails hanging out of its top end like the roots of a pulled plant.

Zale was staring at it.

"A statue breaks," said Emeric. "This comes apart."



It came apart into eleven pieces, at its joints, each one held by its ring of pins; and Osric and Declan, who had come up the stair behind them on the laid line at a quarter to three with the lift bags, carried the pieces back up the street one at a time to the stair's top and laid them on the step in a row in order, the head and the chest and the arms and the legs and the hips, with the chest last, very carefully, with Emeric walking beside it all the way.

Zale did not ask what it was for. He stood at the stair's top with his arms folded and watched them lay the pieces down, and when the chest went down, he looked at it for a long while; and then he said, quite pleasantly, "For the garden, is it?"

"For the winter garden," said Emeric.

"Of course," said Zale, and did not ask again.



They found Vey at a quarter past three, in the house at the end of the square, behind the great door.

It was not a house. It was a hall, long and high, with pillars down both sides and a floor of polished stone, swept; and the walls of it, both walls, the whole length, were carved.

Emeric held his lamp up and looked at the wall nearest him, and stopped.

It was a picture. The whole wall was one picture, cut in the stone in low relief, very fine, and coloured once, though the colour was nearly gone; a great long scene going away down the hall into the dark, of people. A great many people, of many kinds. Some of them were like the four of them standing there with their lamps, and some were not. There were short ones, broad, very broad, with heavy beards and heavy hands, standing at forges and at the mouths of tunnels in the rock, with the figures like the ones at the doors standing beside them, the same height and twice the breadth. There were tall ones, very tall and very slender, with long faces and long hands, standing among trees: and the trees were the tree. He knew it at once. Their trunks braided, their leaves in wide flat tiers, the long vines hanging down with the pods on them, carved so finely that he could see the plates of the pods' shells. A grove of them. A forest. And there were others, too many to take in, going away down the wall into the dark, some with things about their faces he could not make out, some very small.

And over them all, across the top of the wall, the whole length of the hall, in the carved sky, there were wings.

Long ones, ridged, wide as a ship's sail. Necks. Tails. Shapes in the sky over the people and the trees, a great many of them, flying; and below the nearest, carved hanging from its claws, a long pod.

Emeric stood in the hall with his lamp up and looked at the wall, and the names came into his head, all of them, at once, unbidden, out of every book he had read on the other side of everything as a child under the covers with a torch; and he did not say any of them. He did not say anything at all. He stood there and looked until Ward touched his arm.

"Emeric."

He lowered the lamp.

There was a scale on the floor at the wall's foot.

He saw it because his lamp was low; it lay in the angle where the wall met the floor, under the carving of the wings, as if it had been put there on purpose or had fallen there a very long time ago and never been swept up. A plate of something dark, curved, the size of his two hands together, ridged across its face like the carved wings above it. He bent and picked it up.

It was dead. Utterly. Drained, the way a stone is drained at the edge of a dead zone, to nothing, so that his sense went into it and found only the cold of it; and it was very light, much lighter than its size, and very hard, harder than any metal he knew. He turned it over in the lamplight. Its inner face was smooth, and shone faintly.

He put it in the dry bag at his hip, without a word, beside the other thing that was there: a chip of grey stone the size of a thumbnail that he had taken from a diver's hand on the K Endeavor's deck, four hours before, off the foot of the west cliffs.

He had not said anything about that either.

The K Discovery had gone round to the cove first, at ten, and hung off the K Endeavor's quarter for an hour while the hose was coupled, before she went round to the east shore to set down; and Emeric had been on the K Endeavor's deck when her diver came up the ladder at a quarter to eleven, in his great brass helmet and his canvas dress, and stood on her deck dripping while they unscrewed the helmet, and said that there was something at the foot of the cliff by the third tank that his pick would not go into. He had been clearing the weed off the tank's mouth with it and had swung at a lump of rock to knock it out of the way, and the pick had slowed as it went in, and slowed, and stopped, a hand's breadth short of the rock, as if it had hit a cushion, and hung there in the water. He had swung again. The same. A hand's breadth short, and stopped, soft, every time, and nothing he did would make it go closer. He had brought up the one chip that had come away on its own, in the weed.

Emeric had held the chip in his palm, on the deck, in the dark, and felt it.

It was alive. Not like a control stone, quiet. Alive like a held breath, very small, very dense, and watching; and when he had moved his other hand toward it, slowly, his hand had slowed, a hair's breadth off it, as if the air had thickened.

"Stasis stone," he had said aloud.

He had never said it aloud before. It was the name he had given it in his head, years ago, the first time he heard of a shard stone, from the other side of everything, out of the same books the wings had come from. The diver had looked at him without understanding. Ward had looked at him and understood exactly, and said nothing, and gone to tell the K Endeavor's master to mark the third tank on his chart.

Nobody could cut it, on this run. There was no time, and no tool, and Emeric did not yet know how. But it was there, under the waterline, at the foot of the west cliffs, where nobody had ever looked because nobody had ever dived there. And now it was on a chart.



Vey was at the hall's far end.

He was sitting with his back against the end wall, under the last of the carving, where the tree stood alone, very large, its canopy filling the wall above him; with his legs out in front of him and his hands in his lap, as a man sits who has walked a long way and sat down to rest. He was bones. Brown, very light, the cloth that had been on them gone to a dust that lay round him on the swept floor like a shadow. His skull had tipped forward a little, onto his chest, as though he had fallen asleep reading.

In his lap, under the bones of his hands, there was a case. Leather, black, oiled once, cracked now, the size of a book.

Zale went to him first. Nobody had told him to. He went down the hall past the rest of them to the end wall and knelt there, in front of the bones, on the swept stone; and stayed kneeling a while, with his hands on his knees, not touching anything.

Then he said something. Emeric did not catch it; it was very low, and not in any language he knew. It might have been the fo'c'sle tongue. It might have been something older. Zale said it to the bones, a few words, three times; and then he reached out, very carefully, and lifted the bones of the hands off the case, one at a time, and laid them down beside the body, on the stone, gently, as if they might still feel it; and took the case.

He did not open it. He brought it back down the hall to Emeric and held it out.

Emeric took it. It was light.

"His second half," said Zale.

"Yes."

"We leave him?"

Emeric looked down the hall at the bones sitting against the end wall under the tree, with their hands on the stone beside them now, empty.

"He came here on purpose," he said. "He drew the way so that somebody could follow. He didn't draw a way back." He held the case. "I think he wanted to be here."

Zale looked at the bones a while longer.

"I'd want to be here," he said. "If it was me." He turned away. "I'd not want to be in a box in your garden with that statue."



They went out at twenty past three, with the slack running out, all of them at once, in one trip, because there would not be a second.

The pieces went with them, the way they had planned it in the hall in Kaldo with Ward asking why: each piece in its canvas lift bag, the bag filled from a stage flask until the piece hung in the water on its own, weightless, and the diver towing it on a short line behind him down the passage, never clipped to it. Osric and Declan took two each. Ward and Zale took two each. Rylan took one, because of his gauge, and did not argue about it, which told Emeric more than the gauge had. The chest went last, with Emeric, and Rylan's second piece with it, both bags on one short line behind him; and in his hands, for the first time below the surface of anything but the boat shed's tank, the pump-jet.

He let the others get ten yards down the passage before he woke it. Then he turned the stone on, a quarter, and the tube shoved against his palms like a dog on a lead, and the water went out of the back of it in a hard cold rope; and behind him, where nobody was, the floor of the passage went up in one great pale slow cloud and filled the passage to the roof and swallowed the line.

He did not look back at it. He felt it with his sense, rolling along behind, a wall of drifting silt with nothing in it but a quarter of a mile of dark. It did not matter. Nobody was in it.

It pulled. It did not pull fast; no faster than a strong man swimming hard. But a strong man swimming hard with two bags of stone behind him would have been breathing like a bellows inside a hundred yards, and Emeric was not. He hung off the grips with his fins still and the bags trailing and the line running past his shoulder, and breathed slow, and held the overlay over Ward's eyes and Rylan's without his breath going ragged for the first time all night; and when the passage bent he leaned the tube and it leaned him, and the bags swung wide and knocked the wall once, softly, and came back. Ducts, he thought. Two of them, either side, and you'd steer by the difference. Six round a hull, and gravity for up and down, and no propeller to hear. He put it away in the place he kept things for later.

Its pull went soft in the last hundred yards. The stone was tired; he had sized it for the tank, not for a quarter of a mile with two pieces of a stone figure on the line. He let it sleep, and clipped it to his belt, and finished the way the others had, with his fins.

When he looked at his gauge at the stair's foot, he had more than Ward.

The current was beginning to turn when they came to the far stair's foot. He could feel it, in the water against his face: the first slow pull, back the way they had come. Behind him Zale's note, and Ward's, and Rylan's. Ahead of him, up the stair, faint through the water, Arthur's note on the top step, and Imani's. Waiting.

And in the rock of the passage's walls, in the last fifty yards before the stair, where the walls were rougher and cracked and the carvings had worn away, sparks.

He had felt them on the way in and not stopped. Small, bright, quick, many of them, tucked into the cracks in the walls, dozens of them, each one a little cluster of life with a faint mana in it like a candle in a window. They moved when the lamps passed. Long thin feelers came out of the cracks after the light, waving.

He stopped. Ward, behind him on the line, stopped.

What? said Ward.

Supper, said Emeric.

He took them by hand. It was easy; it was the easiest thing he did all night. They were in the cracks in the walls at his shoulder, in their dozens, and his sense showed him every one, and he reached into a crack with his gloved hand and took the first by the back of its shell behind the head, where Zale had said in the hall in Kaldo that the islanders took them, and drew it out: long, spiny, mottled, its two great whip feelers waving, its tail snapping under it. No claws. A spiny lobster, the length of his forearm. He put it in the mesh bag at his hip.

The next one he took out and turned over in the lamplight, and looked at the underside of its tail; and the little paddles under the tail were big and branched, and between them, clinging, a great dark mass of eggs, like a bunch of tiny grapes.

He let her go. He let her go back into her crack, gently, tail first; and then he took her out again, and took the knife from his leg, and cut a small V in the edge of one of the flippers at the end of her tail, quickly, the way you clip a sheep's ear; and put her back.

Ward, behind him, watching through the door, did not ask. Rylan did.

Why'd you cut her?

So nobody takes her, said Emeric. She's full of eggs. Every one of those is a lobster in a few years. You take her, you've had one supper. You leave her, you've had a hundred. The notch says leave her, to anybody who knows. He took the next. It grows out in a year or two, and she'll have bred again.

Rylan was quiet.

You'd think of that, he said at last. Down here. With the door shutting.

Somebody has to, said Emeric. Or there's none.

He took fourteen, and notched three, and let them go; and they went up the last stair into the air with the slack gone and the current pulling at their legs, and the mesh bag at his hip heavy and full and snapping, and Arthur's hand reaching down out of the dark at the top to take the lift line from him.

On the sand at the sinkhole's top the pieces came out of their lift bags and into the haul bags with the lift stones sewn in their bottoms, and went across the mile of dark desert to the beach on men's shoulders, light as sacks of chaff, with Emeric's sense on the ridge the whole way; and at half past four the K Endeavor came round the headland low over the sea, her pods full, and hung off the beach while the bags went up her side on her falls.



They were in the K Endeavor's hold by five, with the dawn coming grey over the sea and the eleven pieces of the figure laid on the floor of the hold in a row in their canvas, and the month's oil in her pods, four hundred tons, and her master swearing at the weight of the stone; and the K Discovery behind her on the swell, with her boat back on its falls; and Imani sitting on a crate in the K Endeavor's hold with her bag open, going over every one of them in turn.

She went over Rylan first, because of his gauge, and found him cold and nothing else. She went over Zale, who let her, and then told her about the whistle, which nobody had told her about yet, and watched her face. She went over Ward, who did not let her, and she did it anyway. She went over Osric and Declan, who had towed two pieces each of a stone figure through a quarter of a mile of flooded passage and carried them a mile over sand and were lying on the hold's floor beside the pieces like men who had been shot.

Emeric she went over last.

She found nothing. She looked at him a long time. Then she looked at the mesh bag beside him on the floor of the hold, which was still moving.

"What," she said, "is that."

"Supper," said Emeric.



Maren looked at the lobsters for a long time.

They were in a tub of seawater on the kitchen table, in the castle, at four in the afternoon of the Sunday, eleven of them, alive, slow, waving their long feelers at the kitchen ceiling; and Maren stood over them with her arms folded and her mouth turned down and the sheet in her hand that Emeric had written out for her on the K Discovery coming home, and did not touch them.

"Sea spiders," she said.

"They're good."

"They look like something you'd find under a log." She looked at the sheet. "Boil them. In the sea water they came in, with salt in it as well, as if it wasn't salt enough. Till they're red. Then split the tails. Then butter." She looked at him. "Butter."

"Butter, and a little garlic in it, and lemon if Zale left any."

"He left limes."

"Limes, then."

Maren read the sheet again, all the way through. Then she put it down on the table, and picked up a lobster out of the tub by the back of its shell behind the head, the way he had shown her, and held it up, and looked it in its small black eyes while it waved its feelers at her face.

"If anybody in this castle dies of these," she said to it, "it'll be him first. I'll see to it."



Nobody at the table would try them.

They came out of the kitchen at seven on four great platters, red, bright red, split down their tails with the white meat steaming in the split and Maren's lime butter pooling in it; and Maren set them down the long table in the hall among the bread and the greens and stood back with her arms folded; and the table looked at them.

The six were there, and Imani and Ferra, and Rylan and his family because Rylan had been on the dive, and Tess and Osric, and Zale, and Saban at the end. And nobody put out a hand.

"They look like insects," said Bram at last.

"They are, near enough," said Emeric. "Cousins. Like a shrimp is."

"I don't eat shrimp either," said Bram.

Zale was laughing at them. He had eaten lobster all his life; the islanders cooked them on the beach in a fire of driftwood, he said, and the fishermen at Emberfell threw them back because they fouled the nets and nobody would buy them. He reached across and took a tail off the nearest platter and ate it in two bites, butter running into his beard, and sat back.

Still nobody moved.

Rylan looked at his son.

Finn was seventeen. He had been on the quay three nights out of four that week with a length of tarred line behind his back and his eyes shut; and he had been looking at the red things on the platter in front of him since they came in, with an expression Emeric remembered from a small boy in a doorway who had wanted very badly to touch a strange man's sword.

"Go on," said Rylan. "I dare you."

Finn looked at his father.

Then he reached out, and took a tail off the platter, and bit into it.

The table watched him chew.

He chewed slowly. He swallowed. He sat there for a moment with the rest of the tail in his hand, and the butter on his chin, and a look on his face of a man listening to something a long way off. Then he took a second bite, much bigger, without a word.

Wren, beside him, had been watching his face as closely as the rest of them. She was fifteen, and she had not taken her eyes off her brother since he reached for it.

"Well?" she said.

Finn swallowed.

"Get your own," he said, and pulled the platter toward him.

Wren got her own. She got it so fast that she knocked her cup over, and did not care; and bit, and chewed, and her eyes went very wide, and she said something with her mouth full that nobody could understand and everybody did. And then Rylan reached out. And Nessa. And Tess. And Osric, who took one for Tess first. And Ward, slowly, who ate it in silence and took another in silence. And Bram, last, scowling, with the face of a man doing a dangerous thing for the sake of his friends, who bit into it and stopped scowling and said nothing at all for the rest of the platter.

There were none left in a quarter of an hour.

Maren came back from the kitchen door, where she had been standing, and looked at the platters, bare shell and empty butter, and at the table, every face at it shining; and said, to nobody, "I've the shells. I'm making a stock of them," and went back in.

"The fishermen," said Saban, at the end of the table. He had eaten four. He was looking at the empty shells with an expression Emeric knew. "At Emberfell. They throw these back."

"They foul the nets," said Zale.

"They'll not throw them back next month." Saban put down his napkin. "There's a man of mine on the Emberfell quay. Coasters come up the river to us twice a month. In a tank of sea water, with a cold stone in the lid." He was already writing, in a small book on his knee, under the table's edge where he thought nobody could see. "They'll have to start bringing them in."

"Not the ones with eggs," said Emeric.

The table looked at him.

He told them. The notch. The berried female, and how to tell her, by the little pincer on her last walking legs and the big branched paddles under her tail; and the V cut in her tail flipper, and her let go, and never kept again until it had grown out. And the gauge: a measure, a length of tail, below which none was kept. And why.

"If they bring us every one they catch," he said, "every one, the big and the small and the ones with eggs, we'll eat lobster for three years, and then there'll be none in the sea at Emberfell, or at the Isle, or anywhere they fish, and nobody will ever eat one again." He looked at Saban. "We'll not buy a berried one. Or a notched one. Or one under the gauge. Put it in the order. The fishermen who want our coin will keep to it, and the ones who don't won't get it."

Saban was writing.

"That's not a law," said Ferra, from beside him.

"No. It's what we'll pay for."

"It'll be a law," said Ferra, and wrote it down too.



The fizz came after.

It came out of the kitchen in a great glass jug, on Maren's tray, with Bram behind her carrying a second one and looking pleased with himself in a way he did not usually let anybody see. The jugs were cold. They were beaded down their sides with cold, and full of something orange, and something pale gold; and the stuff in them was moving. Bubbles. A great many small bubbles, going up through the orange and the gold in long fine streams, and breaking at the top with a sound like rain on a far-off roof.

"Soda water," said Emeric, before anybody could ask. "Bubbles in it. Made, not brewed."

Bram had made it. He had made it in a week, out of Emeric's one sheet and the word soda written across the top, and a great deal of his own swearing: a small stout pressure vessel of the kind the shop made for the express's air brakes, with a gauge on it, and a tap, and a crank to shake it; and chalk from the hill and a little of the acid works' acid in a separate flask, so that when they met they gave off a gas, and the gas went down a pipe into the cold water in the vessel and was shaken into it under pressure until the water would take no more. Tess had set the safety valve. Tess had scratched a line on the gauge's face with a needle and said that if anybody filled it past that line she would take the crank off it and keep it in her drawer. Maren had made the syrups: the orange from the oranges Zale had brought at midwinter, boiled down with sugar till they were thick and bright; and the gold from the ginger in syrup in Zale's spice box, that she had been saving for something and had decided, on the Saturday, that this was it.

She poured. Orange for the children. Gold for anybody who wanted it.

Finn drank his too fast.

He drank it the way he drank water after the yard, in one long pull, his head back; and at the bottom of the pull the bubbles came back up the way they had gone down, all at once, and came out of his nose, and he put the glass down on the table and sat with his eyes streaming and his face screwed up and orange fizz running out of both nostrils, too astonished to make a sound.

Wren laughed so hard that hers came out of her nose too.

Then the whole table was laughing. Rylan was laughing with his head on his arms. Zale had his arm over his face. Tess was laughing so hard she had to hold onto Osric, who had a hand flat on her back and was laughing too, without any sound, the way he did. Imani was wiping her eyes. Ward had put his glass down very carefully and was looking at the ceiling with his jaw set.

"Fizz," said Wren, when she could breathe, and pointed at the jug. "It fizzes. That's what it is."

"Soda water," said Emeric.

"Fizz," said Wren, with great firmness; and it was fizz, from that night, in the whole of Kaldo, and nobody ever called it anything else.

Rylan had stopped laughing. He was looking down the table at Finn, wiping his nose, with an expression of great satisfaction.

"I dared him into the lobster," he said, to Ward.

Finn put down his napkin. He looked at his father. He looked at the jug of gold, the ginger, in front of Rylan's plate; and he picked it up, and poured his father a full glass of it, to the brim, so that the bubbles stood up over the rim in a little foaming dome, and pushed it across the table.

"I dare you," said Finn.

Rylan looked at the glass. The table had gone very quiet.

He picked it up. He drank it, slowly, all of it, with his eyes on his son's the whole way; and put the glass down empty; and sat there, with his face perfectly still, and a little colour coming up in it from the collar; and did not let anything come out of his nose at all.

Then he hiccupped.



There was ice cream after that, because Maren had decided that if there were going to be lobsters there would be a pudding to set against them; and it was not the coffee ice cream the table knew from the decaday, out of the churn Emeric had drawn for her, but a new one, dark brown, made with the bitter beans out of Zale's second sack, roasted and ground fine and stirred into the cream with sugar. Chocolate. Emeric had told her the word in the spring, for the drink, and she had used it for the ice cream without asking him, and he did not correct her.

Finn put a spoonful of his into his orange fizz.

He did it to see what would happen. He said so afterward. He had a glass of orange fizz in front of him, half drunk, still bubbling, and a dish of chocolate ice cream beside it, and he wanted to know what would happen if you put the one in the other; so he took a big spoonful of the ice cream and dropped it in the glass.

The glass foamed over.

It went up out of the glass's top in a great brown and orange cloud of foam, like a pot boiling over on a stove, all at once, and down the glass's sides and across the table toward his plate in a slow sweet tide; and Finn sat and watched it come, with his spoon in his hand, entranced.

Emeric got up from the table.

He did not say why. He went out of the hall through the door to the gallery, quite quickly, with his napkin in his hand, and stood in the gallery in the dark with his back against the wall and his hand over his mouth; and stood there until he could trust his face. He was thinking of a drugstore. He was thinking of a long counter and a row of red stools and a boy in a paper hat, in a town he had not thought of in eleven years, on the other side of everything, that no one in this hall would ever see; and of a glass like Finn's, foaming over, that he had sat and watched foam over when he was seven, entranced.

When he came back in, Wren was demanding one with ginger. Finn was drinking the foam off his with a spoon. Imani was watching Emeric over the rim of her glass, her eyes narrowed, and then not narrowed.

"What's it called?" said Finn.

"Ice cream soda," said Emeric, and sat down, and took a spoon, and did not look at Imani.



There were three lobsters left. He had kept them back, in the tub in the kitchen, when the others went out to the hall; and at a quarter to twelve that night Maren cooked them for him without a word, because she had seen him keep them back and had already decided what they were for, and put them on a tray with a dish of the lime butter and a loaf and a jug of the gold fizz, and gave him the tray at the kitchen door, and shut it in his face.

He took them up to the keep leads.

Ferra and Imani were already there. He had not told them to be. He came up out of the stair door onto the leads at midnight with the tray and they were sitting with their backs against the parapet on the south side, in the warm, in their shawls, side by side, looking at the stars; and they both turned their heads together when the door opened, and looked at the tray.

"I told you," said Imani to Ferra.

"You said ten past," said Ferra. "It's midnight. I win."

He sat down between them with the tray on his knees, and the town below them asleep, and the Mere a sheet of pewter under the stars, and the river going away to the west in the dark toward the sea, and the Isle somewhere out beyond the end of the world where they had been twenty hours before.

Imani dissected hers.

She did it the way she did everything with her hands: completely. She took the tail off and split it with her knife along the line the shell made, and laid it open on the tray in the lantern light, and looked at it; and then she took a leg off and cracked it and looked at the inside of that; and then she turned the body over and lifted the plate on its underside with the point of the knife and looked inside it for a long time, with her face very close, and said "Gills," to herself, with deep satisfaction, and then something else that sounded like the name of a vessel in a human chest; and then, at last, when she had seen everything there was to see, she ate it, all of it, every bit of meat, from every part, in order, and licked her fingers.

Ferra would not touch the shell.

She looked at hers on the tray, red and spined, with its long feelers and its small black eyes, and put her hands in her lap.

"I'll eat it," she said, "if it doesn't look at me."

So he did it for her. He took the tail off and split it and took the white meat out in one piece and dipped it in the butter and held it out to her, on the end of his fingers; and she looked at it, and at him, and leaned forward and took it, off his fingers, with her teeth, slowly, her eyes on his. And chewed. And swallowed.

"Oh," said Ferra.

"Yes."

"Oh. That's." She did not finish it. She opened her mouth for the next piece.

He fed her the whole of it, a piece at a time, off his fingers, while Imani took the shell from him as he finished with it and looked inside each part of it before she put it on the tray; and the three of them sat with their backs against the parapet on the keep leads in the warm summer night and ate lobster and drank ginger fizz out of the jug, passing it, and did not talk much.

Ferra fell asleep first. She went with her head on his shoulder, between one breath and the next, the way she did, with her shawl round her and a smear of lime butter at the corner of her mouth that he did not wipe off. Imani lasted longer. She sat with her head back against the parapet, looking at the stars, with the jug in her lap; and after a while she said, without looking at him:

"The ice cream soda."

He did not say anything.

"You went out." She was still looking at the stars. "When the glass foamed. You went out, and you stood in the gallery, and I felt you there. With my sense." She turned the jug in her lap. "Your note went small. Like a boy's."

"I had one," said Emeric. "When I was seven. On the other side. In a shop with red stools." He looked at the stars too. "I'd forgotten I'd had one. Until he dropped the spoon in."

Imani was quiet a long time.

Then she put the jug down on the leads, carefully, and leaned over, and put her head on his other shoulder; and in a little while, she was asleep too.

He sat there between them on the keep leads, with Ferra's head on one shoulder and Imani's on the other, and the empty shells on the tray on his knees, and the jug on the leads; and the town asleep below, and the Mere, and the river, and the Isle somewhere out there in the dark, with its swept city under it and Vey sitting at the end of his hall under the carved tree with his hands at his sides on the stone. And eleven pieces of a figure in canvas in the K Endeavor's hold at the port, with its chest stone in it, grey, asleep, full of a language he did not know. And a dead scale in the dry bag in his room, and a chip of a stone that slowed his hand.

He did not sleep. He had not expected to.

The sun came up over the keep at a little after five, and found the three of them there, with the gulls already at the shells on the tray; and Imani woke all at once the way she did, and sat up, and looked at him, and at the gulls, and at Ferra still asleep on his other shoulder with the butter at the corner of her mouth.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"No."

"Again."

"No."

Imani looked at him a moment longer. Then she reached across him, and wiped the butter off the corner of Ferra's mouth with her thumb, very gently, so as not to wake her; and licked her thumb; and lay back down against his shoulder, and shut her eyes.

"Then don't move," she said. "She's comfortable."
