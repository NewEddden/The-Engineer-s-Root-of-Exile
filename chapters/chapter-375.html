Chapter 375 - Mother

She had asked him in October, on the Thursday after the ride-up, sitting against the tree with the made hand on his head, and he had said yes without thinking about it, the way you say yes to your mother.

"I want to see what you can do," she'd said. "Properly. Not on a board. Not in a delve. On your feet, with your hands." And then, because she was who she was: "And I want to see what I can do. Tess won't let me hit anything heavier than a sack."

He'd thought about it since about once a day. He'd thought about it at six every morning in the Works yard with Maeve's hand between his shoulder blades. He'd put it off through November because of the Board and the towers and then the table, and on the Thursday after the table his mother had said, quite gently, "Next week, then," and he'd heard in her voice that she knew something had happened and that she'd stopped waiting to be told what, and that this was what she'd decided to have instead.

So it was the eleventh of December, a Thursday, at nine in the morning, in the covered yard at the knights' barracks, with the big doors shut against the cold and the high windows letting in a white winter light that lay on the sand in long bars.

Ward had cleared it. He'd sent the duty knights to the outer yard to drill in the frost, which they'd done without complaint and with a great many looks over their shoulders at the shut doors. Inside there were five people and his mother. Ward, by the weapons rack, in his shirt, to call it. Harrold on the bench along the north wall with his coat on and his hands between his knees. Maeve beside him, still in the grey coat from the Works yard, her hair still standing up a little from the morning. And at the far end of the bench, with a notebook open on her knee and a pencil in her teeth, Mira, who had not been asked, and who had arrived at a quarter to nine and sat down, and whom Ward had looked at and then not sent away.

Amara stood on the sand in the middle of the yard in the plain grey working clothes Abloh had cut for the made body, the ones she wore in the Motion Shop, and looked at him.

"Two bouts," said Ward. "A clean hold, a hand flat on the chest, or a man on his back and staying there. I call it. If I call stop you stop." He looked at Emeric. "You can't hurt her. She can't heal. Every dent's a week on Tess's bench and Tess will come and find you."

"I know."

"And you," said Ward to Amara. "You can hurt him. He heals. Don't."

"I'll try," said his mother.



The first bout took about as long as it takes to tie a boot.

He went in carefully. That was the mistake, and he knew it was the mistake about halfway through making it, and it was too late by then. He went in the way you'd go in on a machine: watching her weight, which was in the hips and the bucket-steel thighs and would take a moment to move; watching the joints, where the drives were and where Ottoline's bearings were and where a man who knew the drawings could put his hand to stop a knee bending. He'd drawn those drawings. He knew where every one of them was. He went in thinking about them.

She wasn't thinking about any of that.

He felt her go slow. It was the strangest thing he'd ever felt in a fight. He had his sense open, as he always did, and her note was there in the yard in front of him, warm and quick, the woman who'd taught nine people on boards how to sleep and wake; and as he came in it changed. It stretched. Not in the made body; in her, the mind riding it, going down into the slow time she could go into now and he couldn't. It was like watching somebody step back from a window into a room where the clock ran differently. And from that room, she watched him.

He knew she could read his tells. Ward had told him for years he had them. His left shoulder dropped before he threw anything with the right. He looked where he meant to put his foot. When he was going to pull, his jaw set. He'd worked on all three and thought he'd got two of them.

She saw all three.

He reached for the pull, the gravity, to drag her a half-step off her line toward him, the way he'd have unbalanced a man; and his jaw set; and before the pull had properly gone out of him she was already where it would have dragged her, a half-step forward, inside it, so that it took nothing; and her hand came up flat between them, quite slowly, it seemed to him, as if she were reaching past him for a cup on a shelf; and touched his breastbone, and stayed there.

She wasn't even pushing. He could feel how much she wasn't pushing.

"Bout," said Ward.

On the bench, Maeve laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, one short bark of a laugh, loud in the shut yard, and she put her hand over her mouth, and said through her fingers, "Sorry. Sorry. It's only that's what Mother used to do to me."

Harrold, beside her, didn't laugh. He was looking at his wife's hand on his son's chest with no expression at all.

Amara took the hand away.

"You were fighting a body," she said.

"I know."

"You were looking at my knees. I could see you looking at my knees. You were thinking about Ottoline's bearings." She stepped back. "I wasn't fighting a body. I was fighting you. I've known you since before you could hold your head up." She tilted the made head, a little to one side, the way she'd done alive; he'd seen it in the delve, in the memories she'd given him, a young woman at a window doing exactly that. "Your jaw, Emmy."

"I thought I'd got that one."

"You've got it with Ward. Ward's not your mother."

At the far end of the bench, Mira took the pencil out of her teeth and wrote something down.



The second bout was different, because he stopped trying to fight a machine, and because she let it be.

He went in as himself. Not carefully. He went in the way Ward had taught him and the way he'd taught himself in the years since: density first, so that the sand under his feet took his weight like a floor under a forge hammer; then the pull, short and hard, not to unbalance her this time but to bring the ground up under her own feet and make them slow; and inside it, as she stepped, the Crumple, the padded strike, his own mana laid over his fist like a glove of packed wool so that whatever it hit would be pushed and not broken. He'd made it after the cave golem cracked his hand, years ago. He'd never thrown it at anything he cared about.

She took it on the made forearm and went back two steps, and the sand sprayed up round her heels, and the bucket steel in the forearm rang like a struck bell.

He went after her.

It was a long bout, for them. A minute, perhaps. More. He would never be sure afterwards, because she spent so much of it in the slow time and dragged him half into it after her, the way being near someone very calm makes you calm. It was the closest thing to a dance he'd ever done that wasn't with Ferra and Imani, and it was nothing like a dance at all. She was very fast. She was faster than anything he'd ever stood in front of, faster than Ward, faster than the cave golem. But she was heavy, and a whole made body took a moment to throw, and he'd built it and knew how long the moment was, and twice he was inside it, close, with his hands on her.

The first time he had her wrist.

His fingers were round the made wrist, the duralumin under the silicone skin under Abloh's knit, and through it, with his sense open, he could feel the canister in her chest. The liquid mana in it that her heart moved, going round and round in the sealed loop, quick and bright. He could drain it. He'd done it a hundred times to stones in the Works, a hand on a stone and the mana coming out of it into him like water out of a cut bucket. Two breaths with his hand where it was and her loop would run dry and she'd snap back to her own body asleep in the dome, and the made one would stand down where it stood on the sand, and Ward would call it.

He didn't.

He didn't even begin to. He felt himself not begin to, and she felt it too; he knew she did, because in the slow time she turned her head and looked straight at him, at the hand on her wrist, with the made face doing absolutely nothing; and she left her wrist in his hand. On purpose. For a whole breath. To make sure he knew she knew.

Then she took it back, and turned him with it, and he went down onto one knee in the sand.

He came up. He came up fast, with the pull under her feet again and his left hand coming round, and this time he had the nozzle. The fire, narrowed down to a needle the way he'd narrowed it in the Works for cutting plate. A white thread of it the length of a finger, an inch from the side of her knee, where the drive sat behind the joint. One push, and that knee wouldn't bend for a month; she'd stand in the Motion Shop on one leg while Tess and Dov and Ottoline took it apart and said things about him.

He held it there.

And somewhere in there, between the knee and the next thing, he thought: lightning.

It was the thing that dropped a knight in plate. He'd learned it badly from a book and was learning it less badly every morning at six from his sister. It went through steel. It went through anything. He could throw the worst bolt he'd ever thrown and it would go into her the way it went into Bram's rod.

And it would go into her the way it went into Bram's rod, because he had built her. He'd spent the whole of last winter making sure of it. Every bone in her was bucket steel and duralumin and every bone wore the enamel coat with the dust in it, and he'd sat up night after night with Tess over the drawings making sure that if anything ever struck her it would run round the gossiping bones and round the sealed loop in its glass and down through her legs and out of the soles of her feet into whatever she was standing on. He'd tested it. He'd hit a made shin on the bench with Ambrose's coil until Tess threw him out. The worst it would do now was make her stumble.

He'd made her lightning-proof. On purpose. Against exactly this.

He didn't bother.

The nozzle went out. He never decided to let it; it just went out, the white thread shortening back into his fingers, because he wasn't going to push it into his mother's knee and his hand knew that before he'd finished thinking it. And she'd been waiting for that too. She'd known he wouldn't, the way she'd known about the wrist. She'd stood there with her knee an inch from a thread of fire and watched him, in the slow time, not do it.

Then she put him on his back.

He didn't see how. Afterwards Ward told him, and Mira showed him on the sand with a stick, and he still didn't see how. There was the yard's white light, and her hand, and then the sand against his shoulders and the high windows overhead and the roof beams, and his mother kneeling beside him on the sand with one hand flat on his chest again, pushing this time, a little, enough that he knew she could push a great deal more.

"Bout," said Ward.

Nobody on the bench laughed.



She didn't get up at once. She stayed kneeling on the sand beside him with her hand on his chest and looked down at him, and he lay there and looked up at her, and the made face in the white light from the windows was very good, Tess's best, and behind it she was his mother and she was not smiling.

"You could have had me twice," she said.

"I know."

"The wrist. And the knee. Twice."

"I wasn't going to."

"No. You weren't." She took the hand off his chest. "And that's right. That's what I wanted to see. If you'd done either I'd have been very proud of you and very cross, and I'd have stood on one leg for a month telling Tess it was my own fault." She sat back on the made heels. "A fight you lose because you won't hurt your mother. That's a good fight to lose. I'd want you to lose every one of those."

He waited, because there was something else. He could feel there was. She'd asked for this in October for a reason, and it hadn't been to see whether he'd hurt her.

"But you do it with the others too," said Amara.

The yard was very quiet.

"I've watched you. Not today. Two years and more. And I've been in your memories, which is worse." She didn't look at the bench. "You had your brother on his back in the yard and you let him up. You held the pope's own cardinal in a square in front of a whole town, and talked to him." She was quiet a moment. "Some of those were right. Most of them, maybe. But you don't do it because they're right. You do it because you won't. Because you can't make yourself. You stop the way you stopped at my knee. It's the same stop."

"Mother."

"A fight you lose because you won't hurt someone who means your people harm," said Amara, "is not a good fight to lose. That's a failure. That's somebody else's child in the ground because you stopped." She looked down at him on the sand. "I'm not telling you to be cruel. I'd never tell you that. I'm telling you to know the difference between your mother and a man who wants your people dead. And to know it before your hand does. Not after."

He lay on his back on the sand and looked up at the made face, and at the white light behind it, and heard it.

And he thought, without wanting to, of a band of steel with gold leaves on it, round the ankle of a man who mended benches, with a piece of himself sitting cut loose in the stone under the leaves, waiting.

A week ago. A man who hadn't harmed anyone. Not in two years and nine months.

He didn't say anything. There wasn't anything he could say. She didn't know, and she couldn't know, because he'd made sure she couldn't, and she was kneeling on the sand beside him telling him he was too soft with the people who meant his own harm; and the one place in the world where he'd been hard, he'd been hard on the wrong man, and in secret, and it was the kind of hard she'd have stopped him being.

"I hear you," he said.

"Do you?"

"Yes."

She looked at him a while longer. He felt her look, delver to delver, and he felt her feel the thing he was holding shut, the edge of it, the way he'd felt the edge of Ilenne's shut room in October; and he felt her decide, as he had decided then, not to go near it.

"Good," said Amara. She stood up, in one movement, the made body unfolding off its heels without any effort at all, and held out her hand to him. "Up. Your father's been sitting on that bench with his hands between his knees for twenty minutes, watching me throw his son about. He'll want a drink."

He took her hand. She pulled him up. She was right about the weight; the made hand was a little too heavy, and she knew it, and kept it light.



Harrold got up off the bench when they came over and looked at his wife for a long moment, and then at his son, and then at the sand behind them where the two bouts had dug it up in long scars, and said, "Well."

"You might have stopped me," said Amara.

"I tried that for eight years," said Harrold. "It didn't take then, either." He put his hand on the made shoulder. He did it as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world, which by now in Kaldo it nearly was. "Are you dented?"

"Forearm. A little. He hit me."

"He's a good boy," said Harrold. "He should hit his mother more often." He looked at Emeric over her shoulder with the face he wore in a room full of lords, and Emeric saw, under it, quite clearly for once, a man who had sat on a bench for twenty minutes and hated every one of them and wouldn't have missed it. "Your jaw sets when you're about to do something. It did when you were six."

"Everybody's been meaning to tell me."

"Then you should listen to everybody," said Harrold, and went to find Ward a drink, because Ward was the one who looked as though he needed it.

Mira had shut her notebook. She was standing at the end of the bench with it under her arm, looking at the sand where the second bout had been, at the place where his mother's feet had turned him, with a small frown, as if she were working out a sum.

"Mira," said Maeve.

Mira looked round.

"You were writing it down." Maeve had got up too. She'd taken off the grey coat at some point during the second bout without noticing, and had it over one arm, and her hair was still standing out at one side from the morning. "What were you writing?"

"Where her feet went." Mira held up the notebook, not opening it. "You can't see it from the bench. You can see it on the sand after. She never once stepped where he was looking." She put it back under her arm. "I want to know how she does that. I'll be fighting things that are faster than me for the rest of my life."

"Can you teach it?"

"Not that." Mira nodded at the sand. "Nobody can teach that. That's a whole life." She said it quite plainly, the way she said everything, and Amara, three steps away with Harrold's hand on her shoulder, made a small sound that might have been a laugh. "The spear, I can teach. If that's what you're asking."

"Is that what I'm asking?"

"You keep looking at mine."

Maeve looked at her for a moment. Then, very slightly, she went pink, which Emeric had not seen his sister do since she was six years old at the river.

"I've never held one," she said.

"Good," said Mira. "Then you've nothing to unlearn." She was looking at the sand again, not at Maeve. "Tuesdays and Fridays, after my watch, the outer yard. Bring gloves. Not court gloves."

"I haven't got any other kind."

"Then you'll have blisters," said Mira, "and you'll buy some," and went off across the yard to the doors with her notebook under her arm, and pushed one of them open with her shoulder, and let in a gust of white cold and the noise of the duty knights drilling in the frost, and went out into it.

Maeve stood watching the door swing shut.

"Hmmph," said Emeric, very quietly, to nobody.

His sister turned round and looked at him.

"What was that noise?"

"Nothing."



She walked back up to the town with him, the long way, by the frozen ruts of the barracks lane, because Harrold had taken Amara back in the closed cab and Ward had stayed to rake the sand.

She waited until they were past the last of the barracks fences and there was nobody in the lane but a carter going the other way with a load of turnips and a dog on top of it.

"You didn't try it," she said.

"Try what?"

"You know what." She looked straight ahead up the lane. "I've been out in that yard with you at six every morning for three months. You're not good. You'll never be good. But you're not dangerous any more, and you could have thrown one. In the second bout, when she had you at the knee. You thought about it. I saw your hand." She glanced at him. "My brother the lightning student. Why didn't you?"

"Because I built her."

Maeve frowned.

"Last winter," he said. "Tess and I. I sat up every night for a month making sure that if anybody ever hit her with lightning it'd go round everything that matters and down her legs into the floor. I put a coil on a shin on the bench till Tess threw me out of the shop." He watched the turnip cart go round the bend. "It'd have gone straight through her into the sand. She'd have stumbled. Maybe. And then put me on my back anyway."

His sister stopped in the lane.

"You made your own mother lightning-proof," she said slowly. "So that nobody could hurt her with it."

"Yes."

"Including you."

"Including me."

"So you lost because you were too good at your job last winter." Maeve's mouth was beginning to go at the corner. "You over-engineered your own loss."

"That's what Tess said."

"Tess was right." She started walking again, and then she was laughing, properly this time, the way she'd laughed on the bench, and not trying to stop. "That's the most you thing I've ever heard. You've got five elements and a stone in your chest and you can set yourself on fire, and you lost to your mother because you'd made her a lightning rod."

"Two," he said. "The rest I pay for."

"Five, then. Paid for. I've seen you do light. Very badly." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist. "And that's why you don't throw all of them at everything. Isn't it. I wondered. Arthur says you can do anything, and I wondered why you never did."

"Most things," he said, "one of them's enough. And the rest cost three times over." He thought about it. "And sometimes somebody's already made sure the rest won't work."

"Usually you."

"Usually me."

They came up out of the lane onto the road at the top, where the tram ran, and the town was below them, the castle and the dome and the bay grey and flat beyond it with the two towers' caps glowing on the sea wall and the point. Maeve stopped at the tram stop and looked down at it.

"I'm going to ask you something," she said, "and you're not going to answer it, and that's all right."

He waited.

"In the spring," said Maeve. "The ships."

"I know."

"I know you know. Everybody knows. Mira's going. Arthur's going. Bren's going. Half the knights in that yard this morning are going." She didn't look at him. "I've got a stone, now, and I'll have eaten it by the time you sail. I've got what I showed the board. I've got what I showed Tess's tower." She was quiet a moment. "And I've got a mother at Emberfell who'd want me home, and a father in Kaldo who doesn't know the half of it, and I know what you're going to say. Not yet. Let me think. Ask me in February."

"That's what I was going to say."

"I know." The tram was coming up the hill, its bell going. She turned and looked at him at last. "So I'll tell you now, so you've got it before February. If you say no, I'll find a way to follow you. I don't know what way. I'll find one." She said it quite calmly, the way she'd said it to Tess on the rock about the next tower, as a thing that was simply going to be true. "I've spent thirty years being kept somewhere safe. I'm not doing another."

The tram stopped. Its doors opened. Prudence Fairley was on the step in her grey jacket with her punch round her neck, looking at the two of them as though they were exactly the sort of passengers she'd expected at this stop.

"In or out," she said.

"In," said Maeve, and got in, and paid for both of them before he could, and sat down on the slatted seat by the window with her coat on her knees, and didn't say anything else all the way down the hill.
