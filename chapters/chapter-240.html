Chapter 240 - The Second Chest

He did not answer Keller.

There was nothing to answer. A cardinal had asked a chancery for copies, in writing, in order, and the chancery would comply, and a careful man had written to say so on no instruction of anyone's. Every word of it was true, and none of it asked for anything back. Emeric read it a third time at the long-room table with his hand flat on the rest of the morning's bag, and then folded it along its creases and gave it to Ferra, who put it in the box without being asked, under the silk, with the others.

Then he went down to the works, because that was what he did.

The oil had come off the Wain the night she came in over the bar, down her hose and down the slope into the tanks under the grassed mound in Fletcher's yard, the way the rule said, so that she lay at the quay by first light clean and empty and smelling of nothing worse than tar. Ten thousand gallons and some. Ferra had it on a page. Bram had it on a slate on the mound's timber gate, in chalk, with the date, under the painted board that said NO FLAME.

It was more oil than Kaldo had ever had in one place, and it was the wrong kind for most of what Kaldo wanted it for.

The glassworks still had been built for the Duvrenne casks, the winter after Emberfell: a copper pot the size of a washtub, a lid with a neck, and a worm of copper pipe going down through a barrel of cold water to a spout, where what came over dripped into a jar. It had taken the light part out of five casks in a year and put it into every lamp in the long room. It could take the light part out of ten thousand gallons in something over two years, Bram said, if nobody wanted the pot for anything else in the meantime, and if Orrin did not mind a man tending a still in the corner of his shop every day until the prince was grey.

So Bram built another, in the week after the cave, in the yard behind the glassworks where the cullet heaps were.

He built it out of a boiler shell that had failed its proof at the rail works in the spring, a long iron drum on its side on a bed of brick, and he did not put a fire under it. He put fire stones under it, in an iron box, on a deciding stone, the same as the hot box at the glassworks that had tempered the dome's panes: a row of them lying in sand under the drum's belly with no flame on them anywhere, only heat, held where it was told. The prince had asked for that. He had asked for it first, before the drum or the worm or the tanks, and Bram had known why before he finished asking, because Bram had been in the gas shed the day the valve went.

"What comes off the top of this," the prince said, standing in the cullet yard with his hand on the cold drum, "lies on the ground like water when it's spilled, and you can't see it, and it runs downhill to the nearest flame and brings the flame back up to the still. It's worse than the gas. The gas goes up. This goes down." He looked at the fire box under the drum. "So there's no flame. Not in the box, not in the yard, not a pipe, not a lantern. The lamps here are stones. It's the gas shed's rule and the mound's rule, and it's this yard's rule, from today."

"The glassworks has a pot at white heat forty feet away," said Bram.

"The glassworks has a wall between it and here. And the wind's off the lake nine days in ten." The prince looked at the wall. "Build it higher."

Bram built it higher.

The first run went through on a Thursday, three days after the Wain came home, with the prince and Bram and Ferra and a works man called Dunning who had been picked for the still because he did not smoke, and had never smoked, and said so, with some pride, when asked. They let the oil into the drum by a pipe from the mound's tanks, forty casks' worth of it, and closed the drum, and woke the stones.

It came over in the order the prince had said it would, which was the order of what boiled first.

The first was clear, and thin as water, and smelled so sharp that it made the eyes run from across the yard, and when Dunning held a drop of it on his fingertip it was gone off his skin while he watched, into the air, leaving his finger cold. The prince had them run it off into iron, not glass, into a tank by the wall with a lid that screwed down.

The second was clear too, and a little yellow, and oily between the fingers, and smelled like the lamps in the long room. The third was heavier and darker, the colour of small beer, and it slid. And after a long time, when the drum had been hot all day and Dunning had changed the jars under the spout nine times, the fourth came over thick and brown and slow; and they ran it off into a trough to cool, and in the evening, in the cold, it went cloudy, and then white, and a skin of something pale came up on it and set, the way fat sets on a stock.

What was left in the drum when they opened it the next morning was black, and stiff, and smelled like the first avenue on a hot day.

Ferra stood at the trough with her book.

"I'm not writing the lighter part," she said. "Or the heavy part. Or what comes over third. I've had a year of the lighter part in the rubber shed's line, and I've never once known how much of it there was, because it wasn't a thing, it was a part." She looked at the prince. "What are they?"

He told her what he knew. It was not the names he had for them. Those were no use here; they were names out of the door he could not find the handle of, and one of them was the name of a place that did not exist. So he told her what each one was for, and she named them by that, the way she had named the pourers, standing at the trough with her pencil, and wrote them down one under another as if they had always been called that.

Spirit. The first. For cutting rubber into cement, and for nothing else, because it would burn the yard down the first time anybody forgot. Iron only. No flame within a hundred paces. Kept in the paint store.

Lamp oil. The second. For the long room, the counting room, the ward. And the bearings on the line shafts, where the light oil had been going.

Engine oil. The third. For the bench engine and its twin, and for the injectors Bram was forever taking to pieces.

Wax. The white skin off the trough, which Bram lifted off in a sheet the next morning with a spatula and broke in his hands, and smelled, and held to the stone lamp to look through. It was harder than tallow and did not stink. It would take a wick. It would soak into cotton and keep the wet out of it, better than the soap house's wax, which the island women had been running the dynamo's thread through since the first winding.

Black end. The drum's bottom. For roads, as the first one had been. For the seams in the dock. For anything the island pitch was wanted for that did not need to be the island pitch.

"And the spirit's worth what?" said Ferra.

"To us? A pot of rubber cement a week. To anybody else, a fire." He looked at the iron tank by the wall. "We make too much of it and we can't sell it. I'll think of something."

"You said that about the gas that goes up the pole."

"I'm still thinking about the gas that goes up the pole."

She wrote Spirit: too much, and drew a line under it, and gave him the look she gave a figure she did not believe yet.

It was the lamps that did it, in the end. Not the still. The lamps in the long room.

He sat up late at the table on the fourth night after the cave, alone, with the day's papers put away and Keller's letter in the box and the new lamp oil in the lamps, and the lamps were good. They burned white and clean and did not smoke, and they had not smoked since the first Duvrenne casks. He could read by them. He could read by them better than any man in the kingdom could read after dark, probably, except the man across the court in the counting room, who was reading by the same oil.

And below the palace, in the three quarters of the city that had houses in them, the windows were yellow.

He went to the window and looked at them. Lane oil and candles. Rush lights, some of them, in the houses where the lane oil was too dear for a Tuesday. A kitchen here and there bright at the back where a woman was working late, and the rest going out one by one as he watched, because a candle cost what a candle cost and people went to bed with the dark. They had gone to bed with the dark on the island when he came. They had gone to bed with it for three hundred years. They had moved four miles up a road into houses with glass in every window and hot water in every kitchen and a drain under every floor, and they still went to bed with the dark, because light was the one thing he had not brought them.

He had said it at this table. Years ago, in the thaw, with the red copper in front of him and his plans round it like a hand of cards. In the quarters. In the streets. White ones. And he had put the lamps on the drops, at the table in the still week, the lamps in the quarters, when there are lamps in the quarters, and Ferra had written it down, and then there had been a dock to light, and a pump to turn, and a ship, and an island, and a cave; and the lamps in the quarters had stayed a line in a book with nothing under it.

The white lights over the dock were no use in a house. He knew that. They were arcs: two rods of carbon with a gap between them and the current jumping the gap, too bright to look at and too hot to stand under, eating a rod a night. An arc in a kitchen would be like keeping the sun in a cupboard. A room wanted a small light. A light the size of a candle's, that did not flicker and did not smoke and could not set a curtain alight or fill a cellar with gas, and that a child could put out with one finger and put on again.

He knew how it was done. He had known for years. He had put it off because it needed a thing Kaldo did not have, and then, when Kaldo had it, because there had been a ship and an island and a cave.

There was no ship now. The island could wait a season. The cave was done.

He looked at the yellow windows until most of them had gone out. Then he went to bed, and did not sleep much, and in the morning he went down to the works and asked Bram for a crucible, a handful of charcoal, and a reel of cotton thread.

He showed them the first one in the pattern store, the next afternoon, on the long bench, with the pair's wire coming down to it from the shed behind the foundry and a switch Bram had made for it out of a hinge and a brass tongue.

The thread had been in the coke ovens all night. It had gone in as cotton, coiled in a little iron box packed round with powdered charcoal and the lid clayed shut, so that the heat got at it and the air did not, and it had come out in the morning black and stiff and brittle, still the shape of a thread, still coiled the way it had been coiled, and so light that Bram, lifting it out of the charcoal with a pair of tweezers, had not been able to feel it in them. Emeric had clamped the two ends of a short piece between two brass posts on a block of slate, a finger's length apart, and run the wires to them.

"That's a burned thread," said Bram.

"It's a burned thread. It's nearly all carbon now. Like the rods in the dock lamps, only thin." Emeric put his hand on the switch. "Watch it."

He threw the switch.

The thread went red. Then orange, and then, for the space of a breath, nearly white, a hair of white light in the air between the posts, so bright and so small that it left a line on the eye; and then it went out, and there was a thin curl of grey going up from where it had been, and a smell of scorched cloth, and the two posts had nothing between them.

Bram looked at the posts for a while.

"It burned," he said.

"It burned. Anything burns that hot, in air. The air's what burns it." Emeric unclamped the black stubs. "So you take the air away. You put the thread in a glass, and you take every breath of air out of the glass, and you seal it. Then there's nothing in there for it to burn in. It gets as hot as that, and stays that hot, and gives that light, and doesn't go anywhere."

"Every breath," said Bram.

"Every breath."

Bram did not answer at once. He looked at the stubs of black thread on the slate, and then across the pattern store, past the benches and the lathe and the racks of patterns, to the door at the back that went through to the locked room, which had one key, and the key was his.

He did not say anything. He went and got it.

The chest had stood in the locked room since the spring before last, lead-lined, with NOT OPENED BUT BY B. on its lid in red lead a hand high, and beside it the second chest, empty, which had been waiting since the spring before last for what would come after. Bram opened the first on the bench in the pattern store with the door to the yard propped wide and the windows open, though nothing in it could hurt anybody until it was heated, because the prince had said so and Bram wanted to see the prince be right before he trusted it.

The stone lay in its two wrappings of oilcloth. He unwrapped it, and it was what it had been on the island: a lump the colour of dried blood, streaked darker where it had broken, dull, and very heavy for its size. It had been in Ferra's box and then in this chest for the better part of two years, and it had not changed at all. Stones did not.

"How much will it give?" said Bram.

"Most of what it weighs. Six parts in seven." Emeric weighed it in his hand. "Not quite two pounds, I'd think. It'll be enough for one pump. One good one."

"And the island has more."

"The island has more. Where this came from." He put it down. "Next spring."

Imani came down from the ward in the afternoon, because he sent for her, and he told her what it did when it was heated, all of it, as he had told Bram the spring before last, and she listened with her arms folded and her face going very still, the way it went when somebody described a sickness she had seen and not been able to name.

"Say it again," she said, when he had finished. "The signs. In order."

He said them again. The hands first, a shake that a man does not notice until he tries to thread a needle. Then the gums, soft, bleeding when he eats, and the teeth loosening in them. Then the man himself: shy, and short-tempered, and weeping for nothing, and not able to say why. Months, it took. Sometimes years. And by the time anybody noticed, most of it did not go back.

Imani looked at the stone on the bench for a long time.

"Then I'll set the rule," said Imani.

She set it standing in the pattern store, and Ferra wrote it, and it was longer than any rule Emeric had ever made, because Imani had spent her life burying people who died slowly of things nobody had been able to name, and now she had a name for one.

The roasting was done out of doors, on the works' old quarry floor above the lake, on a morning when the wind was off the land and going out over the water, and there was no house and no boat and no man downwind of it for a mile. The retort was iron, closed, a bottle with a long neck that bent down into a trough of water, so that what came off the stone came out of the neck into the cold and turned back from a breath to a liquid before it could be breathed. It was heated with fire stones on a deciding stone, like the still; no flame, and no man at the fire, because there was no fire to be at. Nobody stood on the lee side of it. Nobody stood within twenty paces of it on any side, and Imani paced the twenty herself and set a rope round them on stakes. Three men were let inside the rope, and she wrote down which three: the prince, and Bram, and Dunning from the still, because Dunning was steady and did not smoke and she wanted a man at it who would still be steady in twenty years. Every man who went inside the rope washed his hands and his face at the trough by the gate when he came out, and every one of them would come to the ward once a month for a year and show her his gums and hold out his hands flat, so that she could watch them.

And never in a room with a door shut, she said, which was the prince's own line, from the spring before last; and Ferra wrote it under the rest.

Zale came up to the quarry floor to watch. He was not supposed to be out of the palace. His throat was still raw from the canister, and he could still only whisper, and Imani had told him to sit in a warm room and drink things; he came anyway, wrapped to the eyes in a coat of his brother's, and stood outside the rope with Ferra, upwind, and watched the three men at the retort in the grey light with the lake behind them.

The stones woke. The retort warmed. For a long while nothing happened at all.

Then, at the end of the neck, where it went down into the trough, something came out that was not water.

It came out as a bead. A bright one, the size of a lentil, that ran down the inside of the neck and dropped into the trough and lay on the bottom under the water, silver, whole, round, trembling a little when the trough was touched. Then another. Then a thin bright thread of them, running together at the bottom of the trough into a little pool that shone up through the water like a coin in a well, and moved when the water moved, and broke into beads when Bram, very carefully, with an iron spoon, touched the edge of it, and ran together again when he took the spoon away.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Quick silver," Zale whispered, outside the rope. Ferra looked at him. "That's what they call it on the Kesrath shore. Live silver. There's a physician in the port who sells it in a sealed horn, a thimble at a time, for the pox." He looked at the trough. "I never knew where it came from. He said it came from a long way off."

"It came off the island," said Ferra. "The one you stood on." She wrote it down. Then, after a moment, she turned a page back in her book to one near the front, where there was a list in her own hand with a heading at the top of it, Zale, going, and wrote a line at the foot of it, and turned the book so that Zale could see.

The red stone. Any port. Any price in reason. And the white metal, if anybody has it, by the ounce.

"What white metal?" whispered Zale.

"I'll tell you when I know," said Ferra, "whether we need it."

It took the whole of the morning. At noon the stones were put to sleep and the retort left to cool inside its rope until the next day, and then opened, and there was nothing in it but a pale crumble, like old mortar, that Imani had buried on the quarry floor under a yard of clay, on the spot, before anybody went home. And in the trough, under the water, a pool of silver that Bram poured off, water and all, through a leather, into an iron bottle the size of a man's fist, with a screw stopper, and weighed, and then put into the second chest. It had been waiting since the spring before last.

It weighed not quite two pounds. Bram locked it in.

The pump was glass, all of it, and Orrin made it, in four days, with two of his best blowers and the prince's drawing pinned to the post by the pot, and a good deal of swearing at the drawing.

It was a tube. That was the heart of it, and Orrin could not at first believe that was all it was. A glass tube as tall as a tall man, and a little more, standing upright in a frame against the wall of the pattern store, as thin as a reed inside, so thin that a bead of the silver would just fill it from wall to wall. At its top, a glass bowl that held the silver, with a little glass tap under it that let it out a drop at a time. And just below the tap, joined into the side of the tube with a seam that Orrin's best blower made three times before the prince would pass it, a second, shorter tube, going off sideways; and on the end of that the thing to be emptied.

"The drop falls," the prince said, to Bram and Orrin and Ferra and Dunning, round the frame on the first evening. "Down the tube. It fills the tube as it falls, wall to wall, like a cork going down a bottle's neck. And as it passes the side tube it shuts off a little of whatever's in there and takes it down with it, in front of it, a bubble, all the way to the bottom and out." He touched the glass. "Then the next drop. Then the next. Every drop a bubble. Every bubble a little of the air out of the glass on the side tube. It's slow. It's very slow. But it doesn't stop until there's nothing left to take, and then the drops start to fall with a click, like a pebble on a slate, instead of sliding. When you hear the click, the glass is empty."

"And the silver at the bottom?" said Orrin.

"Goes back to the top and falls again. It's the same two pounds, round and round. That's why two pounds is enough."

Bram put Kaldo's own pump in front of it. That had been his idea, not the prince's, and the prince had stopped in the middle of the floor when Bram said it, and then laughed out loud, which he did perhaps once a year. The pump off the vial rig, the three iron cylinders each smaller than the last, that took the band's air and squeezed it down into a glass vial the length of a finger. It could be run backward. A pump that pushed air into a glass could be made to pull it out of one, if you turned the valves round; it could not get all of it, not nearly, not the last thin breath of it, but it could get most of it in a quarter of an hour, and leave the drops only the last of it to take. Bram turned the valves round in an afternoon and belted it off the line shaft through a clutch, and Ferra wrote vial pump, backward in the book, and the prince said it was the best thing anybody had done in the pattern store that year, and Bram said it was only a pump.

The glass was the next thing, and the glass was where it nearly stopped.

A bulb was a small glass pear, blown thin, with a neck. Orrin could blow them by the hundred. The thread went inside it, clamped at its two ends to two wires; and the two wires had to come out through the glass, through the neck, sealed into it, so that the current could get in and the air could not. Orrin's blower melted the neck down round two copper wires in the flame, and pinched it shut, and it was a beautiful seal. It shone. It held the whole of the afternoon.

In the evening, when it had cooled, there was a crack in it. A fine one, from each wire, going out into the glass like a hair.

They did it again with thicker glass, and it cracked. They did it with thinner wire, and it cracked more slowly. They did it with iron wire, which was worse, and with the brass off a hinge, which was worse again. Every seal they made was beautiful when it was hot and cracked when it was cold, and every one of them cracked from the wire outward, as if the wire were pulling at it.

"It is pulling at it," the prince said.

He was sitting at the end of the bench with a cracked neck in his fingers, turning it to the stone lamp. Ferra saw his face do the thing it did. Nothing. Very still.

"It's the tear," he said. "The wrong way round." He held the neck up. "Everything shrinks as it cools. The glass shrinks, and the copper shrinks, and the copper shrinks more than the glass does, a great deal more. So the copper pulls back out of the glass as they cool, and the glass round it is pulled. Not squeezed. Pulled. And glass doesn't break when you squeeze it." He put the neck down on the bench. "It breaks when you pull it."

"Then find a wire that doesn't shrink," said Bram.

"Find a wire that shrinks exactly as much as the glass. Not more. Not less. The same." He looked at the little heap of cracked necks. "There's one. One metal. It's white, and it's heavier than gold, and it doesn't rust, and it melts hotter than iron, and it shrinks the same as glass, so near that you could seal it into a window and it would never crack." He stopped. "I've never seen it in this world except on a coin, and nobody who mints a coin tells you what's in it. I don't know where it's found. I don't know that it's found at all."

It was Ferra who said it. She said it from the far end of the bench, where she had the glass book open, and she did not look up from it, because she had not quite decided whether to say it yet and was deciding while she said it.

"We have some."

They looked at her.

"White. Heavier than gold. Doesn't rust." She put the pencil down. "It's in the treasury box. With the Crown's arms on every piece of it."

Nobody said anything.

"A Platinum Rand," said Ferra, "is supposed to be platinum. I've never asked. Nobody who's held one has ever had a reason to cut it." She shut the book. "We have a reason."

She brought it down herself, the next morning, one coin, in her glove, and put it on the bench, and stood there while Bram looked at it.

It was a heavy grey-white disc a little larger than a Gold Rand and a great deal heavier, with the king's head on one side, younger, and the Crown's arms on the other, and a milled edge. It did not look like much. Coins that were worth most never did. Bram weighed it in his palm, and then on the small beam, and then he put it in the vice and took a file to its edge, and filed a little bright notch, and looked at the filings on the paper, and looked at the notch, and said nothing, which meant he did not know.

"We'll know by the glass," said the prince. "If it's what the name says, it'll hold."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then the Crown's been cheating dukes for three hundred years and none of them noticed," said Ferra, "and I'd rather know that too."

Bram cut it. He cut a sliver off its edge with a cold chisel, which took some time, because it was harder than it looked, and made Ferra wince in a way she was careful not to let anybody see, and drew the sliver through his finest plate, and through it again, and again, until it was a wire as fine as a hair and as long as his arm and bright as a new needle. The coin went back in the box with a bite out of its rim. There was a line in Ferra's book beside it before it went, in her own hand, which said, One Platinum Rand. Cut. Drawn to wire, for the glass. Not spent. Used. And the date, and her initial, which she did not usually put.

Orrin's blower sealed two short pieces of it into the neck of a bulb in the flame, and pinched it, and let it cool.

It did not crack. It did not crack in the evening, or in the morning. The prince held it up to the window and turned it for a long time, and then gave it to Bram, and Bram put it in the vice, gently, by the glass, and turned the handle until the vice held it hard, and it did not crack then either.

"It's platinum," said the prince. "The Crown's honest."

"Write that down," said Bram, "somebody."

Ferra had. She worked out, that afternoon, on the back of the page, how many bulbs a coin would do if the wire was as fine as Bram could draw it and each bulb wanted two lengths of it the length of a fingernail. She did it twice. Then she wrote the figure down and underlined it and did not say it aloud, because it was larger than the number of houses in the barony, and larger than the number of houses in the barony would be in twenty years, and she did not want Bram to start thinking in those terms yet.

The thread was the last thing, and it was the thing that took longest, because a thread in a glass with nothing in it did not burn away; it only went slowly thin, and grey, and broke. The cotton ones broke in an evening. The prince sat up with the first one on the long bench and watched it: a pear of glass a little larger than an egg, on a stand of slate, with a black loop of burned cotton inside it clamped to two bright wires, and the two wires going out through the sealed neck to the switch, and the neck's tip, where it had been drawn off the side tube of Orrin's pump, closed in the flame to a little glass point like the end of a teardrop. He threw the switch. The loop went red, and orange, and then white, and stayed white, a small fierce steady light inside the glass that lit the bench and the benches beyond it and the lathe and the racks and the faces of the men who had not gone home, and did not flicker, and did not smoke, and did not move at all.

It burned for four hours and a half. Then it went orange, and dim, and out, and there was a grey smudge on the inside of the glass where it had been.

He tried silk, which was worse. He tried linen, which was worse again. He tried a hair off the tail of a cart horse in the works' stable, which Ferra wrote down without comment and which burned for eleven minutes.

Then he tried paper.

It was a sheet of Kaldo's own, off the paper works' finest vat, the thin hard sheet the printers took for the notices. He cut a strip of it the width of a pin, bent it into a horseshoe, and packed it in the charcoal box and sent it to the coke ovens overnight; and in the morning it came out a black horseshoe of paper, as fine as the thread and stiffer, and he clamped it and sealed it and Orrin's drops emptied the glass round it, click, click, and he threw the switch.

It burned all that night.

It burned all the next day, and all the next night. On the third day Bram stopped coming to look at it every hour. On the fifth day nobody looked at it at all, except the boys who came through the pattern store and stopped, every one of them, and put their faces close to it, and went away blinking. It burned for sixteen days, and on the morning of the seventeenth it had gone out in the night, and the inside of the glass was grey, and the horseshoe had broken at the top of its curve.

Ferra wrote it in the glass book. Paper. Sixteen days. And then, because she had watched him look at it on the first night, and had seen his face, she wrote something in the margin, small, which he did not see until later. Kaldo paper.

He did not need to see it. He had thought it himself, on the first night, sitting at the bench with the white light on his hands. That the paper the barony made had carried every honest line Kaldo had ever written: every roll and every return and every list, fair-copied and on time; and that a cardinal had a desk of it now, three rows of it, squared to the edge; and that the same paper, off the same vat, bent into a horseshoe and burned in the dark, would give a light a man could read by for sixteen days and hide nothing at all.

He had not said it. It was not a thing to say. But he had thought it.

They brought a new one up to the palace the evening after the first paper one went out, and put it on the long-room table, with the wire run in through the window from a pole Bram's men had put up in the court that afternoon, on a spur off the yard's line where it came round the ring road to the second tower. It would do for one night. It stood in the middle of the table on its slate among the papers, a pear of clear glass with a black horseshoe in it. Nobody had lit the oil lamps. The room was nearly dark. They were all there: the table, and Armand in his chair, who had come across the bridge for it because the prince had asked.

The prince put his finger on the switch and looked round the table, and then did not make a speech, and threw it.

The horseshoe went red and orange and white, and the room came up round it.

It was not a great deal of light. It was less than the two oil lamps would have given together, and the corners of the room stayed dim, and it was a shade warmer, a shade nearer yellow, than it had been on the bench in the pattern store; Emeric saw that, and Bram saw it, and neither of them said anything yet. But it was steady. That was what they saw first, all of them; he watched them see it. It did not move. There was no flame in it to move. It did not bend when Saban leaned across the table to look at it, or when the door opened and Hobb came in and stopped dead, or when the draught came under the door behind him. It lit the papers and the ink on the papers and the grain of the table and everybody's hands, and it made no sound, and gave off no smell, and did not smoke, and sat in the middle of the table as still as a stone.

"It doesn't flicker," said Imani.

"It can't. There's nothing in it to flicker."

Armand had got up out of his chair. He did not often do that without being helped, in the evenings. He came to the table on his stick and stood over the glass with his face close to it, the way the boys in the pattern store had, and his eyes narrowed against the light, and Emeric watched him do the thing he had known he would do, because every mage did it. He put out his hand, not touching the glass, and reached. Past the glass, into it, for the working, the way a mage reaches for a stone to see what is in it.

He held his hand there a long time.

"There's nothing in it," he said at last.

"No."

"Not a stone. Not a dead one. Not the smallest shaving of one." Armand took his hand away. "It's like the quarry lights. I went and stood under one, the first winter, and reached, and thought I'd reached wrong." He looked at the glass. "I could show this to the Circle."

"Yes."

"I could take it up the road and set it on the table in the tower hall in front of every master in the kingdom, and let them reach for it all day, and there'd be nothing for any of them to find." Armand's voice was very quiet. "There'd be nothing in it to try."

"That's what it's for," said Emeric.

He had not meant to say it. He heard it after he had said it, in the quiet room, with the white light steady on everybody's hands, and he knew it was true, and he knew that it was the reason he had gone down to the works with a reel of cotton thread instead of sitting at this table with a clean sheet and a pen.

Everything he had built in Kaldo that mattered had a secret in it. The ship. The drops. The water in the towers. The dome, and the thing under the dome. The room he took the table into. Every one of them was a thing that could be found, and he had built walls round them and lied round them with the truth and put them in a box with a key on a cord, and every one of them had made the box heavier. And a cardinal had a desk now, three rows of paper, and was waiting for somebody to find the rest.

This had nothing in it. Anybody could stand under it. Anybody could look at it all night and take it to pieces in the morning and find glass, and paper, and a coin, and a drop of silver going round. There was nothing in it to find because there was nothing in it to hide.

He wanted, very badly, to have made one thing like that before the winter.

Ferra did the sum at the table, afterward, by it. She did not light the oil. She turned her book to the light and did it by the bulb, and did not remark on it, and he saw her notice that she did not have to lean.

"The quarters," she said. "Three of them with houses. Ilsa's book says eleven hundred and forty doors, this morning. The fourth's still stakes." She wrote. "One of these to a kitchen and one to the room above, that's two to a door. The school in each quarter. The bakehouses. The watch houses. The palace." She looked up. "How many rooms in the palace?"

"Too many."

"The palace. The ward. The school on the island. The tower, if Armand wants it."

"I want it," said Armand, from his chair.

"The tower." She wrote. "Streets. Every street, or the gates and the avenues?"

"Every street."

She wrote a long figure and looked at it.

"Orrin blows bulbs by the hundred," she said. "That isn't the trouble. The drops are. One pump, one tube, one drop at a time." She looked at Bram. "How long to empty one?"

"Half a morning. Less, now the vial pump's in front of it." Bram rubbed his jaw. "Put six on the side tube at once. Twelve. On a comb of glass. The drops don't care how many there are behind the tap."

"Twelve at a time. Three times a day. Six, if two men stand it by night." Ferra wrote. "Seventy a day. Near enough. More, when Orrin's blowers stop cracking the combs." She looked at the figure. "Five weeks for the quarters and the palace. The streets after. The island after that."

"And the drops?" said Bram. "At the yard. The house'll burn more."

"A flask a day more. Two, on the long nights, till the second hour." She had it already; she had had it since the still week, in a column with nothing under it. "Hull two goes up every clear night instead of every second. And we're not at the mercy of a month any more. The island pitch holds a season. The shelf fills." She put the pencil down. "It'll carry it. Just."

"It won't reach," said the prince.

They looked at him.

"The wire." He put his hand flat on the table beside the bulb. "The yard's a mile from the south gate. The far streets of the first quarter are half a mile past that, and the island's four past that. The current goes up the wire from the yard, and every yard of wire it goes up it loses some, and the further it goes the more it loses. I said so at this table in the still week." He looked at Ferra. "You drew a ring round it."

She did not need to look. She knew the page. Loss on the wire. A blank, with a ring round the blank.

"The tower at the corner gets enough to turn a pump," he said. "It gets less than the yard sends, and a pump doesn't care. A lamp does. You're looking at it." He nodded at the bulb. "That's the same glass and the same paper that burned on Bram's bench a few yards from the pair, and it's yellower here. That's the mile. A lamp at the end of two miles of that wire would be a red thread in a glass. And the island wouldn't see anything at all." He took his hand off the table. "So we don't send it that way."

"Which way do we send it?" said Bram.

"Harder," said the prince. "Much harder. Down a thinner wire. And let it down again at the other end, gently, before it gets into anybody's house." He looked at the bulb. "It's the same current. It's only pressure."

Bram looked at him for a long time across the white light.

"How do you push lightning harder?" he said.

"Coils," said the prince.

He sat up after they had gone.

He did not light the oil. The bulb would burn on the yard's line until morning, Bram said, and then he wanted it back in the pattern store to see what the ride up had done to it. So he sat with it, at the long-room table, alone, with the papers put away and the bulb in the middle of the bare wood, and the white light lying steady on the grain.

After a while he got up and went to the box, which Ferra had left with him, and unlocked it, and took Keller's letter out from under the silk, and brought it back to the table, and unfolded it, and read it again.

It said the same thing. It said the same thing by a light that did not move as it had said by a light that did. Fair copies of every quarter's rolls. The entrance list, as printed. The request is in order. The chancery will comply.

He read it twice. Then he folded it along its creases and put it back in the box under the silk, and locked the box, and went to the window.

Below the palace the city was dark. The last of the yellow windows had gone out an hour ago. Eleven hundred and forty doors, and a cold night coming down on them, and the long nights after it; and somebody in each of those houses would get up in the dark before the light came, all winter, and feel for the flint.

Five weeks, Ferra had said. The quarters and the palace.

He stood at the window with the bulb burning behind him, and the light from it went out through the glass and lay on the court below, a pale square with his own shadow in it, the only light in the city; and he thought that he could do nothing about the cardinal, or the rolls, or the list, or the paper on the desk; but that he could do this, and that it could be done before the snow, and that nobody could ever copy it into a case, because there was nothing in it.

He went to bed. He slept, for the first time since the cave, all night.
