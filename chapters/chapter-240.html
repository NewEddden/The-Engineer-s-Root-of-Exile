Chapter 240 - Not for Sale

Vantic priced it on the fourth of December, at the long table, after supper, in the order she priced everything: the smallest thing first.

She had asked for an hour. She had asked in writing, a week before, in the House's hand on the House's paper, which she had never once done in all her years of sitting at that table whenever she liked; and Ferra had read the note and put it in her book without a word and given her the Friday. So the table had stayed after supper. The plates had gone. The lamps were lit. Ward was at the door, as he always was, and Bram at the far end with his pipe unlit, and Saban with his hat on his knee, and Armand in his chair at the head of the table where Emeric had put him in June and where he had sat since, as if it had always been his; Imani with her register's day-book; Ferra with hers. And Vantic at Emeric's left hand, in the grey she wore for the House now that she was the House, with a leather case on the table in front of her and her hands folded on it.

There was business first. There was always business first. Saban had the posting-house driver, or rather he had not: the summer was gone, he said, and so was the man with the scar through his eyebrow who had been driven north two Decembers ago; the driver had been paid off in the autumn and gone over the border to his wife's people with more money than a driver had a right to, and Saban had a man going after him, slowly, because a man running from a question ran faster if he heard you were asking it. Ferra had the last of the steam: the pipe had reached the long hall and the market hall the week before, the last two buildings on the old line, and the works could stop laying steam in the town and start laying it nowhere until the plan's next ward, which suited the works. Bram had the rotary kiln, which had made more cement in a fortnight than the old kilns had in a season, and which he was still calling unnatural.

Then Ferra closed her book a quarter of an inch, which at that table meant the business was done, and looked at Vantic.

Vantic opened her case.

She took out three sheets of paper and laid them side by side in front of her, squared, and did not push them across the table to anybody. She did not need to. Everybody at that table had watched her do this for years, and knew that the sheets were for her and the figures were for them, and that she would say the figures and the sheets would go back into the case.

"A vial," she said.

She did not ask what was in it. She had sat at that table the night the plant first ran and been told, and she had been sworn to the ships' oath since the night she told Emeric her own name; she knew what was in a vial and where it had been sealed and by whose finger.

"A vial holds what a flask cell holds. A hedge mage's charge, a stone that size filled, fetches six Silver in the capital in a good year. The House has bought enough of them for its clients to know the price to a Copper." She touched the first sheet. "Nobody else in the realm can sell a cell's charge that will sit in a glass for a year and come out at the end of it as full as it went in. Nobody can sell one that does what this does when you open it. The House would ask twenty. It would get thirty." She did not change her voice. "In the first year. In the second, when the foundries understood what they had, more."

"Go on," said Emeric.

"A canister." The second sheet. "Eight vials in a cage that can't be broken by dropping it, that a ship can run on in a dead zone, that a forge can run on for a season, that a mage can pour from into his own stones as long as it lasts without spending one breath of his own. The Circle has wanted a thing like this for three hundred years. The Church shut the climbs that might have found it." She looked at the sheet. "I don't know what a canister would fetch. I know what I'd ask. I'd ask in Platinum."

Bram, at the far end, took his pipe out of his mouth.

"And a market," said Vantic.

The third sheet. She did not touch it.

"Not the vials. What they do." She looked down the table at Armand. "Master Armand's study, which I've not read and been told enough of. A man grows, slowly, in a dose. He grows the way a knight grows in the rite, without the rite. Every house in the realm with a son it would like to be a knight and does not want to bury. Every house with a daughter it would like to be a mage and was not born one. Every lord with a garrison. Every Circle chapter with a promising boy whose reading is a shade too low." She folded her hands on the case. "The House has done this sum three times. It doesn't come out as a figure. It comes out as the largest trade the realm has ever had, run by whoever holds the Band, for as long as nobody else can reach it." She looked at Emeric. "That's the price of the third sheet. I thought you should hear it said once, in this room, by somebody who means it."

Nobody at the table said anything.

"No," said Emeric.

Vantic did not move.

"Not the vial," he said. "Not the canister. Not the dose, not a cup of the water, not a flask, not a stone that's been to four miles. Not to the foundries and not to the Circle and not to the king. Not this year and not when the debt's long gone and the House is the crown's bank and I'm old." He did not raise his voice. "Never."

"Why?" said Vantic.

She did not say it as a protest. She said it as she said everything at that table, as a woman entering a reason in a column.

"Because of who would buy it." He looked at the third sheet she had not touched. "Every house with money. You said it. Every house with a son. Every lord with a garrison. They'd buy it for their sons and their garrisons and their friends' sons. They'd buy a class of knights who never had to lie on the stone, and a class of mages who were made and not born, and they'd buy it for themselves and the people they eat with." He put his hand flat on the table. "And not one of them would buy a canister to put in the well of the village under his hall. Not one. Why would he? A village of commoners who could feel a stone is a village he can't keep."

Vantic said nothing.

"Right now," said Emeric, "the line between the people who have it and the people who don't is the rite, and the rite kills nine in ten, so it's a line a lord has to pay for in his own sons' blood. It's a bad line. It's a cruel one. But it moves. A farmer's boy can lie on that stone and get up a knight, if he lives." He looked at her. "Sell this, and the line stops moving. The ones with money are made, slowly, safely, and their children after them, and nobody else ever is. In twenty years it sets like cement." He took his hand off the table. "I'll not sell anybody the cement."

Vantic sat with her hands folded on her case a while.

Then she picked up the three sheets, one at a time, squared them, and put them back in the case, and closed it, and buckled the strap.

"Then it's worth nothing," she said.

"It's worth nothing."

"The House will note it." She looked at the buckled case. "The House will note that it has been shown the largest trade it will ever be offered and been refused, at a table, in front of witnesses, for a reason it could write down." She was quiet. "I think I'd rather have the reason. I'll not tell my father that."

Then, to Armand, without turning her head from the case: "And the study?"

Armand had been sitting very still at the head of the table with both hands on his stick.

"In a box," he said. "Since July. With a key. Ferra has the key." He did not elaborate. "I wrote it in the tower's form and then I read it as the tower would read it, and saw it was a manual for anybody with a cellar and a hired mage and no patience. It's not for sale either, Mistress Ducatis. Not even to you."

"I'd not have priced it," said Vantic. "I'd have wanted to read it."

"So would every Circle in the realm," said Armand. "That's the trouble."

Vantic inclined her head to him, a tower master's depth exactly, which she had learned from watching him give it to Imani in September, and picked up her case and stood.

"Thank you for the hour," she said, to Ferra. "I'll not ask for another on this." And to Emeric, at the door, with her hand on it: "The House will want to know what you mean to do with it, then. If not sell it. Not tonight. Some night."

"Some night," he said.

She looked at him a breath longer, the look she had given him across this table for years, the one that said she had seen something and would not write it down; and went out. They heard her on the stair, unhurried, all the way down.



Nobody got up.

That was the first thing he noticed: that the table did not rise. On any other night, with the business done and the guest gone, Saban would have had his hat on and Bram his pipe lit and Armand would have been asking for his arm; tonight none of them moved. They sat where they were in the lamplight round the long table, the six of them, and looked at him.

Saban turned his hat on his knee.

"That's a great deal of money," he said, "to say no to as quickly as that."

"Yes."

"A man who says no to that much that fast has said it to himself before." Saban looked at the hat. "A good many times, I'd guess. Lying awake." He looked up. "We've all sat here eight years watching you say yes to things nobody's ever said yes to. I've never once seen you say no to that much. You'll want to tell us why it was easy."

Emeric looked round the table.

At Ferra, who knew. At Imani, who knew; who had made him tell it at a fouled well with a lantern on the step in the snow. At Ward, who had stood behind his chair for fifteen years and at his door for nine and knew everything about him but this. At Bram, who had made half of everything at this table and the other half's tools. At Armand, who had come south at seventy-eight to finish something. At Saban, who had asked.

He had thought about this for a year. He had thought about what he would say and how he would say it, in what order, and he had never once thought it would be on a Friday after supper because a banker had laid three sheets of paper on the table.

"Because I hate it," he said.

They waited.

"Not the money. The thing the money would buy. The thing it would keep." He put his hands on the table. "The rite. Armand proved it in June. It's a method, run too fast, that kills nine men in ten, and the Church stands at the head of the table and calls it the Light choosing. I hate that." He looked down the table. "The cellar. Placit and Vennor and the chairs, and the racks, and the ring. Lords who drain the people under them because the people under them are there to be drained. I hate that."

"Everybody hates the cellar," said Bram.

"Everybody hates the cellar. Nobody hates the rest of it, because the rest of it's just how things are." He did not look away from Bram. "A commoner isn't let near a glyph. You know that. You've never in your life been allowed to touch a stone that wasn't a lamp. Because a commoner with a stone is dangerous. Dangerous to who?" He did not wait. "The hearth tax. The same from a hovel as from a hall. Four houses at the Staple holding more than the whole of Wayford, the people and the roofs and the road, put together. And the word." He stopped. He had not meant to say the word, and he was going to.

"What word?" said Imani.

"Commoner." He looked at his hands on the table. "There's no such thing as a commoner. There are people. And some of them were born in a castle."

The table was very quiet.

"You were born in a castle," said Bram.

"I was born in a castle," said Emeric. "And I sat in it for fifteen years on my father's coin and did nothing at all."



Ward, at the door, moved. Not much; his weight, from one foot to the other. It was the only thing Ward did when something was said in a room that he had been waiting a long time to hear.

"It was an act," said Emeric. "You know that. You all know that by now. The drunk at the end of the table. My father built it for me when I was eight, to keep me alive, and I wore it for fourteen years, and it was easy." He looked at Ward. "That's the part I've not said. It was easy. Nothing was mine, so nothing was my fault. Nobody expected anything, so I could never fail it. People tried to kill me now and then and I didn't mind, because there was nothing they could take." He was quiet. "And I thought that was keeping out of it. I thought if I stayed out of the way the weight stayed off. Whatever it was. The crown, the realm, the people in it. If I never picked anything up, I'd never have to carry it."

"And?" said Armand.

"And it doesn't stay off." He looked at the old man. "It waits. It sits on the other side of the door for years and you think it's gone, and then one night it comes all at once. In a cellar. With a ring in the floor." He looked down the table. "I can't stand out of the way any more. I tried that. It doesn't work."

Armand had opened his eyes.

"I watched you do it," he said. "The standing aside." His hands lay still on his stick; it was two days after a flight. "I taught you from four to eight, and you could do anything I set you, too fast, and then make a small mistake at the very end, as your mother did. And from eight I watched you decide to do nothing at all, very thoroughly, for fourteen years, and I let you." He looked down the table at Emeric. "I thought it was grief. Then I thought it was drink. I never once thought it was a man waiting for something to pick up that was heavy enough to be worth it." He was quiet. "I'd not have said that last spring. I'd have said you were lazy."

"I was lazy."

"You were frightened," said Armand. "It looks the same from the outside. I was a very poor judge of the outside of things in those years." He shut his eyes again. "Go on."

Saban had stopped turning his hat.

"So what do you mean to do," he said, "instead?"

Emeric looked at Ferra. She was looking at her book, closed under her hand; she did not look up, and she did not need to. Imani was looking at him. Imani's face said, quite plainly, go on, the way it said it on the ward when somebody had started telling her where it hurt and stopped.

"The money first," he said. "Because Vantic just showed you the money, and because it's the easiest to see."

Saban sat up a little. Money was his country.

"Coin's weight," said Emeric. "It's metal in a purse. There's never enough of it, because there's never enough metal, and when the mint's short, trade stops, and a man with a cart of turnips can't sell them because nobody's got a Copper to give him. And when a lord shaves it, every man who touches that coin after pays for the shaving and doesn't know it." He looked at Saban. "You know all that."

"I've known that since I was nine," said Saban.

"Then paper. A sheet that says the House will pay the bearer so many Silver on demand. Vantic's House. It's lighter than coin, and it's as good as coin as long as everybody knows the House will pay." He put a finger on the table. "Then one paper. For the whole realm. Not the House's promise. The realm's. Backed by what the realm makes, the turnips and the iron and the cloth and the work. Not by what's in a vault."

"And what stops a king," said Saban, slowly, "printing it until it's worth nothing? Because that's what a king would do. Any king. Your father. Your brother. Paper costs nothing to print. A king who's short for a war prints a war's worth and every man in the realm wakes up poorer and doesn't know why."

"A bank the king doesn't own," said Emeric. "That prints it, and not him. A limit, written down, on how much, that the king can't change by saying so. And an audit. Of the bank and the limit and every sheet, that anybody in the realm can read."

"Anybody," said Saban.

"Anybody who can read. Which is why the schools come first."

Bram took his pipe out of his mouth.

"My grandmother was paid in a lord's paper once," he said. "For a summer's carting. A sheet with his seal on it, that said he would pay at the quarter day." He looked at the pipe. "She kept it in the tea caddy till she died. My mother kept it after her. It's in my kitchen drawer now. It's a very good seal."

"That's a promise from a man," said Emeric. "This is a promise from a bank with its books open, that a man can't touch."

"It's still paper."

"It's still paper. Coin's still metal. Neither of them's bread. They're both only worth what people believe they'll get for them." He looked at Bram. "Your grandmother believed the seal. She was wrong to, because nobody could look inside the lord's purse and see it was empty. Make the purse glass, and people can look."

Bram looked at him for a while with the pipe in his hand.

"A glass purse," he said, and put the pipe back in his mouth, and said nothing more, which from Bram was not agreement but was not the other thing either.

"And who does the audit?"

Ferra looked up from her book.

"So me," she said.

"So you."

Ferra looked at him across the table for a breath. Then she looked down at her book again and opened it a quarter of an inch, which at that table meant the business was not done, and did not write anything.

"And after paper?" said Saban.

Emeric thought about it. There was an after. There was a thing after paper that was only numbers, kept everywhere at once, that nobody could burn; and there was no way in the world he could say it at that table in words that would mean anything to anybody but two of the people sitting at it.

"After paper's a long way off," he said.

Saban looked at him with his head on one side, the look of a man who has been told a price is not for sale and has noted that there is a price.



"Corbin," said Ward.

He said it from the door. He had not spoken since Vantic left. He said it flatly, as he said a name on the garrison roll that had not answered.

"Corbin," said Emeric.

"He is next. If you do nothing, he is king."

"If I do nothing, he's king." He looked down the table. "He's the eldest. The court's his. Half the north's his since Aldane. The day my father dies, Corbin sits in the chair, and the first thing he does from the chair is look south." He put his hands together on the table. "So there are two roads. If he's crowned and he leaves us alone, the four baronies go on growing, the way they've grown since the first winter. Every year richer than the last, and more of them reading, and more of them able. Until one day they could stand on their own, if they had to, and nobody could make them kneel." He looked at Ward. "And if he doesn't leave us alone, if he comes south, we'll be able to stand when he gets here."

"He will not leave you alone," said Ward.

"No. He's never once in his life left a thing alone."

"Then it is one road."

"Then it's one road," said Emeric. "And at the end of it, if it goes all the way, the crown comes to me."

Nobody said anything.

"And if it does," he said, "I'll be the last."

He had not known how it would land. He had said it to Ferra under a tree and to Imani at a well, and both times it had been the two of them alone in the dark, and he had been able to watch their faces. He watched these.

Bram's went still. Not hard; still, the way his face went still over a casting with a flaw in it he had not expected, before he decided what to do about the flaw. Saban's did nothing at all, which on Saban was the most it ever did. Armand had shut his eyes. And Ward, at the door, had not moved; but his hands, which had been behind his back, were not behind his back any more. They were at his sides.

"The last what?" said Bram.

"King." He did not look away from Bram. "The offices go to whoever can do them. Proved, written down, read by anybody. Not to whoever's grandfather had them. The schools first, till most people can read. Then the offices, one at a time. Then, when there are enough people reading and enough offices run by people who earned them, the people choose who holds them. Every few years. And if he holds them badly they put him out." He looked at his hands. "And the crown answers to a written law, the same as a magistrate. And then it doesn't pass to anybody. It stops."

Bram looked at him for a long breath across the table.

"A king," he said, slowly, "who ends kings."

Ferra made a small sound.

Bram looked at his daughter.

"That's what I said," said Ferra. She did not look up from her book. "Near enough. A king who wants no kings. Under an apple tree, a year ago, with apples on his head." She turned her pencil in her fingers. "And then I told him they'd kill him." She did look up then, at her father, and then at Emeric. "I still think so."

"They'll try," said Emeric.

"You said that then as well," said Ferra.



It was Bram who asked first, because Bram always asked the thing that came after the clever thing.

"The levy," he said. "Three hundred men. Ward's. Who leads them, with no lord? A lord leads his levy. That's what a lord's for, when it comes to it. You take the lords away, who stands in front of the men?"

"Whoever's best at it. The same as Ward's yard. Ward didn't give Rylan the yard because of Rylan's grandfather." He looked at Ward. "Officers, made on what they can do, answering to the law and not to a man. Your yard already works like that. It works better than any lord's levy I've seen. You saw the stream ground."

Bram grunted, which was Bram conceding a point he had already known.

"The Church," said Armand, without opening his eyes.

"Believes what it likes. Anybody does. Doran preaches in his church on Sundays and nobody stops him and nobody makes anybody go." He looked at the old man. "Nobody lies anybody down on a stone in the Light's name. Your own result says why."

Armand opened his eyes.

"And the Church will not care for that."

"The Church will hate it," said Emeric. "More than it hated the climbs."

"Good," said Armand, and shut his eyes again.

"And who pays?" That was Ferra, who knew, and asked it anyway, for the table, in her rent-roll voice. "For the schools and the offices and the bank and the audit. When there's no lord taking a tenth."

"Everybody. On what they make and what they own. Not on what they are, and not on their hearths." He looked at her. "And the books open. Your books. To anybody."

"Mine," said Ferra, "are already open," and wrote something.

Saban had been turning his hat again, slowly. He stopped it.

"And when they choose a fool?" he said. "Your people. With their reading and their votes. Because they will. I've stood in a market forty years, and I'll tell you what people choose when you give them the choosing. They choose the man who bought the beer. Every time."

"Then they'll vote him out again."

"And in the meantime?"

"In the meantime he's a fool with a written limit and an audit and a magistrate who doesn't answer to him." Emeric looked at the merchant. "That's the whole trick. Nobody stops them choosing a fool. You make it so a fool can't do much, and can be got rid of, and the people who chose him can read what he did." He was quiet. "And you teach them to read first. Thirty years first. That's why the choosing's last."

"Water first," said Imani.

They all looked at her.

"Before the reading," said Imani. "Water first. Then food. Then a bed in a ward for anybody, not just for those who can pay a Copper. Then the school. Nobody learns sick." She looked down the table at Saban. "I told him that at a well in Aldane's town in the snow. He had it the wrong way round." She looked back at her day-book. "He's got it the right way round now."

"She's right," said Emeric.

"I'm always right about water," said Imani.



He had answered them, every one, and taken Imani's correction; and he found, as he answered, that the answers came out of him finished, in the right order, as if he had been reading them off a page. He had been. He had been reading them off pages for a year, at night, in his own head, from books that had been written in another world by people who had spent their lives on exactly these questions and had argued about them for three hundred years; and the answers came out of him the way a bridge came out of the books, whole, with the arguments already had.

He watched Bram take it as Bram took everything Emeric knew: grudgingly, completely, as a thing out of the books, which it was. He watched Saban do the same and file it with the price that was not for sale. He watched Armand open his eyes once more and look at him, a long look, the look of a teacher at a pupil who has given an answer too good for the pupil's age, and shut them again without asking where it came from, because Armand had stopped asking that years ago.

Across the table, Ferra and Imani looked at each other.

Once. It was nothing; anybody watching would have seen two women at a table glance at each other at the end of a long evening. It lasted the time it took to blink. And in it he saw that they both knew exactly whose books he had been reading off, and in what language, and in what life, and that neither of them would ever say so at this table or any other; and that they were, both of them, in the middle of the most serious evening of his life, very slightly amused.

Then Ferra looked back at her book, and Imani at her day-book, and it was gone.



Ward came away from the door.

He did not sit. He came and stood at the end of the table, behind Bram's chair, where the lamplight reached him; and he looked down the table at Emeric with no expression at all.

"And your father?" said Ward.

It was the last question. Everybody at the table knew it was the last question, and that it was the only one any of them had been waiting for.

Emeric looked at him.

He had known for years that Ward was his father's man. Not his. He had known it since the year he told Ferra and Imani about the other life, when the last of the things he had not let himself see about Ward had come together in the dark of the room: the fifteen years behind his chair, the pair of practice blades kept in a kit the whole time, the letters that went north in a code nobody else in Kaldo could read. Ward had been sent. Ward had been sent by a king to keep a boy breathing, and had kept him breathing, and had never once lied to him about anything; he had only not said.

"Tell him," said Emeric. "If you want to. I'd rather he heard it from you than from Corbin."

Ward did not move.

"He will ask if you mean it," said Ward.

"Tell him yes."

Ward looked at him a long breath longer. Then he nodded, once, the way he nodded when a name on the roll had answered.

Saban put his hat on the table.

"Well," he said. "That's a road." He looked at the hat. "I'd only say this. I've a trade I like and a chair by my own fire and a good many people in four baronies who owe me money. An ordinary life. I'd quite like to keep it." He looked up. "But if you fall, Grace, and Corbin comes south to tidy up after you, he'll not leave me my shop. He'll not leave Bram his forge or Imani her ward or Ferra her book. He'll not leave any of us anything. A man who takes the trouble to come south doesn't go home with half the job done." He picked the hat up again. "So we may as well earn it. Whatever it is. If we're to lose our ordinary lives either way, I'd sooner lose mine going somewhere."

Bram took his pipe out of his pocket and looked at it, and put it back.

"I've made you a lot of things," he said. "I'll make you a few more." That was all Bram said.

Armand did not open his eyes. "I came south to finish something," he said. "It appears to be larger than I thought. I'll need to live longer." He settled his hands on his stick. "Arrange it."

And Ward, at the end of the table behind Bram's chair, looked down at Emeric, and said, quite flatly, in the voice he used for the garrison roll and the weather and the death of kings:

"On the road south, at the fire, you asked me why I never told you I knew. I said I was your guard. I said I kept you breathing, and I was not given the rest of you to keep." He did not look away. "I will keep the rest."

Nobody at the table said anything after that. Nobody needed to.

They went in ones and twos, the way the table always went, as if it had been any Friday. Saban put his hat on and said goodnight to the room. Bram gave Armand his arm, without being asked, and the two old men went down the stair together very slowly, arguing about the kiln. Ward went out last but one, and did not look back.

Imani stopped by his chair on her way past it, with her day-book under her arm. She did not say anything. She laid two fingers to his wrist inside the cuff, out of sight of a table with nobody left at it, and looked at the clock on the wall over his shoulder, and counted.

"Ninety-four," she said. "You said all of that at ninety-four."

"Is that bad?"

"It's a man running up a stair." She took her fingers away. "You've been running up it since Vantic opened her case. Go to bed. Sleep. I'll know if you don't." She went to the door. "It went well," she said, from the door, without turning round, in the ward voice, the one she used to tell a family that the fever had broken; and went out.

Ferra did not get up. She sat at her end of the table with her book open in front of her and wrote for a while, and he watched her do it and did not ask; and when she had finished she blotted the page and closed the book on her pencil and looked at him down the length of the empty table.

"It's entered," she said.

"What is?"

"The date," said Ferra, "and the six names, and that you said it." She stood up and put the book under her arm. "Not what you said. That you said it, and to whom, and on what night." She went to the door. "If they kill you, I'd like there to be a page that says you told us first."



The light was on under Ward's door at one in the morning.

Emeric saw it on his way down the keep stair, not going anywhere in particular; he could not have slept, and had not tried. A line of yellow under the door at the end of the passage, where Ward's room was, the small square room Ward had chosen nine years ago because it was nearest the stair and had the thickest door.

He stopped in the passage.

Behind the door there was no sound. Then, after a while, a small one: a pen, on paper, slowly, the scratch of a man who wrote seldom and carefully and in a code. It stopped. It started again. It stopped for a long while.

He thought of the letter that had come up from the post house in October of the year before by a king's rider, that he had opened at the orchard gate with apples falling out of his coat: one word in the middle of a sheet in a large slow heavy hand, and an H under it. Satisfied. He had never known what with. He had carried the sheet in his coat for a month and then put it in the drawer of his table, and taken it out sometimes and looked at it, and never known.

He wondered what word would come back to this.

He did not knock. He did not go nearer. He stood in the dark of the passage and listened to his knight write to his father, a line at a time, whatever it was that Ward would write; and then he went on down the stair, over the fourth step, to nowhere in particular, and left it to them.
