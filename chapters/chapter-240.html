Chapter 240 - Stolen Grace

The cardinal's clerk brought the note to the chancery himself, on the second day of the winter sitting, and waited in the outer room for an answer, which was a courtesy, and meant that the answer had better be yes.

Keller read it at his table. It was four lines in the clerk's hand under the cardinal's seal. His Eminence would be glad if the Crown's man of law would call at the close that afternoon, before the matter His Eminence meant to lay before the council in the morning. His Eminence would not wish the Crown's law to be surprised by anything on its own table.

He read it twice, which he did with everything, and then laid it square to the edge of the roll beside it and looked at it.

He had had letters from the close for as long as he had kept the Crown's table. Petitions, answers, the lists of benefices at the quarter, a complaint once about a tithe barn. He could not remember one of them in the cardinal's own hand. That was not a thing he had ever thought about. It was the kind of thing he noticed and did not think about, and kept, the way a man keeps a key he has found in the road without knowing what door it is for.

"Tell His Eminence I'll come at the third bell," he said, and the clerk went away.

He knew what some of it would be. He had copied some of it himself.

Not with his own pen. His clerks had done the copying, in the autumn, in the rolls room, by lamplight, six weeks of it: every quarter's return from the private company of the barony of Kaldo since the settlement, each man with his name and his parish and his trade, as the settlement required, fair-copied onto chancery skins and bound in a cover with the chancery's mark. And the entrance list of a school on an island, which had come north in the ordinary way with the barony's other printed papers and been filed where printed papers were filed, and had been found and copied in an afternoon. The cardinal's clerk had asked for them in writing. The request was in order. The chancery complied with requests that were in order, because that was what a chancery was for, and Keller had signed the covering note himself and sent it across the square to the close. Then he had written a short letter to the lord of Kaldo, in his own hand, on no instruction of anyone's, and sent that south by the ordinary bag.

He had not asked what the rolls were wanted for. He had not asked the lord of Kaldo anything either. He had spent his whole working life not asking questions whose answers he would then have to write down.

There was snow on the close. It lay on the leads of the cathedral's north side and along the backs of the gargoyles, and hung from their open mouths in short grey teeth of ice, and the porter at the gate had swept a path to the cardinal's door that was already filling again.

A young priest took his cloak and took him up the stone stair and along the passage with the red runner, into the room with the books. There was a brazier. There was a window full of snow-light, white and flat and even, the best light in the capital to read by. Keller had been in the room twice before, both times about land.

Augustine rose from behind the desk. He wore black in his own house, like any priest, and a plain cross on a cord, and he was smaller standing than he was on the east bench, and older, with a great deal of white hair and very clean hands.

"Thank you for coming," he said. "Sit, please. You'll take something warm. It's a cold walk across the square."

Keller sat and took something warm and did not drink it. The desk was between them. He looked at it, because it was what he had been asked there to look at.

The paper lay in rows. Not heaped, not bundled. Laid out, sheet by sheet and book by book, in three rows across the whole broad top of the desk, each one squared to the one beside it and the rows squared to the desk's front edge, so that the whole of it made a single ruled page of pages. Keller's left hand came up off his knee toward the nearest of them, the way it always did, and stopped, because there was nothing there to put straight. Every edge on the desk was already true. He put the hand back on his knee.

"I thought you'd rather see it here than be handed it in the hall," Augustine said. "One reads better sitting down." He did not touch any of it. "Take as long as you like. I'll tell you what each thing is, if you want, but I think you'll know most of them."

He knew most of them.

The rolls were at the left end of the first row, in the chancery cover, with the chancery's mark: his own clerks' hands, his own covering note tucked inside the board. Beside them, a Visitor's report of eleven pages in a careful upright hand, signed and sealed, Benedek Orme, Visitor of the southern parishes. Keller had never met the man. He turned the report's pages with one finger, not lifting them from the desk, and read the figures down the margin where the Visitor had set them out. Sixty-eight in the first year. Forty-nine. Then the parish growing, and the burials growing more slowly behind it. Then, three winters before the Visitor came, a step down, and another, and another. One soul in ninety-five. The Visitor had set the census beside the burials and written the sum under them, and written under the sum that he had done it twice.

It was good work. Keller had read reports all his working life, and he knew good work the way a mason knows a true course. The man had not written a word further than he could stand behind. Where he did not know a thing he had written that he did not know it. It was the report of an honest man, and it was the most dangerous thing on the desk, and Keller did not yet know why.

A letter, three times folded, in an upright, careful hand that he did not know, a hand that did not shake. That the rite be slowed, to the same total over three nights instead of one. Signed Armand, sometime master of the Circle. It lay squared to the desk's edge at the head of the second row, and it had been read many times, and not by the man who wrote it.

A printed leaf of Kaldo paper, folded very small once and pressed flat, with a brown stain along one edge gone stiff. Keep us, lord, in the hour of the fight. Keller looked at it for a while before he saw it. The small letter.

A single page, fair-copied from the tower's register and certified at the foot by the tower's assessor, a Master Hebden. A name, Rafe; a date; and in the column for the stone, the tenth, twice. In the column for remarks, No marks. No rite. Aged fifteen. In the column for the place of raising, one word, very neatly written, in the hand of a man who had expected to be asked to find it again.

Kaldo.

Keller did not know what the tenth stone was. He knew what a knight was, and he knew what a boy of fifteen was, and he knew what no rite meant, and he looked at the page long enough to be sure he had understood it, and then he went on.

Two pages from the tower's postings register, fair-copied like the first, in a young clerk's careful hand.

He knew the numbers before he read them. He had heard them said aloud at the door of his own inner office, in the week of the duke's matter, by a master in grey with the black bar on his sleeve who had come to complain and been listened to politely. Posts vacated: walls and cisterns and drains and the crown works on the north road, nineteen since the spring before and fourteen the year before that, and every man of them who had said where he went had said south. Thirty-three. Keller had told the master that the Crown knew now, and seen it entered in the day book, and thought no more about it, because no post had failed and the capital had not lost a cistern.

It had been entered, though. It was in his own book, in his own clerk's hand, three paragraphs with the numbers in them, on a shelf in the rolls room, for as long as the chancery stood. If anyone in the hall tomorrow wanted to know whether the Crown had been told, the Crown would have to say that it had.

A petition in a priest's hand, three drafts' worth of care in it, asking the licence of a chapel of ease in the ancient form for the ease of those parishioners who could not walk to their church; and the close's grant of it, four lines, under the cathedral's seal. The petition gave the number of souls the chapel was for. It gave the distance by the road. It gave, without being asked, the name and the year of a hill chapel the priest had once served as a curate, for a precedent. A man who explains to a chancery has already admitted there is something to explain, Keller thought, and this one had not explained anything. He had only counted his people and asked for a roof.

And at the right-hand end of the last row, the school list. The printed one, Kaldo paper again, the same clean type as the prayer. Names in order of merit, with a parish or a town beside each. The eighth line.

Aubrey, second son of the lord of Esterby, north riding.

Keller sat back.

"That's all of it," Augustine said. "There's nothing else. I've kept nothing back from you, and I'll add nothing tomorrow." He folded his hands on the desk's front edge, below the rows, the only part of it that was bare. "I wanted you to see that there is nothing in it that isn't true."

"I can see that."

"Every line of it was written by a man doing his office well. A clerk, a priest, a Visitor, an assessor, a master of the Circle. The barony's own printer." He said it the way he said everything, evenly, so that none of the words stood higher than the others. "I have not had to ask anyone to say a thing that wasn't so. I don't think that has ever been true of a matter I have laid before the council. I find I don't much like it."

Keller looked at him.

"What act is charged?" he said.

"None."

"Then there's nothing here for the council to try."

"No. There isn't." Augustine said it at once and pleasantly, as a man agrees about the weather. "The council isn't going to be asked to try anything. It hasn't the office, and it knows it hasn't. You said as much yourself, in the hall, at the duke's matter, of the demons. The fourth article is for the lords spiritual." He inclined his head very slightly, as though returning something that had been lent. "You were right."

Keller said nothing.

He had spent his life convicting people on evidence. He had said once, in his own room, with its window at the wrong height, to a young lord from the south who had brought him a satchel of paper and no name, that he had never convicted anybody on a pattern, and that a pattern was a thing a clever man made out of the world by choosing what to count. He looked along the three rows on the desk and understood that he could say it again tomorrow in the hall, word for word, and that it would be true, and that it would be beside the point. Nobody on this desk was going to be convicted of anything. Doctrine was not proved. It did not have to be. It was kept, and the men who kept it said what it was, and the only thing they would want from the council was a body in a room.

"I'm obliged to you for the courtesy, Eminence," he said.

"Not at all." Augustine rose, and Keller rose with him. "You'll have it on your table in the morning, just as it is. My clerk will carry it across." He looked down at the rows, once, with something in his face that Keller could only have called fondness, as a man looks at a garden he has been told is doing well. "They are such careful people in Kaldo," he said. "They write everything down."

He did not sleep much. He did not expect to. He sat up in his room over the chancery with a lamp and a blank sheet and did not write on it, and considered, one after another, every procedural door he knew of that might be shut before the morning, and found each of them already shut, or never built, or opening outward onto the same floor.

He considered writing to Kaldo. There was nothing yet to write. A cardinal had shown the Crown's law some paper, all of it true, all of it the Crown's own or the Church's own or the barony's own, and meant to show it again in the morning. That was not news. It was the weather.

At some point in the small hours he found he had squared the blank sheet to the table's edge perhaps forty times, and put it away in the drawer, and went to bed.

The hall was set for a sitting of the council, and that meant grey.

He had noticed the grey two summers ago, at the elevation, as he noticed most things: the princess Maeve's household, young men and older women in the same dark cloth with no badge on it, who moved about the hall in the hour before a sitting with lists in their hands and put every man where he was to be put, so quietly that nobody put there ever thought of it as having been done to him. He had watched them do it at every sitting since. It was, by a long way, the best-run office in the building, and it had no name and no roll and no seal, and Keller, who did not admire many things, admired that.

One of the young men showed him to the Crown's table, where he always sat. It was not necessary. It was done anyway. Maeve herself was at the foot of the gallery stair with a single sheet in her hand, not looking at it, and she did not look at him, and he did not look at her for longer than it took to see that she was there and that her face was the face she wore for a sitting, which told him nothing, which was its purpose.

The winter light came in low through the east windows and lay across the benches in long pale bars. The braziers along the pillars did nothing for the floor. Somewhere up in the black oak of the roof there was a leak where the snow had got in over the night, and a drop fell every so often onto the stone in front of the west bench and made a small dark coin there that nobody looked at.

The benches filled. The lords spiritual on the east bench, nearest the dais, grey and black and one red. The lords temporal under the windows, their stewards behind them. On the first bench, west side, among the old blood, the Duke of Aldane, heavier than at the elevation and sitting the bench as though it were a horse he did not trust. On the second bench, a red face above a soft coat, and a cup already, at the ninth bell: Grier. Keller knew Grier the way he knew most men on those benches, from the other end, as a name on suits. Three of the men Grier owed had brought actions before the Crown in the last five years, and two of them had won, and neither had been paid.

The chancellor was helped to his stool below the dais. The steward took his place with his staff.

On the dais, the king. The beard was white now; it had gone white between one summer and the next and stayed. He sat in the chair with his hands on its arms, and his face was the face Keller had seen on him at every sitting since the file, which was no face at all. At his right hand, a pace behind the chair, stood the prince Corbin, easy, fair, in a dark coat with nothing on it to show what he was. He nodded to Keller across the floor, friendly, as a man nods to a colleague on a dull morning.

Keller nodded back.

The cardinal's clerk came in at the east door with a plain wooden box, walked the length of the floor with it, and set it on the Crown's table at Keller's elbow. Then he opened it, and took the papers out, and laid them on the table in three rows, in the same order they had lain on the desk at the close, squared to the table's edge and to one another. He did it well. He had done it before. When he had finished he bowed to the Crown's table, and to the dais, and went away to the east bench and stood behind his master.

So Keller sat through the sitting behind the case against the lord of Kaldo, with his hands on either side of it, like a man keeping a stall.

The steward called the matter, which was a matter of the lords spiritual laid before the council for its assistance, and the cardinal rose.

He did not come down onto the floor. The floor was for parties and advocates. He stood at the head of the east bench, in the red, in a bar of the low light, with his hands folded in front of him, and spoke from where he was. He did not raise his voice. It was an unhurried voice, and level, and it put its weight on no particular word; it went to the back of the hall the way water goes to the lowest part of a floor, without being pushed. Keller had heard it a hundred times at a hundred sittings and had never once heard it raised.

"My lords," Augustine said. "I'll be short, because the matter is short, and the paper is long, and the paper is on the Crown's table for any of you to read."

He began with what the Church held. He did not argue it. He stated it, as a man states the date. That a man's capacity was given, not made. That it was given once, in the chamber, through the rite, by grace, over fourteen hours, to men who were kept in it by eight priests who laid their hands on them and prayed. That it was violent, because it was holy, and that it marked the men it was given to, to the elbow or to the waist, and that the Church did not apologise for either the violence or the mark, since a holy thing is not made gentle for anybody's convenience.

"That is what we hold," he said. "Your fathers held it. It is why you send us your sons."

Nobody on the benches moved. Most of them had sent the cardinal a son.

"What is given," Augustine said, "is had by gift. What is had and was not given was taken." He let that stand a moment, and then went on, in the same even voice, to the paper.

He did not read it out. He named it. The Visitor's report, and the burials in it, year by year, and the second step down, the one the Visitor could not account for, and the sum: one soul in ninety-five, in a parish of four thousand and a half. The Visitor's hand on children in the market street, and a baker, and a carter, and what the Visitor had felt there, which the Visitor had felt before only in knights. The boy in the tower's register with the tenth stone lit in his hand, twice, and his arms clean to the shoulder, and never a night in the chamber in his life. The thirty-three mages of the Circle who had walked away from their posts and gone south without a word, and a master of the Circle's own letter to the close proposing that the rite be changed. The prayer printed in a barony for its own soldiers, with its lord in a small letter.

"I don't make anything of the letter," Augustine said. "The Visitor went into it. It was a boy's error, after the proof. I believe that." He paused. "I only say that a barony that prints its own prayers for its own men has already decided who keeps them."

Keller listened with his hands flat on the table on either side of the rows.

He was listening, as he always listened, for the weak joint: the place where an advocate says more than his paper will bear and the paper can be turned against him. He did not find it. Augustine did not once say more than his paper said. And somewhere in the second quarter of an hour Keller noticed a thing, and noticed that he had been noticing it for some time without putting it into words.

The cardinal never said I saw.

He said I often enough. I hold. I don't know. I believe that. He said it of what the Church held and of what he did not know, and never once of anything he had looked at. The Visitor found. The register shows. The parish book gives. Every figure had another man's name on it, and every name was the name of a man who had been there. The cardinal had built the whole of it, stone by stone, out of what other men had seen, and had laid not one stone of his own in it anywhere. He did not describe the town, not a street of it, not a roof. He argued from the table and nowhere else.

Keller turned it over and filed it. A churchman's caution. The Church had been standing in front of councils for four hundred years and had learned, as the law had learned, that the only testimony nobody can cross-examine is somebody else's. It was what Keller would have done himself.

"I do not say how," Augustine said. "I don't know how. The Visitor doesn't know, and he looked for a fortnight, honestly, in all the places a sensible man would look. Nobody knows how, I think, except the people who are doing it. I don't need to know how. A steward who finds the plate gone from the press doesn't need to know which window it went out of to know that it didn't walk." He turned his head a little, toward the benches under the windows, the lords temporal, as though the next thing were for them. "A parish in which the old do not die when the old die, and the children do not die when children die, is living on something that is not its own. It is living on what should have been given, and was not. It had been doing so for three years when the Visitor came. Nobody has told us that it has stopped."

He unfolded his hands.

"And now it is giving it away," he said.

He did not look at the table. He did not need to. He said the eighth line of the school list from memory, evenly, the way a clerk reads a name off a roll.

"Aubrey, second son of the lord of Esterby, north riding."

The sound went along the benches.

Keller had heard that sound twice before in this hall. Once when Serle read a knight's age in the duke's file, aged nineteen, and once, a different sound, when he himself had said that every knight in the article had died charging. This was a third sound. It was lower than either, and it did not quite stop. It went on under the cardinal's next sentence the way a draught goes on under a door, and Keller did not need to turn his head to know what it was. Esterby was nobody. Esterby was three villages and a hall and a name on the tax rolls. But every man on those benches had a second son, or a third, or a daughter, or a grandson who would want the rite in five years and cost a year's rents to put through it. And a barony in the south had printed, on good paper, for anyone to read, that it would take a lord's second son into its own parish for a fee, and keep him, and give him back.

Nobody on the benches had asked, yet, what he would be given back with. Keller watched them not ask it. It was the loudest thing in the hall.

He stood once.

He had known in the cardinal's room that it would do nothing, and he stood anyway, because the Crown's table did not let a matter go by it in silence, and because if it went into the roll with no word from the Crown's law against it then the roll would say the Crown's law had had no word to say.

"The Crown's table asks," he said, "what act of the lord of Kaldo is charged."

"None," said Augustine.

"Then the Crown's table notes that none is charged, and that the council tries acts."

"The council does," Augustine agreed. "And the Church does not ask it to try anything. The Church asks it to note what the lords spiritual find, which is their office and nobody else's, and to give the Church the assistance the old form allows." He inclined his head to the Crown's table, courteous, entire. "As the Crown's table itself was good enough to remind this hall, when it sent a matter of demons to this bench."

A little laughter along the benches, warm, at a lawyer answered in his own coin. Keller let it go by and sat down, and squared the nearest sheet on the table, which was the Visitor's report, and which was already square.

The chancellor read the old form. He read it in his soft voice without inflection, as he read everything, and it was very old and very short. That where the lords spiritual, sitting as the lords spiritual, found a practice to be against the faith, and the practice was held under a peer of the realm or in his lands, the peer should come in his own person before the lords spiritual and answer for it, and the council should assist; and that the assistance of the council was the Crown's writ.

The lords spiritual gave their voices. They gave them from the east bench, in order of rank, as they had given them at the elevation. Augustine gave his first, one word, pleasantly. His bench followed him. At the far end sat the same old abbot who had said no to the elevation and looked at nobody; he said aye this time, slowly, and looked at nobody.

The steward read it to the hall.

That the practice at Kaldo is heresy, and that the lord of Kaldo must come in his own person and answer for it before the lords spiritual.

The clerk wrote it. Keller watched the pen go across the roll and knew, the way he knew the weight of a sentence, that it was the shortest line that had been written in that roll in his lifetime, and the heaviest.

Then the hall turned and looked at the dais.

It did not do it all at once. It did it the way a crowd does a thing it has not agreed to do, a head here and a head there, the stewards first, and then their lords, until the whole of the hall was facing the chair. Keller did not turn. He had been facing the chair all morning.

Everybody in the hall knew. Everybody in the capital knew. The king had had his third son into the day room in the summer of the duke's file and roared at him behind a door for an hour, and every loud word of it had been in the kitchens by supper and the chancery lanes by morning, and the last of them had been the loudest: you will not come up this road again unless I send for you. The lord of Kaldo did not come north except at the king's word. He had not come north without it since. It was the most famous thing the king had said in ten years. And a writ to bring a peer in his own person was the Crown's writ, and the Crown was the man in the chair.

So it had to be his. Nobody else could send for the boy. He had seen to that himself, in front of the whole house.

The king sat with his hands on the arms of the chair. His face did not change. It had not changed all morning. But Keller had sat below that chair for a great many sittings, and he saw the forefinger of the right hand lift very slightly off the wood, the way it lifted before the king said anything at all, and hang there.

Before it came down, the prince Corbin stepped forward to the edge of the dais.

He did it easily, as he did everything, as though the thought had just come to him, and the hall, which had been looking at his father, looked at him instead, with relief. He had a way of giving a room somewhere else to look.

"My lord," he said, to his father, and then, turning a little, to the hall. "If I may. It needn't be a hard thing, this. I'd rather it wasn't." He spread his hands. "The Crown's rider goes south at the quarter in any case, to count my brother's company, as the settlement says. The settlement gives him entry to the works at Kaldo at any hour, where the powder's made. My brother put his name to that himself. He keeps his word; I'll say that for him before anyone. Let me ride with the rider. Let me go in at his elbow, and find my brother, and tell him myself, and bring him up the road beside me." He smiled, a little ruefully, at the benches. "Better his brother than a writ nailed to his gate. It's a family matter as much as it's anything. I'd like it done as family, so it's done gently."

It was well received. Keller heard it received. A murmur along the benches that was nearly approval, the sound of men who have been handed a hard thing and shown that it can be carried by somebody else. The heir to the crown, going himself in winter down a long road to fetch his exiled brother home to answer, so that it should not be done by strangers. It was generous. It was public. It had been said on the floor of the council in front of every lord in the kingdom who could get there through the snow.

And Keller, who had read the settlement more times than any man alive, sat behind the Crown's table with his hands on either side of the cardinal's paper and watched the door close.

If the king refused his heir, he refused him here, on this floor, with the lords spiritual's finding still wet in the roll. He would be seen to refuse the gentlest road that could be offered for bringing a peer before the Church, and there was only one reason the benches would find for that. The king, in open council, shielding a son the lords spiritual had just found in heresy. Every man under the windows would carry it home. Every priest on the east bench would carry it further.

And if he did not refuse, then the prince Corbin would ride south with the Crown's rider, and go through the gate at Kaldo under a clause of the settlement, lawfully, at any hour, as far as the works and wherever the works led. He would go in by a door the lord of Kaldo had opened himself, in his own name, in good faith, the way he seemed to do everything that went on paper. The lord of Kaldo could not bar it without breaking the settlement, and a man who breaks the settlement in front of the Crown's rider is not a man answering a charge any more. He is a man refusing one.

Either way the boy came north. Either way it was done lawfully. Either way it was the Crown that did it, and the Crown's own paper that let it be done, and not a line of it anywhere in any hand that had not been doing its honest office.

Keller looked at the rows on the table in front of him. The rolls in his own clerks' hands. The tower's register. The printed list. And on a shelf across the square, his own day book, with the numbers in it.

They write everything down.

On the dais, the king's forefinger came down onto the arm of the chair. Then, slowly, the king put both his hands flat on the arms, the way an old man does before he stands, and the hall went quiet for him, all of it, the benches and the gallery and the grey young men along the walls; and in the quiet a drop came down out of the roof where the snow had got in, and struck the floor in front of the west bench, and nobody looked at it.
