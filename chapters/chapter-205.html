Chapter 205 - What Makes Air Thick

"There are three ways it can be," Emeric said, "and I don't like any of them."

It was the long room, after supper, the week after the inventory, with the boards cleared and the fire built up against the first real cold of the autumn. The six were at the table because he had asked for all six, and Ward was in his chair by the door because Ward was always in his chair by the door. Ferra had her book open. Armand had his hands on his stick. Bram had come straight from the dock with lime to the elbows and had washed his hands and not his arms. Imani sat at the end with a cup of something hot. Saban, who had come in off the Wayford train an hour before, had not yet taken his coat off.

They all knew what was in the box now. He had told them the evening the dealer's stone went into the wall board, in the same flat order Ward told a road: the drawings, the eight, the teaching stones, the weights. He had watched each of them take it. Saban had laughed, once, out loud, at the eight, and then apologised, and then laughed again. Imani had asked to see the page with the pods and held the book for a long time. Bram had asked what the tree was made of, which nobody could tell him.

"It woke in the band," Emeric said now. "A little. In a summer. It didn't wake in the box in this room since the spring, or in a comb case in a drawer in my father's treasury for twenty years. It wakes in thick air. The book says so, near enough, and the scale says so, and Armand's controls say so." He laid his hand flat on the table. "The band's thick. The ground isn't. So either somewhere on the ground is as thick as the band, and nobody's found it. Or somebody grows them up there, in the band, somewhere, and always has, and that's where the dealers' boxes come from. Or the ground used to be thick, and isn't any more, and every seed we've got was grown before it thinned, and has been lying about in boxes ever since, waiting for air it can't get."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"The second one," said Saban, "means somebody flies."

"Yes."

"Somebody who isn't us." Saban took his coat off at last and hung it on the back of his chair. "I'll say this now so it's said. I've bought and sold in every port from here to the western water. If there's a people who fly, they've never bought anything, and they've never sold anything, and I'd know."

"Unless what they sell is grey stones in boxes of eight," said Ferra.

Saban opened his mouth, and shut it again, and looked at her with something close to respect.

"Where does it come from," Imani said. "The thickness. That's the question under yours. Not where is it thick. Why is it ever thick anywhere."

It was the question he had been walking round for a week, and he was glad somebody else had said it, because he had been afraid it was only his.

"I don't know," he said. "But I've felt it be thicker, twice, and not known what I was feeling." He told them about the byre. Hollis's byre on the home farm in the spring, the forty head of cattle standing in their straw steaming in the cold, and the pump house next door with its bound stone and its rack of charged spares; and how the byre, with no stone in it at all, had been fuller in the medium than the room with the stones. Not round any one beast. Everywhere. As if forty cattle standing breathing in a building were together a sort of dim stone, spread through the whole of the air.

"And the hearing," he said. "In the capital. Keller's hall, with a thousand on the benches. I felt the weight of them on the sense like a hand. I thought it was the number of them. I thought a thousand people were just a lot of people. I didn't ask what it was they were doing, being a thousand people, that made the air lie thicker round them."

"Living," said Imani.

He looked at her. She did not look at him; she was looking at her cup.

"I've sat with the dying for twenty years," she said. "In the ward, in the plague, in the lanes before there was a ward. You learn a room. There's a thing that goes out of a room when somebody dies in it. I always thought it was only the quiet." She drank. "I've never had a word for it, and I didn't want one. Don't give me one now."

He did not.

"Life gives it off," he said instead, to the table. "That's the only way the byre and the hall make sense together. Anything that lives puts a little of the medium into the air round it, the way a fire puts heat into a room. More life, thicker air. A crowd reads thicker than a street. A byre reads thicker than a store. And the capital," he said, and stopped, because he had got there in the night and had not liked it then either.

"The capital has three hundred thousand people in it," said Armand. "More."

"And nobody there gets old," Emeric said.

Armand turned his head and looked at him.

"They do," Emeric said. "Of course they do. But slowly. Keller has been the same age in my eyes since I stood in front of him the first time, and he was grey then. My father looked older in the spring, at court, and it was the first time I have ever seen him look it. Everybody in the capital looks well. Everybody says it's the living. The best food in the kingdom, the best water, the best physicians, soft beds, no plague. I said so myself, the night I was sent south. I said the capital was the best place to live, and that was why its people lived so long." He looked at the fire. "Maybe it's the living. Maybe it's also three hundred thousand people breathing the same air, and all of them putting a little into it, and all of them taking a little back, the whole of their lives."

It was very quiet at the table. Ward, by the door, had not moved at all.

"Then the capital's thick," said Bram slowly. "Thicker than here."

"Thicker than here. Not thick enough." Emeric put his hand in his coat, where his own stone was, and did not take it out. "I carried one of these in the capital for all the years I lived there, and it never woke. It woke in a summer in the band. Whatever the capital's got, three hundred thousand people's worth, the band has far more. And the band has nobody in it at all."

Armand went to fetch his books.

He did not say he was going to. He got up in the middle of Ferra asking something, with his stick, and went out, and they heard him on the stair, slowly, and Ward got up and went after him without a word and came back ten minutes later carrying four books and a portfolio, with Armand behind him carrying nothing and looking as if he had been done a discourtesy.

They were old books, three of them bound in the tower's grey and one in a red leather Emeric did not know, and the portfolio was a thing of stiff boards tied with tape, and Armand opened them all on the table among the cups and did not explain any of them for some time. He turned pages. He found things and lost them and found them again.

"I have been in every kingdom there is," he said at last, turning. "And the Empire. In my years at the tower I went wherever there was a stone nobody understood, because I was young and that was what I thought a life was for. And in every one of them, in some cellar, in some lord's hall, in some priest's back room, there is a bone." He found the page he wanted and turned the book round.

It was a drawing of a skull.

It was drawn from the side, in brown ink, with a man drawn beside it for scale, a small neat man in a hat with his hand on it, and the skull came up past the man's shoulder. It was long and low, with a great many teeth, and holes in it where the eyes had been that a child could have climbed into, and the back of it went up in a kind of crest, or a frill, or a broken fan of bone; the drawing was not clear, because what it had been drawn from was not whole.

"The Duke of somewhere in Velmoor has that," Armand said. "In his undercroft. His grandfather dug it out of a riverbank. It's called the Devil's Head, in the family, as a joke. Nobody has ever found more of it." He turned the page. "This one is a thighbone. In a church in the south of the Empire. It stands on its end in the porch, and it is taller than the door, and the priest says it's a saint's." The next. "Backbones. Twenty of them, in a row, in a quarry, where they came out of the stone. The quarrymen used them as seats." The next. "A tooth. I held that one. It was the length of my forearm and it had a saw's edge on it."

He went through them all, one after another, in the brown ink of men who had been dead a long time, and the table watched. A skull. A thighbone. A row of vertebrae like a wall of millstones. A claw. A thing that might have been a shoulder blade and might have been a shield. A jaw. Never a whole of anything. Never two pieces of the same beast. Every one of them in a cellar, or a porch, or a quarry, or a riverbank, a fragment, drawn by somebody who had not known what it was and had drawn a man beside it so that whoever came after would believe him.

"Nobody knows what they looked like," Armand said. "Nobody knows what killed them. Nobody knows if they were ten kinds of beast or one. The tower has a shelf of papers that say they were the bones of the drowned before the flood, and a shelf that says they were stone that grew into the shape of bones, and I have read both shelves and I don't believe either." He shut the book. "They were large. That's all anybody can say. They were very large, and they were alive, and they are gone."

Emeric looked at the drawing of the tooth with its saw's edge, and at the small neat man in the hat standing beside a thighbone taller than a door, and thought, before he could stop himself, of a museum hall in a city in another life, and a skeleton the size of a house on iron stands with children running under it; and then, because his mind did that, of the other thing, the thing he had read in the last year of that life and laughed at: that they had got the old blood out of the old bones at last and made some of the great beasts live again, a few, in pens, and that when they came out of the egg they had turned out to be less like lizards than like enormous chickens, bright as parrots, stamping about their pens and eating everything and frightening nobody who had ever kept hens.

He kept that to himself. He kept it with some difficulty. Saban was looking at him in a way that suggested some of it had got as far as his face.

"There's one more," Armand said. He had untied the portfolio. "It isn't a bone. I don't know why I kept it. I think because nobody else wanted it."

It was a sheet of heavy paper, much folded, with a drawing on it in pencil gone silver: the face of a cliff, or the side of a cutting, drawn from below, in layers of rock one above another like the leaves of a book. And in one of the layers, standing straight up out of it side by side like the pipes of an organ, a great many columns of stone. Each one had flat sides. The man who drew them had drawn the tops of three of them, where they had broken off at the layer above, and every top had six sides.

"A cutting in the north of Solreth," Armand said. "The road went through a hill and found those. They're stone. Only stone. The rock does it, in some places; it cracks as it cools, into those shapes. I've seen it on a beach in Velmoor." He shrugged. "I took the drawing because it was pretty, I think. I was young."

Emeric looked at the six-sided tops of the columns for a moment, and at the layer of rock they stood in, and something at the back of his head turned over once and lay down again. He did not know what it was. He let it lie. Beside him, Ferra looked at the drawing for exactly as long as he had, and then at him, and then at her book, and did not write anything.

"Big," Bram said. "Alive. More life. More in the air." He said it as a sum, one term at a time, the way he said anything he was working out in front of people. "So the ground was thick when they were on it. And they went, and it thinned. And the seeds were from before."

"That's the third way," Emeric said. "Yes."

"You don't like it."

"I don't know if it adds up."

Ferra turned to a clean page.

"Then add it," she said. "How much does a big thing give off. More than a small thing?"

"More in all. Less for its size." He had thought about this in the night too. "A big beast doesn't burn as hard as a small one, pound for pound. A mouse eats half its own weight in a day. A cow eats a fraction of hers. The bigger a thing is, the slower its fire burns, for every pound of it. If the medium's given off the way heat is, by a thing living, it'll go the same way." He thought about how to say the rest without the numbers he did not have a way to have. "It goes up by less than the weight goes up. Double the weight, you get something like two-thirds more. Not double."

Ferra looked at him the way she looked at him when he said a thing in a way that meant he knew it from somewhere and she would never be told where. Then she wrote it down with a ring round it, and began to work.

"A man," she said, "is a man. Call a man one. Hollis's cattle weigh eight or ten men each, and they were forty of them, and the byre read thicker than a store of stones but it did not read like the hearing." She wrote. "Your beast. Armand's thighbone taller than a door. How heavy?"

"Fifty tons," said Bram at once. He had been looking at the drawing of the vertebrae like millstones with a founder's eye. "The one with that bone. Near enough. As much as a laden coaster."

Ferra worked. It took her a while, because she did the powers by hand, the long way, twice, the way she did anything she was going to be asked to stand behind. Nobody talked. Imani refilled her cup. The fire settled.

"A beast of fifty tons," Ferra said, "gives off what a hundred and forty men would. About." She put the pen down. "If your rule's right."

"A hundred and forty men," said Saban. "That's a village."

"It's a village, walking about." Ferra did not look up. "The capital is three hundred thousand. Say four. To make the air as thick as the capital's, all over a place the size of the capital, you'd want near three thousand of those beasts standing in it. Every one of them the size of a hull. Every day." She looked at the drawings of bones on the table. "And the capital isn't thick enough. You said so. The band's thicker than the capital by a great deal. So to wake a seed on the ground, you'd want more than three thousand of those, in one place, all the time, for years."

Nobody said anything.

"Unless there were far more of them than anybody's ever found," Ferra said. "Or they were bigger than the bones. Or they gave off more than the rule says. Or the rule's wrong." She drew a line under the sum. "It doesn't add up. Not on the bones alone. There's something we haven't got."

"Or a lot of something," said Imani. "A lot of anything that lives. Forests. Seas of fish. The whole ground covered."

"Still not the band."

"No," Imani agreed. "Still not the band."

Ferra wrote the gap at the foot of the sum, and drew a ring round it that took up most of the width of the page, and sat back.

Emeric looked at the ring for a long moment. He found he was glad of it. A sum that came out would have been a thing to distrust, in a room full of bones nobody could put together and a book nobody could read. A sum that failed honestly at least told you where the hole was.

It was Bram who found the other road, at the end, when they were all tired.

He had been sitting with his big hands flat on the table looking at nothing for a while, the way he did when something at the works had gone wrong and he was walking back through it. Then he said: "Grayrock."

They looked at him.

"The bad air," Bram said. "In the old workings. The cursed shafts." He had heard Bryda's men tell it at the pithead for a week, the year of the dives, and had not forgotten a word. "There were two airs down there. The one that kills you on the floor, heavy, sitting in the low places like water. And the one that burns, up at the roof, light, sitting in the high places. Firedamp. It goes up and it sits under the rock at the top of a working and waits, and nobody knows it's there till a man lifts a lamp." He looked at Emeric. "You said the stuff floats. The medium. That's why the band's up where it is. It goes up."

"It goes up," Emeric agreed. "That's what I think."

"Then it'd sit under rock," said Bram. "Same as firedamp. If there was a roof over it. It'd go up and hit the roof and sit there and wait. And if there was a lot of life under that roof, once, a long time, it'd fill up."

Emeric thought about it honestly, because Bram had never once said a thing to him that was not worth thinking about honestly.

"It'd find a crack," he said at last. "Over time. Firedamp sits under a roof for a season, or a year. Not for longer than there've been kings. Rock cracks. Water goes through it. Roots. Anything that could leak out would have leaked out, a long time ago, a bit at a time, and gone up to the band with the rest." He shook his head. "I don't think so. Not for as long as it would need to be."

Bram nodded slowly. He did not look convinced, and he did not look as if he meant to argue. He looked like a man putting something on a shelf in his workshop that he did not have a use for yet and was not going to throw out.

Ferra wrote it down. Bram: pooled under rock, like firedamp. E.: it would find a crack. And a ring round both, and between them, very small, a question mark that Emeric saw and did not remark on.

They broke up late. Armand took his books; Ward carried them. Saban went to bed with his coat over his arm. Imani stood at the end of the table a moment longer with the drawing of the tooth in her hand, and then put it down very carefully on the others, and went.

Bram was the last, apart from Ferra, who was always the last. He stood at the door with his hand on the frame.

"Three thousand beasts the size of a hull," he said. "All in one place." He shook his head. "Something ate a lot of grass."

Emeric laughed, for the first time that evening, and Bram looked pleased with himself in the way he never let anybody see for longer than it took to turn round, and went.

Ferra closed her book. She looked at the ring round the gap for a while before she did.

"It's in the book," she said. "The rest of it. The part we can't read."

"Maybe."

"The man went to that island to find where the signs came from. He found a way down." She put the book under her arm. "If I'd found a way down, on an island where the glass grows out of the ground, I'd want to know what the air was like at the bottom of it."

She went. Emeric sat on alone at the table for a while with the fire going down, and thought about a roof of rock, and about a crack, and about air that had been waiting under it for longer than there had been kings, and about how sure he had been, ten minutes ago, that it could not.
