Chapter 205 - Forty and Not a Copper More

Placit wrote with the black for the first time on the eleventh of August, at night, and he wrote about Emeric.

The printer brought the page to Sethe's counting room himself, at a run, at a quarter to midnight; and this time he did not bring it to the prince. He brought it to Ferra, and stood in front of her table while she read it, white, with his hands at his sides.

It was short. Placit's square old-fashioned hand, every stroke of it, faint on the sized sheet; a letter to Strade, by the salutation, the only one of the five whom Placit addressed by his first name.

He read it over Ferra's shoulder. He read it twice.

Hollin's man has been to me. He will not say why, but he is frightened, and he is not a man who frightens. Something in the house has been read. I do not know how. I know whose ink it was.

You were right about the prince and I was wrong. I have sent to the one who nearly had him once through his own guard. He will do it again, properly this time, and for forty and not a Copper more, which is what we paid before, and which was too much for what we got.

Burn this. Burn the book. Keep the charter.

P.

Nobody in the counting room said anything for a while.

Ferra did not look up. She sat with the page under her hand and the lamp on it, and he watched her finger go to the fourth line and stay: forty and not a Copper more. Then she reached for her own book without looking, and opened it at the page from April, the page with Sethe's second matter copied into it in her hand, and laid it beside Placit's letter; and put her other finger on the line there.

Forty Gold. To M.

"Forty," said Ferra. "What we paid before. Too much for what we got." She did not raise her voice. "It's the same man. The same price. They're hiring him again."

"Yes."

"For you." She looked up at him, then, at last. "Properly, this time."

He had known since the second line. He had known before that; he had known since a night in April when he sat in the counting room at Kaldo with Sethe's sheet in front of him, and went back, for a breath, to a hold in the dark, and a quick voice on the deck over his face, and let it go past him like a lamp on a far bank because he did not want to follow it. He followed it now. He stood in Sethe's counting room with Placit's letter on the table and the knot's grey ink still drying on the printer's bed a hundred yards away, and heard the quick voice say it, on the deck, in the dark, five winters ago: Marrow says it reads better than a ransom.

"Marrow," he said.

Ferra looked at him.

"On the boat. The one who talked. He said a name. Marrow says this, Marrow says that. As if Marrow were the one who'd planned it." He put his finger beside hers on the M. "Marrow. He's the M. He's the one Sethe paid forty Gold. Through Garrin, through the boat, through Rylan's door." He took his finger away. "And they've sent for him again."

"You knew," said Ferra. "In April."

"I half knew."

"You told me M. Not known." Her voice was very level. "And I wrote it down."

"I know. It was true, in April. I didn't let it be anything else." He looked at the page. "I didn't want it to be a man I'd heard. I wanted it to be a letter in a dead man's ledger." He was quiet. "Change it. Enter a correction. With the date."

She looked at him for a long breath across the lamp. Then she dipped her pen and wrote, under M. Not known, without striking it: 11 August. M. is Marrow. Named on the boat in the dark, heard by E., known to E. since April, not said. And her initial.

Then she read Placit's letter once more, and looked up at the printer, who was still standing at the end of her table, white.

"Burn this. Burn the book," she said. "Is the bed still moving?"

"Yes, Chancellor. Placit's still writing. Short ones. To all of them, one after another."

"Then go back to it," said Ferra, "and bring me every one as it comes off. Every one. Don't read them. Run." The printer ran. Ferra looked at Emeric. "Wake Saban."



By two in the morning they had Placit's night.

Four letters more, one to each of the others, all short, all in the same order: something has been read; the prince is not what he seems; Marrow is sent for; the books are to burn, every page, tonight and tomorrow, in every house, in the kitchen fires, and nobody is to write anything further down on any account; and the charter, which was the Staple's whole right to exist, which hung in its frame on the wall of the strongroom, was to be kept, whatever else was burned. And then nothing. The bed stopped. Placit had put down his pen and, presumably, taken his own advice.

The bed did not move again that night, or the next day, or ever, from Placit's knot.

From Vennor's it moved once more, at dawn. It was Vennor's wife, writing to her sister in the capital in her round hand, about smoke. Her husband had made the kitchen girl burn all his papers in the range before it was light, she wrote, a whole chest of them, and the kitchen was full of smoke and black flakes and the girl was crying because Vennor had shouted at her, and he had never shouted in his life, and what in the world was going on in this town, and she was frightened, and did her sister think the cough that had taken poor Anna might be in the town too, because Vennor had looked so grey this morning.

Ferra struck it. She did not hesitate this time. She struck it and turned the page and put her hand flat on it, and sat like that for a while, at the table, in the grey dawn.



The summons went that afternoon.

Not on Kaldo's paper. On the plain grey rag of the Wayford mills, bought by Saban at a stationer's on the square that morning, written in a clerk's ordinary hand by a clerk Saban had brought up from the posting house, who did not know what he was writing. Unsigned. Five copies. Saban's groom carried them to the five doors himself, at three in the afternoon, and put each one into the hand of whoever opened the door, and walked away.

It was Ferra's wording. She had written it four times and struck it three.

The holders of the Staple at Wayford are required to attend at their counting house on the square at noon on the fourteenth of August, without arms or men, to answer for the matter and the second matter and for what is held against rent. Those who attend will be heard by the law. Those who do not attend may stay where they are. Their transport will be with them shortly.

"Transport," said Saban, reading it over her shoulder in Sethe's counting room. He looked at it a long time. "That's not a word I'd have chosen."

"It's the right word," said Ferra.

"It's a threat."

"It's an accurate description of what will happen to anybody who doesn't come," said Ferra, "which is not the same thing." She sanded it. "If they come, they'll come to the counting house, on the square, at noon, in front of the whole town, and Aster will be there, and the circuit magistrate, and they'll be arrested under the law of the barony for paying Sethe to have a prince of the blood taken off his own street. Openly. On what's in Sethe's boxes, which is what can be got openly." She folded it. "Nothing from the knots. Condition four."

"And if they don't come?"

"Then they've been told," said Ferra. "In writing. Once."

They did not come.

Nobody had thought they would. At noon on the fourteenth the counting house on the square stood open with Aster at its door in his brown coat and the northern circuit's magistrate in a chair inside and a crowd on the square, because a town like Wayford always knew; and at half past twelve Saban's groom came down off the hill road at a run, and told them, out of the side of his mouth, beside the horse trough, what the five had done instead.

They had gone up the hill. All five, with their households, in carriages, at dawn, to the Staple house in the fork of the roads, where the chain was. They had taken their men. The chain was up across both roads with a cart behind it. The windows of the long panelled room under the weathervane were shuttered. There were men on the roof by the chimney stacks with crossbows, and men in the weighing shed, and men at the gates of the yard behind the house, where the stable was; and the groom had counted them, from the edge of the wood across the road, lying in the bracken with a piece of bread in his hand so that if anybody came he could say he was eating it.

"Thirty-eight," said the groom. "House men, mostly. Some of Dray's. Some I didn't know, harder, from the north." He spat. "And a knight. One. In plate, with a crest I didn't know, on the gate."

"And smoke," said Saban.

The groom looked at him.

"From the chimneys," said the groom. "All of them. On a hot day in August. Thick, and grey, and black bits in it." He jerked his chin up the hill. "Go and look. You can see it from the square."

They could. Emeric stood at the counting house door beside Aster and looked up the hill at the Staple house in its fork of the roads, a mile off, small and grey against the green of Lisle's woods; and over it, from every chimney, in the still August noon, the smoke went straight up into the blue, thick and grey, and leaned a little, high up, and drifted north over the woods.

Two hundred years of books.



He rode out of Wayford at four that afternoon, south, by the capital road, in the gold-laced coat.

Openly. With Ward and Saban and the two garrison men and the empty paper cart behind them, down through the square, past Ottoline's shop with the stopped clock, past the posting house and the trough, where the carters on the bench stopped talking to watch him go; drunk, a little, on Maren's water, swaying in the saddle and singing something from the capital that he had not sung in eight years and found he still knew all the words to. Leaving. The prince who had wanted a share and been refused it, and had sent a silly letter about transport that nobody had answered, and had now gone off south in a sulk, back to his ruin and his iron road. Every carter on the bench would say so by nightfall, at every inn on the river. And somewhere up the hill, in a shuttered room full of smoke, somebody would be told so, and would believe it, because it was what they had always believed.

Twelve miles down the road, in the woods past the twelfth milestone, the cart turned off the road onto a ride between the trees, as if to rest the horse; and the riders followed it. A mile in, where the ride opened into a clearing among the beeches, the K Discovery was lying on her legs in the bracken with her stones dark and her hatch open and Fletcher on the deck, eating an apple.

"You took your time," said Fletcher.

"I was singing."

"I heard." Fletcher threw the core into the bracken. "So did half the county." He looked at the gold lace on the cuffs. "Get that off before you come aboard. You'll catch it on something."



She lifted at dusk, and turned north.

Not south. Emeric had asked for it at the long table in Kaldo in May, when the summer was still being planned, and Ward had not liked it and had said so; and in the end Ward had agreed because Emeric had pointed out, quite reasonably, that a ship that could hang three hundred feet over a junction at one in the morning to hang knots could hang three hundred feet over it at nine at night for a quarter of an hour, dark, with the town at its suppers and the five behind their shutters, and nobody look up.

So she came in over the Staple house from the north at the last of the light, very slowly, with her stones shut down to a whisper and the wind behind her, and hung.

Emeric lay on the deck at the open hatch with his chin on the coaming and opened his sense.

All of it. Not thin, as he kept it day to day, a film over everything; wide, and down, as far as he could push it, until his head began to ache behind the eyes and the deck under him went far away. Three hundred feet of dusk. The slate roof, a smear of nothing; slate held almost no mana at all. And under it, the house. The long panelled room, shuttered, full of people: he could feel them all, a cluster of notes, some dense, some thin, the five and their wives and their sons and their clerks, sitting very still; and one, at the head of them, small and old and dense and quite still, Placit. Men on the stairs. Men in the hall. Men on the roof by the stacks, six, cold and bored, with the faint hard pinpoints of something in their hands that was not mana but steel, crossbows, he could not feel steel but he could feel where a man's hands were and how he held them. Men in the weighing shed. Men at the yard gates.

The knight, at the main gate. A big dense steady note, a house knight's, with the tracery in him like a lit lamp.

Thirty-eight. The groom had counted right.

And below the floor of the house, under the cellars, under the stone, something he did not understand. A stillness. Not an absence; there was a difference, he would think afterward, between a place where nobody was and a place where something was being kept very quiet. This was the second. A patch of stillness under the north end of the house, about the size of a long room, where his sense went in and found nothing it could name, and came back to him with a feeling like a hand pressed flat over a mouth.

He did not understand it. He set it aside. He went on reading.

In the stable yard behind the house, by the gate that let onto Lisle's woods road, there were horses. Eight, saddled, already saddled at nine at night, with their heads down at a manger and their notes slow and thick, the way a horse's are; and two men with them. Grooms, he thought. Two notes, ordinary, waiting.

No. One.

One of them was a groom. He went past it, and then he came back to the other one, slowly, the way a man comes back along a shelf to a book he has passed and only now recognised by its spine.

Still. Standing. By the gate, with his back to the stable wall, in the dark of the yard, not moving at all, the way a man stands who has stood watches all his life.

He had felt it on a road in the forest in his first week in the south, on a night with a fire, before he had known he could feel anything. He had felt it through the deck of a boat in the dark five winters ago, by a hatch, listening, while his hands were tied behind him and a boy's heart beat beside his feet. He had felt it go away from him over the water into the reeds, thinner and thinner, until he was not sure whether he was feeling it or remembering it.

He was not remembering it now.

"Ward," he said.

Ward was lying beside him on the deck at the hatch. He did not move.

"Garrin's in the yard," said Emeric. "By the stable gate. With the horses. Saddled." He did not take his eyes off the dark below. "They're not waiting for Marrow to come. He's already here. That's Marrow's man, holding Marrow's horses." He pushed his sense once more at the house, at the cluster in the long room, at the stairs, at the hall; at the dense still notes and the thin ones; and could not tell which was the man who had planned the boat. They were all only people. "Marrow's in that house."

Ward said nothing for a while. Below them the five sat in their smoke and the knight stood at his gate and Garrin stood by the stable wall with the horses breathing beside him.

"Rylan," said Ward.

"Yes."



Rylan came up to the long room at Kaldo at seven the next morning, because Ward had sent the wire from the gate tower at three when the ship came in: one word, his name.

He stood at the end of the table in his black with his hands behind his back and listened to all of it. Placit's letter. Forty and not a Copper more. The summons, and the smoke, and the thirty-eight, and the horses in the yard at nine at night, saddled. He did not move while Emeric told it. He did not move when Emeric said the name. His face did not change at all, the pleasant easy face he had worn every day since the river, that teased the class and laughed at Osric and had not, in nearly five years, once looked like the face of a man whose son had been taken off a street. It did not change now. Only his hands, behind his back, which Emeric could not see, and could feel; the note of them, closing.

"I want you to go in," said Emeric. "With me and Ward. By air. Over Lisle's wood, in the dark, on the wings. The column takes the gate by road; we take the house from the top." He looked at Rylan. "Garrin will be in the yard, by the stable gate, with the horses, waiting to get somebody out. When it starts he'll run, or he'll fight. Either way, you're the one between him and the gate."

Rylan's face did not change.

"One condition," said Emeric. "Alive."

The room was very quiet.

Ward, at the window, did not turn round. Ferra at her place did not look up from her book. Imani, at the far end of the table, who had come up from the ward at a quarter to seven without being sent for, sat with her hands folded on the wood and watched Rylan's face.

"Alive," said Rylan.

"He killed his own man on that boat and fired it with you and Finn in the hold. He's the only one who knows who Marrow is when he isn't being Marrow. He's the only one who knows who paid Cobb, and for what, and how far back it goes." Emeric did not look away from him. "Dead, he's a body in a stable yard. Alive, he's every answer I haven't got."

"Then send Ward," said Rylan pleasantly. "Ward will bring him back alive. Ward doesn't want anything from him."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you do," said Emeric. "And you'll bring him back alive anyway." He was quiet. "That's why it's you. Not because you're the best blade at this table. Because you're the one man in Kaldo who has every right to kill him and won't." He looked at Rylan's face, the pleasant easy face that did not change. "I'm asking you to be that man. In a dark yard, with him in front of you and a sword in your hand. I'm asking a great deal. I know."

Rylan stood at the end of the table a long while.

Then he brought his hands round from behind his back and put them flat on the table in front of him, and looked down at them. Across the back of the right one, from the wrist to the knuckles, was a burn, old, healed white; from a night five winters ago, Emeric knew, in the hold of a burning boat, when his own fire had taken the cord off Rylan's wrists.

"Nessa will want to know," said Rylan, to his hands, "why I didn't."

"Tell her I asked."

"She'll say you'd no right to."

"She'll be right," said Emeric.

Rylan looked up. His face, for the first time in nearly five years, was not pleasant at all; and then it was again, slowly, as a man puts a coat back on that he has taken off in a cold room.

"Alive," said Rylan. "Highness." He straightened. "When?"



The women came for him that night, both of them, at ten, in the long room, where he was sitting at the end of the table with the plan of the Staple house that Saban's groom had drawn from memory, and a candle, and a cold cup of tea, and a face, Imani said afterward, like a man trying to add up a column of knives.

Ferra came in first. She did not knock. She shut the door behind her, and turned the key in it, which she had never done in that room in seven years, and put the key in her pocket.

Imani came in behind her by the other door, from the stair, and shut that one, and leaned on it.

He looked up from the plan.

"No," said Ferra.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to say work first." She crossed the room and took the plan out from under his hands, the whole sheet, and folded it in four, exactly, along the creases it already had, and put it face down on the far end of the table. Then she took the candle and moved it there too. "You've been at this since seven this morning. Ward says. Ward says you've drawn the yard ten times and the gallery stair nine and Rylan's line from the roof to the gate four, and every time it comes out the same, because it's the same yard." She came back to his chair. "It's ten. The plan is folded. Hale's gone home. Nobody's dying, Imani says."

"Nobody's dying," said Imani, from the door.

"And the forge is tomorrow night," said Ferra, "all night, and I'll be sitting up with you with the book open, and you'll not sleep, and nor will I. And the night after that, you'll be in a yard in Wayford." She stood beside his chair and looked down at him. "So tonight is ours. It's in the terms. Imani says it's in the terms."

"It's not in the terms," said Emeric.

"It is now," said Imani. She came away from the door. "I've written it in. Under the fourth. Ferra initialed it." She came round the other side of his chair. "Fun first. Before yards."

He looked from one to the other. Ferra on his right, with her hands folded at her waist and her face perfectly composed and pink to the hair. Imani on his left, with her hair already coming down and her mouth already curving.

"There's a gallery stair," he said, "that I don't..."

Ferra sat down on his knee.

Not across it. Sideways, neatly, as if it were a chair she had chosen in the counting room, with her back very straight; and put one arm round his neck, and looked at him from very close, steadily, and said in her exact voice: "The gallery stair has fourteen steps and a turn at the ninth. The groom said so. I wrote it down. I have it by heart. So do you." And kissed him.

Imani laughed, low, and pulled the second chair round beside his, close, so that her knees were against his thigh, and sat in it, and leaned in, and put her mouth against his neck under the ear while Ferra was still kissing him; and he made a sound he did not know he could make, into Ferra's mouth, and felt Ferra smile against his lips.

"There," said Imani, into his neck. "That's the pulse I wanted."

He gave up on the gallery stair.

It was a very long time before anybody in the long room said anything sensible. Ferra kissed him, and then Imani pulled his face round by the jaw and kissed him, slowly, thoroughly, with Ferra's arm still round his neck and Ferra's mouth moving to his ear; and then Ferra again, turning his face back with two fingers on his chin as if correcting a column; and somewhere in it he got an arm round each of them and pulled them in, and Ferra shifted on his knee to face him, astride now, her skirt rucked over his thighs, her knees tight against his hips on the old oak chair; and pressed down, slowly, and then not slowly, with the small fierce sound she made that he had heard once in Sethe's chair and had thought about every night since. Imani was on her knees on the seat of the other chair beside them with both hands in his hair, laughing, breathless, into his ear, saying something about the terms that made no sense at all and that Ferra answered without opening her eyes. The candle at the far end of the table guttered and nobody looked at it. His hands were at Ferra's waist and then lower, pulling her in against him, and she let him; and Imani caught his other hand and put it flat on her own waist, under her loosened dress, on warm skin, and held it there with hers, and looked at him from a hand's breadth away with her eyes very dark.

"Not all the way," said Imani, against his mouth. Breathless. "Not tonight. I know."

"I know."

"The three of us," said Ferra, with her forehead against his and her hips still moving, very slowly, as if she could not quite make them stop. "Whatever it is. Somewhere better than a chair." She laughed, shakily. "I wrote it down. I'm going to kill whoever wrote it down."

"You wrote it down," said Imani.

"I know," said Ferra, and kissed him again, harder, as if it were his fault.

Somebody tried the handle of the stair door.

All three of them went perfectly still.

The handle turned, and stopped against the lock, and turned back. A pause. Then, outside, very flat, through the oak, Ward's voice.

"Highness. Declan's wire from Wayford. The column is ready at Sethe's hall. He asks if you wish to see the masks before they go aboard."

Nobody in the long room breathed.

Ferra, astride him in the chair with her hair half down and her collar open and her face crimson to the roots, looked at him from an inch away with an expression of pure horror; and then, slowly, the horror went out of it, and something else came up in its place, and her mouth began to shake.

"Tomorrow," said Emeric, at the door, in a voice that was nearly steady. "I'll see them tomorrow."

A pause, through the oak. Ward did not move away from the door. Emeric could feel him there, the big dense steady note, standing quite still on the landing outside, deciding something.

"The Chancellor's key," said Ward, through the door, in exactly the same flat voice, "is on the inside of the other door also. Should anybody require it." And then his boots, going away down the stair, unhurried, one step at a time; and at the turn, very faintly, a sound that Emeric had never once heard Ward make in seven years, and which he did not, afterward, believe he had heard at all.

Imani fell off her chair.

She went sideways off the seat onto the floor and lay there on her back on the long room's boards with her hair everywhere and her hands over her face and laughed until she could not breathe; and Ferra put her face down in Emeric's neck and shook; and he sat in the chair with Ferra in his lap and Imani on the floor and laughed with them, helplessly, at the ceiling, until the candle at the end of the table went out on its own and left them in the firelight.

"He knew," said Ferra at last, into his neck, muffled. "He knew the whole time. He came up the stair to tell us he knew."

"He came up to tell us he'd not tell anybody," said Imani, from the floor. She took her hands off her face. "That's different. That's Ward." She lay looking up at the beams. "Oh, God. I'll never be able to look at him."

"You'll look at him tomorrow at breakfast," said Ferra, "and so will I, and we'll neither of us say anything, and nor will he, and that'll be worse." She lifted her head off his shoulder and looked at him, and her face was still crimson and her eyes were wet from laughing, and she kissed him once more, gently, on the corner of the mouth. "That's enough," she said. "That's enough for tonight. Before I forget why it's enough."

She got off his lap. She stood in the firelight and did her collar up, button by button, with her fingers not quite steady, and pinned her hair, and was the Chancellor again, very nearly; and held out her hand to Imani on the floor, and pulled her up.

Imani stood, and shook out her dress, and looked at him in the chair.

"Sleep," she said. "Tonight. Because tomorrow you won't, and the night after you'll be on a roof." She bent and kissed his forehead, as she might a patient's. "And when you're in that yard, with Garrin in it," she said, quieter, close, "come home. That's all. Come home with all your skin on and let me look at your eyes. That's the only work that comes first."

They went out by the stair door, together, with Ferra's key. He heard them go down the stair, and stop at the turn, where Ward had made the sound; and heard Imani say something, low, and Ferra laugh, the real laugh, the one he heard about twice a year; and go on down.

He sat in the dark long room in the firelight for a while longer. Then he got up and went to the far end of the table, and did not unfold the plan.

He went to bed. He slept, for once, all night, like a dropped stone.
