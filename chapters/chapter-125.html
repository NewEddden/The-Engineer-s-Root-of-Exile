Chapter 125 - Terms

Saban was waiting for him at the foot of the harbour house stair in the morning, with his coat on and his chain of beads back round his neck, and the look of a man who has been to the quay before breakfast and come back with something.

"You told her it was worth something," Saban said.

Emeric did not ask how he knew. The boatyard at the stream mouth was two hundred yards from the fish market, and a sovereign had stood in it with a foreign prince, and Saban had been drinking on that quay for three nights.

"It is," he said.

Saban opened his mouth, and closed it again.

It was new. In all the years Emeric had known him, he had never once seen Saban without something to say about a price. He watched him look for it now, the thing a merchant says to a man who has just told a seller that her goods are dear to him, and not find it, because every one of the things a merchant says to that was wrong, and Saban knew they were wrong, and could not think of a right one.

"Stone pitch," Saban said at last, instead. "Out of the ground. I smelled it, the time I went up to Duvrenne for the grain. Their whole harbour smelled of it."

"You never said."

"It wasn't grain." Saban looked at the door. "I'd gone to sell grain."

They went up to the hall together, and did not say anything else about it.

The room off the hall was the same room, with the long table and the windows open on the mountain, and the same people were at it, and some more. Rowena sat at the head, in the blue coat. The harbour-mistress sat on her left and the Treasurer on her right, as before. Across from the harbour-mistress sat the thin head of the great works, with soot still on his cuffs, and beside him a knight-captain in a plain coat with a sword, a square grey man Emeric had last seen walking between pairs on the yard with his hands behind his back.

Emeric sat across from her. Ferra sat at his side with her minute book open at the page she had begun in the long room the morning before, which now ran to three pages. Imani sat on his other side with her canvas roll on the table in front of her, and Saban at the end with his figures, and Vantic at the far end with her book, as before.

On the table in front of Emeric there were three books bound in boards.

He had fetched them from the shelf in the day room himself, at first light, because they were the only things aboard that made the argument he meant to make without his help. He did not touch them yet.

"I made you an offer two days ago," he said, "and it was the wrong one."

Nobody at the table said anything. The harbour-mistress looked at Rowena, and Rowena did not look at anybody.

"I read your quay," Emeric said. "I didn't read you. I didn't know how you came to hold your seat, or what you'd done since. I didn't know about your law." He said it plainly, because he had decided in the night to say it plainly. "I know now. My factor went down to your taverns after the table rose and came back after midnight with it, near enough word for word, from a clerk in your harbour office who's proud of it. I should have sent him a month ago. I didn't."

Rowena looked at Saban, at the end of the table, for a moment. Saban looked back at her with no expression at all.

"Go on," she said.

"Your law is very good," said Emeric. "It's better than anything my father's kingdom has. Anybody may be named. Blood's no claim. The table that confirms it sits by office, so the office holds the seat and not the family. And a blade raised over the chair costs the man who raised it everything he might have had." He put his hands flat on the table. "And it's never been tested. It's a piece of paper. The only reason anybody in this town respects it is that you're standing behind it. You know that better than anybody alive."

The knight-captain shifted in his chair. Nobody else moved.

"I haven't come to offer you anything you can buy off a boat," Emeric said. "I've come to offer you the things that make a piece of paper outlive the person who wrote it."

He put his hand on the first of the books then, and pushed it across the table to her.

It was a primer. It was one of Orrin's primers, off the printer, the letters and the numbers and the first hundred words a child needed, in the barony's plain hand, printed on Kaldo paper and bound at the finishing shop in boards. Rowena opened it and turned the pages. They were all the same. They were all exactly the same, every letter, from one page to the next, because nobody's hand had made any of them.

"There's a shelf of those in Orrin's school," Emeric said. "Every one of them says the same thing. Every child in my barony who learns to read learns off the same page, and they learn it from teachers who learned off the same page, and when those teachers are dead the page will still be there, and it'll still say the same thing."

He pushed the second across.

"That's a well," he said. "Eleven lines. How deep, how wide, what goes round the top, how far from a privy. Any mason who can read it builds the same well, and it's a good one, and nobody has to stand over him while he does it."

The third was the barony's measures.

"An inch," he said, "a pound, a measure of grain, a foot of rope. Every one of them struck into a rod, and every rod in four baronies has the same mark on the end, cut by the same man. When he's dead the inch will still be an inch. That's all a standard is. It's a man's judgment put somewhere it can't die with him."

Rowena had the three books in front of her. She had not closed any of them.

"And the last," said Emeric, "isn't mine. It's my physician's."

Imani unrolled her canvas on the table.

She did not have drawings in it. She had notes, in her own hand, three days of them, written on the balcony of the harbour house and in the streets on the way up and down the slope and at the fish market and the well at the foot of the upper town. She read them as she read everything, as a dosage.

"Your streets carry everything downhill in open channels," she said, "past people's doors, to the harbour. Your wells are dug where it's easy to dig, which on a hill is at the bottom, which is where everything the channels carry ends up. You're a hot town, and a crowded one, and you're built on a slope." She looked up from the canvas at the harbour-mistress, who had gone very still. "You bury children every summer. More than you should. You'll have been told it's the heat."

The harbour-mistress did not answer.

"It's not the heat," said Imani. "It's the channels. It can be fixed. It's slow and it's dull and it wants a great many men with shovels and one person who knows what they're doing, and then it wants to be written down so the next person who knows what they're doing doesn't have to be me." She rolled the canvas halfway closed. "That's the part he means."

The head of the great works had been looking at the well standard across the table, upside down, the way Emeric read Ferra's book.

"Your furnace," Emeric said to him. "The one that cracked. You said you hear it change its note a day or two before."

"Yes."

"And that you can't write down a sound."

"No."

"You can't," Emeric agreed. "But you can write down everything else about the day before. How hot, how long since it was lined, what went into it, who was on it, what the note was like in your own words. Every time one cracks. And in twenty years you've got a book, and the boy you've got now reads it, and the boy after him, and one day somebody reads it who couldn't hear a furnace from the next shed, and still knows it's going to go."

The thin man looked at him for a long time.

"My master would have hated that," he said.

"Would he have been wrong?"

The head of the great works thought about it honestly, as he had in the shed.

"No," he said.

Emeric sat back.

"Printing," he said. "A school. Standards. Drains. Carried in your name, not mine. Kaldo sends you the men who can start it, and the presses, and the paper, and the books, and your own people learn it, and in ten years none of it needs Kaldo at all." He looked at her. "And none of it needs you, either. That's what it's for."

It was quiet at the table.

Rowena closed the primer, and then the well standard, and then the measures, one after another, and laid them in a pile with their edges square, and put her hand flat on top of the pile.

"I have been necessary," she said, "for sixteen years."

She said it to the table. She did not say it to him. She said it to the harbour-mistress and the head of the works and the knight-captain and the Treasurer, who had sat at her table by office and watched her be necessary for sixteen years, and every one of them understood it, and none of them looked away.

"I would like very much to stop," said Rowena.

Then she took her hand off the books, and her face went back to where she kept it for tables, and she said, in the voice of a woman setting terms: "What do you want for it?"

He told her.

Sulphur, first, because it was what he had come for and he did not pretend otherwise. Sulphur off her ground, by the boatload, at a price Saban had worked out with the harbour's own figures in front of him and which she could check against her own, and did. Then heat. A throat of the mountain, in the great works, with a furnace over it that Kaldo could put its own work into, when Kaldo needed a heat it could not afford to make, under the head of the works' eye and on his terms. And the lane: the water between her harbour and his, coasting boats both ways, with the sulphur going east and the presses and the paper and the men coming west.

The head of the works asked what kind of work Kaldo would want to put in his furnace. Emeric told him glass, at first, and steel later, and the man nodded as though he had expected worse.

After that it was drafting, and Ferra did it.

She did it with the Treasurer, across the corner of the table, from her three pages. Emeric had watched her draft a great many things, and he had never watched her draft across a table from somebody who knew the work as well as she did, and it was like watching two people carry a long beam through a narrow door, each knowing which way the other would turn before they turned. The Treasurer read Ferra's clauses and asked for one word changed in each, and Ferra changed it or did not, and when she did not she said why in one sentence, and the Treasurer considered the sentence and let it stand. The harbour-mistress put the dues in. The knight-captain put nothing in and read everything.

Vantic read every clause as it was written.

She did it at the far end of the table, with her own book open beside Ferra's fair lines as they came down to her, and her pen moving in her own book and not in theirs. She read each clause against something in her book that nobody else could see, and when she had read it she went on to the next. Once she asked how long the lane was to run, and was told, and wrote the figure down. Once she asked Saban what a coaster charged by the ton between here and the lower water in winter, and Saban told her, and she did a sum in the margin and looked at it.

At the end she closed her book and spoke to the table for the first time.

"There is nothing in it," she said, "that touches the covenant on the House's loan to Kaldo. The lane is new revenue and not a charge on the old. The House will want to be told when it opens." She looked at Emeric, not at Ferra. "I will tell them."

"Thank you," said Emeric.

"It is not a kindness," said Vantic. "It is the covenant." And she went back to her book.

Saban was named Kaldo's factor for the lane in the eleventh clause, in Ferra's hand, with his full name and the words *for the barony*. He read it upside down when it was written, and did not say anything, and sat very straight for some time afterward.

It was near the end that the knight-captain said the only thing he said all afternoon.

"If she signs this," he said, to Emeric, "your quarrels are hers."

It was quite mild. It was the voice of a man stating the length of a road.

"Anybody who wants to close your lane," the knight-captain said, "closes hers. Anybody who comes for your barony has come for the other end of her sulphur. You have quarrels, Highness. Everybody knows you have them. Everybody on this coast has heard what the Crown thinks of you." He did not look at Rowena. "I'd want it written down that we knew."

Emeric looked at Rowena.

"He's right," he said.

"I know he's right," said Rowena. "That's his office." She looked at Ferra's pages. "Write it down that we knew. Don't write down what we'd do about it. We'll find that out when we have to."

Ferra wrote it down that they knew. She did not write anything under it. She left a line of space under it on the page, which Emeric had never seen her do in ten years, and went on.

They signed at the end of the afternoon, with the light going red on the mountain. Rowena signed, and the Treasurer witnessed, and Emeric signed, and Ferra witnessed, and each of them signed the other's copy. The table stood. The harbour-mistress shook Emeric's hand as though it were a rope she was making fast.

Then Emeric said that there was one more thing, and that it was not in the terms, and that he would need an hour and some men with a cart.

The crate came up out of the hold at dusk.

It wanted both keys. Ward unlocked the hatch over the hold from the open deck, with the key off the cord round his neck, and Osric went down through the inside passage and unlocked the armoury door with his, and went in and rigged the tackle over the crate's stand from below. Orrin had been in the evening before, with Osric at the door, and filled her hearts by hand on his cushion off the saloon bench, and said nothing about whose they were. The crate came up through the hatch on the tackle, swinging, with Fletcher at the fall, and down onto a harbour cart on the black quay, and six of Rowena's knights walked it up the slope to her hall with Ward walking beside it and Osric behind.

They opened it in the hall, on the hot stone floor, under the lamps.

Rowena had sent for her armourers. There were two of them, a man and a woman, both grey, both in leather aprons, and they came in from the shops behind the hall with their sleeves rolled and the look of people who had been called away from something in the middle. The man was Lorcan and the woman was Hesta, and between them, Rowena said, they had kept every knight in Emberfell in plate for twenty years.

Ward took the lid off.

It came out of the straw in pieces, wrapped, and Osric unwrapped them and laid them out on the floor in order, the way they went on, the way he had laid out his own in the pattern store: the sabatons first, flat, and the greaves, and up. The breastplate last, with the front heart in it behind its pane. The helm. The cloak, folded, with its battens along the edges.

It was bright steel, as bare as the day it came off the forge. There were no lions on it and no device of any kind. There was blue glass over every pane.

Nobody in the hall said anything.

"It's for you," Emeric said to Rowena.

She looked at the pieces on the floor for a long time. Then she took off her coat and handed it to her tall knight without looking round, and stepped out of her boots, and stood on the hot stone in her shirt and breeches, and held her arms out from her sides, and Lorcan and Hesta came forward and put it on her.

They did it the way armourers do a thing they have done ten thousand times on a thing they had never seen, carefully, with their hands going slower than their eyes. Emeric told them the order and they did not need telling twice. The sabatons, the greaves, the cuisses. Hesta found the straps on the backplate and looked at them and did them up. Lorcan settled the breastplate onto the shoulders and closed it, and behind its blue pane the front heart woke.

Rowena drew a breath.

"It's cool," she said.

It was the first thing she said. She had worn plate on a harbour in the sun for thirty years, and on a quay where men went about in their shirts at midwinter, and every day of those thirty years she had been hot inside it, and she stood in her own hot hall in a full harness with her arms a little out from her sides and said that it was cool, in a voice that did not quite belong at a table.

"Water," Emeric said. "In the lining. And air through it. It runs whenever you're wearing it."

She moved her shoulders. She moved her arms, and bent, and straightened, and turned from the waist, and he watched her find out the thing he had hoped for since the night Ward stood in the room and gave him her shoulders.

It fitted.

Lorcan and Hesta went over it anyway, because it was their trade. They went strap by strap, the way Bram went over a casting, with their fingers under every edge, and they did not find anything to take up. Hesta found a strap at the back of the left cuisse that she thought was a finger loose, and tried it, and it was not. She stood back and looked at Lorcan, and Lorcan looked at her, and between them there passed the look of two people who had been fitting plate for twenty years and had never once put a harness on a knight that did not want something done to it.

That surprised them more than it surprised Rowena. Rowena was looking at Emeric.

"How?" she said. "The last time you saw me, you came up to my shoulder."

"Yes."

"You were shorter. You'd have built me a giant."

"I did," said Emeric. "My guard corrected me."

Rowena turned her head and looked at Ward, standing by the door where he had put himself.

She looked at him the way one knight measures another across a yard, from the feet up, as she had on the quay through the cloaks: at where he stood and how, and what he would do if something came through the door, and how good he would be at it. And Ward met it the same way, with nothing in his face at all, one fighter to another, and let her look.

She took it for what it was, which was the best compliment one knight could pay another, that he had watched her closely enough and long enough to get her right to a finger. There was nothing else in it. Emeric had known there would not be, and he watched her know it too.

Then she laughed.

It was not the short surprised laugh of the northern goddess. It was a real laugh, from somewhere under the plate, and she put her head back to do it.

"A giant," she said.

Her armourers had not stopped looking at the suit.

Lorcan had the helm in his hands and was turning it under the lamp. He turned it over and looked inside it, and turned it back and looked along the brow, and ran his thumb along the edge of the plate where it went round the ear. Then he put it down on the floor very carefully and went and looked at the breastplate on her, close, with his face a hand's width from the steel.

"There's no hammer on it," he said.

He said it to Hesta, not to anybody else.

"There's not a hammer mark anywhere. Not a file mark. There's no join." He put his fingertip on the breastplate at the place where the front heart sat behind its pane, where the plate went in round the housing and came out again as though the housing had grown there. "That wants five joins. Five at least, and rivets at every one. There's none. It's one piece." He moved his finger along to a node seat at her ribs, sunk into the steel, with no rivet head anywhere near it. "That's one piece too. It goes right through."

He straightened up and looked at Emeric.

"Who made this?" he said.

"We did. In Kaldo."

"Where's the smith's mark?"

"There isn't one."

Lorcan looked at the steel again. It was plain what unsettled him, and it was not the lack of joins. It was that he had spent his life looking at plate, and he could look at any piece of it and tell you where the smith had stood and how he held his hammer and whether he had been tired at the end of the day, and he could not find where anybody had ever touched this at all.

Hesta was not looking at the plate. She was looking at the glass.

She went round Rowena slowly, once, and then again the other way, and she looked at every pane: the two over the hearts, and the small ones at the joints over the nodes, and the long one down the forearm. They were all the same blue. They were set into the plate at angles that did not follow the plate, some tilted forward and some back and one at the back of the knee that faced almost straight down, and she crouched on the hot floor and looked up at that one for a long time.

"Nobody holds a stone like that," she said. "Not in a hand. Not in a setting."

"No."

"They're not the same colour, are they." She was not asking. "Under the glass. They'd not all be the same stone, not on a thing like this, not if they do different work. But you can't tell. The glass makes them one colour, all over her." She stood up. "So nobody watching her can see which one's firing."

"Yes."

Hesta looked at him, and then at the suit, with the face of a woman who had set stones into knights' harness for twenty years so that they would do their work and be out of the way, and had just been shown stones set into a harness so that they would do their work and be the look of the thing at the same time, and had never once thought of it.

They found the second ring together.

Lorcan found the seats first, at her forearms and along her thighs and up her spine, and asked what they were, because nothing on the plate led to them. There were no rods. Hesta put her bare hand flat on one at Rowena's forearm and held it there, and nothing happened, and she looked at Lorcan, and Lorcan tried the one at the thigh, and nothing happened.

"They're dead," said Lorcan.

"They're hers," said Emeric. "They wake for a caster pouring into them with her own hands. Nothing in the hearts feeds them. If they didn't wake for you, you haven't the gift, and for you they're stone."

Rowena looked down at her own forearm. She did not put her hand to it. Not yet, and not in front of the hall.

It was Hesta who said it, at last, and she said it to Lorcan in a low voice, the way two people in a trade talk about a thing in front of a customer.

"We'd want to open it."

"We would."

"And then we'd not know how to put it back."

"No," said Lorcan. "And if we did, and had it on the bench a year, I don't think we could make another. I don't think we'd know where to start." He looked at the breastplate. "I don't think anybody in the world could start."

Hesta was looking at the blue panes again, and Emeric could see her counting. She was counting the stone. She did it by eye, the way Saban priced a cargo from the quay, from the size of every pane and what must be behind it, and he watched her arrive at a figure, and he watched the figure arrive at her.

"There's more stone in that," Hesta said, "than our shop turns in five years."

Then she stopped, and went quiet, because she had got as far as the hearts, and had not counted them yet, and had just understood that she was going to have to.

"Nobody," said Rowena, "takes a knife to my suit."

She said it to her armourers, not to him, and they both looked at her and nodded at once, the way people nod who were going to say it themselves.

It was the leash. He had known it would be there from the table in Kaldo, when he gave Ferra the reasons in the order he had them, and he had meant to say it himself before the night was out. He did not need to. She had heard it from her own people, in her own hall, in their own words, before he said anything at all.

Hesta turned to the cloak, which Osric had laid out on the floor at its full length, and crouched beside it.

"What may I put on this?" she said.

"Nothing heavier than the cloth it's sewn to," said Emeric.

She looked up at him.

"It's not a cloak," he said. "Not only a cloak. I'll show her in the morning. But nothing heavier than the cloth. Silk thread. Down. Seed pearls, if you must. No silver wire, and no plate on it anywhere." He crouched beside her and showed her with his finger, the path down the inside of the cloak from the clasp to the breast, and the catch behind the clasp where the bellows sat, and the line of the pack down the back of the suit where the cloak lay over it. "Nothing over that. Nothing over that. Nothing over that. If you sew across any of those, you've killed her."

Hesta looked at the three places for a long moment, and he watched her fix them the way Lorcan had fixed the missing joins.

"And the rest of it?" she said.

"The rest of the cloak's yours, as long as it's light. And anything you put on the harness that isn't the cloak is yours. Skirts at the back. Whatever you like at the helm. Tie it on." He stood up. "I built it bare so you could."

Lorcan had been listening.

"You'll not want holes in it," he said. It was not a question.

"I'd not drill a plate on this," said Hesta, before Emeric could answer, "if you paid me the stone. I don't know what's under it." She stood up. "It'll all be laced. Laced and tied, every piece of it. We'll dress it tonight."

Rowena had been standing in the middle of her hall in the suit through all of it, with her arms at her sides, not moving, letting her armourers crawl round her on the floor. Now she looked at Emeric.

"You said you'd show me in the morning," she said. "Show me what?"

So he told her what it was, and what it cost, in the order he had given it to Ferra.

"It flies," he said.

The hall went very quiet. Rowena did not move.

"The story off the coasters," Emeric said. "The black ship that went up off the sea. Your boatmen told it to each other on the first day, when we came in, and one of them stood up in his boat to say he believed it." He kept his eyes on hers. "It's true. And that is the same work, made small, for one woman. You can fly it. The levers are the knight's levers, and the second ring's yours."

She did not say anything.

"Nobody can make another," he said. "Not your people, not the Crown's. Nobody but Kaldo. And if a heart in it ever fails, it comes back to Kaldo to be mended, or Kaldo comes to it. There's nobody else in the world who can."

Rowena looked at him for a long time, standing in the bright steel in her hot hall, with the lamps on the blue glass.

He watched her understand it, all of it, in the order it was: that she had been handed a gift, and a secret, and something shaped exactly like a leash, and that the man who had handed it to her had told her so to her face, in front of her own people, before she had asked.

"Thank you," said Rowena.

She took it anyway. He had known she would. She was not a woman who refused a thing because it came with a string; she was a woman who wanted to see the string, and he had shown it to her, and she had looked at it, and put her hand on it, and kept it.

Lorcan and Hesta took the suit off her an hour later, piece by piece, and carried it away to the shops behind the hall between them, with the cloak over Hesta's arm like a flag somebody had been asked to keep dry. They were talking in low voices before they were out of the door. Emeric heard the word *pearls*, and the word *never*, and then the door shut.

It had been bright and bare when it came out of the forge, with no lions on it and no device and nothing painted anywhere. He had built it that way on purpose, the night the forge took the pattern, for this.

A knight dressed her own.
