Chapter 125 - Commencement

They told him about the stoker in the gate passage, on the evening he came home, before he was off his horse.

Bram told him. Bram was standing in the passage behind Vantic when she went back to her book, and Emeric had not seen him there, and then he did, and saw his face, and got down.

It had been the twenty-fourth of July. Two days before the hearing. The mill engine, the first one, the big slow beam engine that drove the dynamo and had run every night for two years: its boiler had opened a seam. Not burst. Bram was very careful to say that it had not burst. A seam along the bottom of the drum, low down, near the firebox end, where the plates were riveted to each other, had given along a hand's length of its rivets, all at once, in the middle of the night, with the engine under load and the lamps on the wall; and the steam and the boiling water had come out of the gap like a fist.

The stoker had been in front of the firebox. Wick, a dock quarter man, forty, who had come to Haddon in the spring because the oven shed paid better than the eel boats. He had lived until the morning. Imani had been with him.

"I was there," Bram said. "I was asleep on the sacks in the engine house, because she'd been knocking." He stood in the gate passage with his hands at his sides, quite still. "I heard it go. I've never heard a sound like it. And I couldn't get to him for the steam." He stopped. "Haddon got to him. Haddon went in through it with a wet sack over his head and brought him out. Haddon's hands are still bound."

Emeric stood in the gate passage and did not say anything. There was nothing to say that would not be about himself, and he knew it, and did not say it.

"I went in," Bram said. "After."

Emeric looked at him.

"The next night. With the grey stone you gave me at the reading. Alone." Bram's jaw was set. "You weren't here. Nobody was here who could take me. And I wanted to know where she'd gone, and why, before I let another man stand in front of that firebox." He looked at the stones of the gate passage. "So I went in. On my own. It took me most of the night to get through the door. Then I was in the room, and I put the boiler up, the whole drum, from what I'd seen of it every day for two years. And I walked the seam. Inside the plate. Rivet by rivet."

"You went in alone."

"Somebody had to." Bram did not look up. "You said nothing in Kaldo should wake one man."



He showed Emeric the drawing on the Monday, in the lean-to, on the big slate.

It was the drum of the mill engine's boiler, in Bram's square chalk, opened out flat like the peel of an orange, every plate and every row of rivets. And along the bottom of it, near the firebox end, a line of rivets drawn in red, nine of them; Bram had found red chalk somewhere and used it for nothing else. And beside the line, in Bram's slow careful hand, notes. The rivets had been driven too cold on that seam, two years ago, in the bay, by a heater boy who had not yet learned the colour. They had never closed the holes fully. Every day for two years the drum had heated up and swelled and cooled and shrunk, and every day the rivets on that seam had worked in their holes a hair, and the holes had worn, and the plate round them had cracked in fine hair cracks from hole to hole, like a line of perforations in a sheet of stamps, until the night the drum swelled one more time and the plate tore along the line.

"I couldn't see the cracks," Bram said. "Not out here. I'd looked at that seam every week. They were inside the plate. You can't see inside a plate." He put his chalk down. "In there you can."

Emeric looked at the drawing a long while.

It was right. All of it. He had not been in the room with Bram. He had not walked the seam. Bram had found it alone, in the room, in a night, the first of the six to go in by himself with nobody to take him: a smith who had never read a word about fatigue or hoop stress or the working of rivets in a hot drum, who did not have any of the words, and had found the thing that the words were for, by walking along it inside the iron with his hand on it.

"It's right," Emeric said.

"I know it's right." Bram did not look pleased. He looked tired. "I want to know why it was that seam. Why there. Not the top. Not the ends. The bottom, by the fire, along its length."

So Emeric told him. Once, plainly, at the slate.

A pipe with steam in it is pushed on from inside, all over, equally. And the wall of the pipe has to hold that push. But the wider the pipe, the more push there is on each hand's length of its wall, because there is more inside pushing against the same amount of iron. Double the width, and the wall carries double the load. A seam along the length of a drum carries the whole of it; a seam round the drum's end carries half. So the long seams, along the drum, are the ones that go. And the bottom, by the fire, is the hottest, and swells most, and works most.

"So a big drum is worse," said Bram slowly.

"A big drum full of steam at pressure is a bomb," said Emeric. "The bigger it is, the more it wants to tear itself along its length. And when it goes, everything in it comes out at once." He drew a circle on the slate, and beside it a much smaller one. "A thin tube at the same pressure isn't. The wall of a thin tube carries almost nothing. You could hold steam at the same pressure in a tube the width of your thumb with a wall a tenth as thick. And if one does go, you lose one tube's worth of steam, not a drum's."

Bram looked at the two circles without speaking.

"Then why," he said, "in the name of every saint Doran's got, did you let me build a drum?"

"Because two years ago you couldn't have made a hundred thin tubes," said Emeric. "And we needed an engine."

Bram looked at him. Then he picked up the chalk and wiped the big circle off the slate with the side of his fist, and stood looking at the little one.

"She goes back on at half pressure," he said. "Tomorrow. The old drum. With that seam cut out and a new plate in, and every rivet on her drilled out and driven again hot, by me, not a boy. She'll pull half what she pulled. The lamps can have the wheel back, when the river's up." He did not look round. "And then I'll build her a new boiler. Tubes." He put the chalk down. "And I'll draw it. Me and Tess. You'll keep your hands off it."

"Yes," said Emeric.

He went out of the lean-to. Haddon was in the yard, by the coke heap, with both his hands still bound to the wrist in Imani's linen, directing two boys with a barrow by pointing with his elbow. He looked up when Emeric came out, and nodded, and went back to the barrow.

Emeric went to him, and stood beside him, and did not know what to say, and said nothing, and Haddon let him stand there until the barrow was done.

"He'd a wife," said Haddon at last. "In the dock quarter. And a girl." He did not look at Emeric. "Ferra's paying her his wage. Every week. Same as he was getting. I asked her how long." He shifted his bound hands. "She said she'd not decided to stop."



The commencement was on the last Friday of September, four years to the week since a room at the back of the old exchange had first opened its door at seven in the morning to children who had been fed at it.

It had been Sela's idea, in the end, though she would have said it was Ferra's, and Ferra would have said it was in the book. Four years. The ones who had come through the door that first morning as ten and twelve were fourteen and sixteen now. Old enough to be apprenticed. Old enough, in any town in the kingdom, to have been working for three years already. And Sela had stood in the long room in August with the school roll Ferra had made with her in April open in her hands and said that she was not going to have the first of them simply stop coming one Monday, the way children stopped coming to every school she had ever heard of, without anybody saying they had finished.

So they finished.

They did it in the square, because the long room would not hold everybody, and everybody came. The whole school, every grade, fifty-four of them now on the benches Bram's boys had carried out, in rows, in their best, scrubbed to the ears. Their mothers and fathers behind them standing. The lower ward, the dock quarter, Farris and the farm crews in from the fields, the soap yard, the works, the garrison in a line down the side of the square in their black crestless plate because Rylan had said they should. And at the front, on a long bench under the exchange's windows, facing the rows, the teachers: everybody in Kaldo who had stood in front of Sela's benches one morning a week for four years and taught them something they knew.

Imani. Ferra. Bram, in a clean shirt, very uncomfortable. Ward. Abloh. Armand in his chair, carried down by Arthur, with the blanket over his knees despite the sun. Saban, who had taught figures on the rare mornings he was in Kaldo and told the children stories about the coast on the mornings he was supposed to be teaching figures. Emeric, at the end.

And beside Bram, in her chair, Tess.

She had been one of the first. She had come through the door that first morning at fifteen with a burn scar on her thumb, already Bram's, and sat at the back, and learned her letters at sixteen faster than anybody in the room and her figures faster still, and after the dynamo house she had come back to the school in her chair and sat at the front of it on Thursday mornings and taught the oldest of them what a current was. She sat on the teachers' bench now with her hands in her lap and her face very still, and Emeric saw her look out over the rows of benches, at the children, at the first ones on the front bench who had been her classmates once, and look away, and look back.

Sela read the names.

She stood in front of the teachers' bench, in her dark school dress, with the roll, and read them. Not all fifty-four. The oldest. The first. Nine of them, the ones who had come through the door in the first autumn and stayed. She read each name in the voice she used for the roll every morning, and each one stood up from the front bench and came forward and stood in front of the teachers.

And chose.

It had been Ferra's idea, that part. There was no college in Kaldo; there was not a college in the south. So each of the six who led a part of the barony, and Abloh, who led the craft, would take on apprentices: paid, at an apprentice's wage, from the day they were chosen, and taught, and put to work, and if they were good at it, kept. Medicine, under Imani. Accounts and records, under Ferra. Works, under Bram, with Tess's electrical line under him. Trade, under Saban, which everyone in Kaldo by now understood meant something more than trade, and which Saban had told Sela he would take quiet ones for, who were good at noticing things. Craft, under Abloh: cloth, soap, the dyehouse. Knighthood, under Ward.

Not the rite. Ward had said so to the table in August, before Emeric could, and Emeric had been glad he did not have to. Every knight in the kingdom was made in a stone room in the capital by four mages forcing mana into nineteen young men on a floor, and one in ten of them got up. Emeric had stood against the wall of that room in the winter after his first summons with a ladle in his hand and watched it. Rylan had stood on that floor, and got up, and had told the yard fire in April what it had been like. Nobody from Kaldo was going to lie on that floor. Ward's apprentices would learn everything a knight learned that was not the rite: the blade, the drill, Rylan's program, the three books, the horse, the wall. And if a way were ever found to make a knight that did not kill nine men in ten to do it, they would have the first choice of it.

And stones, under Armand. Etching and theory. For those who had the faculty, which was very few, and which Armand had tested every child in Sela's room for in the spring, one by one, with his hand on their wrists and a dull lamp stone in their fists, and found in four.

The first to be called was a girl from the north well, fifteen, with her hair in two plaits, who had been the first child through the door on the first morning because she had been sitting on the step since dawn. She came forward. She stood in front of the teachers' bench with her hands folded and looked along it, at all of them, and her eyes went past Ferra and past Bram and stopped on Imani.

"Medicine," she said.

Imani stood up. She came forward from the bench, and stood in front of the girl, and looked at her, all of her, the way she looked at everything, and then held out her hand, and the girl took it.

The square cheered. It cheered for every one of them after that. It cheered when a big slow boy from the dock quarter went to Bram, and Bram shook his hand so hard the boy's eyes watered. It cheered when Arthur, who was nearly sixteen and very large and knew everything, walked straight past the bench and stood in front of Ward and said knighthood before Sela had finished reading his name, and Sela said, I haven't called you yet, and Arthur said, I know, and the square laughed. It cheered loudest of all for a thin little girl from Foulard Street with ink on her fingers who stood in front of the bench a good while, looking from Ferra to Tess and back, and then went to Tess, and said electrical, quite clearly, and Tess sat in her chair and looked at the girl standing in front of her and said nothing, so long that Bram leaned over and touched her shoulder; and then she held out her hand.

Ferra took one, a boy who had never once in four years got a sum wrong and never once spoken in class. Saban took a quiet one. Armand took the only one of his four with the faculty who was old enough, and said, from his chair, that she was too young and he was too old and they would both have to make the best of it.



Afterward, when the square was full of families and the benches were being carried back and Maren's barrows were going round with bread and the first of the season's apples, Imani found him by the exchange door.

She had the girl from the north well with her, at her elbow, already, as if she had been there for years. Imani sent her to the ward with a basket, and the girl went at a run, and Imani watched her go. Then she turned to Emeric and held out her hand, palm up.

"The kit," she said.

He had it. He had carried it in his coat since July, because she had told him to bring it back, and he had not been able to make himself send it up the hill by a boy. He gave it to her. She untied the tape and unrolled it, there by the exchange door, with the square going on round them, and looked at what was in it, all of it, the way she looked at a ward in the morning.

"Linen," she said. "Half gone. The spirit, half." She looked at the needles in their paper, all there. The silk, all there. "You didn't stitch it."

"Ward said it didn't need stitching."

"Ward isn't a healer." She rolled the kit up again and tied it and put it under her arm. Then she held out her hand again, palm up. "Show me."

He looked at her.

"You said you'd tell me," said Imani. "What you used out of it, and on whom, and why. Linen and spirit. On whom?"

He rolled up his left sleeve.

The cut had healed. It ran from the wrist half to the elbow along the inside of his forearm, a long straight pink line, raised a little, puckered where it had pulled in the first days on the road before the binding settled, already fading at the ends. Beside the old shiny burn scars from the bridge, it looked new and clean and very straight.

Imani took his wrist.

She did it the way she had done it in December, at the desk, with the lamp: her fingers round the wrist, her thumbs on either side of the cut, and turned his arm over in the light. She ran one thumb along the line of it, slowly, from the wrist toward the elbow, pressing, the way she pressed along a healed bone to feel whether it had knit true. He felt her thumb go all the way along. He felt her feel the place where it had pulled.

"A knife," she said.

"Yes."

"Low. Under the arm. A man who knew how to hold one." She did not look up from it. "And you turned. Or you'd have it here." She touched the inside of his elbow, once, with her fingertip, where the big vessel ran under the skin. "Which is why it's on the outside of the line and not the inside."

"Ward's drill."

"Ward's drill," Imani agreed. She went on holding his wrist. "It pulled. Here. Because you rode on it the first three days and you didn't keep it still, and Ward bound it too tight at the start, because Ward binds everything as if it were a saddle girth." Her thumb was still on the scar. "It'll fade. It'll be white in a year. You'll be able to see it in a good light." She was quiet. "Was it the night before the court?"

"Yes."

"Ferra said there were five. Ward killed two. Rylan held one." She looked up at him then. "Ferra didn't say what you did."

"Fire."

"Fire," said Imani. She looked at him for a long breath, with his wrist in her hands and the square loud round them. And then she let go of his wrist, and rolled his sleeve back down herself, over the scar, carefully, and buttoned the cuff, which he could have done, and smoothed the sleeve flat along his forearm with the palm of her hand, once, from the elbow to the wrist.

"Next time stitch it," she said. "Or bring it to me before it's three weeks old."

She turned to go, with the kit under her arm, toward the ward and the girl from the north well. Then she turned back.

"Thank you," said Imani, "for bringing it back."

She meant the kit. She also, he thought, watching her go up the high street at her own pace through the families and the barrows and the children running between them with apples, did not entirely mean the kit.



Ottoline Fesk arrived on the Monday, on the coast carrier's cart, with the brass box on her knees and four crates of clocks that had not sold.

Ferra met her in the counting room, the long room on the ground floor of the keep under her own, where the barony's three clerks sat at a table under the windows with the year's books and Ferra's new apprentice sat at the end of it learning to rule a column. Ottoline came in and put the brass box down on the end of the table, in the middle of a ledger, without asking whose ledger it was.

Ferra looked at the box. Then at Ottoline.

"That's the accounts for Milford's dairy," Ferra said.

"Is it," said Ottoline Fesk, and did not move the box. She looked round the counting room, at the clerks and the apprentices and the shelves of books, the way she had looked round her own shop on Wayford square. "Your prince says you add eight figures before breakfast."

"Twelve," said Ferra.

"With a pen."

"With a pen."

"And no mistakes."

"One," said Ferra. "In four years. In the second winter. I found it myself the next morning."

Ottoline looked at her over the top of her spectacles. Then she turned the eight little wheels on the top of the box, quickly, with one finger, to a number, and the second set under the lid to another, and put her hand on the crank.

"Add," she said. "Now. You, with your pen. Me, with this."

Ferra looked at the two numbers. She did not pick up her pen.

"Nine thousand, one hundred and four," she said.

Ottoline turned the crank. The box clicked and rippled. The wheels stopped.

Nine thousand, one hundred and four.

The two of them looked at the box, and then at each other, across the end of the counting room table, over the Milford dairy's accounts, and the clerks at the table had stopped writing, and the apprentice had stopped ruling, and nobody in the room breathed.

"I'll want my own table," said Ottoline Fesk.

"You'll have the end of this one," said Ferra.

Emeric, in the doorway, went away quietly before either of them saw him, and up the stair, and did not come back down for a week.
