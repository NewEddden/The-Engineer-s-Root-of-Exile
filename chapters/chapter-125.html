Chapter 125 - Whatever Else You Brought

He came up from the ship at first light with three books under his arm, and Rowena was in the long room.

She was standing at the window in the blue coat, with the tall knight at the door and nobody sent ahead to say she was coming. Ferra was at the end of the table with the lamp and the minute book, sitting very straight, in the way of somebody who has been sitting very straight for some time. Ward was on the stair. Osric had stopped halfway through a slice of bread.

Rowena turned from the window. She looked at the books under his arm and did not ask what they were.

"Before your table," she said. "On the yard."

He put the books down.

"I tied your hands," she said. "No plate and no casting. It was my rule, and I'd make it again for the reason I made it. I wanted to see what you were with a sword and nothing else." She looked at him down the length of the room, past the lamp and Ferra's book. "Now I've seen it. I'd like to see the rest."

"The rest."

"When you were seven the tower had you down as the best runesmith Randillia would ever raise. It was said at dinner. Everybody heard it." She said it flatly, the way she had told him about her face. "When you were eight you were drunk at dinner. You stayed drunk at dinner until your father sent you south, and I sat at that table and watched that too." She let it sit. "Then you brought a ship into my harbour with no smoke and no sail, and put something over my whole quay that I felt through my plate. I'd like to know which of those boys came to Emberfell, before I sit down across a table from him."

Nobody in the room moved. Ferra had become very interested in the grain of the wood.

"Plate," said Rowena. "Casting. Whatever else you brought. Blunt steel, the same as before, and the same end: first to a touch that would have finished it. And I don't only mean steel."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no." She did not make it sound like a threat, and it was not one. "You come to my table, and I wonder."

He looked at her at the window, and at the tall knight at the door whose face was doing its best to be a wall, and he thought of her standing over the damp by the water butt with her blade lowered, not able to say how she had been moved. She had put it wherever she put things she meant to come back to. She had come back to it.

"Yes," he said.

She nodded once, as if he had told her the weight of a cargo, and went.

Ward came down off the stair when the door had shut behind her.

"Her knights'll be on that wall inside the hour," he said. "More of them than the first morning. Every one of them looking at the plate."

"I know."

"We came ashore in hoods."

"We came ashore in hoods so nobody on the quay would look up." Emeric turned the top book over on the table. "Nobody's looking up this morning. I'm not leaving the ground. What they'll see is black plate on a yard, and a man in it moving better than a man in plate should, and that's a story about plate." He set it down square to the edge of the table. "Let them go home and argue about the plate. Every hour they spend on the plate is an hour they don't spend on anything else."

Ward considered that for a long time.

"And her?"

"She won't argue about the plate," Emeric said. "She'll know better by the second exchange."

Ward said he would fetch Orrin, and went. Osric put down his bread and went up the stair to the small room at the top of the house, where the suit stood in its corner under its cloak, because Osric had never once in his life missed a chance to handle somebody else's armour.

Ferra had not said a word. She closed the minute book on the page she had begun the morning before, and put it under her arm, and looked at him once across the lamp, and went up to dress.

Emeric sat on at the table.

He was not going to win. He had decided it before she reached the bottom of the stair, and he sat with it now to see whether it would stand, and it stood. It was not modesty. He did not know whether he could beat her with everything, and he thought it more likely than not that he could not. But if he could, he would not, and the reason was the same reason that had kept a drunk at his father's dinner table for eight years.

A queen of Randillia, the first of the kingdom's women knights and near enough the first of anybody, put down on her own yard in front of her own people by Harrold's third son. That was not a story that stayed on a yard. It would be in the fish market at the lower water inside a fortnight, and on the coast road inside a month, and in the capital after that, in rooms where men had worked a long time to be sure they need never think of him as anything but a drunk in a barony at the bottom of the map. His eldest brother had a chair he wanted. A third son who could beat Rowena of Emberfell was a man with a claim on nothing and a use to everybody. Some would want to use him and some would want him gone, and from Kaldo he had no way of telling which was which.

So he would lose. But he would not throw it. She would know a thrown bout inside three strokes and never forgive it, and she would be right. He would fight every exchange as hard as he could fight it and take nothing that ended it. If she beat him, she would have to beat him.

Then he went up to his room, and sent Osric down for a breakfast he did not want, and shut the door, and tried something he had never tried.

He had been in the room on his feet perhaps twice in ten years, both times by accident, and both times he had sat down at once. It was a place a man went sitting or lying, with his hands still and somebody at the door. Everything about it assumed a body left somewhere safe. Time ran long in there, an hour inside for a minute or so outside, badly and never the same way twice, and that was the whole of its use to him: a thousand drills in an afternoon nobody noticed him having.

He stood in the middle of the floor and opened it, and did not go in.

It was wider than the crack and short of the door. The room came up behind his eyes the way a lit room shows in a dark window, laid over the real one, and he could see both. His own shelves, faint. Through them, the harbour house and the mountain going grey in the window. He let the sense out, low, only as far as the walls, and the room took what the sense gave it and built it: the bed, the chair, the chest under the window, grey and without colour and exactly the right size, and in the corner the shape of the suit under its cloak, where it had stood since the night of the dinner.

He walked to the window. In the room the chest went past him slower than it should have, and he counted.

The rate had dropped the moment he stood. It dropped again with every step, and again when he lifted a hand and held a little turning air in his palm. It was nothing like an hour to a minute. It was something like ten of his breaths in the room for one on the floor, and it would not hold even that. It wandered. Once it ran long for no reason he could find. Once it closed so nearly shut that the two rooms ran together and he lost which floor his feet were on and had to put a hand to the wall.

He sat on the bed. The room steadied at once and ran long and easy, the way it always had, and he closed it.

Ten for one, and wandering, and worse for every thing he held at the same time. He sat with that and did the sum he always did, and it came out the same however he did it. It was not much. It was more than anybody else on that yard would have.

He had slept, at least. He had slept the whole night through in his clothes, for the first time since they tied up, because she had told him to, and he found he was glad of it now.

Orrin filled the hearts on the end of the bed, with the breastplate across his knees and his palms flat on the dark pane, complaining about the stair, and about the bed, which was too low for a man his age, and that he had been promised the cushion. The band had filled the suit on the way down the coast, and it had stood three days in port since. Orrin topped the front heart and then the back, and sat with his eyes shut, and said it would do. From Orrin that meant full.

Ward put him into it.

The thin breastplate. The back, with the yoke and the rear heart in the yoke, and the lattice of the pack down his spine. The greaves and the heavy nodes at the heels. The shoulders with their lions. The fur at his throat, which he had never needed and was least glad of here, where the morning was already warm. Ward did every buckle himself and did not let Osric near them.

Then the cape. Ward hooked it at its two catches, looked at it, and unhooked it again.

"Not on a yard," Emeric said.

"No." A cape on a yard was a sail tied to a man's back, with a toggle behind it that a blade could strike. Abloh had made it to come off at the shoulders, and it came off, and Ward folded it over his arm, battens and chute and all, and carried it down the slope himself.

Emeric took the silver star from the coat in his room and opened the catch at its back, and the grey stone came out into his palm. He opened the small iron door in his breast and set it in its seat, and felt the deciding stone come up against it inside the iron like a hand taking a hand.

The helm went on last.

There were more of them than the first morning. They were along the racks and on the steps and three deep on the wall, and some of them had brought bread, and when he came through the gate in black plate with the winged helm on, the talk did not drop. It stopped.

He did not blame them. He had never seen himself whole in it outside the pattern store's glass. Black steel with nothing on it to say it had ever been the Crown's, and the lions' heads snarling at both shoulders, and the fur, and the dark round pane at his breast, and wings on his head. It was a great deal of man to walk onto somebody else's yard.

Kaldo's people were where they had been the first time. Saban at the wall. Osric beside him, already talking to the knight at the top of the steps, who had a slate and was not writing on it. Imani with her roll across her knees, untied. Ferra with her book shut. Vantic a little apart, as she always was, watching the gate and not the yard. Ward walked beside him to the edge of the flags with the cape over his arm and no further.

Rowena was in the middle of the yard in the capital's armour.

Bright steel, engraved from gorget to knee with the fine running pattern he remembered from the quay, and no blue cloak this morning, and a plain open-faced helm with the steel rings of her hair hanging below it down her back. Two blunt blades, one long and one short, one in each hand. On the first morning she had fought him with one. He saw the second and knew what it meant, and this time he minded a little.

The tall knight brought him a blade from the rack, hilt first. He took it in his right hand. His left he kept open.

Rowena was looking at his feet. Then at the lions.

"Your guard told my knights you weren't a knight," she said.

"I'm not."

"That's knight's plate."

"It's thin."

Her eyes went to the breastplate and stayed there a moment, reading the steel the way she read a man's weight.

"Then don't get hit," she said.

The knight on the steps lifted his hand.

Emeric opened the sense before it fell.

There was no rule this morning to keep it off her, and he did not keep it off her. He let it out low along the flags and down into their bedding and up over the whole of the yard, the racks and the butt and the steps and the woman in the middle of it, and felt it go over her, and felt her feel it. Her head came up the width of a hair. That was all. She did not step back. She had been waiting for it.

The room came up behind his eyes, and the yard came up in it, grey.

The hand fell.

She did not come at him.

She put her heel down, once, on the loose flag by the water butt, and Emeric felt it through the sense before he heard it. The sand under the flag drew tight and hard, all at once, the way mortar goes off in a week, and the flag that had rocked toward the butt under a hundred years of heels did not rock any more. In the same breath the damp round the butt's foot went up as a thin sheet of steam and was gone, and the stone under it was dry and pale and hot.

She had spent the two days learning her own yard.

Only the fall was left. Nobody could lift a yard half an inch, and she did not try. She walked, not quickly, to the high side of it, and turned there, and looked at him from uphill.

Then she came.

She had been fast in a padded jacket. In plate, with her magic in her, she was something else. He could see it in the sense before he saw it with his eyes: her own mana running through her like heat through iron, packed dense into the long muscles of her legs and back, so that the whole of her read in the medium like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool. She crossed half the yard in the time it took the knight's hand to come to rest at his side, and the long blade came at his head and the short one under it at his ribs, the same long unbroken movement as the first morning with twice the steel in it.

He told the suit *lighter* and pushed.

The weight went out of him. The stones at the backs of his calves breathed, and he went left off his toes, three yards across the flags in one stride with no floor under him that he could feel, and both blades went through the place where he had been. He came down light and skidded, because a light man has nothing to hold a floor with, and told the heels *heavy*. They bit. Stone rang under the steel. He was standing.

The wall made a noise he would remember.

She was already turning. She had not overcommitted. She had struck at where a man in plate would be, and when he was not there she had known it before the blades arrived, and she came round out of the stroke with her weight still under her and looked at him across three yards of flags.

Then she dropped the short blade's point toward the stone, and fire came off it.

It was not a gout. It was a sheet, knee-high and flat, running out along the flags toward his legs with the speed of water off a tipped bucket, orange at the root and white where it licked the stone. The heat of it reached him through the greaves before the fire did.

He did not meet it. He took the air in front of it away.

It was a small thing. Air moved; he had moved it all his life. He drew it back out of the fire's path in a thin sleeve a yard deep, pulled it aside and let it go behind him, so that the sheet of fire ran forward into nothing to burn. It died a foot from his greaves. It fell over on the flags like a wave going down on sand and was gone, and left a line of scorched stone and a smell.

Along the wall nobody said anything at all.

Rowena looked at the line of scorch, and then at him, and he saw her decide something. She stamped.

The flags in front of him stood up. Four of them, in a line across his path, lifted out of their beds on edge like the teeth of a comb, sand pouring off their undersides, and she came in behind them, going round the end of the line to cut him off from the rest of the yard.

He went over them.

Lighter, and the calves, and his own legs under it, and he went up. Not far. The height of a tall man and half again, which was higher than any man in plate had any business going, in a flat arc over the standing flags, and the whole yard turned under him in the room behind his eyes, grey, slow, her shape coming round the end of the line exactly where he had known it would.

She was there when he landed. She had read the arc. An arc was a thing a fighter could read: it went up and it came down where it had always meant to come down, and she had put herself on the end of it.

The long blade came at him as his boots touched.

He met it, and at the instant the steel met he told the suit *heavy*, all of it, and the back stones pushed.

The blow went into her guard with the whole weight of the plate and more behind it, and the stones driving it, and his arm and his hips last and least. It was not a strong man's blow. It was a falling wall's. Her guard held, because it was her guard, but it held a yard further back than she had set it, and her heels went scraping across the flags, and the air from his vents hit the sand in the joints and threw it up round both of them in a ring of dust that rose to the height of a man's chest and hung there in the still morning.

Through the dust he saw her face inside the open helm. It had not been given permission to do what it did.

The wall was on its feet.

Ferra had come to watch because the others had. She stayed because she could not have left.

She did not know anything about fighting. She knew what the first morning had looked like from here: a thing everybody on the wall could read, a better fighter wearing down a lesser one, and money changing hands on how long it would take. She had understood it the way she understood a harbour account. She did not understand this.

The knights around her did not either. That was the part she found she could not stop listening to.

"He lost," said a young man two places down, to nobody. "He *lost*. Two mornings ago. She took his sword off him."

"On steel."

"So he threw it?"

"Nobody throws against her. She'd know."

"Then it's the plate."

"Plate doesn't *do* that." That was a woman, older, a knight with grey in her tail and a burn scar across the back of one hand. She had not taken her eyes off the dust. "Watch his feet. He lands like a cat and hits like a hammer. Plate does one or the other. I've worn plate for thirty years. It doesn't do both."

"Then what is it?"

The older woman did not answer.

At the top of the steps the knight with the slate had given up on the slate. Osric had leaned over to say something to him and been told, curtly, that the book was shut, and Osric had said *pity* in a voice that did not sound as though he thought it was a pity at all.

Ferra looked along the wall at her own people. Saban was watching the knights, not the yard, and his lips were moving slightly; she knew he was counting how many of them there were and what they would say and to whom. Imani had her roll open across her knees now, not unrolled, only open, one hand flat on it. Vantic had turned from the gate at last and was watching the yard, and her book was open too, and her pen was over it, and it had not moved.

Ward stood at the edge of the flags with the cape folded over his arm and did not move at all.

Out on the yard the dust came down and Emeric was not where it had been.

He opened the room properly after the dust.

He had held it at the edge until then, a pane over the yard, and it had been enough to find the arc. Now he let it come up behind his eyes as far as it would come without taking his feet, and the yard built itself in there out of everything the sense gave it: the flags and their beds, the four standing on edge, the butt, the steps, the drain in the far corner, and her.

Her above all. Grey, and exact, rebuilt every instant out of the medium moving round her. Every weight she set down. The long blade drawn back and the short one low. The packed heat of her in her legs. He could walk round her in there while her blade was in the air. He could see the back of her, which his eyes could not. And he could see, a little way ahead of her, not a picture but a lean in everything, where all of her was about to go.

She came again with both blades, high and low and high, the long and the short trading places behind each other so that a man watching the long blade would never see the short one until it was in him.

In the room he saw both. He had time, not much, ten breaths or near it, to look at the whole of it laid out in grey and choose.

Then he did the other thing.

He had been doing it for ten years, alone, where nobody could see, because it was only mana, and mana was the thing he had always been able to hold finer than anybody: tighter, smaller, wasting nothing. A body was a thing that moved on orders. The orders came slowly, down long roads, and arrived at the muscles late and rough, and the muscles did what they could with them. He could write the orders himself. Not the whole body, and not for long. But he could put the order into the muscle in the room's time and not his own, and the body would do it before it knew it had been asked.

He wrote it: *parry high, turn, step through, parry low, cut.*

The body did it.

It did it too fast for itself, and he felt that, even as the steel rang: his arm going up and his wrist turning and his left foot going through, not quite his, driven. The long blade met his and went wide. The short blade came under and met his steel coming down to meet it, and went wide. And his own blunt edge came round in the gap, not hard, and touched her vambrace, and came away.

Not a killing touch. The knight on the steps did not call it. But she had felt it. She stepped back, and looked at her forearm.

Then the body paid.

It was not much the first time. A hot wire drawn down the inside of his right forearm, and his calves shaking inside the greaves as if he had run up a long stair. He breathed through it. The room thinned. He held it.

She said, across the yard, not loudly: "What was that?"

He did not answer. She had not expected him to.

She put the short blade down toward the flags again. Not fire this time.

Heat.

It came up out of the stone under his boots like a stove lid coming up to temperature, all at once, across the whole middle of the yard where he stood. The air over the flags shook. He felt it through the soles, and through the greaves, and then through the plate, black steel drinking it in the way black steel does. The fur at his throat was suddenly a punishment.

Inside the suit the water began to run.

Bram had built it for the Emberfell quay in the noon sun, in the pattern store in the first week of winter, with Imani and her sand-glass: water round through the tubes against his body and out to the plate across his back, where the air from the vents crossed it and took the heat out of it. It ran now. It did not make the stone any cooler. But it meant the heat went into the water and out of the water into the air behind him, and not into him, and he could stand on it.

He did not stand on it. That was what she wanted. She wanted him moving, off the hot stone and onto the stone she chose, and he let her think she had it, and went where she was driving him, toward the racks, and the room showed him what she meant to do when he got there.

She was coming round to cut him off from the cool stone at the rack end. Two of her strides and she would plant her right foot to turn, and he could see which flag she would plant it on, because everything in her was leaning at it: the third flag out from the rack, a good sound flag, bedded hard. She had not touched it this morning. She had had no reason to.

He did not touch the flag either.

He reached under it.

The bed was sand and old lime, dry and packed. He put a little of the water from the air into it, very little, and loosened it, the way a frost loosens the ground under a paving stone. Not the flag. Only the thing the flag rested on. He had put his hand on this yard's wall on the first afternoon and told himself he would never touch her ground, and he had meant it, and she had taken that rule away herself that morning, at the window of the long room. *Whatever else you brought.*

Her right foot came down on the third flag, and the flag went down under it the width of a finger at its near edge, and her weight went with it.

It was nothing. It was less than the damp by the butt on the first morning. And he was already moving before her boot touched, because he had written it in the room: *light, and the calves, and in.*

The body went. He crossed the yard to her in a stride and a half with the flag still settling, and she was a hand's breadth low and a hand's breadth late, recovering, and his blade came round at the open side of her helm where the steel stopped and her face began, and there was nothing between it and her.

That was one.

He turned it. He could not stop the blade; nobody could have stopped that blade. He turned his wrist and brought it down instead onto the steel of her left shoulder, and told the suit *heavy* as it landed.

She went down to one knee. The flags cracked under the knee. The dust went up round her in a ring and hung there, and in the room he saw her, grey and exact, kneeling with her head a hand's breadth from where his blade had been going, and knew she had seen it too.

She came up out of the dust already cutting, and he went back, and she did not follow at once.

"That one wasn't mine," she said.

"No."

She looked at the third flag, sunk at its near edge. Then at him. She did not say anything else. She did not need to. It was the same trick as the first morning, with the gloves off, and she had seen that it was, and she had seen what he had not done with it.

Then she stopped trusting her floor.

He watched her do it in the room, and did not understand it at first, and then did. Every step she took, the flag under her boot drew tight. She was putting her stone into the ground under each foot as it came down, the way she had fixed the loose flag in the first breath of the morning, one step at a time, bedding her own road across the yard as she walked it. It must have cost her. He could see it cost her, in the medium, a small steady drain out of all that packed heat. But nothing he did under her feet would take now. She was walking on ground she had made.

He tried something else, then, once, and learned from it.

He reached for her. Not for the ground. For the weight of her, the way he had reached for the weight of stones since the summer: *lighter.* A knight made suddenly light would lose her footing on any floor, however she had bedded it.

It did not take. It went into her and slid off, like water off oiled leather. She was full of her own mana to the skin, packed and moving, and there was no room in her for his. It would have been like trying to pour a cup into a full barrel. He took it back at once, and saw from the room that she had felt that too, a draught going over her and finding no door.

So it would be the ground, and the air, and the steel, and never her. That was fair. It was only fair.

She threw a flag at him.

She did not pick it up. She tore it out of its bed with her stone and flung it, the size of a table top, edge on, at the height of his chest, and it came across the yard faster than anything that heavy had a right to.

He made it heavier.

Weight he could do. Stone was not a knight. He told the flag *heavy*, all of it, the way he told his own heels, and it dropped out of the air halfway across the yard as if a string had been cut, and hit the ground in front of him on its edge and broke in two, and the pieces lay there on the flags between them.

Somebody on the wall laughed. It was not a laugh at anybody. It was the sound a person makes when there is nothing else in them to make.

She came through the broken stone at him, and he met her, and this time he did not get the room open in time.

It had been wandering all morning. It ran long when he did not need it and short when he did, and now, with the suit held and the sense spread across the whole yard and a cast still going cold in his left hand, it closed almost to nothing. Ten breaths became two. He saw her long blade come and he wrote the answer, and the answer arrived in his body a beat behind where he had written it.

Her short blade hit his left shoulder.

It hit the lion. He heard it crumple. The whole of his left side went numb from the neck to the elbow, and he went back three staggering paces with his feet not under him, and in the room he saw the long blade coming after the short one for his throat, and there was nothing in him to stop it.

He did not stop it. He went down. He let the suit go *lighter* and the calves push and his own knees fold, and he went under the long blade backward, low along the flags like a man slipping on ice, and came up two yards off with his left arm hanging and the taste of blood in his mouth. He did not know where it had come from. His nose, he thought. Or the room.

Along the wall someone had cried out, and stopped.

Rowena did not follow up. She stood where the long blade had finished, and looked at him, and he knew she could have come on. She was letting him get the arm back. That was worse than if she had come.

The arm came back slowly, in pins.

It was not like being watched.

Rowena had been watched her whole life. On yards, in halls, on the field in the war, at Harrold's table for years by people who wanted her to fail. Being watched was a pressure on the skin, and you learned to fight inside it the way you learned to fight in rain.

This was not on the skin. It was all round her, close, in every direction at once, the way water is all round a swimmer. And it was not still. She could feel it moving over her, and more than that, she could feel it taking her shape. She lifted the long blade, and the lift went out of her into it. She set her weight, and the setting went. It was like standing in a mould that was being poured around her, and then poured again, and again, over and over, fast as a pulse, every pour carrying away a copy of her. And somewhere at the end of it, the boy in black was holding every copy.

She did not know what it was. She knew what it was not. It was not a ward, and it was not a working of any kind she had ever had cast at her or seen cast, and she had seen a great many. It was not in the ground and it was not in the air. It was simply there, and it was his, and it knew where she would put her feet before she did.

She had fought men faster than he was. Not many, and none this morning, but some. She had fought men stronger. She had never fought anybody who was always already where she was about to be.

And he stopped. That was the thing she had found by the fourth time.

Before the worst of it, before each of the moves that came out of him too quick and too clean for the body that made them, he stopped. Half a heartbeat. Less. His feet went still and his blade went still and the whole of him went still like a man at a door with his ear to the wood. Then the thing came out of him, whatever it was, and it was always right.

She had it now. She put it with the rest.

He got the arm back, and she let him, and then she began to take him apart.

She did not fight him harder. She fought him later. She stopped deciding where she was going until she was already on the way, so that the lean the room showed him ahead of her was not a lean at all, only a woman standing balanced on both feet, going anywhere. She had been fighting on instinct since she was a girl and she could decide as late as anybody alive. The room gave him nothing to read.

And she started giving him things to read on purpose.

A step, clear as a signpost, toward the butt. He saw it, and went still, and the room took him. And she was not going to the butt. She had never been going to the butt. She was coming into the stillness, into the half-heartbeat where his feet stopped, with the long blade already moving, and he got out of it on the suit and the calves and nothing else, and felt her edge go past the side of his helm close enough to hear.

She had found it. He had not known there was anything to find.

He did not know how many he had left. Five, he thought, or six, since the first. The body was telling him in every language it had. Both calves were cramping in the greaves. The wire in his right forearm had become a rope. His left shoulder had come back and brought the lion's crumpled steel with it, grinding at the collar when he turned. The room was thin as paper and running wrong, and the sense across the yard had begun to flicker at the edges, the far wall going in and out like a lamp in a draught.

He was running too much at once. He had known he would be. He had worked out the sum an hour before, sitting on the bed. He had not known what it would feel like at the far end.

She threw fire. A great deal of it, all at once, off both blades, a wall of it across the yard coming at him from the high side of the fall with the heat of the mountain behind it.

He could not take the air out of that much. He took the water out of the butt instead.

It came up out of the butt in a rope as thick as his arm, the whole of the butt's water and the damp in the flags round it, and he flung it across the face of her fire, and the yard went white.

Steam. Hot and thick and blinding, from wall to wall, so dense that the knights on the steps vanished and then the steps did. It was hot enough to scald, and the suit's water ran and took the heat out of him, and she, in her open-faced helm, could not see her own hand.

He could. He could see everything.

He went round her in the white. The room showed him her, grey and exact in the middle of the steam, turning slowly, long blade out, listening. He came up behind her, and there was the back of her neck below the rim of the helm, bare, where the steel rings of her hair hung down over the gorget and did not quite cover the gap.

That was two.

He stood there with his blunt blade a foot from it for a count of one. Then he stepped away, three paces, to the side, and let the steam begin to clear.

She turned as it thinned, not toward where he was but toward where he had been, and stood looking at the empty flags there. Then she turned the rest of the way and found him with her eyes. She did not say anything. Her face inside the helm had gone perfectly flat.

He understood, looking at it, that it had been worse to leave her standing than it would have been to take her.

She came at him with everything, then. Fire off the short blade into the slits of his helm, so bright and close that his eyes were gone, white, nothing in them at all. It did not matter. He had not used his eyes since the first exchange. He met her long blade with his eyes full of light, and turned the short one, and stepped off the line, and heard the wall make a sound that was nearly fear.

The room was going.

He felt it go, not all at once. The rate came down and down until a breath in there was a breath out here, and then it was less, and then the room was only a faint grey drawing laid over the yard, and he was a man with a sore arm and cramping legs and a head full of light, standing in hot plate on her ground.

She gave him a step toward the rack.

He saw it. He went still. The room reached for the answer, and nothing came.

It was only half a heartbeat longer than the others. It was enough. Her long blade came into the stillness and took his, hard, at the hilt, and turned it, and his blunt steel went out of his hand and rang on the flags as it had rung two mornings before. And the short blade came up under the rim of his helm, into the gap at the front of his throat where the fur met the gorget, and rested there, lightly, cold, and stayed.

The knight on the steps called it.

The yard was silent. Then it was not. Then it was so loud that Emeric could not hear the drain.

Imani reached him before Ward did, which Ward allowed.

She had his helm off before he had finished sitting down on the edge of the water butt, and looked at his face, and then at the blood on his lip, and put two fingers under his nose and took them away and looked at them.

"From where?"

"I don't know."

"You do." She had his right gauntlet off by then and his forearm in both hands, running her thumbs down the inside of it from the wrist, and he made a noise he had not meant to make. She did not stop. "That's not a blade. Nothing touched that arm." She went down on her knees on the scorched flags and put her hands on his greaves, and felt the calves shaking through the steel, and looked up at him. "What did you do to your legs?"

He could tell her. She held a stone. She had been in the room. He said, low, "I drove them."

"With what?"

"With me."

Imani looked at him for a long moment, and he saw her put it together, the way she put together a wound from the shape of the thing that had made it.

"Then don't," she said. "Not till I've seen what it does to you over a week. Not once." She stood. "You'll be stiff as a gate by tonight and worse tomorrow. You'll sit at her table today like a man with nothing wrong with him, because I'll not have her people watch you limp to it. And then you'll let me look at that arm, properly, in a room with a lamp."

"Yes."

"Don't say yes to me the way you say yes to Ferra," said Imani, and went to get her roll.

Ward came then. He did not look at Emeric. He looked at the lion on the left shoulder, which was crumpled flat across the brow, one ear gone, and put his thumb on the dent, and then looked up at the wall, where a great many of Emberfell's knights were not leaving.

"You showed forty of her people what we can put on a man," Ward said.

"I showed them plate."

"You showed them a man in it." He took his thumb off the lion. "They'll go home and talk about the man."

"They'll say he lost," said Emeric.

Ward considered that, and did not argue with it, and began unbuckling the shoulder.

On the steps the knight with the slate was talking to Osric, and neither of them looked happy. Osric caught Emeric's eye across the yard, and held up his empty hands, and shook his head. Nobody had bet on anything. Nobody had known what to bet on.

Saban was not on the wall. He had gone the moment the knight on the steps called it, down the slope toward the quay, and Emeric knew why. The quay would have it inside the hour, and Saban wanted to hear which version.

Rowena came across the flags with her helm under her arm.

She had taken it off on the yard, which he had not seen a knight do in front of her own people before, and her face was wet with the steam and red with the heat, and she was breathing harder than she had breathed at the end of the first morning, a good deal harder, and no harder than that. She stood over him where he sat on the edge of the butt. Ward stepped back a pace without being asked.

"Your legs are a clerk's," she said.

He looked up at her.

"You fight with your arm. The plate puts weight behind your cuts because your hips don't. When you go light your feet can't hold the yard and you skid on every landing, and fix it with the heels, and I can hear it, every time, before I see it. You breathe from the top of your chest like a man running for a cart. Your wrist turns under a heavy cut because there's no forearm behind it, and you knew that on the first morning, and you didn't fix it in the two days since." She was not angry. She was listing it, the way Ferra listed what a thing cost. "You don't attack. Not once, in two mornings, have you made me answer you. You wait for me, and you're very good at waiting. And you stop."

"I stop."

"Before you do the thing. Whatever it is. You go still, like a man at a door. Half a heartbeat." She held up her thumb and forefinger with no space between them. "I had it by the fourth. Anybody who fights you twice will have it by the fourth."

He said nothing. It was all true, and he had known some of it and not the rest.

"The gap's not far," Rowena said. "Two years of mornings with somebody who'll hurt you, and eat like you mean it. Your guard would do. He's been too careful with you." She did not look at Ward. Ward did not look at her. "But what you've got that I haven't is carrying you. The first morning showed me exactly how much of you there is without it."

Then she crouched, in her plate, so that her face was level with his, and spoke so that nobody but Ward could have heard.

"It's more than the armour."

"Yes."

"You had me twice. By the rack. And in the steam."

He did not deny it. She would have known.

"Why?"

He looked past her at the wall, at the knights three deep on it, not leaving, talking, and at the gate, and at the road beyond the gate that ran down to the harbour and the fish market and the coasters.

"Because of what it would be worth," he said, "in the capital."

Rowena followed his eyes to the wall, and then back to him, and something went across her face and was gone. She had sat at that table longer than he had.

"I know what it's worth," she said. "I married into that court." She stood up. "Next time, we close the yard."

She walked away across the flags to her knights, and they parted for her the way they had on the quay, and she did not look back.

She stayed on the yard a little while after the rest had gone, with her table waiting.

Her knights had gone, slowly, arguing. The boy's people had taken him up the slope with his guard holding him under one arm in a way that was meant to look like walking beside him. The water butt was empty and steaming a little in the sun, and the scorch lay across the flags in a long black line, and the table-top stone lay broken in two where it had dropped out of the air, and nobody had dared move it.

She walked to the third flag out from the rack and stood on it.

It rocked. Toward the rack, a finger's width, the way the old flag by the butt used to rock before she fixed it. She had not touched this one. She had walked it all her life. He had not touched it either. He had touched what was under it, a thing she had not known a man could reach from across a yard in the middle of a fight, and she had put her foot down on it exactly when and where he had known she would.

Two years, she thought. Perhaps three, if he was stupid about his legs, and he was not stupid about anything she had seen yet.

She thought about the thing that had held her. Not the plate. She had worn plate for longer than he had been alive, and plate did not know where a woman was going to plant her foot. It was the other thing, the mould being poured round her over and over, carrying her away in copies.

She thought what it would be on a field.

A captain who knew where every man on a line was going to put his weight, before the man knew. Who could stand behind a wall and read the men on the other side of it. Who could find the loose stone under an army's feet, and loosen it, and wait. She had been in a war. She knew what she would have given for a quarter of an hour of that, on a morning long ago with the sea on one side and Solreth's line on the other, and she knew what the men on that line would have given to have it instead.

He did not know yet what he had. Or he knew, and had not let himself think past a yard. Amara had been the same, she remembered. She had always been three thoughts past everybody at the table, and never once looked up to see who else had noticed.

Rowena stepped off the flag. It settled back, a finger low at its near edge.

She did not have it relaid. She left it for the next drill to find.
