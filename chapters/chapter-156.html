Chapter 156 - The Receiver

The wire from Wayford came on the third of May, from Saban's man in the corner of the posting house taproom, and said four things.

The crown's receiver for the southern baronies had come through Wayford going south, with his clerk and his books. He had two crown riders with him. He had, as well, a man in a grey coat with a Circle ring on the outside of his glove, who rode beside the coach and spoke to nobody and was called, by the riders, the master. And the receiver had asked the posting house keeper, at supper, how far it was to Kaldo, and whether the ford at the foot of the downs was up.

"It's up," said Saban, at the table, folding the slip. "It's always up in May. The snow's off the downs." He put the slip in his hat. "He'll be at it on the sixth."

"The fifth," said Ferra.

"The fifth of the mine," Saban agreed. "Grayrock's first full year under the crown's grant. A fifth of every stone sold, to the crown, by law, and the crown sends a man to count it." He turned the hat on his knee. "The crown's never sent a man to count anything in the south in my life that it didn't think was worth the horse."

"Then we'll meet him at the ford," said Emeric.



They rode out on the morning of the sixth, Emeric and Ward and Saban, with Holt and two of the levy, and were at the ford by noon.

The ford was where the south road came down off the downs into a wide shallow valley and crossed the stream that drained them. In August a child could have walked across it. In May it was forty yards of brown water running fast and loud over the gravel, knee-deep on a horse at the edges and a good deal more in the middle, where the road's stones went under and did not come up again for a long way. The ferryman's hut on the near bank had a punt drawn up beside it on rollers, and the ferryman, and his two grown sons, and all three of them were standing on the bank looking at the coach on the far side.

It had stopped at the water's edge. A heavy black crown coach with the arms on its door, four horses, the two riders behind it in the crown's blue, and beside it the man in the grey coat, on a grey horse, looking at the water.

"They'll want the punt," said the ferryman, without much hope. "Two at a time. And swim the horses." He looked at the coach. "And that won't swim."

The man in the grey coat did not want the punt.

He rode his horse to the very edge of the water, where the coach's lead horses stood with their heads down blowing at the flood, and stopped, and stood up in his stirrups. He was in his forties, Emeric thought, broad, with a short grey beard and a plain face, and nothing about him that a man would look at twice on a road, except the ring on his glove. He took the glove off. He held up his bare right hand toward the stream, palm out, as a man holds up his hand to stop a cart.

The glyph came up off his palm.

It was big. Emeric had seen big glyphs; he had seen the hired mages' at the war, and Armand's, and his own on the quay with the balloon, which had been a small thing by any measure. This was not like any of them. The outer ring formed first, blue-white, as wide as a cartwheel, a hand's breadth in front of the man's palm, and hung there in the noon light, open, turning; and the core character came up inside it, the water sign, sharp and clean as if it had been cut in glass; and then the strokes, one after another, more of them than Emeric had ever seen go into a single glyph, laying themselves round the core like the spokes of a wheel. Two breaths. Three. The ferryman's sons had stopped breathing. And the ring closed.

The stream stood up.

Upstream of the ford, ten yards above the road, the whole width of the water rose. It did not splash. It came up off its bed in a long smooth hump, brown and glassy, from one bank to the other, as if something under it had shrugged; and it went on coming up, a wall of water as high as a man on a horse, standing across the stream, with the river behind it piling against it and spilling round its ends over the banks in two broad sheets. Below it, where the ford was, the water went down. It drained away downstream in a long rush, and the gravel of the ford came up out of it, wet and shining and steaming a little in the sun, the stones of the old road on it, all the way across. Forty yards of dry road where a moment before there had been a river.

The man in the grey coat stood in his stirrups with his hand up and held it.

"Drive on," he said, not loudly.

The coachman drove on. Down off the bank onto the wet stones, and across, at a trot, the coach's wheels hissing on the gravel, with the wall of water standing over it on its left hand, higher than the coach's roof, so close that the lead horses rolled their eyes at it and the coachman did not look up. The two riders came after it. The grey horse came last, at a walk, with its rider sitting down now in the saddle but his hand still up, held out to his left at the standing water, his face quite empty.

He came up the near bank, past the ferryman's hut, past Emeric and Ward and Saban on their horses, and did not look at any of them. When his horse's hind feet were out of the water and on the grass, he lowered his hand.

The wall came down.

It came down all at once, with a sound like a barn falling, and the whole of the held river came after it, a brown wave the height of a man going down over the ford and the road and away downstream, and the ferryman's punt, which had been drawn up on its rollers on the bank, lifted, and turned, and went with it. The ferryman's sons ran after it along the bank, shouting. The ferryman did not move. He stood on the bank with his hands hanging and looked at the water, which was a river again, brown and loud and forty yards wide, as though nothing had ever been done to it.

The man in the grey coat put his glove back on. His face, under the short grey beard, had gone the colour of putty. He rode up to the coach, which had stopped on the road above the bank, and got down from his horse, slowly, the way an old man gets down, and one of the riders took the horse, and he opened the coach door and climbed in and shut it behind him; and when the coach came on toward Kaldo with Emeric's party riding ahead of it, Emeric looked back once and saw him through the window, asleep, with his head against the glass and his mouth open, as he stayed, Holt said afterward, for the whole of the rest of the day.

Ward had not said anything at the ford. He did not say anything for the first mile.

"Circle," said Ward at last. "Not tower. A good Circle man. Water." He rode a little way. "The crown doesn't lend those to a receiver for company."

Emeric did not answer. He was thinking, honestly, the way he had been taught to think about any machine he had just watched work, about whether he could have done that.

He could not. Not today, and not, he thought, this year or the next. Water cost him three times what it cost that man, and that man had held a river up for the time it took a coach and four to trot across forty yards of gravel; Emeric could have lifted the ferryman's punt, perhaps, and held it a while. And it was not only the cost. It was the speed of it. The glyph with more strokes than he had ever seen, built in three breaths, every stroke clean, by a man who had built it, plainly, a thousand times. That was not capacity. Armand had read his capacity a month ago and underlined it. That was the other thing, the thing that came only from doing a thing ten thousand times until the hand knew it before the man did, and the man in the grey coat had it, and Emeric did not.

A pump outlasts any mage. He had said it a hundred times. He still believed it. But for the length of time it took a coach to cross a ford, a good mage was better than any pump that had ever been built, and he had just watched one be it.



The receiver was a small neat man of fifty in black, with a clerk's stoop and a soft voice and the kind of face that had spent thirty years being told things by people who would rather not have told them. His name was on his papers and he gave it once, at the gate, and after that everybody called him the receiver, which seemed to suit him.

He sat at the long table the next morning across from Ferra with the crown's book open in front of him, and Ferra's book open in front of her, and the clerk at his elbow with a second book; and for four hours he read.

He was good. Emeric, sitting at the end of the table where he could see both their faces, knew within the first hour that he was good. He did not hurry and he did not skip, and he asked questions the way Ferra asked them, one at a time, exactly, and wrote down the answers and then asked the next. How many stones raised from the first body in the year, by month. How many sorted into each grade. How many sold, how many held. At what price each grade, on average, by quarter. The total. The fifth of the total. The coin paid to the crown's account at Wayford on the quarter days, and the receipts for it.

Ferra had all of it. She had it in her small square hand in the Grayrock book, in columns that ran down the page as true as the rails ran down the hill, and the receipts in their envelope, and she answered every question before the receiver had quite finished asking it and slid each page across the table for him to read for himself; and at noon he sat back in his chair and took off his spectacles and looked at her.

"Your sums are right," he said.

"Yes," said Ferra.

"I've been receiver for the south for nine years," said the receiver. "I don't think I've said that to anybody before in this chair." He put his spectacles on again. "Your sums are right, madam, and your total's right, and if your total's right the crown's fifth is right." He turned a page of the Grayrock book back. "And your total is the sum of your sales. And your sales are written here as so many stones of such a grade at such a price, a quarter at a time." He laid his finger on a line. "Sold to whom?"

Ferra did not answer at once.

"At what quay?" said the receiver gently. "On what day? For what price, each? Not on average. Each." He looked at her over his spectacles. "Because an average is a thing a clerk writes. A sale is a thing a man makes, with another man, on a quay, and if I can't see the man and the quay and the price, I can't see the sale. I can see a sum that's been made to come out right." He turned to the end of the table. "Who sells the stones?"

"I do," said Saban.

He was in the window seat with his hat on his knee. He had been there since eight, and had not said a word, and Emeric had watched him not say it.

"Then you'll have a book," said the receiver.

"No," said Saban.

The receiver waited.

"I've never written a trade down in my life," said Saban. He said it simply, as a man says he has never been to sea. "Not one. A trade's between me and the man I make it with. If I write it down, and the paper goes somewhere, somebody else knows what I paid and what he took, and next time he won't take it, and the man after him won't either." He turned his hat once round on his knee. "I give Ferra the coin and the grade and the count, the same day. She writes that. The rest's in my head."

"The crown," said the receiver, "can't audit a head."

"No," Saban agreed.

The receiver closed the Grayrock book. He did it gently, the way he did everything, and laid his hand flat on it.

"Then I'll write what I've seen," he said, "which is a right sum of sales I can't see. And the crown, reading that, may decide, as it's entitled to, that the fifth is to be reckoned on the crown's own estimate of what a mine like Grayrock's ought to have sold, and not on yours." He looked at Ferra. "The crown's estimates are seldom low." He stood up. "I'll be here until the morning after next. If the sales are on this table by then, every one, with the buyer and the quay and the day and the price, I'll read them. If they aren't, I'll write what I've seen." He bowed to Emeric, a small exact bow. "Highness."

He went out with his clerk. Ferra sat looking at the closed book.

"A year," she said, without looking up. "Four hundred and some sales. Forty-odd buyers. Nine quays." She turned her head and looked at Saban. "In your head."

"In my head," said Saban.



They dined the receiver in the long room that night, because it was owed, and because a man who has eaten at a table writes about it a little differently from one who has not.

The man in the grey coat came down to it. He had slept from the ford to the morning and through most of the morning after, and he came down to supper looking like a man who had been ill and was better, grey still under the beard but steady on the stair, and sat where Hale put him, between Armand and Bram, and ate a great deal and said very little.

Emeric felt him the moment he sat down.

Not his note; his sense. It came out of the man the way it came out of Armand, thin and easy, a breath on a cold morning, without a glyph and without any sign on his face; it went out round the table at the height of a man's chest and touched everybody at it, lightly, one after another, the way a man walking into a strange room lets his eyes go round it once without seeming to. Nobody could have called it rude; it was habit. Armand did it too, every room he went into, and could not stop.

It touched Bram, and went past. It touched Ferra, and Imani, and the receiver, and his clerk, and Hale, and went past. It touched Ward, and lingered, a breath, on the band at Ward's wrist under his cuff, the row of charged beads against his skin, and went on.

It came to Emeric.

He had it ready. He had had it ready since the ford. He held his own down, close in, all of it, the way he had held it in the capital in the yard of the posting house with Ward's blade at his head and in the passage with a knife coming; down, small, a man of no account at the end of a table, Circle range and not a grain over, his band's beads under his cuff damped under his own note as well as he could damp them, the push in his body banked like a fire at night. He felt the man's sense touch him. He felt it go over him the way a hand goes over a table in the dark looking for a cup.

It went past. Then it came back.

Only a breath. The way a man's eye comes back to a picture on a wall that he has walked past, because something in it was not quite where it should have been, and looks again, and sees nothing wrong, and goes on. Emeric sat at the end of the table with his cup in his hand and his face doing nothing and held himself down so hard that his teeth ached. And the sense went past again, and on to Armand, and settled there, and did not come back.

"You have a great deal of stone in this house," said the man in the grey coat, to Armand, pleasantly.

"It's a stone barony now," said Armand. "Grayrock's. We sell the stuff. We can hardly help having it about." He passed the salt. "You'll find it in the floor of the dyehouse and the foot of the stair and the bottom of half the cooking pots."

The man in the grey coat smiled, and took the salt, and said nothing else.

It was the receiver who asked it, at the end, over the cheese. He asked it lightly, of nobody in particular, the way a man asks a question he has asked at a great many tables.

"How many mages does Kaldo feed, these days?"

"Fifteen students," said Armand, at once. "Two hedge mages, who keep the stones charged. An earth mage, who builds my lord's railway. An air mage from the capital who jumps off walls." He looked at the receiver over his cup. "And an old man."

The receiver laughed, and wrote nothing down. His clerk, at his elbow, a thin sharp-faced young man who had not spoken all evening, looked at Armand, and then round the table, once, slowly, at every face, as the man in the grey coat's sense had gone round it; and then he looked at his plate.



Saban went into the room at ten.

He had never gone in alone. He had been in with the six, every month since the seating, and with Emeric twice, to see a trade laid out on the shelves the way the room laid out anything a man had seen; but never alone, with nobody else's sense beside his in the dark to hold the door. Ferra had done it, in the winter. Imani had done it. Bram. Saban had said, each time, that he would when he had a reason.

"This is a reason," said Saban.

He lay on the couch in Emeric's study with his boots off and his hat on his chest and the grey stone in his hand, with Ferra in the chair beside the couch keeping the count, as her rules said, and Imani in the next room with the door open, and Emeric in the passage outside. He shut his eyes. His note went quiet, the way they all went quiet going in, the way a room goes quiet when a man leaves it and shuts the door behind him.

Emeric went up on the wall.

That was Ward's rule, when there were guests in the town that nobody had asked for: one of them on the wall at night with his sense open, and Ward was on the north stretch already, and Emeric took the river side. He walked it slowly from the water stair to the north tower and back, with the arc lamps humming over his head on their posts and the river going by below, and let his sense go out, thin, over the dark town. Thousands of small warm notes, asleep. The still crew's, denser, in their row by the dyehouse. The hospital's sick, in their beds, faint. Imani's, in the keep, awake, in a chair. Ferra's beside the couch, steady, counting. Saban's, gone small and far off, the way a man's note went when he was in the room.

The guests were in the long chamber over the gate tower. The receiver, asleep, an old tired note, like a candle at the end of a long evening. The two crown riders in the room below him, asleep. The man in the grey coat, asleep, very deep, a heavy flat note like a pond under ice; whatever he had spent at the ford, he was still paying it back.

The clerk was not asleep. At a quarter past one the clerk's note left the gate tower.

Emeric felt it go. Down the tower's inner stair, and out across the gate yard, quick and quiet, the note of a thin young man walking carefully, and up the lane behind the gate yard, and left at the well, and on. North. Up the hill along the lane that ran under the town wall on the inside, past the tannery and the coopers' yard and the old wool hall, toward the north wall.

Toward the shed.

And in the shed there was a lamp.

He could not see it from the river wall; he did not need to. He knew it was there because he could feel Tess, in the shed, in her chair, a quick bright note at the foot of the K Discovery's stair, where she had told him at supper she would be until two, because the matrix had a case in the third group that read cold on the dial and she wanted to know why before the flight. She would have a lamp. She always had a lamp. The shed's doors were shut and the shed had no windows on the lane side, but it had a gap at the top of the north door where the boards had shrunk, and Emeric had seen lamplight come out of it on winter nights in a thin gold line along the lintel, and so would anybody standing in the lane under the north wall who looked.

The clerk was walking. The clerk was nearly at the coopers' yard, and the coopers' yard was a quarter of a mile from the north wall lane by the road, and Emeric was a quarter of a mile from the shed by the road the other way, the long way, down the water stair and through the lower ward and up the hill, and he would meet the clerk at the shed door with nothing to say.

By the roofs it was a good deal less.

He did not think about it for long. He went up the steps from the wall walk onto the parapet of the river wall where it met the roof of the dyehouse's long shed, which ran up against the inside of the wall there, and stepped out onto the dyehouse roof.



Tile. That was the first thing he thought. A year ago this would have been thatch, wet and soft and sagging, and a man going over it would have gone through it into the vats. It was tile now, the long red tiles the whole town had laid all winter, pitched and pegged and hard under his boots, wet with the night.

He ran.

Heavy, light. Down, up. He had done it in the stable yard every night for six weeks and gone into the water butt one night in two, and he went into nothing now, because there was no water butt, there was a roof, and then the gap of a lane, and then another roof, and the clerk was walking. He put his foot down on the dyehouse ridge and put his weight into it, all of it, through the boot, so that the ridge tile under him took him like a block; and as the foot came up he let the weight go, all of it, and was in the air, light, going, the long low stride of a man who weighed nothing between one footfall and the next; and the next foot came down on the ridge tiles a body's length on, heavy, and pushed, and let go.

It worked.

It worked the way the Stop had worked the second night: not because he had learned it, but because he had stopped trying to. In the stable yard he had thought about every stride. Here he did not have time to think about any of them. His feet did it. Heavy, light, heavy, light, down the ridge of the dyehouse roof at the speed of a horse's canter, every footfall a single soft knock of a boot on a tile and nothing else, no slither, no scrape, because a foot that weighs nothing in the air comes down light. The lane between the dyehouse and the tannery came up under him, a black gap the width of a cart, and he put his weight into the last tile and let it go and went over the gap with the lane's gutter a long way under him and came down on the tannery roof's ridge, heavy, and the tiles took him, and he was going again.

At every footfall, at his heels, the violet. A flicker, small, the size of a coin, a little glyph of gravity opening and closing like a moth's wings each time the weight went off and came back. There was no stopping it. A glyph glows; every spell has one. He tried to keep it small, and it was small, and in the dark over the roofs of Kaldo it went with him from ridge to ridge like a trail of sparks off a smith's grindstone.

The coopers' yard. The old wool hall, with its great steep roof, which he went up the face of in three strides and over the top of and down the other side, light, with the town below him and the north wall rising ahead. He could feel the clerk below him now in the lane, close, a hundred yards back and walking, and then fifty, and then he was past him, over his head, on the wool hall's ridge, and the clerk did not look up.

The north wall lane. The shed's roof was on the far side of it, long and low against the wall. He went off the end of the wool hall's roof into the air over the lane, and it was too far, he saw at once, a full stride too far, the shed's eaves a long way off across the dark; and he did not fall short. He had the Stop. At the top of the stride he took the weight off entirely and hung, a heartbeat, over the lane, in the dark, with the violet at his heels and the clerk's note fifty yards down the lane coming on; and let himself down, slowly, into the shed's yard, between the shed and the wall, where nobody could see from the lane.

He came down onto the cobbles with Ward's wave going through him top to bottom, everywhere at once, ankles and knees and hips and back, and his knee held. He did not even feel it.

He put his hand on the shed's side door and opened it.

Tess looked up from the foot of the stair in her chair with a lamp on her knee and a case open in front of her and her data book on the case.

"Lamp," said Emeric.

She did not ask why. She looked at his face once and put her hand over the lamp's chimney and blew down it, and the shed went black.



They stood in the dark, Tess in her chair and Emeric by the door, and he felt the clerk come up the north wall lane.

Slowly now. The quick careful walk had gone slow. The thin young note came up the lane on the other side of the shed's wall, and past the side door, and on to the end of the shed where the great north doors were, and stopped there. Emeric could feel him standing in the lane outside the doors, close, ten feet away through the boards; could feel him look up. At the gap over the lintel where the boards had shrunk. At the dark gap over the lintel.

The clerk stood there for as long as it would have taken a man to say grace twice.

Then he turned round and walked back down the lane, the way he had come, not quickly, past the wool hall and the coopers' yard and the tannery, and at the well he went right, and across the gate yard, and up the gate tower stair, and Emeric felt his note go quiet in the long chamber over the gate, beside the receiver's, and stay quiet.

"Somebody came," said Tess, in the dark. It was not a question.

"The receiver's clerk."

"To look at the shed."

"To look at whatever he could see." Emeric leaned his back against the door. He found he was out of breath, a little; not from the running. "He saw a dark shed."

Tess was quiet.

"How did you get here," she said, "from the river wall, in the time it took him to walk from the coopers' yard?"

He did not answer. After a while he heard her strike a match, under the shelter of her own body, and the lamp came up again, small, turned down to a bead, and she was looking at him across the dark shed over the top of it with the K Discovery's great dark flank going up behind her into the roof. She was looking at his boots. There was tile dust on them, red, from the ridges.

"Never mind," said Tess, and went back to her case.



Saban came out of the room at half past two.

He came out of it the way they all did, slowly, a note coming back up into a body like a man coming up out of deep water, and Ferra, beside the couch, said the count aloud: four hours and a half. He opened his eyes and lay looking at the ceiling of Emeric's study for some while without saying anything.

"It was a week," he said at last. "In there. About a week." He sat up and put his boots on. "It's all on the shelves. Every one. You go in and it's a long room, and down both sides, every trade I've made since the mine opened, laid out like bolts of cloth in a draper's, and you take one down and you're on the quay again with the man, and the weather, and the price, and what he said." He looked at his own hands. "I'd forgotten half of them. I hadn't. They were there." He stood up. "Give me paper. All of it."

He wrote until seven. He sat at Emeric's table with the lamp and wrote in his long sloping trader's hand, sheet after sheet, without stopping and without looking anything up and without crossing a word out: the date, the quay, the buyer by name, the grade, the count, the price each, the total. Four hundred and thirty-one sales. Forty-four buyers. Nine quays. And as each sheet was done Ferra took it and sat at the other end of the table and added it, on the adding machine she had had Hale bring up from the counting room at three in the morning, and checked each sheet's total against the quarter's figure in her own book, and set it on a pile.

At seven Saban put the pen down. Ferra turned the crank on the last sheet and read the eight little windows and looked at her book.

"Exact," she said. "To the copper."

"Of course it is," said Saban, and went to bed.

The receiver read the pile on the morning of the ninth, at the long table, with his spectacles on, every sheet. It took him until two. Then he wrote, in the crown's book, in his small neat hand, that the crown's fifth of the Grayrock mine for its first full year had been received and found exact, and turned the book round for Ferra to sign under it, and she signed; and he closed it.

"I shall keep these," he said, laying his hand on Saban's pile.

"They're the crown's," said Ferra. "We've a copy."

The receiver looked at her. "You made a copy," he said, "of four hundred and thirty-one sales, between seven this morning and now."

"I had help," said Ferra.

The receiver left at three. The man in the grey coat rode beside the coach out of the South Gate on his grey horse, looking straight ahead, and the clerk sat inside the coach beside the receiver, and as the coach went out under the gate arch Emeric, on the gate tower stair, saw the clerk's thin face at its window, turned not toward the road but back, over his shoulder, up the hill, toward the north wall.



Ferra was in her office at ten that night, with the lamp lit and no work on her desk, which he had never seen.

He knocked and went in. She did not look up. She was sitting with her hands flat on the empty desk, looking at them.

"Four hundred and thirty-one," she said.

"Exact."

"Exact." She looked up at him then, and her face was white and set, and it was a face he knew, the one she had worn in the passage in the capital with the summons book in her hand. "In his head. For a year. Every stone the barony sold, and the crown's fifth of it, and the whole of Grayrock's book that I keep and Vantic reads and the House lends against. Resting on a man's memory." She pressed her hands on the desk. "If he'd fallen off a quay. If he'd caught a fever on the coast boat. If he'd gone into the room alone for the first time and not come out, which he could have, which Armand says a man can, the first time. The receiver would have written the crown's estimate against the mine, and Vantic would have written it against our credit, and every one of my columns would have been right, and it wouldn't have mattered."

"It didn't happen."

"That's not an answer!" She had never raised her voice to him. She had not raised it now, not really; it was only louder, and sharper, and it filled the small room. "You'd not take that from Bram about a boiler. You'd not take it from Tess about a gauge. It didn't burst, so it was fine." She stood up. "You knew. You knew he never wrote anything down. You've known it since the first caravan."

"So did you."

She stopped.

"You knew it before I did," said Emeric. "You hired him. You tested the traders on the spring caravan and he passed, and you knew then that he carried it all in his head, because you asked him for his books and he laughed at you. You've had his coin and his counts every quarter for five years. You never once asked him for the rest."

"Because he'd have gone," said Ferra. "Because a man like Saban, you don't ask him to write his trades down, he goes and trades for somebody who doesn't ask."

"Yes."

"So I let it be."

"So we both did."

She looked at him across the desk. He looked at her. And he saw it come up in her face at the same moment as it came up in his own, the thing neither of them had expected to find in that room, at that hour, at the end of three days of it; not anger at all, or not any more. The thing under it. That they had both been wrong about exactly the same thing in exactly the same way for five years, and had each of them, separately, at their own desks, decided it was the other one's fault, and had been about to have the first real quarrel they had ever had over it.

Her mouth moved.

"Don't," he said.

"I'm not."

"You are."

"I am not," said Ferra, and laughed. It came out of her badly, all at once, a short helpless snort that she put her hand over far too late; and he laughed, worse, a sound like a man who has been holding a door shut against a wind and has let go of it; and they stood on either side of her empty desk at ten o'clock at night and laughed at each other until she had to sit down.

When she could, she pulled a sheet toward her and dipped her pen.

"Every trade," she said, writing. "Written down. The day it's made. Buyer, quay, grade, count, price. In a book that lives in this office, not in his hat." She blotted it. "He'll hate it."

"He'll go."

"He won't go." She looked at the line she had written. "He's been in the room alone now. He's seen his own year on the shelves. He knows what it's like to have it all there and have nobody but himself able to reach it." She put the pen down. "He'll do it."

Saban did it. He hated it. He began the book on the twelfth of May and wrote in it every day after, in his long sloping hand, and never once in the rest of his life said a civil word about it to anybody.



The wire from Wayford came on the twentieth.

Saban read it out at the table with his hat on his knee. His man in the corner of the posting house had watched the receiver's coach go north on the sixteenth; and the receiver had gone on north to the capital as receivers did. But the clerk had stayed a night. He had sat in the taproom and asked the keeper, over a good deal of ale, the same question he had been asking, Saban's man now learned from three other houses down the road, at every posting house between Wayford and the downs: how many mages each barony fed.

And the ostler who did the clerk's room had found, under the bed, a sheet torn from the clerk's pocket book. A list. Baronies, and against each, a number. Against Kaldo the clerk had written, with a question mark after it, the number Armand had given at the table. And under it, a line.

Saban read the line out in his dry voice, exactly, without looking up.

"Kaldo. Past one. Lights moving on the roofs. Violet. Fast. Not lanterns."

He folded the slip and put it in his hat. Then he looked down the long table at Emeric. So did Ferra. So, from the end of the table, with his eyes open for once, did Armand.

Ward did not look at anybody.
