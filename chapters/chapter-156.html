Chapter 156 - Type

Nevin had kept the punches in a tray lined with green baize, in the drawer under his bench, and he took them out one at a time on the morning the lead came and laid them in a row along the bench under the window, as if he were laying out the dead.

There were ninety-four of them. Every capital, every small letter, every figure, the stops, the marks, two sizes of space. Each one was a short bar of the converter's steel as long as a finger and square in the section, and on the end of each one, cut in relief and reversed, there was a letter. He had cut them with a graver and a file and a lens, under the window, on the mornings when the light was good, for a year. He had cut some of them four and five times. The small g he had cut eleven times.

Emeric picked one up. It was the capital R, and he turned it to the light, and looked at the tiny reversed letter on its end, standing up out of the steel with its bowl and its leg, perfectly clean, sharp as the day it was cut, and not much bigger than a grain of barley.

"That took me nine days," said Nevin.

"It's beautiful."

"It's backwards." Nevin took it from him and put it back in its place in the row. "It's no use to anybody backwards. It's been a year of nobody being able to read my work."

What happened next happened in Nevin's shop and Bram's, over three weeks, and Emeric was not there for most of it. He had told them what he knew and then left them alone, because what he knew was the shape of the thing and not the doing of it, and the doing was theirs.

Each punch was struck into a small bar of soft copper, straight down, with one blow of a hammer, so that the letter went into the copper the right way round and sunk, and the copper round it bulged. That was the matrix. Nevin filed each one flat and true on every face until the letter sat in it exactly at the same depth and the same place as every other letter, because if the a sat a hair deeper than the b then a line of type would print up and down like a fence in a field. That took most of the three weeks.

The mould was Bram's. Two halves of steel that fitted together round a narrow slot, with a place at the bottom for the matrix and a funnel at the top for the metal, and a spring that held it closed. A man held it in one hand and poured with the other, and jerked the mould up as he poured so the metal went all the way into the letter before it set, and then opened it and knocked out a piece of type. A stick of grey metal the height of a thumb joint, with a letter on its end the right way round.

The metal was not lead. Lead was too soft; the first letters cast in it pulped the first time they went under a press. Nevin and Bram worked the metal out between them in the second week, with the lead from Bryda's crate and some tin from a tinker and a little of something else, which took them most of the week to get right. Emeric did not go into the shop while they did it. By the end of the second week the metal was hard enough that a letter stood a hundred pulls and cast sharp enough that the e had a hole in it you could see.

In the third week Nevin cast four thousand letters.

He did it at the bench under the window with a ladle and the little iron pot on a charcoal fire, one letter at a time, all day, with his lips moving as he counted. By the end of the week his right hand was blistered where the mould's spring bit it and the floor round the bench was grey with the jets knocked off each letter, which were swept up every evening and remelted every morning.

The four thousand went into a case. Bram's carpenter made it: a shallow tray of oak with a box for every letter, big boxes for the ones used most and little ones for the ones used least, the whole set up in the order Emeric drew for them, which was an order nobody at the table had ever seen and which put the e in the middle.

The press was the part Emeric had cared about most, and he had drawn it himself, and he had drawn it wrong three times.

Not wrong in how it worked. Wrong in what it was made of.

The first drawing had been beautiful, and had a lever and a toggle that brought the platen down harder and more evenly than any screw, and needed a joint turned so fine that only Jorrin could turn it and only on the big lathe. He had looked at it for an evening and burned it. The second had been nearly as good, and had a frame cast in one piece, which wanted a mould larger than any the foundry could pour and a furnace hotter than the island's. He had burned that too.

The third drawing was a screw press.

It was the oldest shape there was. Two uprights of oak, as thick as a man's thigh, standing on a heavy base and joined at the top by a head beam. Through the head beam, a great iron screw, as thick as a wrist, with a bar through its top to turn it by. At the bottom of the screw, a flat iron plate, the platen. Under the platen, a flat bed of stone on rails, which slid in and out. The type went on the bed in a frame, locked tight with wedges. The ink went on the type with a pair of leather balls stuffed with wool. The paper went on a hinged frame that folded down over the type. The bed slid in under the platen. A man pulled the bar, and the screw went down a quarter turn, and the platen pressed the paper onto the type, and he pulled the bar back, and slid the bed out, and lifted the frame, and there was a page.

There was nothing in it that a competent smith in any town in the south could not mend. Any smith could cut a screw; it was slow and he would curse it, but he could do it. Any carpenter could make the uprights and the head. Any mason could dress the bed. The platen was a flat plate. The balls were leather and wool. The whole of it was oak and iron and stone and leather.

There was not a single thing in it that needed Kaldo.

That was the point. That was the whole point, and Emeric had known it from the day he told Rowena at her table that the press he sent her would be a screw press and not the stone printer. The stone printer needed charged stones and a man who could write their instructions, and there was one of those in the world. A press like this needed a smith and some oak. When he was dead, and Bram, and Nevin, a boy in Emberfell who had never heard of any of them could look at a broken screw press and fix it.

Bram's shop built it in eleven days from the third drawing, and it was slow, and it was ugly, and it creaked when the screw went down, and it stood in the corner of the pattern store on its oak legs looking like something a farmer had built to squeeze apples.

Emeric stood in front of it on the evening it was finished, when the shop was empty.

He was prouder of it than of the stone printer. He was prouder of it than of the stone printer by some distance, and he stood there and was surprised by how much, and did not say so to anybody, because there was nobody in Kaldo who would not have thought it a strange thing to be proud of. The stone printer was a marvel. It laid down a page by itself while a man watched. The screw press was a thing out of a history book, the kind he had looked at once behind glass in another world and walked past.

It would outlast the printer. He knew that the way he knew the level would beat the chain. Every stone printer in the world would stop the day the last man who could write to its stones stopped, and this thing would go on squeezing pages out in the corner of a shop in Emberfell for as long as there was oak.

He put his hand on the upright, the way he had put it on the stake at the datum. Then he went up to supper and did not mention it.

They proved it on the Emberfell primers.

It had to be those. The primers had gone west already, in the autumn, on the first slow boat, forty copies off the stone printer, the letters and the numbers and the first hundred words, Orrin's primer that every child in Kaldo learned from. Rowena's teachers were teaching from them now. If the screw press was going to go west in their place, it had to make those same pages, and make them well enough that nobody in Emberfell would ever notice the difference, or care.

Nevin set the first page himself.

Emeric watched him do it and understood for the first time how slow it was. Nevin held a small iron tray in his left hand, a composing stick, and picked the letters out of the case one at a time with his right, without looking, by feel and by long practice over the three weeks, and set them in the stick upside down and backwards. Every word. Every space. At the end of every line he had to fill it out with thin spaces so it was exactly as long as every other line, or it would not lock in the frame. A line took him the time it takes to say a long prayer. The first page of the primer, which was the alphabet and a picture of an apple, took him all morning.

The picture of the apple was cut in a block of pear wood by the carpenter's son.

At noon they locked the page in its frame on the bed, and Nevin inked it with the two leather balls, slapping them together first to spread the ink and then dabbing them over the type, and they laid a damp sheet of Kaldo paper on the hinged frame and folded it down, and slid the bed in, and Nevin put both hands on the bar and pulled.

The screw went down with a long wooden groan.

He pulled the bar back. He slid the bed out. He lifted the frame and peeled the sheet off the type, carefully, by its corners, and turned it over.

It was the first page of the primer. It was the alphabet and the apple, exactly as the stone printer had made it, except that the stone printer's letters had been drawn by a pen and these had been pressed by metal, and they were blacker, and sharper at their edges, and every a was exactly every other a, because they had all been cast in the same mould off the same punch that Nevin had cut in nine days.

Nevin held it up to the window and looked at it for a long time.

"I can read it," he said.

They printed the Emberfell primer over the next fortnight, all sixty-four pages of it, and forty copies, on the screw press, a page a morning to set and an afternoon to pull. Then they broke up the type and put it back in the case and set Orrin's own.

That was the part Emeric had not thought about.

Orrin's school had been taught from the stone printer's primers since the printer was built, and the stone printer had made them as fast as there were sheets, and there had never been enough sheets. There had been forty quires a week out of the paper works before the works was built and a hundred and twenty now, and the keep's clerks and the barony's writs took most of it, and Orrin's children had shared a primer between three and learned to read upside down. Now there was a second machine, and it did not need the stone printer's stones, which could go back to the barony's own work, and Nevin set the Kaldo primer on the second Monday and they pulled two hundred of it in a month.

Orrin came to the pattern store to see the first of them. He talked the whole time he was there, about the letters and the apple and the weight of the paper and the smell of the ink and how he had learned to read on a hornbook in a village north of the capital that nobody had heard of and which had had one book in it, and he picked up a copy and turned the pages and went on talking. Then he got to the last page and stopped talking.

The last page was a psalm.

It had not been on the Emberfell primer. Emberfell's last page was the numbers to a hundred. But the Kaldo primer had always ended with a psalm, the short one about the lamp in the window, because Orrin had put it there when he first wrote the primer out by hand six years ago, because the hornbook he had learned on had ended with it, and every child in Kaldo who could read could say it by heart. Nevin had set it from Orrin's own copy.

"That's right," said Orrin, and closed it, and started talking again.

Doran saw it a week later.

He saw it in the church, in the hands of a girl of seven in the second row, who had been given her own copy for the first time in her life and had brought it to the service to show it to God. Doran came to the pattern store that afternoon, on his stick, with the primer in his hand, and asked for the prince.

Emeric took him out into the yard where it was quiet. Bram came too, because Doran had asked for him as well.

Doran did not raise his voice. He never did. He stood in the yard with the primer open in his hand at the last page, and looked at the psalm, and said that it was well printed, and that he had not seen type since he was a young man at the seminary in the capital, and that it was a fine thing to see a child with a book of her own.

Then he said that the parish had taught the letters in Kaldo since before there was a school. That the psalms were the parish's, and the prayers, and the creed, and the lessons, and that the parish had always chosen which of them a child learned first and in what words, because that was what a parish was for. And that now a barony printed them.

He did not say it was wrong. He said it was new.

"The barony didn't choose it," said Emeric. "Orrin did. He's always had it on the last page. He had it there when it was in his own hand."

"I know," said Doran. "I've heard every child in the town say it for six years. I taught him the words he wrote it in." He closed the primer. "Orrin wrote one copy, and I could see it. The barony has printed two hundred, and I have seen one, by accident, in a child's hand, in my church. That's what's new."

Emeric understood him. He understood him very well, and he understood that Doran was not being unreasonable, and that in the capital there were men in a cathedral who would one day hear that a barony in the south was printing psalms, and would not be as mild about it.

"What do you want?" he said.

Doran looked at the primer in his hand for a while.

"I'd like to see it," he said, "before it's printed. Anything of scripture. Any psalm, any prayer, any lesson, anything from the Book. Before it's printed in Kaldo, I'd like the parish to read the proof and say it's right. That's all." He looked up. "I don't ask to choose it. Orrin can choose it. I ask to read it."

It was not all, and they both knew it. A parish that read the proof could one day decline to say that it was right. But it cost nothing now. There was one psalm in one primer, and Doran had taught Orrin its words.

Emeric agreed to it. He agreed because it cost him nothing and because the alternative was a priest who had been told no.

Doran agreed to it because the alternative was worse for him, and he knew it. He had not come to the yard to stop the press. He could not stop the press. He had come to get the one thing he could get, before anybody else in the Church knew there was a thing to get.

Neither of them liked it. Bram, standing beside them in the yard with his arms folded, did not like it either, and said so after Doran had gone, in one line, which was, "That's the first of those."

Emeric had Ferra write it into the finishing room's rules that evening, in one sentence, with the date. Nothing of scripture to be printed in Kaldo, on any press, without the proof first read by the parish. She wrote it without comment. Then she looked at it, and wrote under it, in smaller letters, At the parish's request, agreed by the holder, at no cost.

"At no cost," Emeric said, reading it upside down.

"Today," said Ferra.

The coaster was fitted out through the last weeks of spring.

She had her plate. She had her boiler, which had come off the rolls in the fortnight after the hull and been riveted in Bram's shop by men who had never riveted a boiler so big, and hauled down to the lower water on two carts lashed together. She had her engine, a copy of the steamboat's, which Bram had built in the winter on the chance of the plate and kept under a cloth. She had her screw, cast and filed to the steamboat's pattern. She had Fletcher's lines, which were a longer, leaner, deeper version of the steamboat's, for the sea, and she had a mast and a gaff sail Abloh cut, for when the coal ran short and the wind was fair.

She had no stone in her anywhere. Not a lamp stone. Not a bead. Emeric had walked her from stem to stern with his sense on the last evening and she was iron and oak and coal and canvas and nothing else, and he stood on her deck afterwards and felt, very strongly, what he had felt in front of the screw press.

Her crew were Fletcher's yard men and lower-water lightermen, eleven of them, who had taken the steamboat up and down the river for two years and wanted the sea. Her master was the eldest of the lightermen, a man named Hamo Kerrow who had sailed the coast in other men's boats for twenty years before he came home to the lower water.

The table decided who went west in her.

The press, in its crate. The case of type, and the spare type, and two hundred reams of Kaldo paper, and the ink. Nevin, for a season, to teach a man of Rowena's to cut punches, because a press without a punch-cutter would run out of letters. A second teacher from Orrin's school, a young woman named Merrit who had been one of Orrin's first eleven children and could say the whole primer backwards. And a drains man, Ansel, who had laid the lower water's drains to the well standard and could read the standard and teach it.

Saban went for the lane. Ward and Rylan went with Emeric, because Ward went where Emeric went, and because Rylan had not left Emeric's side since the strongroom at the Staple, not in any way anybody could have pointed to, and nobody had suggested he should.

"And Kaldo," said Ferra, "stays here."

She said it before anybody could say anything else, looking at nobody in particular.

"The rail works is three weeks from its first heat. The city has two hundred men on the flat and the kilns going. The House's man could come up the road any day. The mages' answers are coming by every post." She squared her book. "Somebody has to be here who can sign. The Secretary of the Arcane holds the barony under the sealed letter, and he's done it before, and he'll do it again. I'll hold the book."

Armand, at the end of the table, looked at her, and inclined his head.

Emeric had known she would stay. He had known it since the day he chose the hull. He found, all the same, that he minded, and that he had no right to mind, and that she could see him mind from where she sat, because Ferra could see everything from where she sat. She looked at him down the table for a moment with no expression at all.

"Bring me back her Treasurer's figures," she said. "The real ones. Not the ones she shows."

They crated the press on the quay at the lower water on the last evening of spring.

It went into its crate in pieces: the uprights and the head beam and the base, the screw wrapped in oiled cloth, the platen, the bed, the frame, the balls, and the case of type in its own box with the lid screwed down. Nevin supervised every piece and did not let anybody touch the type case but himself. When the last crate was nailed shut it stood on the quay beside the coaster in the low sun, stencilled on every side in Ferra's clerk's hand, EMBERFELL, BY KALDO.

And beside it, on a plank slung over the coaster's stern on two ropes, the carpenter's son was painting her name.

He did it in white, on the black of her plate, in letters as tall as a man's forearm, and he did it slowly, because he had never painted anything so large or so public and everybody on the quay was watching him. Fletcher stood at the foot of the slip with his hands in his coat. The lightermen who were to crew her stood in a row on the quay. Nevin stood by the crates. Ward stood where he could see all of it.

Emeric had let the table choose the name, and the table had argued for an evening and then let Saban choose it, because Saban had said it would help the lane.

The boy finished the last letter and leaned back on his plank to look at it.

EMBERFELL, it said.

It was a courtesy. It was the kind of courtesy a merchant pays a customer who has trusted him with a long credit, and Saban had said as much. But when the boy came down off the plank and the crew went aboard to see to her fires for the morning, and the quay began to empty, Emeric stood for some time in the dusk looking at the name on the iron, white on black, and at the crate beside it that said the same, and thought that it was the first thing Kaldo had ever built that he would be glad to give away.
