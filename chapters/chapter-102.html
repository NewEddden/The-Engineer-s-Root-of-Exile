Chapter 99 - The Four Men's

The section had weighed two tons.

Emeric had the figure in Fletcher's hand, on the sheet that had come up the river with the section in the thaw, and he had it in the notebook in his own, and on most evenings of the early summer he had it in front of him on the table in the long room, beside the other figure, which was his.

A man in harness weighed about an eighth of a ton. A big man, in the full plate, with his sword on.

A hull section had to be built, and hauled up the river, and fitted with housings cut into its frames, and it cost a great deal before a stone went near it, and when it failed it failed in a foot of water with two tons of iron twisting itself apart. A man in harness did not have to be built. There were four of them in the barony, walking about in the yard every morning, and the harness had been forged in Bram's shop and fitted to the man inside it years ago, and it moved when he moved. It was the smallest thing in the barony a man could stand inside. It was also, by a margin of sixteen to one, the lightest thing he could ask a handful of fed stones and a pack to lift that anybody could call flying.

The forge had stood idle since the night it ate the spares, which was what Emeric had told Bram it would do. It was not going to stand idle much longer. The one thing it could make that Bram's forge could not was a thing that needed stones in exact places, and a flying harness was very little else.

Before he drew a single plate, he rewrote the rule.

The forge's deciding stone had answered every hair the plate moved, and every answer had been answered back, and four of the wall's stones had gone grey in an evening paying for it. A man was not a plate. A man swayed when he breathed. A stone that tried to hold him perfectly still would spend itself chasing his breath all night and hold nothing still at the end of it. So he wrote the harness's rule loose. Let him sway a hand before you answer. Answer once, and then wait to see whether it was enough. He tried it in the forge first, on the plate, with Bram's master pound set down on one corner and taken off again. The charge came from lamp stones this time, the plain charge-and- hold stones that went into mine lamps, which Saban had brought up from the coast in a box of forty at four copper apiece, and which Orrin charged in the evenings. Emeric signed for four in the forge's own book, the whole of each, and the plate went down further under the pound than it used to and came back slower, and at the end of the evening the four came back with most of their charge still in them. Ferra read the book and did not comment, which was a comment.

That left the harness.

The rule was older than anything in the forge. It was in the front of his own book, in his own hand, from before he had owned anything worth spending on anything. The four sets are the four men's. He had moved the capital's crystals out of the capital's steel and into Bram's once, one suit at a time, and every one of them had gone back into the harness of the man it belonged to, and he had never once in all the years since thought of those crystals as stock.

He could have taken a suit for a fortnight and nobody would have said a word. That was exactly the reason he was not going to.

He asked Ward in the yard, after the morning's work, while Ward was still standing over him.

"I want to borrow your harness."

Ward waited, which from Ward meant the rest of it.

"Not the crystals. They stay where they are and they stay out of circuit. I want to put plates on the outside of it. Stones at the shoulders and the hips and the heels, and on the back, and on the calves. A pack. A deciding stone on the breast." He got up off the flagstones. "And I want you in it, over sand, on four lines, in the foundry at night. It's the thing that happened to the section, with a man inside."

Ward looked at him for some time.

"It's mine to lend," he said. "You can have it. And you'll want me in it, because it can't be you."

"I'd rather it was me."

"I know you would." Ward said it the way he gave the number of men on a road. "If you go up, every man in the capital who's ever stood in a hull and poured says you lifted yourself, and he's right to say it, because you could have. Nobody could ever tell. Not Armand. Not you." He picked up his practice blade from where it lay on the well-head. "I can't lift a cup. I can't pour into anything. If I go up, it was the stones."

It was the reason, and Emeric had known it was the reason for a week and had not said it aloud, because saying it aloud made it Ward.

"There's a rule," Emeric said. "From the lathe. I don't put a man's hands where only I can see what's coming."

"You'll be able to see it."

"I'll feel the stones go a breath before they do. You won't. You'll feel it when you're already coming down."

"Then I'll be coming down onto sand," said Ward, "on four lines, in a building with a roof on it, with you standing outside with your face screwed up the way it goes." He sheathed the blade. "That's what the lines are for. That's why you asked for them before you asked for me."

That was also true. He did not like it any better.

The forge's first real job took most of a month.

Each node was a plate of steel the size of a man's palm, curved to the harness where it would sit, and each plate was one piece: no rivets, no bolts, no joins anywhere that might shift under a load and put a stone a hair off the line it had been seated on. The gravity nodes went at the shoulders, the hips and the heels, six of them, each with a single blank from Armand's chest seated in a cup worked into the steel, square to the frame of a standing man. The air stones went at the small of the back and on the backs of both calves, three of them, off the etching floor, each with its working cut to push one way and nothing else. On the back plate the angle of the air stone was the whole of the matter. A hair's difference in where it pointed was the difference between a man going forward and a man going forward and slowly over onto his face, and that hair was the reason the plates came out of the forge and not off Bram's anvil. Emeric worked every one of them hot on the fed plate, with the hold off him and the heat and the form in his own two hands, and seated the stones, and closed the steel over the lip of each seat with a pass of the form, so that the stone and the plate were one thing and would stay one thing.

Armand wrote the six blanks in the ledger as he had written the forge's four. Six ounces, for a harness. Lent. The word looked less strange the second time. Vesk issued the three air stones off the floor's stock, and wrote them against the harness, and asked nothing, and Emeric saw him look at the three workings a moment longer than issuing needed.

The charge had to reach every node, and a man bends. The wall's way was a groove with charged grit in the mortar, which was perfect in a wall and would crack the first time a knee went past straight. So on the plates themselves the grooves ran inside the steel, cut on the inner face and bedded with grit and closed over. Across every place a man bends, at the shoulder and the hip and the back of the knee, they ran through leather: tubes sewn by the saddler who kept the garrison's harness, as thick as a finger, packed hard with the same grit and sealed at each end into the plate. The saddler was paid four Silver for eleven tubes and a good deal of swearing. He was told they were for carrying sand, which was true.

The pack went on the back, over the air stone: a box of iron plate, riveted, lined with felt, holding twenty of Saban's lamp stones. Orrin charged them in the evenings, a quarter of an hour each, and said as he always said that it was less than holding a lamp, and then said, looking at twenty of them in a row, that he had not held that many lamps at once in his life.

The deciding stone went on the breast, under a cover of plate, where the grooves from the pack and the grooves to the nodes all met.

Ward could not tell it anything. He could not pour and he could not delve, and a man in a fight did not want to be looking down at a stone to find out what it was doing. So Bram made two levers of brass and set them in slots on the breastplate, one each side of the cover, shaped so that a hand in a gauntlet could find them without looking, and Emeric made two small reading stones like the one in the forge, to watch where the levers sat and tell the deciding stone. The left lever was lift. Forward was lighter, back was heavier, and all the way back, past a notch Bram filed so that a man would feel it through a gauntlet, was heavy: the heels told heavy and the rest cut, for a man who needed to be on the ground now and not presently. The right lever was the push, from nothing to all of it. How a man went, once the push was on, was up to how he leaned.

The capital crystals were not touched. They sat in their housings in Bram's steel under the plates, with Bram's breakers open on every coupling, and Ward would wear the whole of it dead. Nothing in it would help him but the stones on the outside.

Emeric wrote the plan on the first day of the new month, as he had written the section's, three sheets, fair.

The foundry, at night, with the doors shut and the fires drawn. Sand spread a hand deep across the casting floor, in a square forty feet on a side. Four posts sunk in the floor at the corners, and four lines from four rings on the harness, one to each post, long enough to let a man go up to the height of the tie-beams and not an inch more, and no line in any man's hands. The count Ward would keep. When he would come down, which was a count of three hundred, a third of what the arithmetic said the pack would carry, because the arithmetic had been true on the bench before. Who would stand where. Emeric, outside the sand, with the medium open. Bram at the doors. Ward in the harness. And what each of them would do at each count if it went wrong.

He took it to Ferra first.

She read it at her table with the lamp at her elbow, all three sheets, at the speed she read everything, and then she read the third one again. Then she took her pen, the one he had made, and in the margin of the first sheet, beside the line that said the foundry, at night, she wrote a line of her own. It took her some time. She wrote it small, in her even hand, and turned the sheet to face him.

Under a roof. On a line. Over sand. With Imani present.

He read it twice.

Three of the four were already in the plan. He had written the roof and the lines and the sand himself, in the first paragraph, because they were the plan. She had written them again anyway, in her own hand, in a place where her hand had never been before. And she had added the fourth.

It was not a stay. The ninth clause wanted a form, and she had written the form herself in the winter of the Standing Order, and this was not it. A stay that isn't in a form is a mood. She had said that to him once across a table, and she had meant it, and she knew better than anybody in four baronies the difference between what she had just written and a thing he could not overrule.

But in the thaw she had read the section's plan and said she would come and see it float. In the spring she had copied his plan without a word and sent it up the long street. This time she had put her own hand on the page, next to his.

"Imani will want to be there anyway," he said.

"Then it costs you nothing." Ferra capped the pen. "I'd like it written down that she was asked."

He took the sheet back. He did not cross out his own first paragraph, and he did not argue with hers, and when he fair-copied the plan that night for the others he copied her line into it exactly where she had put it, in the margin, in the same words.

He took Imani's copy up the long street himself.

She was on the hospital ground, standing on a plank across a trench with a mason at each end of it arguing about a drain. She read the plan on the plank. She read the margin first, as anybody would, and looked at the hand.

"Ferra's," she said.

"Yes."

"I was coming anyway."

"She wants it written down that you were asked."

"Then write down that I said yes." Imani turned to the second sheet. "If it fails, he comes down from the tie-beams."

"Onto sand."

"In plate. The tie-beams are twenty feet."

"Eighteen."

She looked at him over the sheet for long enough that he heard the difference between twenty feet and eighteen for himself, and he found that he did not mind being looked at like that nearly as much as he ought to.

"I'll bring the splints," she said, and gave him back the plan, and went back to the drain.

Declan came to him the day the plates went on.

He came with Osric, which meant Osric had been brought as a witness, and he stood in the long room door in his own harness, polished, with his helmet under his arm, and said that he understood the prince was fitting stones to the senior man's suit. He said it in the voice he used on the yard.

"I am."

"Then I want the same on mine."

"And mine," said Osric. "Though I'd settle for a smaller one. I don't need to go far. I'd be happy just to be a hand off the ground when Declan is standing in the trough admiring himself."

"I was checking the fit."

"You were checking the fit on the other side of your face."

Emeric let them finish, because it would have been cruel to stop them and because it was the first time either of them had asked him for anything in all their years in Kaldo that was not a posting or a day's leave.

"Not until Ward's has flown," he said.

"And when it has?"

"When it has, and when it has flown more than once, and when I know what it costs." He did not soften it. "The plates on Ward's harness took a month and the forge, and six ounces of the chest, and I don't yet know if they work. If they do, you'll have the next one, Declan, and Osric the one after."

Declan considered that, and then nodded once, the way he took an order he approved of, and went. Osric stayed a moment longer in the doorway.

"Rylan won't ask," he said. "In case you were wondering."

"I wasn't."

"No," Osric said. "But you'd have noticed, round about the time mine went on. I thought I'd save you the evening." He put his helmet on and went down the stair.

Ward tried the harness on in the pattern store at the end of the month, with the stones asleep.

Bram and Emeric fitted it between them, plate by plate, while Ward stood in the middle of the sand-floored room with his arms out and the patience of a man who had been fitted for harness since he was fourteen. The nodes sat where they had been drawn. The leather ran across his joints and did not pinch. The pack sat high on his back, heavier than a pack of stones had any right to be, and the two brass levers sat under his hands on the breastplate where his hands fell.

He walked the length of the room and back. He knelt and stood. He found both levers without looking, twice, and found the notch at the back of the left one, and said nothing about any of it.

"How does it sit?" Emeric said.

Ward thought about it.

"Heavier on the back than the front," he said. "Like the Crown's. You'll want to fix that."

"When it's flown."

"When it's flown," Ward agreed, and stood in the middle of the pattern store in a suit with a dozen stones on it and twenty more on his back, all of them asleep, with his hands resting on two brass levers that did nothing yet, and looked at the high window, where the evening was going, as though he were measuring the distance to it.
