Chapter 102 - The Fault

The new moon fell on the seventeenth, and at dusk on the seventeenth, coming back up the church lane from the powerhouse with Tess's figures for the engine's December coal under his arm, Emeric saw Rylan come out of Doran's vestry.

He came out of the side door, the one that opened onto the lane, and pulled it shut behind him, and stood in the lane with his hand still on the latch, as if he had forgotten something inside. Then he pulled his hood up and went off down the lane the other way, toward the lower ward, quickly, without looking back.

Emeric stood at the corner of the churchyard wall and let him go.

He had seen Rylan at Doran's table in August, at night, with a slate, making letters with his tongue in the corner of his mouth, and had gone the long way round so as not to be seen seeing. He supposed it was the same. A lesson, at dusk, before the board. He went the long way round again, by the well, and up to the keep, and did not think about it.



The bell at the South Gate went at a little after nine.

It was the big bell in the gate tower, the one that meant fire. Emeric was at the window before the third stroke, and he could see it from the window: a glow in the west, out past the wall and the barony's fields, low on the horizon under the black moonless sky, red and growing. A barn. One of the big ones on the west side, by the look of where it was, Farris's or the barony farm's own.

He was in the gate passage by the time Ward was, and Ward already had his sword belt on and half the garrison in the yard behind him with buckets and crossbows both.

"I'm coming."

"No." Ward did not stop moving. He was counting men off at the gate with his hand, one, two, one, two, the way he counted them onto a wall. "Milford's barn burned with nobody in it, in November, three weeks ago. Now ours." He looked at Emeric for the space of one breath. "If it is straw and an accident, I will be back in an hour and you have lost nothing. If it is men, they want you out there in the dark by a fire with half your garrison putting it out. Stay in the keep."

It was a good argument. It was exactly the argument Emeric would have made.

"Who's on the board?"

"Rylan." Ward was already through the gate. "The gate crew has the gates. Stay in the keep."

The gate closed behind them, and the bar went down, and Emeric stood in the passage and listened to the running feet go away down the west road toward the glow, until he could not hear them.

He went up to the line room before he went to bed. Rylan was on the stool with the lamp turned low, and the twelve shutters were up, and the twelve needles were still. Rylan did not get up this time. He sat with his hands on his knees and looked at the board and said that the west side was all dark stretches tonight, with half the garrison out, and that if anything came over he would know it before anybody, and Emeric said that was what the board was for, and went down to bed.

He did not sleep. He sat at the table under the window with Tess's coal figures and did not read them, and watched the glow in the west, which grew and then did not grow, and then, toward midnight, began very slowly to sink.



The knock came a little before one.

Two knocks, quiet, the way a man knocks who does not want to wake the rest of the keep. Emeric had the door open before the second.

Rylan stood in the passage with a lantern in his hand. His face was grey in its light.

"The river stair," he said. "It's chattering."

Emeric looked at him.

"Like teeth," Rylan said. "Same as October. Up and down, up and down. It won't settle." He held the lantern a little higher. "The card says wake the prince."

"Has anybody gone down?"

"There's nobody to send. Half the garrison's at the barn, and the gate crew's on the gates, and Ward said the gates aren't to be left." Rylan's voice was quite steady. "It's straw, the gate says. They'll be another hour. I can go down and look. But I can't do anything about a stone."

No. Nobody could do anything about a stone. He had said so himself, in October, in this keep, to Ward, who had said that is one man.

He was already pulling on his boots.

He took his coat, and his hand lamp out of the coat, the little charged fire stone in its cage on its cord, and put it back in the pocket unlit, because the lantern would do. They went down through the sleeping keep and across the yard and out by the postern into the town.

Kaldo at one in the morning, in December, with no moon, was very dark. The eight lamps burned on the wall above them in their long curve, white, and threw their light out over the walk and the town's roofs, and in their light the lanes below were blacker than they would have been without them, as a room is blacker round one candle. Nobody was about. A dog barked behind a door in the lower ward and was told to stop. Rylan walked half a pace ahead with the lantern, as a man walks ahead of the prince he is guarding, and did not say anything, and Emeric followed his lantern down through the lower ward toward the river.

The water gate was shut and barred. Rylan unbarred it. They went out through it onto the head of the river stair.

Below them the stair went down in its worn steps into the dark, and at the bottom the river went past, high and black and loud with the December rain, and there was nothing to see at all. The wall went up behind them. The lamps on the walk above lit the top of the wall and the sky over it and left the foot of the wall and the stair in a darkness that was almost solid. Rylan held the lantern out over the steps, and its light went down three of them and gave up.

Emeric did not look at the steps. He reached for the stone.

He did not have to go out far. He had cut it himself, in the room, with Ward in the chair by the door, and he knew where it lay under the top step the way a man knows where his own hand is in the dark. He let himself out a little, thin, the way Armand had taught him, a hand's width, two, down through the stone of the step to the stone inside it.

It was whole.

It was whole and quiet and shut tight, exactly as he had made it, sleeping, spending nothing. It was not chattering. It was not faulted. There was nothing wrong with it at all.

And below it, at the foot of the stair, in the black water, something else. Small, and very bright, much brighter than any man, swinging. Out and back. Out and back, dipping into the river and out, on the end of something thin. A charged stone, on a cord, moving in the water. And round it, close, in a boat that he could not see, three people.

He turned round.

Rylan's arm was already round him.



It came from behind, across the chest and up under the chin, and it was a knight's arm, and it was like being taken by a beam. Emeric's feet came off the step. The lantern went over somewhere and rolled down the stair, bouncing, and went into the river and out, and the dark came down on them complete.

He fought. He did not think about it. Ward had spent three years teaching him that a man taken from behind has about two breaths in which to do anything, and he used them. He drove his head back and felt it hit something that grunted. He got his weight down and his heel into the side of Rylan's knee, and the knee did not go, because a rite-made knee did not go for a heel. He got his right arm free, by luck as much as anything, the arm Rylan had not got, and brought it up over his own shoulder, open, toward Rylan's face, and fire came into it.

More than a thumb of it. He felt it come up out of him and stand in his palm a hand's width from Rylan's eyes, white at the heart, so that in its light he saw Rylan's face for the first time since the keep, close, the jaw set and the eyes open and wet, and the light went on the wet.

He could kill him. That was the thing he knew, all at once, in the light of his own hand. He could not out-wrestle a rite-made knight, and he could not out-last one, and he could not out-run one on a wet stair in the dark. He could put this in his face. A knight's tracery blunted a spell; it did not blunt fire at a hand's width. Rylan would let go. Rylan would be blind, or dead, and he would let go.

"Are you one of them too?" Emeric said.

It came out of him as a whisper, through the arm across his throat. Rylan's face did not change. His jaw worked once.

"They have Finn," he said, through his teeth.

The fire stood in Emeric's palm.

Finn on the stool in the line room in October with an apple, reading the card aloud, fast, pleased with himself. Is that you? That's Ward. A week away from school with a cough nobody had heard. At Nessa's sister's. You'll swallow a lot for a dry house.

He let the fire go out.

He let it go slowly, so that Rylan would see it go, and the dark came back down on the stair, and he let his arm drop, and stood still in Rylan's hold, and did not fight any more.

Rylan's arm shook. It went on holding him exactly as hard as before, and it shook.

There were feet on the stair below them. Wet feet, coming up, two men, three, and a hooded lantern somewhere that let out a slit of light and no more. A hand took Emeric's wrist, and then the other, and pulled them round behind him, and cord went round them, rough and wet, and was pulled tight enough that his fingers went cold at once.

"His hands," said a voice. A man's voice, quick and pleased with itself, from somewhere near his ear, smelling of river and onions. "The file says he does a fire trick. Do his hands."

They did his hands. They tied his fingers together, each one, and then bound them flat inside a strip of sacking, wound round and round and tied off, so that his hands were a dead lump behind his back and he could not have pointed at anything if he had wanted to.

"This is him?" said the same voice. "He's smaller than I thought."

Nobody answered it.

They took Rylan's arm away and somebody else's took its place, a smaller man's, harder and less careful, and walked him down the stair. Behind him he heard Rylan say something, low, and the quick voice say, quite cheerfully, that the bargain was what it was. Then a scuffle, short, and a grunt, and Rylan's voice again, and then not.

At the foot of the stair there was a skiff, long and low and black, held against the bottom step by a man in the stern with an oar. They put Emeric in the bottom of it on his face in an inch of cold water. Somebody pulled the cord up out of the river with the little bright charged stone on the end of it, streaming, and dropped it in the bottom of the skiff by his head, where it lay and glowed faintly red against the boards, a lamp stone, a Kaldo lamp stone, like the one in his own coat.

They put Rylan in after him. Bound, the same. Wrists behind him. His shoulder came down against Emeric's in the water in the bottom of the boat, and stayed there, and Emeric could feel him shaking through it.

The skiff pushed off. Up on the wall the eight lamps burned on in their long white curve over the town, and somewhere at the top of the keep the river stair's shutter on the board lay up and quiet, its needle still, with nobody on the stool in front of it to see it.
