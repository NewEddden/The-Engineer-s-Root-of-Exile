Chapter 108 - The Standing Order

It was one sheet, and Hale read it aloud in the hall on the last evening of January in the voice he used for the household's winter rota, which was the voice he used for everything.

Emeric had wanted it that way. He had written it out four times since midwinter at the table under his window, and every time it had come out longer, with more in it, clauses about clauses and a preamble that sounded like the opening of a sermon, and every time he had read it back and heard his brother's voice in it, the voice of a man reading out an honour at a banquet. The fifth time he had written it on one side of one sheet and stopped when he got to the bottom. Then he had given it to Hale to copy fair, because Hale's hand was the barony's hand and nobody would think a thing in Hale's hand was anything but ordinary business.

So they sat at the long table in the hall after supper, with the benches cleared and the knights' end empty and the fire built up, and the board-room table that Kaldo did not have was the hall table it did, scarred down its middle where forty years of barons' men had cut their meat on it without plates. Ward sat with his back to the wall, because he always did. Bram sat with his elbows on the table and his hands round a cup of the spirit and looked as if he wished he were at the forge. Imani had come straight from the ward with her sleeves still pinned up and sat at the end nearest the door, as if she might have to leave. Saban had his hat on his knee. Armand was in his own chair, which Arthur had carried in from the room off the hall and set at the fire end of the table, with the blanket over his knees.

Ferra sat across from Emeric with her pen and a blank page, as she would have sat at any reading, ready to take it down.

Hale stood at the end of the table with the sheet and a candle and read it.

It was short. It said that the barony of Kaldo would be governed, under its holder, by a Standing Order of six, who would sit at his table and hold each a part of the barony's business, and be answerable to him for it, and to each other. It said that the Order would meet on the first Monday of every month and whenever the holder or any two of the six required it. And then it named them, one line each, the way a rota names the men on the wall.

"Ward," Hale read. "Secretary of War. The garrison, the levy, the wall, the gate, the roads and the barony's arms."

Ward did not move.

"Well," said Armand, from his chair, "that's the only title on the sheet that means exactly what it says."

"Bram," Hale read. "Secretary of Works. The forge, the works, the mill, the engine and every building the barony puts up or pulls down."

Bram looked into his cup.

"Saban. Secretary of Trade. The barony's buying and selling, its markets, its roads to the coast and the north, and its knowledge of what passes on them."

Saban turned his hat round on his knee, once.

"Trade," he said. He seemed to taste it. "The most honest lie on the sheet." He looked round the table, pleasantly. "I do trade. Mostly I trade what I hear for what I want to hear."

"Imani. Secretary of Health. The ward, the wells, the water, the drains, the burials, and the barony's food." Hale paused, very slightly, as he did at a line he had been told to read exactly. "The barony's food is added here at the holder's direction."

Imani looked at Emeric down the table.

"Food," she said.

"Half of what comes through your door in the winter comes in because it hasn't eaten. You said so in the summer. You said it to Maren, in the kitchen, so loud the yard heard you."

"I said Maren's beans were the only reason the dock quarter wasn't scurvied." Imani thought about it. "It's true." She sat back. "I'll want Maren."

"She's yours."

"Armand," Hale read. "Secretary of the Arcane. The barony's mages, its stones, its charging and its craft. And by the seventh clause below, the holder's authority in Kaldo when the holder is away from it."

The table looked at Armand. Armand looked at the fire.

"I'm too old," he said.

"You're the only one at this table," said Emeric, "who the Circle can't call a commoner and the garrison won't call a stranger. If I'm in the capital and a mage comes to the gate with a writ, I want the man who answers him to be somebody the mage has to bow to."

"He won't bow to me. He'll pity me."

"Then let him do it at the gate," said Ward, "while I count his men."

Armand looked at Ward, and something in his face moved, and he did not argue again.

"Ferra," Hale read. "Chancellor."

Ferra's pen stopped.

"Not a Secretary," Hale went on, in exactly the same voice. "The barony's books, all of them. Every Secretary's accounts pass through the Chancellor's hand, and every brief laid before the holder is laid before her. The census, and its record of the barony's weaknesses. The barony's schooling. And a fund at her own discretion, for which she accounts to the holder alone."

Bram had looked up from his cup. He was looking at his daughter across the table, and he did not look as though he knew what to do with his face.

"Why isn't she a Secretary?" he said. "Like the rest of us?"

"Because a Secretary holds a column," said Emeric. "The Chancellor holds the ledger the columns sum into."

Bram looked at him. Then he looked back at Ferra, who had not moved, and whose pen was still stopped on the page an inch from the last word she had written, and he picked his cup up again and drank from it, and put it down, and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and said nothing at all, very loudly.

Hale read the next clauses. The meetings. The seventh, on Armand. The eighth, that any two of the six might require the holder to hear a matter at the table, and he must hear it.

"The ninth," Hale read.

Emeric watched Ferra.

"The Chancellor may stay any act of the holder by writing her stay, with her reasons, in the barony's book, over her name and the date. A stay so written holds until the Chancellor lifts it. The holder may not set it aside."

Nobody said anything.

Hale lowered the sheet. He did it carefully, because it was finished, and he put it down on the table in front of the empty place at the end and squared it, and stood back.

Ferra had put her pen down.

She was looking at Emeric across the table. Her face had gone very white, which it did not do easily, because it was white to begin with, and the freckles stood out across her nose like something spilled.

"I didn't write that," she said.

"No."

"Hale wrote that."

"I wrote it. Hale copied it fair."

"I copied what I was given," said Hale, from the end of the table, to nobody, "in the form in which I was given it."

Ferra did not look at Hale.

"Strike it," she said.

"No."

"You can't give a clerk a stay over a prince."

"I'm not giving it to a clerk. I'm giving it to the Chancellor."

"Who was a clerk at noon." Her voice was quite level. Her hand was flat on the table beside the page, and he could see it pressed down. "It says you can't set it aside. Do you know what that means? It means if I write it, it stands. Against you. Against the barony. Whatever I write. If I'm wrong, it stands. If I'm frightened, it stands. If I'm bought."

"You're not going to be bought."

"You don't know that."

"I know you started a column in your book on me the year we built the still," Emeric said. "In your own hand. I read it upside down. You thought I couldn't."

Ferra stopped.

Across the table Saban had very carefully begun to examine the lining of his hat.

"You'd stay a thing," Emeric said, "that you thought would hurt Kaldo. That's all it's for. Not the wrong thing. The thing I can't see because I'm the one doing it." He did not look away from her. "Every time I've done that, you've seen it. You've said so, and I've overruled you, and some of those times you were right. Dunnel's dead because of one of them. I'm not giving you the stay because you'll never be wrong. I'm giving it to you because I'm going to be."

The fire snapped. Somebody's chair creaked.

Ferra looked at him for a while longer. Then she picked up her pen, and pulled the page toward her, and wrote. It was not long. She wrote it in the hand she used for the book, small and upright, and when she had finished she turned the page round on the table and pushed it across to him, the right way up for him to read.

Clause nine. The Chancellor objects. Said so.

He read it. Then he took her pen, which she had left lying on the page, and wrote under it.

Overruled.

He pushed it back.

Ferra read it. Her mouth moved, very slightly, at one corner, and stopped, and she turned the page back round and put it with the others, squared, and folded her hands on top of the pile.

"That's the last time you get to do that," she said.

"I know," said Emeric.



The first thing the Standing Order did was overrule him.

Ward did it. He did it the way he did everything, without raising his voice, before Hale had taken the candle off the table, and he did it under the eighth clause, which Emeric had written that week for exactly this kind of thing and had not expected to be used against him in the first hour.

"The board," Ward said. "The card in the line room. I changed it in December."

"I know."

"I changed it on my own authority, because I was captain and the board was the garrison's, and you let it stand." Ward looked down the table. "It should not stand on my authority. It should stand on this table's. And it should not stand only for the board." He put his hand flat on the scarred wood. "I require the table to hear it. With Bram." He did not look at Bram. Bram nodded, once, into his cup.

"Hear what?"

"That nothing in this barony wakes one man." Ward said it the way he would have said the hour of a watch. "No system. No key. No lock. No board, no gate, no strongbox, no stone. If it can only be opened by one man, or mended by one man, or known by one man, it gets a second, and the second is named at this table and written in Ferra's book." He took his hand off the table. "After Rylan, nobody is going to sleep in this town with a thing that runs on one key again. Not me. Not the garrison. Not the women in the lower ward, who know what happened on that stair as well as we do."

Emeric had seen it coming from the third word. He had seen it on the card in December, and he had let the card stand because Ward was right, and he had known since the corner by the cooper's that Ferra had done the same sum in her own way. He had still not thought it would come to the table in the first hour, from the man who stood behind him.

"The gate keys," he said. "The strongbox. Yes. Of course. Most of those have two already." He looked at Hale, who nodded. "The board, yes. Declan sits it as well as Rylan, and Tess can mend it as well as I can, or nearly. But not everything in Kaldo can have a second just because the table says so." He kept his voice level. "There are things in this barony I made, that only I can mend. Not because I won't teach them. Because the teaching takes years, and the people I'd teach them to don't exist yet. The stones in the wall. Some of what's in the board. You can name a second man for them at this table, and write him in the book, and he'll stand there when they break and not know what he's looking at."

"Then he will know where to look," said Armand.

Everyone turned.

The old man had not moved in his chair. He had his hands folded on the blanket and his eyes on the fire, and he spoke to the fire, in the lecture-hall voice, the one that made a room go quiet because it expected to be listened to.

"The stones have their second," he said. "Since the night of the frost. I can't mend them. I'll never mend them; I'm too old and too slow, and my hands shake. But I know how they're made. I know what's been done to them, and where, and I could teach a mage of the right sort to begin to learn it, if the prince were in the river." He turned his head and looked down the table at Emeric, and his face was perfectly mild. "You came to me with it yourself. On a cold night. Because of a page somebody else burned." He looked back at the fire. "So it seems to me the holder has already conceded the principle, and only objects to the table saying so."

Nobody said anything. Emeric could feel Ferra not looking at him.

"I back Ward," said Armand. "For the stones and for everything else. Every system that wakes one man gets a second. And where nobody yet knows enough to be a real second, then the second is a name in Ferra's book and a promise, and the holder teaches him what he can, as fast as he can, and the table asks after it every month until he's done."

"I'll back that," said Imani at once, from the end of the table. "I've had a second on every key in my ward since the plague. I didn't need a prince to tell me."

"Bram," said Ward.

"Aye," said Bram.

Saban looked at the lining of his hat a moment longer. Then he put it back on his knee.

"I'm away more than any man here," he said. "If a thing in Kaldo needs me to open it, it'll be shut nine months in twelve. Give it a second." He looked at Emeric, mildly. "Give me one too, while you're at it. I'm not as young as the roads."

That was five.

Ferra had not said anything. She had her hands folded on top of her squared pages and was looking at them. Everybody waited for her, and she let them wait, which Emeric had never in his life seen her do at a table.

"It's a vote," she said at last. "Five to one before I say. It doesn't need me."

"Say anyway," said Ward.

Ferra looked up. She looked at Emeric, and he saw that she was going to be exact, because she was always exact, and that it was going to cost her something to be.

"There was a line on the last page," she said. "Of a thing I wrote and burned. It said, the stones, and then it said, nobody." She did not look away. "I'm not going to sit at a table where that line's still true and not say so. Yes."

Six.

Emeric sat and looked at the six of them, at his own table, in the hall, on the first night, and could not find anything to say that was not either a sulk or a speech, so he said neither.

"Write it," he said to Hale. "The first act of the Standing Order. Every system that wakes one man gets a second. Hale, you'll need a fair copy of the list by Monday. Every key in the barony, and who holds it, and who holds the other."

Hale had already sat down at the end of the table with a fresh page, and was writing.

"It's a long list," he said, without looking up, with the faint satisfaction of a man who had been waiting for somebody to ask him for it. "I have most of it."



Afterward, when Hale had gone and Bram had gone and Imani had gone back down the hill to the ward with her sleeves still pinned, Emeric went to the chest in his room and came back down to the hall with a small box of plain wood, and put it on the table, and opened it.

There were nine stones in it, on a bed of old flannel, grey and dull. Corbin's gift, from the banquet. Nine Hollow Crystals, carved over every face in a fine repeating lattice, set down in front of two hundred people with both hands, unhurried. Something to bring back the old passion. He had not looked at them again that night, and he had looked at very little else since.

Ward, Saban, Armand and Ferra were still at the table. He had asked them to stay. He would go down to the ward and the forge in the morning for the other two.

He took out four stones and put one in front of each of them.

Ward looked at his and did not touch it. Ferra looked at hers and put her hand over it at once, as she might have put her hand over a coin left on a trestle, and then looked at the hand as if it had done it without her. Armand did not look at his at all. He had held Emeric's mother's for a quarter of an hour on the night of the frost, and he knew what it was.

Saban picked his up and turned it over. He held it to the candle and looked through it, which was impossible, because it was opaque, and then weighed it in his palm.

"It's a Hollow Crystal," he said. "The court's trinket. My father sold three of these once to a cloth merchant in the capital who wanted something for his mantel that would make him look like a man who read." He weighed it again. "He got a Silver apiece and thought he'd robbed the man."

"Keep it," said Emeric. "On you. In a pocket, where you'd keep a knife. Don't put it on a mantel and don't sell it. In a fortnight I'll ask you to bring it to my room after supper and hold it, and I'll show you what it's for."

"And what's it for?"

"It's a door," said Emeric.

Saban looked at him. Then at the other three, who had their stones in front of them and were not surprised, and he saw that they were not surprised, and Emeric watched him take that in, and do something with it behind his face that a man in his work does with a thing he has been told by the way it was not said.

"The six of you," Saban said slowly. "Or the five of them, and me, last."

"You, last," said Emeric. "Because you're outside the walls more than anyone at this table. Nobody can make a man go through a door. But a man who knows there's a door can be made to talk about it."

Saban thought about that for some time.

"That's fair," he said at last. He put the stone in the inside pocket of his coat, where Emeric had seen him keep his knife. "That's very fair. I'd not have told me either." He picked up his hat. "I'll still have a word about it, on the road, with whoever asks me. It'll be the wrong word."



The room was on the second floor of the keep, on the south side, where the morning came in.

It had been a storeroom for the old baron's linen, and before that a bedchamber for somebody's aunt, and before that nobody knew. It had a window, and a fireplace that drew if you were patient with it, and a floor of good oak under the dust. Hale found it on the list of the keep's rooms on the first of February and Ferra walked up the stair to look at it on the second, and on the fourth there was a trestle in it, and a shelf, and the strongbox's second key on a nail behind the door, and the left-hand box from the exchange on the trestle with its lid shut, because Ferra had carried the left-hand box up the keep stair herself, in both arms, and would not let anyone else touch it.

On the sixth somebody painted her name on the door.

It was not Ferra. Emeric found out later that it had been Hale, who had asked the sign painter from the market square to come up the stair, and stood over him while he did it, and paid him out of his own pocket so that it would not be in anybody's book. It was small, in plain black letters, at the height of a woman's eyes. Ferra, Chancellor.

Emeric came up the stair that afternoon on his way to the line room and found her in the passage outside it.

She was standing in front of the door with a bundle of ledgers under her arm, not going in. She was looking at the name.

He stopped beside her. He did not say anything. After a while she spoke without looking round.

"I told you I don't come up to the keep."

"You told me you don't come up to the keep and stand in a corner waiting to be told." He looked at the door. "That's not a corner."

Ferra looked at it a little longer. The letters were still a little wet. Somebody had put a candle on the sill of the passage window across from it, which was not a place anybody kept a candle, so that the name would show in the dark.

"Hale," she said.

"I think so."

"He'll have paid for it himself." She shifted the ledgers on her arm. "He won't have put it anywhere. I'll never find it."

"Do you want to?"

Ferra did not answer that. She reached past him and opened the door, and went in, and put the ledgers on the trestle, and turned round in the middle of the room with its one window and its fire that drew if you were patient with it, and looked at him standing in the passage.

"You can come in," she said. "It's not your room."

He came in. He stood by the window while she lit the fire, which took her three tries, and did not offer to help, because she would not have let him. When it had caught she stood up and dusted her hands and looked at it.

"Said so," she said, to the fire.

"Overruled," said Emeric.

And she laughed. Not much. It came out of her before she could stop it, short and surprised, and she put the back of her wrist against her mouth as if she could put it back, and turned to the trestle and opened the first ledger, and did not look up again, and he went out and shut her door behind him and went on up to the line room with the sound of it in his ears all the way up the stair.
