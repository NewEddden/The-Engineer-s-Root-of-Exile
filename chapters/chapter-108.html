Chapter 108 - Three Times

The new pack weighed less than the old one, and held three times as much, and nobody who picked it up believed either thing until Ferra had put it on her scales.

It was the same felt-lined box of iron plate on Ward's back, and inside it, where twenty lamp stones had sat in their grit, there were now nine cells in their casings, lying side by side like eggs in a crate, each with its neck turned the same way and seated in a socket. The sockets ran along a rail. The rail was stone, a single rod drawn out on the forge in one piece the length of the box, as thick as a pencil, and from the end of it another rod ran up under the back plate to the deciding stone on the breast, and from there, in rods again, down the plates to every node. There was no grit anywhere in it, except across the places where a man bends, and there the leather tubes were a finger long instead of a hand, because there was a stone rod waiting on each side of them.

On the breast, beside the cover, Bram had set a splinter from the cells' caps, wired to the rail, where Ward could look down and see it. It glowed when there was charge behind it and went dull when there was not.

"So I can read my own," Ward said, looking at it.

"So you can read your own."

Ward considered that for some time. It was the kind of thing he approved of without saying so, which with Ward meant he looked at it again before he put the breastplate on.

They flew him in the foundry on the first cool night of the late summer, under the same roof, over the same sand, with Imani on the same stool and her canvas roll across her knees.

There was one change to the lines, and it was Imani's. She had sat by the east wall in midsummer and watched a man in plate fly circles under a tie-beam eighteen feet from the floor, on four lines that would stop him going higher and would do nothing at all to stop him coming down, and she had said nothing about it at the time, and three days later she had come to the long room and asked what held him if the stones went at the top of the circle. Nothing, Emeric had said. The sand. She had looked at him for rather a long time and gone away, and the next day Bram had come to him with a drawing. So now there were two lines more, from two rings high on Ward's back to two blocks lashed to the tie-beams themselves, running through the blocks and down to cleats on the wall, taken in and paid out by nobody, only belayed, so that if everything on Ward stopped at once he would stop with it, hanging, a foot below the beams, and come down on a rope instead of onto sand.

"It is a floor," Imani said, when she saw it rigged. "It's a floor that happens to be above him."

Ward went up.

He went up the way he had gone up at midsummer, a notch at a time on the left lever, and hung, and swayed, and stopped swaying, and put the push on and leaned into it, and went round. He went round faster than at midsummer and more easily, the way a man goes back to a thing his body has been thinking about for two months without telling him. Emeric stood at his chalk line with the medium open and watched the pack.

It thinned. It thinned slowly. He called it every hundred counts this time, because every twenty would have been saying the same thing over and over, and Armand wrote it down. At three hundred the cells were where the lamp stones had been at a hundred. At six hundred they were where the lamp stones had been when Emeric called Ward down. And there was no heat in any of it. He looked for it, along the rail and at every socket and in the finger-long leather at the bends, and there was none. The rods were cold. The necks were cold. The charge went where it was sent and arrived.

"Six hundred and forty," said Ward, from the air, and then, "Splinter's going."

He had seen it himself, on his breast, and he did not wait to be told. He came out of the circle and took the push off and came down, and a foot off the sand he went past the notch and planted, heels heavy, in the hole he had made at midsummer.

Three times as long. The joints cold. It was still, when Armand had written it out and Ferra had turned it into minutes, a little over ten.

The pack went to Orrin the same night, and he filled the nine cells in turn before he went to bed, and complained about his arms, and said it was still less than a lamp. In the morning the splinter on Ward's breast was bright again.

"Ten minutes," Ward said, at the well, after the morning's work, as though he were reporting how far it was to a village.

"Ten minutes."

"That's not a road."

"No."

"It's a wall," Ward said. "It's a river. It's the far side of a gate before the gate's shut. It's getting a man off a roof." He was quiet for a moment. "It's what a man does in a fight for the length of one fight. Then he walks home."

"Then that's what it is."

"That's what it is," Ward agreed, "for now," and picked up his practice blade.

He flew nine more times before the end of the summer.

Emeric had told Declan not until Ward's had flown, and flown more than once, and he had meant it, and after the third flight Declan stopped asking and started coming to watch, standing by the doors with his helmet under his arm, saying nothing, which for Declan was a considerable effort. On the fifth flight Ward flew a figure of eight. On the seventh he came down onto a mark Bram had drawn in the sand and planted his heels on it. On the ninth, with the cells three-quarters full, he took the push to full for a count of three from the air, on the lines, and the roar went out over the lake again, and Saban had nothing new from the lower town inside the week because by then the lower town had decided that the foundry made that noise sometimes and it was nobody's business.

After the tenth, Emeric sent for Bram and told him to start on the second set of plates.

Declan came to the pattern store to be measured, and stood in the middle of the sand with his arms out like a man being fitted for a wedding, as Osric had once said, and did not look at himself in anything. Osric came with him and sat on the hold bench, which he was not supposed to do, and watched.

"Mine after his," Osric said.

"Yours after his."

"And Rylan's after mine, if he asks."

Emeric looked at him.

"He won't," Osric said. "I'm only saying there's a fourth set of plates in the book, in case anybody's keeping count. Somebody generally is."

The glassworks came next, and it was not planned. It was Orrin's arms.

Orrin had charged Ward's pack ten times that summer, and the printer's stones every evening, and the lamp stones for the forge's cup before the forge began feeding itself, and in the middle of all of it the glass furnace had been lit for its autumn run, which meant that three days in six he sat on a stool by the gauge with Vesk for six hours at a time, turn and turn about, pouring into the fire stones in the hearth by the hour, the way the two of them had done since the first pane. He did not complain about the furnace. He had never once complained about the furnace. He complained about his arms, cheerfully, at length, to anybody on the long street who would stand still for it, and one evening Emeric heard it from the gallery and stopped on the stair, and stood there for some time.

Every pane in the barony existed because a set of stones held a heat and two men sat on a stool keeping them fed. He had said once, to Ferra, that it was the one thing he had built that stopped when the magic stopped. It still would. But it did not have to stop when the men got tired.

The furnace's gauge was a splinter of crystal in the hearth wall, which glowed brighter as the hearth got hotter, and for three years Orrin and Vesk had watched it and poured to keep it at a scratch on the brass beside it. A reading stone could watch a splinter. It had been watching an oil dish in the forge since midsummer. And a deciding stone could be told a rule as simple as that one, and never get tired of it.

He built it over four nights with Bram, and it was the easiest thing either of them had built all year. A bank of twelve cells in a rack against the furnace wall, with their necks in a rail. A reading stone on a bracket, looking at the splinter. A deciding stone in a housing, told once: keep the splinter at the scratch. Feed the fire stones what that takes and nothing over. The rods ran from the rail to the hearth through the wall, and the stool by the gauge stayed where it was, because nobody thought to move it.

On the first night of the run Orrin sat on it anyway, out of habit, for the first hour. He watched the splinter sit on its scratch without him, and put his hand out toward the fire stones twice without meaning to, and took it back, and at the end of the hour he got up and stood by the stool for a while as if he were waiting to be told he could go.

"It'll do that all night," Emeric said.

"All night."

"And tomorrow. Bram will change the bank in the morning and I'll fill the empties in the evening. So will you, if you like. It's a quarter of the pouring and none of the sitting."

Orrin looked at the splinter. He had watched it for three years, six hours at a time. He had talked through most of those hours, to Vesk, to himself, to the fire stones, and at the end of every one of them he had gone home to a bed that he said was the only thing in Kaldo he had never been asked to improve.

"I shan't know what to do with the evenings," he said.

"Teach."

"I teach all day."

"Then sleep."

Orrin laughed, once, and went home. Vesk did not. He stayed by the stool with his arms folded, the way he had stood at the back of the etching floor when Emeric came down to tell the twelve about their sick days, looking at the rack of cells and the rods going through the wall and the small grey reading stone on its bracket watching a splinter he had watched for three years. He did not say anything about any of it. After a while he went out, and Emeric saw the light go on in the etching floor across the yard, late, where Vesk sat with a notebook nobody had ever read.

The Crown's letter came in the first week of autumn.

It came up the road from Milford with the notary who held the Crown's seal at Grayrock, in a packet with the chancery's mark on it, and it was addressed to the holder of the barony of Kaldo under the writ of last year, and it was polite. The Crown had observed that no gravity stone had been offered to it for purchase from the barony of Kaldo in the present season. The Crown had observed that the barony's second raising had been declared at the turn of the year and its fifth duly delivered. The Crown had received reports, which it did not credit and did not dismiss, of works at Kaldo involving that stone. The Crown would be glad to be informed, at the holder's convenience, why none had been offered for sale, and what the barony was doing with it.

Emeric read it aloud at the table in the long room, to the six, once.

Nobody said anything for a moment. It was Saban who spoke first, as it had been Saban in the tally house at Grayrock.

"Reports," he said. "That's the fifth version. The one with flying in it." He turned his cup round on the table. "They've had it off a boat, or a carter, or a man in a Milford inn who had it off a man from the quay. It doesn't matter which. What matters is they've written the word works. You don't write works about a barge."

Ferra had the writ out of her strongbox before he had finished. She laid it on the table beside the letter and put her finger on the clause, near the foot, in the small hand.

"The right of first purchase," she read, "of any gravity stone sold out of the barony." She took her finger away. "Sold. It binds sales. It says nothing about use. The fifth is paid on every raising, as the writ provides. What's left after the fifth is ours, free and clear, with a king's receipt to prove it. The Crown has first call on any stone we sell. We haven't sold any. That's the whole of the answer."

"It's correct," said Saban.

"It's correct."

"It'll not be liked," Saban said. "A man who writes to ask what you're doing with a thing doesn't want to be told that it's yours. He wants to be told what you're doing with it."

"I know."

"Then you'll tell him?"

"I'll tell him what the writ says." Emeric put the letter down. "That the barony has sold no stone this season, and therefore offered none. That the Crown's fifth has been paid on every raising. That what the barony holds after the fifth it holds as the charter allows, and uses as the charter allows, and that the holder is grateful for the Crown's interest." He looked down the table. "Every word of it true. None of it an answer to the question he asked."

"He'll notice," said Saban.

"He's meant to."

Ward had stood at the window through all of it, which was his way of listening. He turned round.

"Works," he said. "Four farms heard her go down the line."

"Works involving that stone," said Ferra. "I signed for every rivet in her. There isn't a grain of it."

Ward looked at the letter for a moment, and then out of the window again. "Then it isn't this one," he said.

Nobody argued. Imani, at her end of the table, had said nothing at all, and was looking not at the letter but at Armand.

Armand had not moved since the letter was read. He sat with his hands folded on the table in front of him, where anybody could see them, and his eyes on the chancery's mark on the packet, and he did not look up when Ferra read the clause, or when Saban said works, or when Emeric said none of it was an answer. He looked at the mark. It was the mark of the chancery, which sealed the Crown's letters. The chancery was in the capital, and so was the tower, and that was the whole of what Emeric knew about how the two of them spoke to each other, which was not enough to know whether they did.

He did not ask Armand what he was thinking. The old man had told him once what his clock said, and had not told him what would stop it.

Ferra wrote the reply fair that night, in her hand, from his draft, and he signed it, and it went down the road to Milford in the notary's packet under Kaldo's seal. She entered it in the dispatch book: the day, the carrier, the Crown. In the margin, where the book had no column for it, she wrote nothing at all.

He noticed that. He did not know what to do with having noticed it, and after a while he stopped trying.
