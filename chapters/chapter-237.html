Chapter 237 - The Dark Between Brothers

The bags came through the water an hour after the line woke.

Emeric felt it wake before he heard it: the talking line, still all day in its rings, going taut in the dark at the water's edge, and then the pull coming down it, one, from Wes at the top, and then Ward's long slow draw that meant we have you. He was at the water before the second one came. He answered, one. And then, a little after, the haul line began to move.

On the working nights Ward's lamp in its tube stayed dark. There were forty men on the benches over the hollow now, with their lanterns, and a light going up out of the rock under them was a thing even a man looking at his own pick might see. So Ward kept the mouth in the dark, under the lowest bench, with the picks going over his head all night, and carried what he had to tell the ship west on foot at the end of the night, a mile, to the boat line in the dunes. Nobody on the benches ever came down to the lowest one. Nobody on them, Ward said, ever looked down.

It went out through the flooded stair on its pulleys from a ring bolted in the rock at the top of the steps, down under the water and up the far side to Wes, and on the third night and the fifth it brought them what they had not been able to carry through on the first. Sir Aled hauled. He hauled the way he did everything, steadily, hand over hand, without hurrying and without stopping, and the line came up out of the black water wet and cold and went round the ring and down into the coil at his feet, and after a long while a shape came up out of the water on the end of it, and then another: two long bags of tarred canvas, lashed tight, as heavy as a man each, streaming.

Bottles, in the first. The half set. Four of the long steel bottles Bram had bored out of the rail works' best bar, three for the divers and one to stand at the bottom, painted round their shoulders with a band of red lead so that nobody in the dark could take one for pure breath by mistake; and three canisters of fresh potash in their black horn cases, sealed with wax. In the second, water, and bread, and oil for the one lamp that was allowed a flame, and a packet from Imani with a note in it in her plain upright hand that said Joints. Tell me every one. and nothing else.

"She means the bends," said Ferra, reading it over his shoulder.

"She means the bends."

"She's worried."

"She's never worried." He folded the note and put it in his suit. "She's careful. It sounds the same."

The inner shaft was at the far end of the city, and it took them most of the night to stage the gas at its head, because the far end of the city was a long way.

They went through the great hall, past the wall, with their lamps down, not looking at it; nobody had said they would not look at it, and none of them did. Out of the hall on its far side by a door in the painted winter, where the branches were bare and the tall ones were carrying baskets down out of them into the dark, there was a stair going down. It went down a long way, the tall steps under their hand lines, down through three levels of the city, each emptier and older and colder than the last, and at the bottom of it there was a room.

It was square, and bare, and cut very clean, and in the middle of its floor there was a hole.

A shaft. Square too, as wide as a cart, going straight down. Its walls were cut as smoothly as the walls of the halls above, and in one of them, going down it into the dark, there were notches for hands and feet, spaced for the tall ones' reach. And an arm's length below the rim, lying perfectly still, flat as a floor, black as the oil in the drum, with their five lamps lying on it in five small blurs, water.

They stood round the rim and looked at it. Nobody said anything.

Emeric knelt at the edge and let the sense go down.

He had felt it from the ridge in the winter, the deep water, at the edge of what he could feel; he had felt it from the top of the steps on the first night. He felt it now close to, and it went down. Straight down, the shaft, for a long way: sixty feet, he thought, a little more, the square walls going on down under the water as smooth as they were above it. Then at the bottom, a turn. A passage, going away level from the shaft's foot, long, a hundred paces, more, he could not tell; and then going up again. Up a long way, as far as it had come down, and more. And at the top of that, where the water stopped, space. Air. A great deal of it. And in the air, thick, heavy, slow, so dense that when the sense touched it he flinched the way a hand flinches from a hot iron and drew back before he meant to: the medium. Not the band. More than the band, in a way he had no word for, packed into that space like grain in a sack.

He took the sense back and knelt there with his hand flat on the cold stone of the rim.

"Well?" said Zale.

"Sixty feet down. Along. Then up. It comes out in air." He did not say the rest. Not here, with the knights standing round. "It's the half set. All the way. There's no stretch of it shallow enough for pure breath past the first ten feet."

They staged it on a ledge cut in the room's wall by the shaft's rim, where the tall ones had kept something once, and there was a smoke mark over it. The three red-banded bottles, the three canisters, the spare mouthpieces, the spare reel. Ferra checked each one against her book and made a mark. Emeric checked each bottle's gauge himself, and then checked it again, and then opened a hair of each valve, one at a time, over the candle in its jar that Sir Aled had carried down, the way he had checked them in the gas shed, and watched the flame, and closed the valve. The flame did what it should. He had made the half set himself, in the gas shed, a week before the ships left, and checked it three times, and he had brought it through the water and down three levels of a dead city, and he checked it again, because he did not know what three levels of a dead city might do to a valve and would rather know now.

Then they laid the line down the shaft. Only a little way. Wes's reel, tied off to a bolt Sir Gavin drove into the rim's edge, and paid out down the notched wall to the waterline and into it, and weighted, and left. The first knot in it lay under the black water a man's length down, pale, like a pale fish.

They put the lamps low after that and went back up the stair, because there was nothing else to be done until the next night, and because the room with the shaft in it was not a room anybody wanted to sit in.

Zale asked about the drunk in the dark, on the way back, on the stair.

They were behind the others. That was not an accident, Emeric thought afterwards; Zale had been behind the others all night, and Emeric had fallen in with him, which was not an accident either. The knights and Ferra were a turn of the stair above them, their lamps going up the wall in a slow bright procession, and the two of them were in the dark between the lamps, on the tall steps, with a hand each on the line.

"Can I ask you a thing," Zale said.

"You're going to."

"I am." Zale went up a step, and stopped, and waited for his brother to come up level. "The drinking. At home. At court. Before." He did not look at Emeric. He looked up the stair at the lamps. "I heard about it in every port. The king's third son. You know the stories. The one about the ambassador and the fish. I told that one myself, in taverns, for a laugh. I told it in Merrow." He went up another step. "And then I came home, and you were this. You've been this for years. You were this before the exile, weren't you. Some of it. You didn't learn to build a flying ship in a barony full of plague." He stopped again. "So what was it? Were you always this, and pretending? Or were you that, and then something happened?"

Emeric went up two steps before he answered, because the steps were tall and he needed his breath, and because he needed the time.

"Both," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the true one. I'll give you the rest." He stopped on the step, with the line in his hand. "When I was seven, after the carriage, somebody tried to kill me four times in fourteen months. I don't know if you knew that. You were at the other end of the palace with the arms master, and nobody told the other end of the palace anything."

Zale was very still on the step below. "I knew about the carriage. Everybody knew about the carriage." A pause. "I didn't know about the four."

"Nobody did. That was the point." Emeric looked at the lamps going up the wall above them. "Father came to my room when I was eight and sat on the bed. He had a face on, the one he wears when he's pleasant over something that would kill a man if he let it out. He said a threat gets removed and a joke gets ignored. He said he was going to make me a joke. He said I was going to help." He heard his own voice in the dark, level, and did not like it much. "So I did. I was good at it. I was very good at it. Drunk in the right rooms. Stupid in front of the right people. I built the question about the fish for two days, Zale. I wanted it to go round three kingdoms by winter. It did. You told it in Merrow."

Zale said nothing.

"That part was a choice. His and mine. I was eight and I thought it was the best game anybody had ever given me, because the prize was nobody putting anything in my food." He started up the stair again, slowly. "The rest wasn't a plan. That's the part I can't give you a better answer to. It worked. That's all. It kept working. Nobody ever came to my room and said the danger's over, take it off. So I kept it on. And one day I was nineteen and I hadn't opened a book since I was twelve and I couldn't remember deciding not to." He stopped again. "The act ate the actor. I told Ward that on the road south, the first week. I'd been looking for the seam between the one I was pretending to be and the one I was, and there wasn't one. I'd become it. The hidden thing was a choice. The years after it weren't anything. They just happened, because it worked."

"And then?"

"And then I was sent south. And I found out, on the road, that the seam was still there after all. That's all. I found it." He did not say how. He had never said how, to anybody, and he was not going to say it on a stair in the dark to a man who had been at sea for half his life. "It took a while. Some of it's still coming back."

They climbed. The lamps above them had gone round the turn and out of sight, and the stair was dark except for their own two, low, at their chests.

"I did the same sum," Zale said.

Emeric stopped.

"From the other end." Zale had stopped too, a step below, and was looking at the wall in front of his face, at the smooth stone with his lamp lying on it. "I was the second son. I was good at things. Riding. Swords. Talking. Boats. Everybody said so. The masters said so in front of Corbin." He was quiet. "And I saw Corbin hear it. I was still a boy. I saw his face, at the table, when the arms master said I had the best seat on a horse he'd trained in twenty years. And I did the sum. A threat gets removed. I didn't have Father to come and sit on my bed and tell me how to be a joke. So I did the other thing. I left." He laughed, very quietly, at the wall. "You made yourself nothing so they'd leave you alone. I made myself nowhere. Same sum. Different answer."

"Your mother left because you left."

"I know." Zale said it very quietly. "I didn't know she would. I've thought about that for half my life." He looked up the stair, at the dark where the lamps had gone. "She went to Emberfell and the court said she'd been sent. She hadn't. She went because there was nobody left in the capital she wanted to see every morning." He started to climb again. "Except you. And you were drunk."

Emeric did not say anything to that, because there was nothing to say.

They climbed the rest of the stair in the dark between the lamps, side by side, a step at a time, with a hand each on the line; and when they came up out of it into the painted winter of the great hall, where the others had waited for them with the lamps held up, neither of them said anything about it, and nobody asked.

Zale went to the scholar when they were back.

Emeric saw him go. The camp under the stair had settled; Sir Aled was asleep on his back and Sir Gavin was on the bottom step with his back to the stair, keeping the watch nobody needed kept, and Ferra was on her cot with the book on her knees and the sand-glass beside her. Zale got up off his cot without a word and took his lamp and went out across the street and into the great hall, and Emeric, after a while, followed him, not close.

He was in the little room in the autumn, sitting on the floor against the wall across from Ansel Vey, with his lamp on the floor between them, turned low. He was not doing anything. He was sitting there with his knees up and his arms round them, the way he had sat in the middle of the great hall on the second night, and looking at the dead man. Emeric stopped in the doorway, in the painted leaves, and did not go in.

"He went looking," Zale said, without turning his head. He had known his brother was there. He always knew where the door was, and who was in it. "That's all he did. He wanted to know where the signs came from. So he went. On his own. Across the world. Off the edge of the map in a boat somebody gave him, with a candle and a notebook." He looked at the brown shape against the wall, with its collar up, and its hands in its lap. "And he found them. He found all of it. He stood in that hall and drew it. And he didn't come back."

Emeric said nothing.

"That's me," Zale said. "You know that. That's what I do. That's the whole of what I've done since I left. I went looking. I've been off the edge of both maps. I've never known what I was looking for, but I went." He was quiet for a long time. "I used to think the ones who didn't come back were the unlucky ones. Or the stupid ones. Or the ones who didn't know where the door was."

"And now?"

"Now I think he knew exactly where the door was. It's in his book. He walked down to it every morning to look at the water. It just wasn't open." Zale put his chin on his knees. "He wasn't stupid. He wasn't unlucky. He found the thing he'd crossed the world for and it was beautiful and he wrote it down. And then the water came up." He looked at the book in its oilcloth, on the floor beside him; he had brought it with him from the camp, Emeric saw, and put it down there, beside the man it belonged to, as if to let them sit together for a while. "It could have been me. Any of a dozen times. It could have been me in a room like this with a book in my lap, and nobody ever coming, and a brother at home who thought I was at sea."

"I'd have come," Emeric said.

Zale turned his head and looked at him in the doorway.

"I know," he said. "I know that now. That's the thing, isn't it." He looked back at the dead man. "You'd have come. You've got a flying ship and a suit for going under water and a way of talking in a man's head. You'd have come and found me, wherever it was, and I'd never have known you could." He was quiet. "I came home to look after you. And you're the one who'd have come."

Emeric came into the room then, and sat down on the floor beside his brother, against the wall, across from Ansel Vey of Merrow Sound, and they sat there, the three of them, with the lamp between them, and did not say anything else for a long time.

He told them about the pocket before they slept. He had to. It was the last night before the shaft, and it was owed.

He told Zale and Ferra, only; not the knights, who would not go through the shaft and did not need to carry it. The three of them at the end of the camp, by the stores, with a lamp stone between them on a cask.

"There's something down there," he said. "At the top of the deep water, where it comes up into air. I felt it tonight, at the rim. It's the medium. The thing the stones run on. The thing in the band." He looked from one of them to the other. "It's packed in there. Thicker than the band. I don't know how much thicker; I pulled back before I could tell. It's a pocket of it, shut in under the rock, the way a flask is shut in iron. It's been there a very long time."

"And?" said Zale.

"And it may do something to you." He made himself say it plainly. "The band did something to the crew. Everybody who flies in the ship for long enough comes down with more in them than they went up with. More capacity. More of whatever it is that lets a man push charge into a rail with his hands. We found it out on the Discovery, over years, from Armand's readings and Imani's books. I told the crew. I told them because it was in them and they had a right to know what was in them." He looked at his brother. "The pocket's thicker than the band. You'll be in it for an hour, perhaps two. Breathing through a loop, in a suit, with a glass over your face, but in it. I don't know what it'll do. It might do nothing. It might do what the band does, faster. It might do something else." He stopped. "I'm telling you now, before, and not after, because after isn't the time. You can stay here. Nobody will think anything of it. Ferra and I can go in."

Zale did not take long.

"I'm going in," he said. "It's my book." And then, after a moment, with something that was nearly the easy laugh and not quite: "And I've been off the edge of both maps. If it's going to do something to me, I'd like to find out what. I've never had anything done to me that I didn't go looking for."

Emeric looked at Ferra.

She had been writing. She finished the line and looked up.

"I've flown the band," she said. "More than most. I've been going up into it with you for years. Whatever it does, it's been doing it to me since the first night over the sea." She closed the book on her pen. "If it's a bigger dose of the same, it's a bigger dose. If it's something else, somebody ought to be there who'll write it down properly." She looked at him. "You'd have gone in anyway. With or without either of us."

"Yes."

"Then that's two of us you're not going in without." She put the book under her cot. "Go to sleep. It's the shaft tomorrow. And the half set. And you'll have checked every valve on it twice more before breakfast, so you'll want the sleep."

Ned Tallis had the Wain on her legs on the western flats at the second hour after midnight, and she stood there in the dark like a church somebody had set down in a field, and he did not like it.

He never liked it. Nobody who had flown her did. A ship on her legs on the ground was a ship that could not go anywhere quickly. She could lift off her legs in the time it took to count ten; he had done it a hundred times, and done it once with a gale coming and the racks half loaded; but ten was a long count on a flat with nothing on it for a mile in any direction but grey shrub and white crust and the dark. And she was big. That was the thing. In the band she was a shape against the stars that nobody would ever look up and see. On the ground she was the biggest thing on the island. Bigger than the west fort. Bigger than the hut on the ridge. A long black roof against the sky, higher than a tithe barn, with her legs spread under her like a beetle's.

Longer than a tithe barn. He had been at her rail the night the man saw her. He had not heard what the man swore till Osric read it out at the table.

They were loading the bales through her belly door. It was the third night, and her first landing, and what the slings could not take on the second night had been stacked in the lee of the dunes under sailcloth dyed the colour of sand, the way the drillers had once left their pit by day, and the slings could not have taken it all in a week. So she was down. The six in their Skins and Fletcher and Tobe were carrying them up the ramp on their backs, two men to a bale, fast, not talking, and stowing them in her hold on the racks, and Ned was at the rail of her deckhouse, high up, where he could see the whole of the flats and the dunes behind them and the long dark line of the outcrops' ridge a mile to the east, and the sea beyond the dunes to the west with the starlight on it. He was the watch. That was his job, on the ground. Somebody who was not carrying had to be looking.

The ridge had lanterns on it tonight.

The row was back. The Kesrathi who had been at the anchorage waiting for the boat on the first night had gone, and the new row had come ashore on the second, and now they were up on the benches, forty of them, with their lanterns strung along the cuts in a long uneven line of little yellow points, a mile off, and the sound of their picks coming across the flats very faint, a tink, and a tink, and a tink, like somebody a long way off tapping a nail. Ned had listened to it all night. It was a good sound. Men who were cutting stone were not looking at anything but stone.

One of the lanterns stopped moving.

He did not notice it at first. They moved all the time, the lanterns, a little, as men shifted along the benches, or went down a cut, or came back up with a basket; they moved the way lamps move in a town at night, never quite still. He noticed this one because it was at the end of the line, the western end, the end nearest the flats, a little apart from the others, a little higher; and because at some point, he could not have said when, it had stopped doing what the others were doing. The others moved. This one did not.

He looked at it. It sat there on the end of the ridge, a small yellow point, perfectly still.

He did not call out. You did not call out; sound carried on a flat. He did not put his glass on it, because a glass might catch the starlight, and a glint on a dark ship was a thing a man could see. He stood at the rail and looked at it with his own eyes, and it did not move.

A man had stopped. That was all. A man had put his lantern down, or was holding it, on the end of the ridge, and was not cutting any more. He could have been resting. He could have been eating. He could have been sitting on a rock with his back to his mates, looking at nothing. He could have been looking west.

The picks went on, the others. Tink, and tink. The lantern at the end of the line did not move.

Below Ned, the bales went up the ramp two men at a time. Fletcher looked up at him once from the foot of the ramp, and Ned did not look back, because he did not want Fletcher to see his face and wonder, and did not want to take his eyes off the lantern either.

He found he had taken out his pencil and the slate from his coat.

He wrote the hour. He had the ship's glass in the deckhouse behind him and he knew the hour to a quarter, because he always did; he had flown her by it for three years. Second hour and a half past midnight. He wrote: Lantern on the ridge, west end, stopped. He wrote nothing else. He did not write looking, because he did not know it was looking. He stood and watched it.

It did not move for a long time. He counted, in his head, the way he counted the lift coming on the rail. He counted to a hundred, and to a hundred again, and a third time. Below him the last of the night's bales went up the ramp and into her hold. Tobe came up the ladder to the deckhouse and said, low, "Loaded," and Ned said, "Stand by the rail," and Tobe looked at his face, and then along his look, east, at the ridge, and saw the lantern, and said nothing, and stood by the rail.

At the fifth hundred, the lantern moved.

Not along the bench. Down. It went down, a little, and then up, as a man stands up who has been sitting; and then it went along the ridge, east, slowly, back toward the others, and joined the end of the line, and after a moment it was moving the way they all moved, a little, never quite still, and he could not tell it from the rest.

Ned wrote: Moved at the fifth hundred. Stood up. Went back east to the others. And then, because he was honest, and because Ferra would ask: Could have been resting. Could have been looking. Don't know.

He put the slate back in his coat.

"Lift her," he said to Tobe, and Tobe went to the rail, and Ned put his hands on her levers, and felt the lift come on in her keel, and her weight come off her legs, and the legs come up out of the crust, and she was off the ground, rising, a big black shape going up off the flat into the stars with the whole of the night's harvest in her hold, silent, for anybody on a ridge a mile off who happened to be looking west to see.

He did not think, then, of the testimony Osric had read out at the long table for a joke. He did not know that on the night of the stream there had been a rider on the hill as well as a scout: a man who stopped, and sat, and watched, and rode north, whom the prince had felt in the dark and told nobody of but the table. He only knew that a man on a ridge had stopped cutting stone for five hundred counts, on the end of the line nearest the flats, on the one night in six the ship had been on the ground.

He took her up into the band, and gave the slate to nobody, and kept it in his coat until they were home.
