Chapter 237 - The Plant

Bram's machine had been running for five days before anything fell out of the bottom of it, and on the fifth night Tobe sent a boy up the lake path at a run.

The plant stood against the inside of the secret dock's wall, at the far end of the lake, where nobody went who had not sworn the ships' oath and nobody could see over: a brick house the size of a barn, built in October by the masons who had built the stone shed beside it and been told it was a cold store for the ice works, which was true. Inside, on the left, the old hoist engine from the derrick sat on a new bed and drove Bram's pump, a squat iron thing with two cylinders that thumped day and night like a heart in a chest; and the pump's pipe went through a tank of lake water and then into a tall lagged column, felted and boxed in pine, taller than a man, with the two coils one inside the other down the middle of it, Ivo's glass inside and Bram's copper round it. That was the machine. On the right, by the far wall, the stone frame from the shed, the tub and the cold stone and the coil, had been rebuilt on a bigger scale, so that both ways stood in the same room, the quick way and the slow, side by side, and fed the same racks.

The racks were along the back wall: glass-lined tanks in iron jackets, each the size of a churn, each with a slot down its front a finger wide. Nine of them. Two had a little in them already, from the stone frame, a pale glow at the bottom of each slot.

The machine had not given anything. It had run for five days, cooling itself, as Emeric had told Bram it would, the cold air coming out of the valve at the bottom and going back up round the coil and cooling the next lot down; and every day the frost had crept a little further up the column's copper where the lagging was thinnest, and every day Bram had come down at noon and put his hand flat on the pine box and said nothing and gone away. Tobe had sat with it every night. On the fifth night, a little before midnight, the glass at the bottom of the column's tap had gone white with frost all at once, and Tobe had opened the tap a hair, as Emeric had shown him, and something had come out of it, a thin pale stream, into the trap below.

Emeric came down the lake path with Ward behind him and the boy ahead, and found Bram already there, in his coat over his nightshirt, standing by the trap with his arms folded.

It was blue in the trap. Not much. The faint glow, the winter-sky colour, at the bottom of the glass, the same as the stone frame made, the same as the vials. Bram had his face close to it. His breath went white over it.

"Nobody told it to," said Bram.

"Nobody told it to."

"No stone. No mage." Bram straightened. "A pump and a hole and five days."

"And Tobe."

"And Tobe," said Bram, and looked at Tobe, who had been awake for most of five nights and looked it, and nodded at him once, which from Bram was a speech.

Then he looked up at the column, at the frost on the pine where the lagging was thin, and past it at the roof, as if he could see through the slates to the reeds where the Hod lay at her mooring, and the four miles of dark over her.

"Why's it down here?"

"Where would you have it?"

"Up there. Where the stuff is." Bram jerked his chin at the roof. "She goes up, she fills her bottles, she comes down, Tobe pushes them along the path in a barrow, and this squeezes them out again. Put it in her hold. Skip the bottles."

"Who mends it?"

Bram said nothing.

"It's going to break. The seat on that valve, the packing on your pump, the glass. You know it is. Down here you walk along the path with a spanner. Up there she comes home anyway, and the night's gone, and Ned's had three hours of the Band for nothing." Emeric put his hand on the pine where Bram had put his every noon for five days. It was cold through the boards. "And she's the only one we've got. This ran five days before it gave a drop. If the Hod sits up there five days waiting on it, nobody's filling anything."

"Hm." Bram was watching his face. "There's a third. You've got the look."

"It has to come down." Emeric nodded at the trap. "That wants to stay cold. Let it warm in a hold on the way down and it's what it was before, only all at once, a cloud a few hundred times the size of what's in that glass, in a shut house with three men in it. The scrubber's for the Band coming in round a door. It's not for a tank going off."

Bram looked at the blue in the trap for a while.

"So never."

"So not yet. When it stops breaking. When there's a ship to spare for it. When I've a tank that keeps it cold the whole way down, or never has to come down at all." He took his hand off the box. "Then it goes up."

Tobe had a stub of chalk in his coat for the gauges. He looked at the two of them, and then he wrote on the pine box at the height of his eyes, in the careful capitals of a man who had learned them late, NOT YET, and underlined it, and put the chalk back in his pocket.

Bram grunted. He did not rub it off.



The Hod's flasks came down the lake path in a handcart every Sunday and Thursday from then on, in their felt, and went into the plant, and came out empty, and went back to the reeds to be filled again on Wednesday and Saturday at four miles. What had been in them went into the racks along the back wall, a little at a time, as a pale glow at the bottom of each slot that rose a finger a week. That was the whole of it. Nothing about it looked like anything. A brick house, an engine thumping, a tall pine box with frost on its corners, nine iron tanks against a wall.

The vials were still filled only aloft, in the Hod's hold, by Emeric's finger. That had not changed and would not; a sealed vial had to start in the Band. What came down in the flasks and went into the tanks was for the things that would never be sealed, the things that drew from a tank through a valve and were filled again from the plant. Emeric knew the list. He had made it at the counting room table in September.

The first thing on it was in the cellar.

Osric was on the dock the night the tanks reached a hand's depth. He had the watch, with Declan; the knights took the plant's nights in turn now, because Ward said a thing worth guarding was worth guarding by somebody who could not be bought for a Silver. Osric came into the plant at midnight to warm his hands at the engine, and stood looking at the slots along the back wall, at the pale blue in each one, with his hands held out to the cylinder and his face lit underneath.

"That's the Band," he said.

"That's the Band."

"In a pot." Osric looked at the nearest slot. "Mira went up a wall on a corner of one of Maren's biscuits. A corner. Imani weighed it." He turned his hands over at the warmth. "What does a spoonful of that do, then? If a man drank it. One spoon."

Armand was in the plant that night, because Armand had asked to see it and come down the lake path on Ward's arm in Wexler's coat, and he was sitting on a crate by the door with his stick across his knees, looking at the column. He did not raise his voice. He did not need to; the engine was loud and his voice went under it like a knife under a door.

"It kills him," he said.

Osric turned round.

"I'll tell you once, Sergeant, and you'll tell the yard, and that will be the end of it." Armand did not get up. "There was a third-year in the tower when I was young in it. Clever, quick, a little vain, a water affinity, from a good house in the west. He made the jar, as third-years do, to show off; and then he made another, and sealed it; and one night, because he had been reading about the old climbs and the men who came down from them with their eyes like lamps, he broke the seal and drank it. To see." Armand looked at the slot. "Liquid. Off the bottom of the jar. Two ounces of his own."

Osric did not say anything.

"A biscuit stops you," said Armand. "You eat a biscuit full of mana and your stomach says enough, and you stop, because your stomach has never in the history of men let anybody eat a whole cartload of anything. That's the brake. That's why Imani weighs a corner and it works. There's no brake on that. And nobody on earth can tell a man what room he's got in him on the night he tries it." He pointed his stick at the slot. "It goes in, and it's in, all of it, at once, faster than the rite. He had the silver on his arms inside the hour, ring over ring, and then his chest, and then his neck. He took it the wrong way round. From inside." Armand lowered the stick. "I knew his name. I'll not say it in front of you. He died that night in the tower's infirmary, with four masters round the bed and not one of them able to do a thing for him."

The engine thumped. The frost crept on the pine.

"Two ounces," said Armand. "Of his own. There's more than that at the bottom of every one of those slots. And it's not his own. It's four miles of sky." He settled his hands on the stick. "So nobody drinks it. Not a spoon. Not a drop off a finger to see what it tastes of. If I hear that anybody in this yard so much as put his tongue to a glass, I'll know, Sergeant, and so will Ward, and so will Imani, and you'll want me to be the one who gets to you first."

Osric looked at him a long breath. Then at the slots. Then at his own hands, held out to the warmth of the engine, with the old silver going up the insides of his forearms where the rite had put it.

"I'll tell the yard," he said.

"Tell it plainly."

"I'll tell it plainly," said Osric, and went back out onto the dock to Declan, and did not come back in to warm his hands again that night.



After the flasks had gone through on the Sunday, Emeric asked Tess to run it longer.

She had come down the lake path in her chair with her data book on her knees, because a machine that cooled itself was the most interesting thing that had happened in Kaldo since the engine house's third set, and she had sat beside the column for an hour with her hand flat on the pine box exactly as Bram did, feeling the cold come through it.

"Longer," she said. "Colder?"

"Colder. No flasks. Nothing from the Band. Just the air off the lake, through the pump, round and round, as long as it takes."

"To do what?"

"To see what falls out at the bottom."

Tess looked at him. She had known him since she was fifteen and had been told a great many things by him in that time with the reason left off the end, and she had learned that the reason usually turned up, a year later, at the bottom of something she was building.

"You know what falls out," she said.

"I think I know."

"Then why do you want me to run it?"

"Because if I'm wrong, I'd like to find out from you and not from a book."

She ran it. She ran it three days, with the Band's tanks shut off at their valves and the flasks gone back to the reeds, and the pump thumping on lake air alone; and the frost went up the column past where it had gone before, and on the third night the tap at the bottom ran again, and what ran out of it into the trap was blue.

Paler than the other. Clear, nearly. A thin pale sky-blue that did not glow at all, and boiled at the edges of the glass, and smoked a white smoke off its top that ran down the outside of the trap and over Tess's hand on the bench like milk.

She had Ivo's silvered flask ready, the double-walled one that Ivo had pumped the space out of on the bulb works' pump and sealed, so that nothing could carry the warmth in through the walls; and she poured the trap into it, quickly, and stoppered it loosely, and sat in her chair with it on her knee, watching the white smoke come out round the stopper.

"It's air," she said.

"It's air."

"Air's not blue."

"It is when it's that cold. Part of it is." He did not say which part. "Pour a little on the floor."

She tipped the flask over the flags by her wheel. A splash came out, a few drops, and hit the stone and did not lie there; it ran, it skittered across the flags in little balls like quicksilver, smoking, and was gone before it reached the wall. Tess watched it go with her mouth a little open. Then she looked round the plant, and found what she wanted on the windowsill, a bunch of dead asters somebody's wife had put in a jar the first week, and took one, and dipped its head into the flask for a count of three, and took it out, and tapped it on the edge of the bench.

It broke like glass. The petals came off in pieces and rang on the stone.

Tess sat and looked at the pieces for a while.

"What's it for?" she said.

"Ask me in the spring."

"You always say that."

"It's always true," said Emeric, and Tess snorted, and put the stopper back in the silvered flask, loosely, and wrote in her data book for a quarter of an hour without speaking; and at the end of it she said, not looking up, that whatever it was for, she would want to run it herself, and he said she would.



The cellar got its tank on the Wednesday.

It came up the canal shaft at night on the castle-works barge, in a crate packed in straw, because nothing came down the keep stair that two men could not carry and this took four: a glass reservoir the size of a cask in a sealed iron jacket with a sight slit down its front, filled from the plant's racks that afternoon and plugged. It went into the niche in the cellar's back wall where the bank had stood, on a bed of felt, and Bram's brass ran from it along the wall in a pipe, a real pipe, to the lattice's rail.

The bank came out to make room for it.

That took the whole of Tuesday night, and Ward and Bram and Emeric did it alone, because the cellar did not have a fourth key. Two hundred charged stones, in their rack from floor to ceiling, uncoupled one at a time from the bank's rail and lifted down and laid in baskets in straw; full, every one of them, because the forge had run off the flask since August and had not drawn a stone from the bank since. They went up the stair in the baskets, on Ward's back and Bram's, over the fourth step, and out by the meeting room and the passage to the yard, and in the morning they went to the works in a cart, and Orrin said they would heat the works for a month.

The niche looked very bare without the rack. A square of clean stone where the rack had stood for four years, paler than the wall round it, with the four holes in the floor where its feet had been bolted.

The first run on the tank was the tank's own. Its valve seat and its metering nozzle, a seat to hold the pipe's end against the reservoir's throat and a nozzle with a hair-fine bore that would let the lattice draw exactly what it asked for and no more; two small hard brass things no shop in the world could have made true enough. The forge made them in forty minutes off the flask, the last flask it would ever run on, and Bram fitted them that night, and in the morning, the Thursday, Emeric opened the reservoir's valve for the first time, and the lattice drank from a pipe in the wall.

Ferra was on the cellar step with her book.

She had come down at seven, when Ward told her the bank was out, and looked at the pale square on the wall, and then sat on the step with her book on her knees and waited for the run.

When it was done she went to the reservoir and looked at the slit, close, with her lamp. The glow at the slit had not moved that she could see. She looked at the lattice, every stone in it drained back to rest. She looked at the niche, where nothing was.

Then she opened her book.

It was the page she had carried since the forge was built. The bank's page: the row at the top in her capitals, STONE BANK, and under it four years of figures in her small square hand, quarter after quarter, every stone bought and every stone filled and every bucket of dust carried out to the shed, the longest and ugliest column in the book. She had said in August, on this step, with a flask on the floor in its felt coat: when it comes down every week, in a tank, to a pipe in that wall, I'll strike it. Not before.

It came down every week now, in a tank. There was a pipe in the wall.

She took her pencil out of the book, and laid her ruler across the page under the row's heading, and drew one line through it, straight, from margin to margin; and under the line she wrote the date, and her initial, small, F., the way Vantic had struck the cap on the covenant years ago, one line and a letter, so that anybody who opened the book would see that it had been struck and who had struck it and when.

She looked at it a while.

"Four years," she said.

"Four years."

"Two thousand and some stones." She did not need to add them up; she had them. "And the dust."

"And the dust."

Ferra closed the book on her pencil.

"I'll want a new page," she said. "For the tank. What it holds, what it draws, a run at a time." She stood up. "And the plant. What the plant costs to run, against the flasks it fills." She looked at the bare niche. "And I'd like that wall washed. It looks like somebody's moved out."



The casks went into the forge on the Thursday night.

Six and a half of them. Rowena's two, that had stood on the quay at Emberfell with her seal on their bungs and no note; the five bought lawfully off Duvrenne's beach on the way home; less the half that Bram had boiled in the still crew's copper that winter, which had given Tess her five jars and Bram a lump of pitch he still kept on his bench. They had stood in a corner of the works store under a tarpaulin for nearly two years, waiting, because a pot still let half of what was in them go up into the air and the rest came out in a muddle, and Emeric had not been willing to waste them.

The forge did not waste anything.

It took the oil into the chamber a cask at a time, through a pipe Bram had run in at the top, and held it there in the lattice's grip and heated it, as a pot still heated it but harder and closer and everywhere at once, the fire stones white and the chamber's glass at its window going dark with the inside of the heat; and the long heavy stuff in the oil cracked under the heat into shorter, lighter stuff, as wood cracks into charcoal and smoke. The light end went up as gas. The forge held the gas in the chamber and squeezed it, a long way past anything Bram's pump could have done, with a trace of air let in through a needle, as somebody in the other life had once done by accident in a laboratory and found the white stuff at the bottom of his vessel in the morning.

It came out at the bottom of the chamber, when the lattice let it down, as a white waxy cake, the size of a loaf, faintly cloudy, still warm.

Bram picked it up off the catch tray in his tongs and looked at it. He put the tongs down and picked it up in his hand. He pressed it with his thumb, and it gave, a little, and came back. He bent a corner of it, and it bent, and did not break, and when he let go it stayed bent, a little, and then not quite.

"What is it?" said Bram.

"Polyethylene."

Bram looked at him over the cake as at a man who has given a dog a name nobody can pronounce.

"It's not anything," he said. "It's not wax. It's not horn. It's not leather." He bent it again. "It doesn't care."

"It doesn't care. That's what it's for." Emeric took it from him. "Water doesn't touch it. Acid hardly. It doesn't rot. You can draw it into a tube and the tube bends and doesn't kink." He turned it in his hands. It was the first in the world, he thought, and nobody in the world but him knew it, and he was standing in a cellar holding it like a loaf of bread. "Bottles that don't break when Imani's nurses drop them. Wraps a wound can't wet through."

And a tube, and a thin disc, for a thing he had drawn already and put in a drawer of his own, that was going to be the end of the year; he did not say that. He broke a third off the cake and put it in his coat, and Bram watched him do it and did not ask.

The rest came out after. A soft grey jelly that Bram smelled and said was for burns, and was right, though he could not have said how he knew; petroleum jelly, Emeric told him, and Bram said he would call it burn grease and did. A heavy oil that Tess would want for the lathe. And the gas.

The gas was what Emeric had wanted most, and it was the hardest to keep. The light end that did not become the white cake was led up the flue into the shed, and there, instead of being burned, it went into a bell: an iron bell the size of a hogshead hanging mouth-down in a tank of water, rising as the gas came in and sinking as it was drawn off. In the morning Tobe carted the bell's gas down the lake path in Bram's flask bottles and put it through the plant's pump; and the gas that a little pressure turned to liquid went into eight steel bottles, the height of a man's thigh, with a valve on each, and sat in them as a liquid, sloshing faintly when they were moved.

"Propane," said Emeric, at the plant, with his hand on the first bottle. "And butane. Mostly." He did not expect anybody to remember the words, and Tobe did not try; Tobe called it bottled gas, which was what it was, and that was the name that stuck.

Ferra priced the night on the cellar step in the morning before anybody had slept, with the white cake and the jelly and the oil and Tobe's figure for the bottles in front of her on a cloth.

"Six and a half casks," she said. "Which cost us a few Silver at Duvrenne and a smile at Emberfell, and two years in a corner of the store." She looked at the bottles' figure. "Eight bottles of a thing that burns. How long?"

"In a kitchen? Months. One kitchen."

"One kitchen." Ferra wrote. "Whose?"

"Maren's."

Ferra looked up. Then she looked down at the figure again, and wrote something beside it, and he leaned to see it and she turned the book a little away from him.

"It's a good kitchen," she said, to the book. "It's fed us for nine years."



Maren did not trust it.

Tess's men put the gas range in on the Monday, against the kitchen's north wall where the old spit hearth had stood cold since the stone range came: a black iron frame on legs with four round burners on top in a row, each a brass ring pierced with small holes in a circle, and under each ring a brass valve with a needle in it and a dial in front, marked round its edge from a dot to a line; and below the burners an oven, with a box on its side and a strip of two metals inside the box that bent when it was warm and shut the gas down when the oven was hot enough, so that the oven held the heat it was set to. The bottles did not come into the kitchen. They went outside the wall, in an iron cage with a louvred roof and open sides, two of them coupled at a time, with a pipe through the wall to the range, so that if anything ever leaked it leaked into the yard and the wind.

And it would stink if it leaked. Tess had seen to that. The gas had no smell of its own, which Emeric had told her was the most dangerous thing about it; so Tess had drawn something off the coke ovens' liquor that smelled like a cabbage that had been dead a fortnight, and put three drops of it into every bottle, and said that if any cook in Kaldo ever smelled that in her kitchen she would open the door and the window and go and get Tess and not light anything, and that she would tell every cook that herself.

Maren listened to all of it with her arms folded.

"A fire with no fire in it," she said.

"A fire with no stone in it," said Emeric. "And no wood. Nothing magic in it at all. If the whole of Kaldo went dead tomorrow and every stone in the town went out, this would still light."

Maren looked at the brass rings.

"Light it," she said.

He turned the first dial to its line, and the gas hissed faintly in the brass, and he put a spill to the ring; and the ring caught, all round, a circle of small blue flames like the petals of a flower, with a soft sound like somebody letting out a breath. Then he turned the dial down. The blue flames sank, smaller, and smaller, until they were a ring of blue beads round the brass, barely there, steady, not going out.

Then up again, to a roar.

Then down to the beads.

Maren watched it go up and down three times. Then she unfolded her arms and went to the old fire-stone range beside it, the one with its four notches and its stone that she had cooked on for five years, and put her hand flat on its top, and said she would cook on that, thank you, and that if he wanted to light his blue flower he could light it in his own kitchen when he had one.

She did not cook on the gas for six days. She walked past it. She put a pan of water on it once, on the Wednesday, when she thought nobody was watching, and took it off again before it boiled. On the Thursday Ferra found her standing in front of it at five in the morning in her nightcap with a lamp, turning the first dial up and down, slowly, watching the flower open and close and open, with the face of a woman trying not to be impressed by a man who had turned out to be right.

On the Saturday she made a sauce on it. A butter sauce for the fish, the kind that split if you looked at it, that she had made on the fire-stone range for five years by holding the pan an inch above the top and moving it on and off the heat with her wrist. She put it on the gas at the beads and stood over it and did not move the pan at all.

It did not split.

By the Monday after she would not cook on anything else. She did not say so. She simply stopped lighting the stone range, and when the kitchen girl asked her why, she said the stone range was for when the gas ran out, and the gas was for cooking, and if the girl had any more questions she could take them to the duke, who was full of answers.



He was in Maren's kitchen late on the Thursday after, at the gas, because there was something he wanted to know about it and the only way to know was to try.

The castle was asleep. Maren had gone up at ten; the stone range had not been lit in a week; the kitchen girl had gone; the long kitchen was dark but for the lamp on the table and the ring of blue beads on the second burner, and the copper pan over it, and in the pan sugar.

Plain sugar from Milford, a cupful, with a spoon of water. He had stood over it for a quarter of an hour with the dial at its lowest, watching it melt, and clear, and begin, very slowly, at the edges, to go gold. On the fire-stone range it could not be done. The stone had notches, and the lowest notch was too hot, and the sugar went from white to black in the time it took to look away; On the gas, at the beads, it went slowly. It went gold at the edges, and the gold came in toward the middle, and deepened, so slowly that a man could watch it and think about other things.

He was thinking about other things. He was humming.

He did not know he was doing it until he heard the door.

Ferra stood in it with a lamp. Imani stood behind her, with her bag still on her shoulder from a late call in the town, looking past Ferra's shoulder into the kitchen at him, at the pan, at the blue flower under it.

"That's not the two notes," said Ferra.

"I'm sorry. Did I..."

"You've hummed your two notes at my table since the first winter, whenever you've a sum you've not told anybody about. Up and down. Your mother's, Rowena said." She came in and set the lamp on the table. "That wasn't two notes. That was a whole tune."

"It's in tune," said Imani, from the door.

He stopped humming.

"Armand says your mother couldn't hold a tune to save her life," said Imani. She came in and put her bag on the end of the table. "And he'd know; he listened to her at it through a door for four years. You've got her two notes. You didn't get her ear." She looked at him.

"No."

"So where's that from?" Imani nodded at the air, where the tune had been. "I've never heard it. Not in the ward, not in the town, not on the coast at Emberfell. Not in any of the songs Kit Bastow knows, and he knows every song with a miller's wife in it in four baronies."

He looked at the sugar. It was the colour of honey at the edges now.

"Home," he said.

They knew what that meant. Two people in the world knew what that meant, and they were both standing in Maren's kitchen near midnight looking at him, and neither of them said anything for a breath; and then Ferra pulled out the bench at the end of the table and sat down on it, and Imani leaned against the table beside her with her arms folded, and they waited.

"There was a lot of music," he said. "Where I was. Everywhere. In the shops, in the street, in everybody's ears all day. Little boxes you carried in your pocket that played whatever you liked." He watched the sugar. "I hum it. I don't know I'm doing it. It's like the books. I've got all of it and it comes out when I'm not looking."

"Do you play anything?" said Imani. "Or only hum?"

"Yeah," he said, and then, because it was true, and because it was late and the kitchen was warm and the sugar was going gold: "I do, actually. Did. A guitar."

"What's a guitar?" said Ferra.

"A box with a neck on it. Six strings. You press them on the neck with one hand to change the note and you strike them over the box with the other." He made the shape in the air with his free hand, without thinking, the left hand up the neck and the right over the hole, and then saw himself doing it and put the hand down on the table. "I picked it up when I was at college. In my second year. To..."

He stopped.

He stopped because he had heard the end of the sentence arriving and had nowhere to put it. The sugar went on going gold.

"To what?" said Ferra.

He did not answer.

Imani looked at him across the kitchen for a breath. Then her mouth moved, very slightly, at one corner, the way it moved when she had read a chart and found exactly what she expected.

"To impress girls," she said.

Ferra turned her head and looked at Imani, and then at him, and he watched her understand it; and he watched her ears go pink, and her face stay perfectly straight, which with Ferra was worse than if she had laughed.

"Was that the reason?" she said.

"It was a reason."

"It was the reason," said Imani.

"There was a girl down the hall in my second year who played," he said, to the sugar. "Very well. Everybody in the hall wanted to sit near her at night. I thought..." He gave up. "I learned four songs. Badly. It took me the whole winter."

"What are they about?" said Ferra.

"A girl. They're all about a girl."

Ferra made a small sound. It was not quite a laugh. She put her hand over her mouth, and her shoulders moved.

"And then?" said Imani. She was not laughing at all. She was looking at him with great interest, as she looked at a symptom she had not seen before.

"And then I kept playing. For years. By myself, mostly. In the evenings." He shrugged. "It turned out I liked it."

"Play one," said Ferra, through her hand.

"I haven't got a guitar. And the ones I liked best had a wire in them. Electric. They played through a box, as loud as you liked."

"You've got a forge and a works and a man who can bend oak like paper." Ferra took her hand away from her mouth. Her face was straight again; her eyes were not. "And a winter. Play one. Someday." She looked at Imani. "We'd like to be impressed."

"We'd like to see if it works," said Imani.

He looked at the two of them, sitting and leaning at the end of Maren's long table in the lamplight, the one with her ears gone pink and her book under her hand, the other with her bag on the table and her arms folded and her curls coming out of their cloth at the end of a long day; and found that he wanted to, quite badly, and that it surprised him.

"I will," he said.

"Someday," said Ferra.

"Before the winter's out."

"You'll forget."

"I don't forget things," said Emeric. "You know I don't."

Ferra looked at him. Then she sniffed.

"Something's burning," she said.

It was the sugar. While he had been talking about a girl down a hall in another world, it had gone from honey to amber to a deep bitter brown at the edges, and was smoking, a thin blue smoke that smelled of every burned pan in every kitchen he had ever stood in; and he snatched it off the beads and stood holding it, and Imani leaned over and looked into it, and said it would make a very good poison, and Ferra laughed out loud at last, a laugh she had plainly not planned on.

He soaked the pan. Maren found it in the sink at five in the morning and said nothing about it to anybody for three days, and on the fourth she asked him, at the kitchen door, in front of the kitchen girl, whether the duke had been making toffee in her kitchen in the night, and if so whether he would like to learn how to do it properly.



He measured Arthur for the suit on the Tuesday, in the armoury, himself.

It was not a thing he gave anybody else to do. The suits were made in the forge from the measurements and nothing else, and a suit made a finger wrong in the leg was a suit that pulled the man in it sideways every time it lifted; Rowena's had been, the first time, and Tess had found it. So he did it with Abloh's tape and a slate and his own hands, the way he had done Rylan's and Osric's and Declan's and Ward's: the reach and the stride and the turn of the shoulder, the hand open and the hand closed, the boy standing in the cold armoury in his shirt with his arms out and his face very carefully showing nothing at all.

Sela was at the armoury door. Nobody had sent for her. She stood in the doorway with her shawl round her and her hands in it, and watched, and did not come in.

When he had written the last figure on the slate, Arthur put his shirt back on and went out past her without a word, because there was nothing he could have said in front of the duke that would not have embarrassed both of them, and he was twenty.

Sela did not go.

"Will it hold him?" she said.

"It'll hold him."

She looked at the slate in his hand, at the column of figures, as if she could read in them whether it was true.

"You promised me a thing," she said. "At my table. At one in the morning."

"I did."

"Keep it," said Sela. She looked at him a breath longer. Then she pulled her shawl closer and went out of the doorway, and he heard her cross the yard toward the school, not quickly, and he stood in the armoury with the slate and the eighth suit's figures on it and the cold coming in through the open door where she had stood.

He began to shape it that week, in the cellar, a night at a time. It would take the winter.



The dock quarter met in the wash house, because it was the warmest room in the quarter, which was why half the quarter came whether it was on the council or not.

The steam came over from the works in its pipe under the bridge and through the drying room on its way to the coppers, so that the long room smelled of hot wet linen and soap; and along both walls the coppers sat in their brick, lids down for the evening, and the women who used them in the day sat on them. The men stood at the back. The children were everywhere.

Wenna Hallam had been chosen to speak for the quarter in October, by a show of hands in that room. She had gone back across the bridge to her mother's people after Harrow, after Garth, because on the quay she had cousins to the end of the street; and in a year she had told Ferra exactly what she thought of the duke's pay to widows, which was that it was decent and late, and been right, and the quarter had noticed.

She stood at the end of the long table with her red hands flat on it, and let Ferra finish.

Ferra did not take long. She never did. Linus's map of the river and its two banks was on the table between them: the dock quarter on the far bank where it had stood since anybody remembered, thatched and crooked and down to its knees in the river every spring; and on the near bank, upstream of the bridge, past the works, the long empty slope above the new port where Linus's pegs went in a grid. Houses. Streets with names not yet given. Water in every house, a closet in every yard, light on every street. A quay for the boats at the bottom of it, deeper than the old one, where the river did not silt.

"Nobody moves till there's somewhere to move in," said Ferra. "That's the rule. It's been the rule on the west side since the summer and it's the rule here. Nobody's put out. The houses go up first. Then you go into them." She put her finger on the slope. "And then this side of the river goes back to fields and orchards, and nobody's down to their knees in the river in the spring again."

"And who owns the house," said Wenna, "when we're in it?"

"Whoever's in it. On the terraces' terms: what you'd pay in rent goes toward the house, over twenty years, and what the house you leave is worth comes off the top first."

"We own these."

"You do."

"My grandfather built ours," said Wenna. "With his own hands. After the flood before last. And when it burned, in the war, the first war, when I was a girl, my father built it again, on the same stones, with the same hands near enough." She looked down the room at the coppers. "Every house on this side of the river was built by the people sitting in it. Every one. And now the other side wants to pull them down and put us in houses it's built, on its own side, where it can see us from the keep."

There was a murmur along the coppers. It was not a friendly one.

Rylan, who had been leaning on the doorpost at the back with his arms folded, because he lived two streets from the river and the quarter would listen to him when it would not listen to a duke, said: "Wenna, it's a house. It's not a cage."

"It's their house."

"It's Bram's brick. Bram's from the forge lane. He'd brick your house for a pie."

A laugh, from somewhere on the coppers. Wenna did not laugh.

"Then we'll build it," she said.

The room went quiet.

"If it's to be done," said Wenna. "If it's to be on that side, and new, and with the water and the light and the closet in the yard, and the boats' quay at the bottom of it." She looked at Ferra. "Then we'll build it. Our men. Paid. Not a crew sent over the bridge to build us a street and look at us while they do it. Our men on the walls and the roofs, and somebody of Bram's to show them what they don't know, till they know it." She took a breath. "And we go as a street. Not a house at a time, with the Pasks on one corner and the Hallams on another and strangers between. The street goes over together, and it's our street, and we name it."

Ferra wrote. Emeric, on the end of the bench by the door, watched her do the sum: their own men meant men who had never laid a course of brick being taught; a street at a time meant a longer wait for the first and a quicker move after; and naming it meant nothing at all to the cost and a great deal to everything else.

"Your men will be slower," said Ferra.

"Then they'll be slower."

"The first street will take the spring and the summer."

"Then it'll take the spring and the summer. We've waited sixty years for a bath. We can wait a summer for a street."

Ferra looked up at her.

For a breath nobody in the long room said anything, and the coppers ticked in the warm, and somewhere at the back a child dropped something and was hushed.

"I'll not promise the street's name," said Ferra. "It goes on Linus's map and the post's and Ottoline's cards, and it has to be a name a carter can find." She turned her book round on the table, so that Wenna could see the line she had written. "Your men, paid at the works rate, with one of Bram's builders to each gang till they can do it without him. A street at a time, finished before anybody moves. And you name it, and if it's a name a carter can find, it stands."

Wenna read the line. She read it twice, with her finger, slowly, because she had learned her letters at Sela's door the second winter with her net bag on her arm, and she had never yet signed anything she had not read twice.

The net-maker with one arm, on the nearest copper, who had said at the bath's opening in the summer that the quarter would think about it, looked at Emeric, and then at Wenna.

"Well," he said. "We've thought about it."

Wenna straightened up and looked down the room at the coppers and the men at the back and the children on the floor.

"Well," she said. "Somebody put their hand up, then. I'm not standing here all night."

Ninety-one hands went up in the wash house that night, Ferra's count, and she wrote down the ones that did not, which were twelve, and their names, so that she could go and ask each of them why.



The cement kiln came up to heat for the first time that week, at the works, beside the brick kiln that had been turning since July: a second long iron tube on rollers turning slowly at a slant over a fire, with the raw meal going in at the top and coming out at the bottom as clinker, hot and grey, that the ball mill would grind into cement; and Bram stood beside it for an hour with his arms folded and said that one kiln that went round had been unnatural and two was a habit, and then went home and told Ferra it was the best thing the works had built since the converter.

The east half of the town was lit by the end of the month. Every lane from the bridge to the lower ward, and the river street, and the hospital; Tess stood at the switchboard on the last night with her hand on the lever and the needle at a figure nobody had seen before, and when it held she went home and slept for fourteen hours, and the lamplighter walked the river street in his new coat with his new ladder and found two lamps out, and told her in the morning.

And Ward's dawn drills began again.

They had stopped in August, for the runs: there had been too many nights at four miles and too many mornings after them, and Ward had looked at Emeric's face at the foot of the keep stair on the first of those mornings and said, flatly, that he did not drill men who could not see. In the second week of November, at a quarter past five, Ward was at his door with a lamp.

"Up," said Ward.

They drilled in the yard under the arc lamp over the gate, in the frost, with the practice blades. Ward put him on the cobbles in the first quarter of an hour, as Ward always did. He put him on them three more times before six. But it was four, and not seven, and on the fourth Emeric had very nearly had him, and both of them knew it; and when Ward put the practice blade back in its rack he stood a moment looking at the rack with his back to the yard.

"You have been eating," said Ward.

"Maren's been cooking on gas."

"That is not what I meant," said Ward, and went in.
