Chapter 184 - A Letter by the Carrier

The letter came on the fourth of November, by Saban's carrier, which meant it had never been inside Lord Sallow's house.

Saban kept two kinds of post. The first went by the crown's riders like everybody else's, now that the crown's riders came, and it said things Saban did not mind the crown reading: prices, cargoes, the weather on the coast. The second went by his carriers, who were men and women of no house, who sold buttons and almanacs and mended pots along the roads between the capital and the coast and the north, and who had been carrying Saban's other letters for twenty years in the linings of their packs. A carrier's letter was never posted. It was handed from one pack to the next at an inn, or a fair, or a ford, and it arrived when it arrived, and nobody had ever opened one, because nobody knew they were there.

In September a carrier had taken Ferra's answer to Isolde's letter north, the one from the summer that said her father had found her a husband. He had put it into Isolde's own hand at a horse fair in the market town below her father's house, where she had gone with her maid to buy ribbon she did not want. And he had told her, Saban said, that if she ever wanted to write to Kaldo again, a man selling almanacs would be at the same fair on the first Thursday of every month, and would buy a letter off her for a copper, and nobody would ever know.

She had sold him one in October. It came down the roads for a month. Saban brought it up the keep stair himself, in the evening, still folded small and soft as a handkerchief from the bottom of a pack, and gave it to Ferra in the counting room without a word, and went out again.

Ferra read it once.

Then she folded it, and got up, and went down the keep stair and across the yard to Emeric's office, where he was at his table with the forge book, and put her head round the door and said, "Come," and went on; and he followed her down the hill in the dusk, through the inner ward and the upper town and past the tower with its blue roof and the square, into the lower ward, to the ward's side door, where the lamp was lit; and Imani was at the desk with the register, and looked up, and saw Ferra's face, and put her pen down.

"Isolde's written," said Ferra. "You'll both want to read it. Together." She put the letter on Imani's desk, open, and stood back from it with her hands folded in front of her. "Term two."



It was short. Isolde did not waste paper; she had learned that at Ferra's table.

It said that she was well, and that she hoped the chancellor was well, and the healer, and that the cough in her father's house had not come back, which the healer would be pleased to know, since the man whose cough it had been was now reading all of Isolde's letters before she was allowed to send them, on her aunt's instructions, and had stayed in her father's house since the spring for no other reason. That was why this letter had come by a man selling almanacs.

It said that the wedding had been moved. Not to midwinter. To the nineteenth of November. She was to be taken from her father's house that morning to Lord Haskett Vane's, nine miles up the valley, for the season; and everybody in the north knew what for the season meant, when a girl was sent to a man's house with her trunks a month before her wedding, and nobody would say it, so she would. It meant that by midwinter it would be too late for anybody to change anything, including her.

It said that Lord Vane was fifty-eight, and kind to his dogs, and owed a great deal of money to a duke in the north who was owed a great deal by everybody, and that her father owed the same duke more, and that the marriage would settle one debt by the other, and that she understood it perfectly and had no complaint to make of it, as a sum.

It said that Sir Jabari had been posted in October to a border tower on her father's land, three days' ride from the house, to command six men and a signal fire on a hill where nothing had happened for forty years; and that her father had told her so at dinner, in front of the steward, and watched her face while he did.

And it said, at the foot, after the place where a letter would end:

I ask for nothing. I know what I'd be asking. I wanted somebody to know who wouldn't be told it at a dinner.

And under that, smaller, in a hand that had pressed harder:

He's written to me. Through the same man; your man found him too. He says he won't. So that's that, and I wanted you to know that too, so that nobody wonders. I.

Imani read it twice. Then she pushed it back across the desk, toward Emeric, and sat with her hands flat on the register.

"A man three times her age," she said.

"Not quite."

"Near enough that I'm not going to argue the arithmetic." She did not look up. "For the season."

Emeric read it again. He had read it once over Imani's shoulder and once on his own and he read it a third time now, slowly, standing at the side of the desk in the lamplight with the ward breathing behind the door, the coughs and the turning in beds and a child somewhere being told to drink; and he thought of the girl on the wall in February with her hands in her cloak, who had told him to decide something real, and then gone home and been refused for it in front of the court.

"She says she asks for nothing," he said.

"She says she knows what she'd be asking," said Ferra. "That's not the same."

"No."

"She wrote to the two people in Randillia who won't tell her she's being foolish." Ferra unfolded her hands. "Because she knows we'd tell her what it costs instead." She looked at him. "It goes to the table. All of it. Tonight."



The table sat at the ninth hour, in the long room, without Hale.

Ferra sent him home. It was the first time she ever had. She said it to him at the door, kindly, and he looked at her and at the chairs and at the letter in her hand, and took his pen and his minutes away without asking anything, which was why Hale had been kept at that door so long.

Ferra read the letter aloud. All of it, in her flat clear voice, the way she read the quarter; the cough and the almanac man and the nineteenth and the season and the tower, and the two lines at the foot. Then she put it on the table in the middle, and sat down.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Price it," said Emeric.

Saban turned his hat once on his knee.

"Lord Aurel Sallow is the first queen's brother," he said. "That's Corbin's mother's brother. Corbin's household runs on Sallow money; it has since he was a boy, and every coast house that lends to Corbin lends against Sallow land in the end. Sallow himself owes the duke in the north, the same one Vane owes, the one everybody owes." He set the hat down. "Whatever Kaldo does, and however it does it, the capital will know by the new year that Kaldo did it. It won't be able to prove it. It won't need to. The first queen will know it was the prince who refused her niece in February who took her niece in November, and she'll have been insulted twice by the same man in one year, and she'll say so at the king's table again, and this time the king may laugh." He looked down the table. "The north will close to us. Sallow's friends and Vane's and the duke's. That's every road north of the capital for a generation. Our paper won't sell there. Our soap won't. Our grain won't, though Ferra says it wouldn't anyway." He picked up the hat again. "That's the price. I can give you the figure, but it's the kind of figure that ends with a house nobody's heard of yet deciding to hate you."

"And if we do nothing," said Emeric.

"Then she's married on the twentieth to a man who's kind to his dogs," said Saban, "and the north stays open, and we sell it paper." He turned the hat. "That's the other price. I've no figure for it at all."

Imani had not taken her eyes off the letter in the middle of the table.

"She's a person," she said. "Not a price. I'll say it once and then I'll stop, because I know it doesn't help." She looked up. "I took that steward's cough out of him in my ward with a kettle and a towel and a week of being shouted at, and now he reads her letters. That's the whole of it. That's what she is in that house. She's the thing that gets read." She put her hand flat on the table. "I'd like it written down that I said so. Not in Hale's minutes. In Ferra's book."

"It's written," said Ferra, writing.

"How many men," said Ward.

Everybody looked at him. He had not moved since the letter was read; he sat at Emeric's right with his hands on the table and his face doing nothing at all.

"Sallow keeps forty under arms," said Saban. "Thirty at the house, ten at the towers. Vane fewer. Twenty."

"Knights?"

"Sallow has six, counting the one at the border. Vane has two, and one of them's sixty."

Ward nodded, once, as if that were a number he had expected, and did not say anything else.

Armand, at the fire end with his blanket over his knees, said: "You'll take the ship." It was not a question. "North. Eight hundred miles, at night, over country nobody's flown over, under a mile because you've promised Fletcher you'll not climb her till midwinter, and with every farm dog in two baronies to hear you if you go fast." He looked at the fire. "I'd go. I'm an old man and I'd go. I only want to hear you say how you'll come back."

"Over the sea," said Emeric. "Up the coast. Flat out over the water, where nobody can hear her but fish, and then inland for the last hour, slow and quiet, under the motor's push and the stones', at a walk if we have to." He looked at Fletcher's charts, which were in his head. "The same way home."

"Ladder," said Bram.

They looked at him.

"If she's coming up into a ship in the dark off a road," said Bram, "you'll want a better ladder than the one you've got." He put his big hands together on the table. "That one's for knights. She's in skirts." He was quiet. "I'll make you one."

Ferra had not looked up. She had been writing since Imani spoke, and she went on writing, a line, and another, and a third, in her small upright hand, with the pen moving steadily and nothing else about her moving at all; and when she had written four lines she blotted them, and turned the book round on the table, and pushed it into the middle, beside the letter.

"Before anybody agrees to anything," she said. "These first. Or I'll use the stay."

Nobody at the table had heard her say that before. Not once since the Standing Order first sat.

Emeric leaned over and read the four lines. So did the others, round the table, one at a time, the way they read a line in the Standing Order's book.

One. Nobody of her father's household dies. Nobody of Vane's.

Two. Nothing is taken but Lady Isolde and what she carries.

Three. Nothing Kaldo does can be proved in a court.

Four. If any of it can be, Kaldo answers for it openly, in its own name, and does not lie.

"The fourth's the one," said Ferra. "The others are how we behave. The fourth's who we are, if the first three go wrong." She took the book back. "I won't keep a lie for this in my books. Not even a good one."

Ward read the four lines twice. Then he nodded, once, the same way he had nodded at the number of knights.

"Then not the house," said Emeric.

They looked at him.

"Not out of her father's house. That's breaking into a lord's house at night, and somebody dies, because somebody always does; and it's taking her out from under his roof, which every court in Randillia calls the same thing whatever she wants." He put his finger on the letter, on the line about the nineteenth. "She's taken from her father's house that morning to Vane's. Nine miles, up the valley, in a coach, with an escort. Between the two gates she's in neither house. She's on the road." He took his finger away. "We take her off the road."

"At night," said Ward. "They won't move her at night."

"Nine miles in November in a coach on a valley road? They'll leave after noon, when the trunks are loaded, and they'll be on the road at dusk." Emeric looked at Saban. "Find out what time."

"I'll find out what time," said Saban.

"And find out about the knight," said Emeric. "Properly. Not the letter. Find out what he said and why."

Saban looked at him for a long moment across the table. Then he put his hat on his knee.

"I already have," he said. "My man that found him came in an hour ago. I thought you'd want it after the table and not before." He took a folded paper out of his coat. "It's not what she thinks."



Imani walked down the hill with him afterward, because it was on her way, and because she had said she would.

The town was asleep, mostly. The new terraces along the low rows were dark under their tile, and the watch house's lamp burned on the square, and somewhere in the upper town a dog barked once at nothing and gave it up. She walked with her hands in her coat pockets and her hair tied up for the ward, and did not say anything until they were past the tower, where the lane went down steep toward her door.

"I'd have gone over a wall for less," she said.

He looked at her.

"Than what she's got," said Imani. "A man three times her age and a cough that reads her letters. If I'd had that, at her age, in a house like that, I'd have gone over the garden wall in my shift with a kitchen knife and walked to the coast." She was looking straight ahead, down the lane. "And she sits in it, and buys ribbon she doesn't want, and writes to us to say she asks for nothing." She was quiet for a few steps. "I don't know if that makes her braver than me or stupider."

"Braver."

"You'd say that. You've never had to sit in anything." She stopped at her door, and turned round on the step, and looked at him in the dark, and her face had the thing in it that it had had on the north tower's leads, with her hair down; only not soft. "Bring her down out of there," she said. "However you do it. I'll come. I'll want the ether, if there's going to be anybody hurt, and there's always somebody hurt." She took her hand out of her pocket and put it flat on the door. "And I'll want to stand next to her in a church. I've never stood next to anybody in a church. It'll be a change from standing next to them in a ward."

She went in. He stood in the lane a while, and then went back up the hill.



Vantic came to the counting room on the sixth, while Ferra was out at the records hall, and left a sheet of paper on Ferra's desk, face down, with a weight on it.

Emeric was at the window table with the forge book. She did not see him at first. She came in with her book under her arm and her gloves on, and went to Ferra's desk, and took a sheet out of the book and laid it face down, and put the brass weight on it from the corner of the desk; and then she turned to go, and saw him, and stopped.

"It isn't for you," she said. "It's for her."

"Ferra told you."

"Ferra told me nothing." Vantic held her book against her chest. "Saban asked the House for a price on what Kaldo's custom in the north is worth, yesterday, without telling me why, as if I would not wonder why a man asks the House to price a road he's never used. And I have read the north's post for five years. And there was a girl at this table in February with green ribbons who told the chancellor she would miss her table." She was quiet. "I can add."

He looked at the sheet under the weight.

"May I?"

"It isn't mine to forbid."

He turned it over. It was in grey ink, in her small upright hand, ruled, as everything of hers was ruled. A column. What the House would charge to lend to Kaldo, now, against four baronies. And beside it a second column: what it would charge after. After the north closed. After the coast houses that lent to Corbin's household against Sallow's land heard who had done what to Sallow's daughter, and put a quarter of a point on every paper with Kaldo's name on it, because they could. After the first queen spoke at dinner. Every line priced. At the foot of the second column, a figure, double-ruled.

It was a large figure. Larger than Saban's.

And beside the two columns, a third. Ruled exactly like the others, the same width, the same lines, running the whole length of the sheet; and at its head, in the same grey ink, one word.

Isolde.

Nothing under it. Every line empty to the foot of the sheet.

"The House has no view," said Vantic, from the door. "The House prices. That is all the House does." She looked at the empty column in his hands, from across the room. "I tried to price the third one for an hour last night. I've priced everything I've ever been given. I couldn't find a number to put in it." She put her gloved hand on the latch. "I thought the chancellor should have the sheet with the column left empty. So she knows I looked."

"And Livia?" said Emeric. "Has she a view?"

Vantic stood with her hand on the latch.

"Livia," she said, and stopped, and opened the door. "Livia has no view she would put in grey ink." And went out.

He put the sheet back face down under the weight. When Ferra came in from the records hall an hour later, she turned it over, and read it, standing, with her coat still on; and he watched her come to the third column and stop; and she stood there for a while without moving, and then she took her pen and wrote something at the foot of the empty column, small, that he could not read from the window table, and put the sheet in the back of her book, where she kept the things she did not file.



Saban's paper from the border tower was two pages, and the second page was the knight's, word for word, as near as Saban's man could get it down, because Sir Jabari Mensah had not written anything.

He had said it. To a man selling almanacs, in the yard of a signal tower on a hill where nothing had happened in forty years, at night, after he had read Isolde's letter and burned it in the signal fire's brazier, because it was the only fire there and he would not keep it. He was a big man, Saban's man said, very quiet, very good at his work, the kind of knight who had his six men's boots inspected every morning on a hill where nobody would ever come to see them; and he had not slept for three nights, by the look of him, and he had been told by a peddler of almanacs that there were people in the south who would help, and had stood in his yard in the dark for a long time before he answered.

Saban read it aloud at the table on the seventh, all of it, because Ferra said all of it.

Tell whoever sent you that I won't run with her.

A girl who runs from her father's house before her wedding is ruined. Every door in the north shuts on her, and her name goes into every story they tell their daughters about what happens to girls who run. A knight who takes her is a thief, and there's no court in Randillia that'll say otherwise, and they'll hang me for it if they catch me and she'll have watched them do it.

I'd rather she married Vane with her name than lived in a ditch with me without it.

Tell them I said thank you. Tell them not to come.

The long room was quiet when Saban finished. He folded the paper.

"She's read it as no," said Ferra. "To her. That he won't. That he doesn't." She looked at the letter in the middle of the table, the one from the almanac man, with its two lines at the foot. "So that's that, she says."

"It isn't no," said Emeric.

They looked at him.

"It isn't no to her," he said. "Read it again. He doesn't say he doesn't want her. He doesn't say one word about what he wants. He says what running does to her, and what it does to him, and he chooses her name over himself, in the dark, on a hill, with nobody watching but a man selling almanacs." He put his hand flat on the table beside the paper. "It's no to running. That's all it's no to. He's protecting her the only way he's got, and the only way he's got is to say no, because a knight with no land and no money has exactly one thing he can give a lord's daughter, and that's not ruining her."

Nobody said anything.

"So we don't ask him to run," said Emeric. "We don't ask her to run either." He looked down the table, at Ferra, at the book, at the four lines. "We ask him to come to Kaldo, and ask her properly, in a church, in daylight, with the doors open and the town watching, under Kaldo's seal. With witnesses. And a priest. And her own yes said out loud in front of all of them." He was quiet. "That isn't running. That isn't a ditch. That's a wedding, in a barony that will say so in writing to every court in the kingdom and send the crown a copy."

Ferra looked at him for as long as it took to read a line.

"Doran," she said.

"Doran."

"He'll want her yes. Out loud. Not in a letter. Not through us." Ferra wrote something in her book. "And the knight's."

"He'll have both," said Emeric. "Or he won't marry them, and we'll have taken a girl off a road for nothing, and we'll answer for it under your fourth line." He stood up. "Saban. What time do they leave on the nineteenth?"

"The second hour after noon," said Saban. "The trunks are going ahead in the morning. She goes after, in the closed coach, with an escort." He turned his hat. "My man didn't know how many. He's finding out."

"Then the eighteenth," said Emeric. "We leave at dusk on the eighteenth. The tower first."

Ward stood up beside him.

"I'll want Rylan," said Ward. "In his suit. He's flown it eleven nights now. That's ten more than he needs to know whether he'll fall out of it." He looked at Emeric. "And I'll want to know exactly what you mean to say to a knight on a hill in the dark when he says no to you too."

"I mean to tell him he's right," said Emeric. "And then I mean to ask him to come anyway."
