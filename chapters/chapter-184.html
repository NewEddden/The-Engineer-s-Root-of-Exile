Chapter 184: Clean Arms

The letter from the tower came at midsummer, on the tower's own grey paper, which Tessaly had not held in two years and which felt, after Kaldo's, like a dishcloth.

Jory Mallen was dead. He had been a water mage at the capital's east cisterns for twenty-two years and had been in her year at the tower, three benches along, the boy who could never keep his ink off his cuffs. He had sat down at his desk at the cisterns one afternoon in early summer and not got up. He was forty-eight. He was to be buried from the tower chapel on the ninth day after the letter's date, and the tower wrote, as it did for every one of its own, to every mage of his year whose post it knew.

It knew hers as Carrow Ford. The letter had gone there first, and the lord's steward at Carrow Ford had sent it on south with a note in the corner that said Gone to Kaldo, I believe, in a hand that did not quite approve.

She read it standing at the south arc of the wall, with the earth warm under her boots and Rafe beside her, and folded it, and put it in her bag.

"Bad news?" Rafe said.

"A friend. Old news, by now." She looked at the wall. It stood two-thirds of its height all round the circuit, and the south gate's towers were up to the first floor, and in another month it would close. "I'll have to go north."

He looked at her. He was fifteen and would be sixteen at harvest, and tall for it, and he still held his lamp stone in his fist most of the day, the way other boys held a knife to whittle with, because she had told him a year ago that the more he held it the better he would know it and he had taken her at her word.

"Can I come?" he said.

She had not thought of it until he said it. Then she thought of nothing else.

He could not be a mage.

That was the plain fact of it, and it had been a plain fact since the day in the etching shed when she had put a cold lamp stone into the hand of the island boy who swept the floor and watched it warm, and hold warm, for a quarter of an hour by the shed clock. He had been fourteen then. He had worked the etching floor since he was eleven. His mother worked at the vats. Nobody in his family, as far back as anybody knew, had ever been anything but island people, and nobody had ever put a stone in his hand, because nobody puts a stone in the hand of a boy who sweeps floors.

She had asked Emeric for him, and Emeric had said yes, and she had taught him for a year. He was the best pupil she had ever had. She had never said so to him and did not intend to.

But only the tower could make a mage. A boy could be taught by anyone, and a boy with the gift could do the work, and lift earth into a wall's line and hold it while the masons set their stone. He could not be posted. He could not be paid a mage's wage by any lord in the realm but Emeric. He could not sign his name under a wall's survey, or be called on at a flood, or be written into the tower's register, or be anything, anywhere, but a clever lad from the south who did things. Armand's school on the flat had a founding stone and a promise and forty students, and no standing whatever. The tower had never licensed a mage it had not measured itself.

Rafe had never been measured.

She went to Ferra that evening for the leave, because Ferra held the purse for the mages' wages and would want to know who was away and for how long. Ferra looked at the letter and at her, and wrote it down, and asked how long.

"Three weeks. There and back."

"And the boy?"

"He should have his licence," Tessaly said. "He's earned it. If I'm going to the tower anyway."

Ferra looked at her for a moment longer than Tessaly expected.

"Has Master Armand seen him?"

"Master Armand has seen him lift earth," Tessaly said. "He's at the site from first light to dark. I'll tell him."

She meant to. She thought about it that night, and decided she would tell Armand in the morning, and in the morning Armand had gone out to the east gate before she was up. She could have ridden out after him. She did not. She knew what Armand would say. He would say wait, in the gentle careful voice he used for everything he did not want to explain, and she had been waiting for two years for somebody to tell her what Kaldo was allowed to be, and nobody ever had. She wanted, for once, to go north with something that was hers and set it down in front of the tower and let them look at it.

She left a note for Armand with Paskin. It said where she was going and that she had taken the boy, and it did not say why.

They went by the Wayford road, on the carrier's coach from the Western Gate, and changed at Wayford for the north.

Rafe had never been further than the new city. He sat by the window the whole way and watched everything: the lake farms, the long straight cut of the rail on its bank, the relay posts on the ridges with their shuttered lamps, and beside every one of them now a taller pole, bare, with a wire going from pole to pole along the road, sagging and lifting, sagging and lifting, mile after mile. Tessaly watched the wire for a mile or two. Then she stopped watching it. It was Rafe who saw where it ended, and told her: past the third ridge the wire stopped and the poles went on without it, bare, and then the poles stopped too. At Wayford he wanted to see the Staple, and she walked him round the square while the horses were changed, and he looked at the building on the north side with the ram over its door and did not ask about it, because every child in Kaldo knew about it and nobody talked about it. He looked at the place by the pump where the gallows had been. The setts there were the same as the setts everywhere else.

A mile north of Wayford the coach went over Harrow Bridge.

There was nothing there now. The toll-house had been pulled down for firewood the winter after the fight, and the pole that had stood across the road in Lisle's green and white lay in the bracken by the roadside with the paint weathering off it. Rafe craned to see it. She did not.

Two miles north of that the road ran past an oak.

It was a big oak, old, standing alone at the edge of a field with a stone trough under it where the carriers watered their horses, and it was in full leaf, and the coach did not stop at it. She had been sitting on the side of the coach away from it and did not turn her head. She knew exactly where it was. She knew which branch, and which way the leaves had not moved.

"Somebody's put flowers on the trough," Rafe said, with his face at the glass.

"Have they."

"Wild ones. Dog roses. Somebody from Wayford, I suppose." He watched it go by behind them. "That's the oak, isn't it? Where they..."

"Yes."

He sat back. He did not say anything more. After a while he took the lamp stone out of his pocket and held it in his fist.

Her bag was on the seat beside her. In it, between the two shirts and the tower's letter, was her working book, the one she had carried since Carrow Ford, and in the working book, at a page near the middle, was an entry she had made on this road two summers ago with her hand shaking so badly she had had to write it twice. It was not a working. The medium moved because he was angry, and for no other cause I could find. And pressed between that page and the next, where she had put it that evening and never taken it out, a printed sheet that had been in a hanged boy's coat.

She knew it was there. She could feel it there the way you feel a stone in your boot, without looking. She did not take it out. She did not take the book out at all, the whole way north.

The tower chapel was full.

Jory Mallen had been liked. He had been the kind of man who remembered everyone's name and the names of their children and which of them had the cough, and half the mages in the capital had come, and a good many who had been in his year and had posts within a few days' ride. Tessaly sat near the back with Rafe beside her, because she had come in late off the coach, and looked at the backs of their heads, and knew a great many of them.

It was only afterwards, in the tower's long refectory where there was cold meat and small beer for the mourners, that she saw their faces.

Idony Vale found her first. Idony had been the cleverest girl in their year and the unkindest, and had gone to the palace cisterns and stayed there, and they had written to each other twice a year for twenty-five years about nothing at all. Idony came across the refectory with both hands out and her face lit, and took Tessaly's hands, and then stopped.

"Tess," Idony said. "Tess, look at you."

Idony's hair was grey all through, iron grey, cut short in a way that showed her ears. The skin of her face had gone soft and loose at the jaw, and there were deep lines from her nose to the corners of her mouth, and the hands holding Tessaly's were a woman's well past fifty, knuckly and spotted, with the tendons standing up on the backs of them. She was forty-seven.

"Look at you," Tessaly said, which was what people said.

"No, look at you," Idony said, and laughed. "What are they feeding you down there? I heard you'd gone south. Everyone's heard. We thought you'd gone mad. We thought old Armand had got you with a letter and you'd be back in a year with your tail between your legs." She had not let go of Tessaly's hands. "You look thirty-five. Don't you dare tell me it's the sea air."

"It's the sea air," Tessaly said.

Idony laughed again, and the others came over.

Roul Denham, who had been a fire mage at the crown kilns on the north road for twenty years and now walked with a stick. Cass Wither, earth, from a wall post in the west, who coughed into a handkerchief between every sentence and folded the handkerchief carefully each time before she put it away. Old Maurin, who had not been in their year at all but had taught them their first hand-forms and was past eighty and was there because he went to every funeral of every pupil he had outlived, of which there were now a great many. They crowded round her. They kissed her cheek. They held her at arm's length.

"You've found a young husband," Roul said.

"I haven't."

"Then you've found an old one with a young cook." He grinned. His hands on the head of his stick were stiff at every joint, and when he lifted one to wave at a servant for more beer, his sleeve slid back, and there was a faint silvery tracery on the inside of his wrist, fine as frost on a window, going up under the cuff. He saw her see it and pulled the sleeve down. "Bad winter at the kilns," he said. "Four years back. Two of the others went sick and I did their shift and mine for a month. It's nothing. It doesn't go any further."

"Of course not."

"Seriously, Tess." Cass folded her handkerchief. "What is it? You were always the plain one. I don't mean that unkindly. You were plain and tired and you had that line between your eyebrows since you were twenty. It's gone."

Tessaly put her hand to her forehead without meaning to. The skin there was smooth.

"Hard work," she said. "Good food. Early nights."

"Early nights," Roul said. "In Kaldo. Where they hang lords in the market square and fight dukes with iron tubes." He laughed. "Early nights."

They laughed with him. It went on for a while, the jokes. The south must agree with her. She must have been bathing in asses' milk. She must have sold her soul to the prince, and wasn't he meant to be a demon, according to the duke, and if so could she put in a word for Roul's knees. It was all kindly meant. They were pleased to see her. They were old friends at a funeral, glad of something to laugh at.

Then they stopped making them.

She could not have said when. There was no moment. Only that at some point in the next quarter hour, Idony looked at her across the table with the joke still on her face and the joke went out of it, and Cass stopped folding the handkerchief and just held it, and Roul looked down at his own hands on his stick, and then at hers on the table. Her hands were a woman's of thirty-five. They were brown from two summers on the wall, and there were calluses on the heels of them from the long work of lifting earth, and they were smooth and quick and there was nothing on the wrists at all.

Nobody said anything about it. Idony asked about the wall. Tessaly told her about the wall. Roul asked about the kilns in Kaldo, whether they burned hotter than the crown kilns, and she told him they did, and why. Cass asked after Bastian Farrant, who had gone south in the same cart, and Tessaly said he was well, and did not say anything about his forearm. They talked about Jory, and his cuffs, and his children. It was all perfectly pleasant. But nobody made another joke about her face, and when they said goodbye in the refectory door at dusk, Idony held her hand a moment longer than she needed to and looked at it, and then let it go.

The assessment hall was on the tower's third floor, up a stair Tessaly had last climbed at nineteen with her heart in her mouth.

It had not changed. The long bare room with its whitewashed walls and its high windows on the north side, where the light was steady and did not change through the day. The table in the middle, oak, scarred by two hundred years of nervous hands. And the rack on the table: the reference stones, eleven of them, graded from one the size of a pea to one the size of a man's fist, each in its own brass cup, each carved with the tower's mark. She had held the first seven, at nineteen. Her hand had failed on the eighth. The eighth was a wall-mage's stone. It was what she had been, all her life.

The assessor was a man she did not know, grey-haired and neat and about fifty, called Master Hebden. He had the letter she had written, signed as Armand's former pupil and mistress of the wall at Kaldo, and he read it without comment, and looked at Rafe.

"Sit," he said. "Right hand on the table, palm up. Have you been measured before?"

"No, sir."

"Never? Not by your village priest? Not at your lord's hall?"

"No, sir. Only by her." Rafe nodded at Tessaly. "With a lamp stone."

Master Hebden looked at Tessaly briefly, the look of a tower man at a wall-mage who had been playing with lamp stones in the provinces. Then he took the first stone out of its cup and laid it in Rafe's palm.

It was the method she remembered. You held the stone and the assessor put two fingers on the back of your wrist and drew, gently, steadily, through you and into the stone, and the stone took what you had until it was full, and then it glowed, and then you went on to the next. The drawing was the tower's. Nobody else was taught it. Every mage in the realm had sat at this table and felt that steady pull through the back of the wrist and watched the stones light one after another until one did not.

The first stone lit in the time it takes to breathe in. So did the second. The third. Master Hebden did not look up. The fourth, the fifth, the sixth. At the seventh he glanced at Rafe's face, and Rafe was looking at the stone with his lips pressed together in concentration, the way he looked at a load of earth he was lifting, and not in any distress at all.

The eighth lit.

The ninth lit.

Tessaly had never seen the ninth lit. She had heard of it. The ninth was a master's stone, and the tower had perhaps a dozen living masters, and she had been in the tower for three years and never once been in the room when a candidate lit it. It lit slowly, with a clear pale glow like a lamp behind oiled paper, and it held.

Master Hebden took his fingers off Rafe's wrist.

"Again," he said. He said it quite quietly.

He emptied the stones, one by one, with a small brass key that went into the base of each cup. He did it carefully and without hurrying, and when they were all dark he put the first one back in Rafe's hand and his two fingers back on Rafe's wrist, and drew.

It went the same. One through nine, in the same time, near enough. At the ninth he did not stop. He took the tenth out of its cup, a stone as big as a hen's egg, and laid it in Rafe's other hand, and drew.

It lit.

The tenth stone was a knight's. It was the stone that was used, once a year, in the cathedral, on the young men who had come out of the chamber at the end of the fourteenth hour, to say that the rite had worked. Tessaly knew that the way every mage knew it, as a fact from a book. She had never seen it done.

Master Hebden looked at the lit stone in Rafe's palm for some time. Then he put it back in its cup, carefully, with the glow still in it. He did not try the eleventh.

"Push up your sleeves," he said. "Both of them. To the shoulder."

Rafe looked at Tessaly. She nodded. He pushed up his sleeves.

His arms were a boy's arms, long and thin still, brown on the forearms from the summer and white above where his shirt had covered them. There was a scar across the back of the left hand from a slipped chisel in the etching shed. There was nothing else. No tracery. Not at the wrists. Not in the crook of the elbow. Not on the inside of the upper arm where it showed first on the ones who had pushed too hard, fine and silver, going in under the skin like frost into glass. Nothing. They were clean.

Master Hebden took each of Rafe's wrists in turn and turned it to the window's steady north light, and looked at it, and let it go.

"Have you been in the chamber?" he said.

"Sir?"

"The cathedral. The rite. Have you been through it? Has anyone ever taken you anywhere at night and kept you there, with stones, for a long time?"

Rafe's face went blank with surprise. "No, sir."

"How old are you?"

"Fifteen, sir. Sixteen at harvest."

Master Hebden sat back.

He did not look at Tessaly. She was grateful for that, afterwards. He looked at the boy for a while with a sort of careful blankness, the way she had seen Emeric look at a machine that was doing something it should not do and had not yet broken.

"Where were you raised?" he said.

"Kaldo, sir. On the island. My mother works at the vats."

"Kaldo," Master Hebden said.

He drew the tower's register towards him across the table. It was a great heavy book bound in black, and every mage the tower had ever licensed was in it, name and year and stone, in the hands of fifty assessors going back two hundred years. He turned to the day's page and dipped his pen.

He wrote Rafe's name, and the date, and the tenth, twice, and under that, in the column for remarks, where Tessaly had seen other assessors write things like nervous or son of Master so-and-so, he wrote No marks. No rite. Aged fifteen.

Then he paused with the pen above the page, as though deciding something, and wrote one more word, very neatly, in the column for the place of raising. He wrote it the way a clerk writes a word he expects to be asked to find again.

Kaldo.

Tessaly looked at it.

She thought of Idony's grey hair. She thought of Roul's wrist, and Cass's handkerchief, and her own hand going to the smooth place between her eyebrows. She thought of the tower in the first quarter at New Kaldo, stone base and lead-lined oak tank and brass tap, and the day she had laid her hand on its stone as it was built and tasted a thing in it she had no name for, and had taken her hand away, and not asked. She thought of the market tower on the island, and the island children drinking at the troughs, and a boy who had swept an etching floor since he was eleven and drunk the island's water every day of his life, holding a knight's stone lit in his fist at fifteen with clean arms.

She thought of Armand, going out to the east gate before she was up. Wait, he would have said.

The ink on the page was drying. Master Hebden sanded it, and blew the sand off, and looked at it once more, and then he looked up, not at Rafe but at her. It was a perfectly courteous look. It was the look of a careful man who had just written down a word he would be asked to find again, and knew it, and wanted to see the face of the person who had brought it to him.

Tessaly understood then what she had brought north, and that she could not take it home again.
