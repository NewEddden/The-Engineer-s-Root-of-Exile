Chapter 183 - The Leash

Rowena's suit came out of the forge on the eleventh of October, and it was wrong in the leg by two fingers, and Tess found it before it was off the rails.

He had built it from memory. There was nothing else to build it from. He had no measurements of Rowena, and no way to get any that would not have told the whole of Emberfell what was coming in her harbor at midwinter; so he had sat in the room with the forge's pattern for the knights' suit open in front of him, the MK2 that Ward and Declan and Osric had flown since the spring, two hearts and one stick and one lift lever, and closed his eyes, and remembered her.

That had been the hard part. Not the plate.

She had been at court twice after his mother died, for a season each time, and he had been eleven the first time and twelve the second, and at twelve she had taken him out into the practice yard behind the stables on a wet morning and put a wooden sword in his hand and shown him how to hold it, properly, the way his mother would have wanted him to, she said, and then knocked him down eleven times. He remembered her standing over him in the mud. The height of her. The reach. The way she set her feet. He remembered the shape of her shoulders under a riding coat, and the scar across the back of her left hand, and the way she looked down at him and did not laugh.

So he built her that. Bright steel, not black. She would never wear black; she had told him once, in the yard, that a knight who hid in the dark was a knight who expected to lose. Blue panes in the helm and the chest heart's port, the blue of Emberfell, instead of Kaldo's violet. No lion. No device at all on the breast, because she would put her own there, and he did not know what she would want it to be. The cooling first, before the heating, for Emberfell's summers: two cold stones in the back under the hearts, set to pull, with their exhaust plates down the spine, so that she could fly in her own harbor in August without cooking.

And one thing no other suit had. A second ring of nodes, six of them, small, at the forearms and the thighs and down the spine, that would wake on her mana and no one else's.

That was the part he had argued with Ward about. A knight's suit lifted on six ounces of gravity stone, because a knight's body could not be held light by anything but the stone. Rowena's would carry twelve. The second six would lie asleep in their barrels, dead weight, for anybody else who put the suit on; and for Rowena they would wake, because they had been cut to her note, or to his memory of it, which he had felt from across a practice yard at twelve without knowing what he was feeling, and kept. She had mana sense. She had earth, and a knight's body over it, and a mother's old training under that. With twelve ounces and her own hand on the stone she would fly heavier, faster, and closer to the ground than any of Kaldo's knights, and the suit would do it for nobody else in the world.

"A leash," Ward had said, when he heard. "You're giving her a suit on a leash."

"I'm giving her a suit nobody can take off her and fly."

"That's what a leash is," said Ward. "From the other end."

The suit came off the forge's rails in the morning, and Bram took the plates off the bed one at a time with his tongs, and laid them out on the cellar floor on a sheet in their order; and Tess came down the cellar ramp on the motor and stopped her chair at the foot of the sheet and looked at it. She looked at it for a long time. Then she leaned out of the chair, and picked up the left greave and the left cuisse, and held them against each other in the air, the shin to the thigh, the way they would sit on a body; and then she put them down, and picked up her book, and turned back through it to a page, and ran her finger down a column.

"How tall is she?" said Tess.

"I don't know exactly."

"You've made her legs two fingers longer than her arms say." Tess held up the left vambrace. "If she's as long in the arm as this, she's as long in the leg as Ward. Not longer." She looked at the greave on the sheet. "This is somebody standing over you."

He looked at the greave.

She was right. He saw it the moment she said it, the way a man sees a misprint once it has been pointed at. He had been twelve. He had been lying in the mud. Everything he remembered of Rowena he remembered from below.

"Take two fingers off the leg," he said.

"I'll take one," said Tess. "And leave the straps long for the other. In case you're right and the arms are wrong." She wrote it in her book. "People don't grow, much, after they've knocked you down."



His own suit came off the rails the same week, and he could not fly it.

It was thin. Thinner than any of the knights' plate, because he did not need the plate to hold a body light the way a knight's did; he had his own gravity for that, and his hybrid side underneath it, and what he wanted from the suit was the hearts for push and the cape and the chute for when everything else went wrong, and as little steel as he could hang them on. Black, plain, no lion. And no levers. No stick in the left gauntlet, no lift lever on the belt, nothing in its quadrant. The suit's stones were cut to his band, the way the K Discovery's stones were cut to their levers; the band's grey bead sat against his wrist inside the gauntlet, and he flew the suit through it, the way he would have flown his own body, by telling it.

That was the idea. It took him a week of bruises to find out what was wrong with it.

What was wrong with it was that a lever stayed where you put it. If he put the lift lever at the third notch and took his hand off it, the suit went on lifting at the third notch until he moved it again; it remembered for him. The band did not remember anything. It did what he told it while he was telling it, and the moment he thought about anything else, the turn of the river under him, a gull, a word, the push faded, and he fell. Not far. A foot, two, before he caught it. But he caught it every time with his own gravity, without meaning to, the way a hand comes up when a man trips, and that was no good, because the whole point of the suit was the nights when his own gravity would not come.

So he made himself not catch it. He fell into the river off the shed's slip on the second night, from twelve feet, and into the shallows off the north bank on the fourth, from twenty, and onto the shed roof on the fifth, which hurt more, and Fletcher came out of the shed in his nightshirt with a lamp and looked up at him lying on the slates and said that if the prince wanted to come in he could use the door like a Christian.

On the seventh night he flew it.

Not well. But he went up off the slip on the push and stayed up, and went out over the river and along it to the bridge and back, with his mind on the push the whole time, and his own gravity held down hard inside him like a dog on a short rope; and when he came down onto the slip, the suit came down because he had told it to and not because he had stopped telling it anything.

Tess wrote the time in her book.

"You've built yourself a suit on a leash too," she said. "It's only that the leash is your own head."



Ward fought him in the suit in the second week, and put him on his back eleven times, which Emeric noted was the same as Rowena, and did not say.

It was the fourth stage of Ward's yard, the one Ward had been building toward since the spring: everything together. Not the sense alone, or the burst alone, or the hybrid side in his arms alone, the way the first three stages had drilled them; all of it, in one breath, against a man who knew every one of them was coming. Feel where Ward was going to be. Burst the sense out wide for a heartbeat to read it, and shut it again before it dazzled. Drive the arms. Go light, the Light Step, to change his ground, or plant himself with the Stop, heavy, so that a blow that should have moved him did not. And at the end, one spell. Not three. One, small, exact, in the one place it would do the most.

He could do each of them. He had been able to do each of them for months. Doing them together was like singing four songs at once.

And Ward feinted. Every time. A shoulder that dropped, a foot that turned, a breath taken as if before a cut; and Emeric's sense read the drop and the turn and the breath, perfectly, and told him exactly what Ward meant to do, and Ward did not do it. Ward did something else, from the other side, a heartbeat later, and Emeric was on his back in the yard's sand looking up at the sky with the black plate of the suit pressing into his spine.

"You read me," said Ward, standing over him. "Good. You read me right every time." He held out a hand. "And you believe what you read. Every time." He pulled him up. "A man who knows you can sense him will give you something to sense. Rowena will know by the first pass."

"How do I stop believing it?"

"You don't," said Ward. "You read it, and you wait for the second thing." He stepped back. "Again."

On the ninth fall Emeric waited. Ward dropped his shoulder. Emeric felt it, and did not move; and the cut came from the other side, as he had known it would, and he went light into it, and the blow that should have put him down turned him instead, half round, like a door on a hinge, and he came back out of the turn heavy with his weight planted, and his hand was at Ward's throat.

They stood like that in the sand.

Ward looked down at the black gauntlet under his chin.

"Once," said Ward. "Out of nine." He stepped back. "She'll give you one in nine too. Make sure it's the last one." And put him on his back the tenth and the eleventh times, quickly, as if to make sure he did not get ideas.



On the night of the eighteenth they flew to Milford in the dark, the two of them, Emeric in his own suit and Ward in his, to see if the door would hold in the air.

The hair-width. It was the only name he had for it, and it was the right one. Not the room itself; nobody could go into the room in the air, flying, with the push to hold and the wind to read. Only the room held open the width of a hair, from the band's grey bead against his wrist, out along the thread of his sense to a note he knew; and words through it. He had done it across the shed with a drawing held up to it. Across the keep yard to Ward on the wall walk. With two at once, Ward and Ferra, which had cost him the rest of the evening and the next morning. He had never done it with a hundred yards of black air between them and the push of two hearts behind each of them and the ground going by underneath at thirty miles an hour.

They went up off the shed's slip at midnight and climbed to a thousand feet over the river and turned west along the line, the rails pale under the stars, and Ward went out ahead. A hundred yards. Two hundred. A black shape against the stars with a dark lantern on his belt, shuttered, so that only Emeric's sense could find him, a hard close note like a wall in the dark.

Emeric opened the door.

It was like threading a needle in a high wind. The push behind his breastbone wanted all of him. The wind wanted the rest. And the thread of his sense went out across the dark toward Ward's note and wavered, and caught, and lost it, and caught it again; and he felt the quiet come in at the other end, the moment before words, the hush of a room where somebody has just stopped talking. Ward would feel it too. A breath ahead.

Can you hear me.

The shape ahead of him dipped a wing, once, which was the signal they had agreed.

Three hundred yards. He let Ward go out to three hundred, and held it there, and felt it fray at the edges like a rope going over a sharp stone, and held it anyway.

Then he put Linus's map over the ground.

He had carried it into the room that afternoon and laid it out on the floor there, the Milford survey, every drain and field and steading Linus had pegged in four years, with the line in red down the middle of it and the steadings in black; and now, holding the door open the width of a hair, he reached into the room through it and lifted the map, and laid it over what Ward was seeing. Faint. A pattern on a window, with the world still there behind it. The real dark fields of Milford under the stars, and over them, in thin red and black lines, as if somebody had drawn on the night with a fine pen, Linus's drains running straight across them, and the steadings, and the line.

The shape ahead of him dipped a wing. Then it did something he had not agreed with it: it turned, a slow banking turn off the line, north, toward the second steading on the bottoms, the one Linus had marked with a cross because its well was bad; and came down low over it, two hundred feet, and circled it once, and climbed away again, back onto the line.

The cost came in behind his eyes all at once, the hand closing, the way it closed when he held two doors. He let the map go. He let the door go. He flew back along the line to Kaldo with his head aching and the push behind his breastbone very steady, because he held it steady with everything he had left.

On the slip, Ward took his helm off.

"The cross was the bad well," he said. "Linus's cross. I could see it on the ground." He stood with the helm under his arm and looked west, toward Milford, in the dark. "I could see a cross on a field in the dark from three hundred yards up that nobody had drawn on the field." He was quiet. "I don't like it."

"I know."

"I'll want it," said Ward. "The night I need it, I'll want it more than anything you've built." He went into the shed with the helm. "That's what I don't like."



Rylan took his suit off the rack on the night of the twentieth, without a word.

It had stood at the end of the row since the spring. Built to the measurements Tess had taken off him for the first suit, with its lions on the breast and its winged helm and its violet panes; its hearts empty, its cape furled, its harness hanging on the hook under it where Tess had hung it in April, waiting. He had looked at it a little longer than at the others every time he came into the shed, and gone back to the line room, and never once asked. Emeric had never offered. A man who had once put a knife to his throat in the dark, for his son's sake, and been told to come back in seven days, and come back, did not need to be offered things. He needed to be let take them.

Emeric was in the shed that night, at the bench by the river doors, with the forge book and a lamp. He did not look up when Rylan came in. He heard him cross the floor, and stop at the end of the row, and stand there a while; and then the small sounds of a harness coming off a hook, and plate being lifted, the cuisse and the greave, laid out on the floor in their order.

Tess came down from the works on the motor a quarter of an hour later, because Fletcher had gone up the hill to fetch her, without being asked either, which Emeric noted. She came in through the river doors in her chair and stopped, and looked at Rylan standing at the end of the row in his shirt with his own suit laid out on the floor at his feet, and did not say anything at all.

She fitted it. It took her two hours, by lamplight, the way she had fitted all of them: a strap at a time, a buckle at a time, her hands quick and cold on the leather, her book open on the drafting board on her knees. She did not ask him why now. He did not say. When the chest heart went in, and she turned the key in its port, and the stones woke, violet, behind the pane, Rylan stood very still in the middle of the shed floor with his arms out from his sides and his face doing nothing at all.

"Lever," said Tess.

He put his hand on the lift lever, and moved it a notch, and went up off the floor a foot, and hung there, in the lamplight, between the racks, with his boots dangling.

"Down," said Tess. He came down. She wrote in her book. "Ward'll take you up tomorrow," she said, and put the book away, and turned the chair to go; and at the door she stopped, with her back to him. "It's been waiting since April," she said, to the river. "It was always yours."

Rylan did not say anything. Emeric, at the bench, did not look up from the forge book, and turned a page he had not read.

Osric came into the shed the next morning, before the shift, while Tess was at the bench writing up the night, and asked her to check his suit.

She looked up.

"It's not wrong," said Osric. "I don't think it's wrong. I only thought, since you were checking Rylan's." He stood by his rack with his hands behind his back. "The chest strap. It rides up when I turn. I thought you'd want to know. For the book."

Tess came over on the motor and checked his suit. She checked the chest strap, and the harness, and the watchdog's springs, and the cape's battens, and the chute's pack, and the cell latches in both hearts, with her quick cold hands and her face very close to the plate, while Osric stood beside his rack and looked at the ceiling of the shed.

"Nothing's wrong with it," she said.

"Good," said Osric. "That's good."

"The strap rides up because you lift your arm before you turn instead of after." She wrote in her book. "Turn first."

"Turn first," said Osric.

He came back the next Tuesday, at an hour when Bram was at the furnace across the river, which Emeric did not think was chance, and asked her to check it again. She checked it again. Nothing was wrong with it. Emeric, at the bench, turned another page he had not read.



He found Ferra in the exchange, on the last night of October, very late.

He had not meant to find her anywhere. He had been in the line room until the eleventh hour with the board man and the new trunk to Wayford, which buzzed, and when he came down the stair the door of the old linen press was open a hand's width and there was a lamp lit inside. The exchange's racks stood in the front of it, three tall frames of oak with the selectors in rows on them, brass and black, humming very faintly in the dark; and behind them, in the back, where the linen press's old shelves still ran to the ceiling and the whole room smelled of lavender from forty years of folded sheets, there was a desk Ottoline had put there for the exchange's call book, and Ferra at it.

She was copying the month's calls into the barony's book. Every call on the exchange was counted by a little wheel on its selector, and the wheels were read on the last day of the month and the count went in the book under each line's name, because a telephone that cost nothing to use would be used for nothing, Ferra said, and she wanted to know what it was being used for. It was the kind of work she did at the eleventh hour because nobody else would have thought it needed doing. Her hair was pinned up with three pins and coming down from all of them.

She did not look up when he came in.

"The counting room's had nine hundred calls in October," she said. "Four hundred and twelve of them from the gate tower."

"Ottoline."

"Ottoline." Ferra wrote the figure. "She rings me to argue about carrying. She could walk across the yard in less time than it takes the selector." She blotted the line. "I've told her so. She says the walk doesn't give her the satisfaction of hearing me pick up."

He stood at the end of the desk. A selector clicked, somewhere in the dark of the racks behind them, a wiper stepping round its bank, and stopped; and somewhere below in the keep a bell rang once, faintly, and was answered. Somebody calling somebody at the end of the evening.

"You're still angry about the grain," he said.

"I'm not angry about the grain." She turned the page. "I'm right about the grain. That's different. Being right isn't a feeling." She ran her finger down the next column. "I wrote it in the book. It's done. You'll be wrong at Emberfell, and you'll say so out loud, and then it'll be over."

"Then why are you still here at the eleventh hour copying out Ottoline's calls?"

Ferra's finger stopped on the page.

She sat for a while without moving. Then she put the pen down beside the book, carefully, the way she always put it down, and stood up, and came round the end of the desk, and put one hand flat on his chest, and kissed him.

Not to stop a speech. He had not been making one. She kissed him the way she had kissed him in the ink at her trestle once he had stopped making it, as if she had been deciding to for some time and had finished the sum; and then she did not stop. Her other hand came up and took hold of his collar. He put his hands at her waist, carefully, the way he always did, as if she might break, and she made a small impatient sound against his mouth, and took one of his hands off her waist and put it back there harder, and he understood that she did not want him careful, and stopped being careful.

The pins went. He did not know which of them took them out. One of them hit the desk with a tick and the other two went somewhere in the dark among the racks, and her hair came down, all of it, red and heavy and smelling faintly of ink and of the lavender that was in everything in that room, over his hands and her shoulders, and she pushed it back out of her face with one hand without stopping kissing him, impatient with that too.

The selectors clicked in the dark behind them. Neither of them heard.

He felt it come up in him. He felt it the way he had felt it at her trestle, the deep quiet thing that did not fall but rose, through his hands at her waist into her, and the glyph came up on its own in the air beside her face, soft and pale and violet, lighting her cheek and her closed eyes and her freckles and the hair across her mouth; and the floor went.

Not far. A foot. Both of them, her weight and his, taken off together, as easily as he would have lifted a cup; and he did not drop them this time. The hold did not waver. More than two years since the ink. Every night of them. It held now, steady, and he turned them, slowly, on a breath of air from somewhere he had not asked it to come from, a lazy half turn in the lamplight a foot above the linen press floor, with the shelves going round them and her hair swinging out with the turn and settling.

She felt the floor go. He felt her feel it. Her hand on his collar closed hard, once, and then she laughed, against his mouth, without stopping, a short breath of a laugh through her nose, and pulled him closer by the collar, and kissed him harder, and did not open her eyes.

The door opened.

It opened all the way, on its hinges, with the corridor's lamplight behind it; and in the doorway, with a tray in both hands, stood Maren.

She had a teapot on the tray, and two cups, and a plate of the honey rings from the Lantern Night cart under a cloth. She had brought it because the chancellor was working at the eleventh hour again and had not come down for supper, and the chancellor never came down for supper when she was working at the eleventh hour, and somebody in the keep had to see that she ate, and it had been Maren for five years. She stood in the doorway with the tray and looked into the linen press.

At the chancellor of four baronies, with her hair down to her waist and her hands in the prince's collar. At the prince, with his hands at the chancellor's waist. At the pair of them, a foot off the floor, turning very slowly in the air in a soft violet light, between the shelves, with nothing under their feet at all.

The glyph went out. They came down. Not fast; he caught it, from long habit, the way a man catches a rail on a stair he has missed, and set them down on the boards, gently, a little apart. Ferra stood with her hair everywhere and her hands still on his collar, and did not take them away.

Maren looked at them both for a long breath.

Then she came into the room, and put the tray down on the desk beside the barony's book, and moved the book out of the way of the teapot, and took the cloth off the honey rings, and straightened the cups, and picked up the pin that had hit the desk and put it on the tray by the plate.

"I saw nothing," said Maren, very loudly, to the shelves.

She was sworn to the ship, as everybody who would sail in her was, to carry its secrets as she carried her own; and Emeric knew exactly which two things she had just seen, and so did she.

"I saw nothing at all," said Maren, louder, going to the door. "Nothing on the floor and nothing off it. The tea's hot. Eat the rings, Chancellor, you've had nothing since noon." She stopped in the doorway with her hand on the latch, and looked back once, at Ferra's hair, with an expression Emeric could not read and was not sure he wanted to. "I never see anything," she said. "Ask anybody." And shut the door behind her, firmly, and went away down the corridor; and they heard her on the stair, going down, saying something to herself that ended in a laugh she did not quite manage to keep in.

The linen press was very quiet.

A selector clicked in the racks behind them, and stopped.

Ferra took her hands off his collar. She looked at the door. Then she looked at the tray, and the teapot steaming on it, and the honey rings, and the single pin beside the plate, put there square, as if it were part of the service.

"She straightened the cups," said Ferra.

"Yes."

"She came in, and saw that, and straightened the cups." Ferra put the back of her wrist against her mouth. Her shoulders were shaking. It took him a moment to see that she was laughing, and then she could not stop, and sat down on the edge of the desk with her hair all round her and her wrist against her mouth and laughed until her eyes were wet, silently, the way she had once laughed in her doorway in January, trying not to; and then not silently at all.

He sat down on the desk beside her. He was laughing too. He did not know when he had started.

"I'm going to eat the rings," said Ferra, when she could. "She'll ask." She picked one up and looked at it. "And then I'm going to finish the calls. And you're going to go to bed." She did not look at him. "Your own."

"Yes."

"Not because of her." She bit the ring. "Because if I don't finish the calls tonight I'll have to finish them in the morning with Hale reading over my shoulder, and I'd rather die." She chewed. "And because I'd rather not be found twice in one night."

He went. At the door he looked back. She was at the desk again with her hair still down and a honey ring in one hand and the pen in the other, writing, and she did not look up; but she had moved the pin on the tray to the corner of the book, and set it there, exactly square, where she would see it.



She told Imani in the morning.

He knew because Imani told him, at the ward's side door at noon, when he came down with the month's figures for the hospital's coal. She was on the step in the sun with her sleeves pinned and a cup of something in her hands, and she took the figures from him and read them without looking at him, and corrected one, and handed them back.

"Ferra came to see me," she said. "Before the counting room opened. With her hair pinned up very hard." She drank from the cup. "She told me all of it. The calls. The pins. The floor. Maren and the cups." She looked at the river. "She stood in my kitchen and told me in order, the way she tells me the quarter's figures. Term two."

He did not know what to say. He said nothing.

"I asked her one thing," said Imani.

"What?"

"Where your hands were." She drank again. "For the chart."

He looked at her.

"She said that wasn't a clinical question," said Imani. "I said everything's a clinical question, if you're a healer and you're asking. She went red. Not her ordinary red. All the way up into her hair, so you couldn't see where the freckles stopped." She looked at the cup in her hands. "I've never seen her do that. Not in five years. Not once." Something happened at the corner of her mouth, and stayed there. "I enjoyed it very much."

"Imani."

"I'm not angry," said Imani. "You're going to ask me that. I'm telling you first so you needn't." She turned her head and looked at him, in the sun, with her eyes narrowed a little against it. "I thought I might be. I've lain awake a few nights since the terms wondering whether I would be, when something like this happened. And then she stood in my kitchen this morning with her hair pinned up so hard it must have hurt, and told me about the cups, and I found I wasn't. I found I was mostly glad it was Maren and not Ward." She finished the cup. "Ward would have said something."

"Maren said something."

"Maren said nothing, very loudly," said Imani. "That's different. Ferra told me that too." She stood up off the step and turned to go back in; and at the door she stopped, with her hand on the frame, the way she had stood in the first summer with her hand on the frame and not on him. "Your hands were at her waist," she said, without turning round. "She did tell me. In the end. I made her." And went in, and he heard her laugh once in the passage, short and dry through her nose, and then her voice telling somebody that the bed by the window wanted changing.
