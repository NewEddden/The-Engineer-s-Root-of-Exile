Chapter 183: Home to a Wall

The page had no heading. Ferra had left it blank on purpose, the morning after the carriage went out of the Western Gate, and every time she wrote on it she was tempted to give it one and did not.

It was the last page of the spring's day book, the one after the index, where nobody would turn by accident. On it she wrote, in the order they happened, the things that were decided in Kaldo while he was not in Kaldo. Not the ordinary things. The ordinary things went where they always went, in the day book and the works book and the fund's book and the box. This page was for the things that he would have decided if he had been here, and had not been, and had been decided anyway.

By the end of the second week there were four lines on it. By the time the relay slip came up from the marsh post to say the Milford carriage had been seen on the coast road, there were nineteen.

The first line was the lamps.

Bram had wanted them since the autumn. The coke ovens at the works gave off a gas that nobody had wanted, a foul yellow stink of rotten eggs that hung in the mill yard on still days and made the men at the ovens cough, and that burned blue and steady if anyone was fool enough to put a lamp near a vent. Emeric had said, the spring the ovens were built, that it could be taken off and kept and burned on purpose, and had drawn a bell of iron standing upside down in a pit of water to keep it in, and had not built it, because there had been a converter and a rail works and a city to build first. Bram had built it in the winter, in the works yard, while nobody was looking. He had called it the bell. It rose and fell in its pit as the ovens filled it and emptied, like a slow animal breathing.

"I want to run a pipe to the market street," Bram said, at the long-room table, in the second week, with Armand in the chair at the table's head because the sealed letter put him there. "Lead. Nevin's cast me the lengths. Along the bridge on the downstream side under the rail, and up the street under the setts. Eight lamps on posts. As a trial."

"What does it cost?" Ferra said.

He told her. Four Gold, two Silver, most of it in lead and the rest in the lamp heads, which Jorrin was turning. She wrote it down.

"What does he say?" Armand asked.

"He said it could be done," Bram said. "The spring the ovens went up. He didn't say when."

Armand looked at Ferra. Ferra looked at the figure. The purse was hers to veto and she had vetoed nothing all spring.

"A trial," she said. "Eight lamps. The market street and no further. And nothing under any house."

The lamps were lit on the fourteenth evening after the carriage left, at dusk, by Bram himself with a taper on a pole, one after another up the street from the bridge foot to the market cross. The whole island came to look. Children ran from post to post. Old women stood in their doorways with their arms folded and said it was unnatural and did not go in. The light was whiter than oil and steadier than tallow and it made the market street look like a street in a dream, with long clean shadows and every sett shining, and people walked up and down under it until nearly midnight for the pleasure of seeing each other's faces after dark.

Ferra wrote it on the blank page. The lamps. Lit on the market street, eight posts, as a trial. B.'s bell. A. in the chair. F. did not veto.

Nine days later the pipe under Pruett's chandlery cracked.

It was the frost. There had been a late one, a hard white night at the start of the thaw, and the ground under the street had heaved and settled, and a joint in the lead where the pipe went under the chandlery's cellar wall had opened a hair's width. Not under a house: under the street, a yard from the house. But a cellar is a hole in the ground, and gas goes where there is room for it.

The chandler's journeyman went down for tallow a little after first light. His name was Kit Hallam and he was twenty and he had carried tallow up those stairs every morning for four years. He did not come up. The chandler's wife went to the stair head to shout at him and smelled it, the egg-stink, thick, much thicker than at the ovens, and she was a sensible woman and did not take a candle down after him. She ran out into the street and screamed for the fire.

The fire company's second watch was at the market post. They had drilled for fire three times a week for two years and they had drilled for nothing like this, but Ward had written a rule into the garrison book the week the lamps went in, because Ward wrote a rule for everything, and the watch captain had read it. If the gas is smelled in any cellar, no flame within ten yards. Two men on a rope. Wet cloth over the face. In, up, out. Nobody goes in without a rope. They went in on a rope, two of them, with wet cloths over their faces, and brought Kit Hallam up the cellar stair between them in less time than it took the chandler's wife to stop screaming.

He was not breathing, or not so anyone could see. His face was a bad colour, flushed and wrong, pink in the cheeks like a man who had run a mile, when everything else about him said he was dying.

Imani was in the street before they had him laid out on the setts. Somebody had run for her. She knelt on the wet stone and tipped his head back and put her mouth over his and breathed into him, and pressed on his chest, and breathed into him again, and the watch stood round in a ring and nobody said anything, and the chandler's wife made a sound in her throat, and the setts were cold, and the morning was grey. Ferra came across the market from the counting room with her pen still in her hand and stood at the edge of the ring and counted, because counting was what she did. She counted a hundred and forty.

At a hundred and forty-one Kit Hallam coughed, and turned his head, and was sick on the setts.

Imani sat back on her heels. She did not look relieved. She looked like a woman who had been doing arithmetic and had got the answer she expected.

"Out of the street," she said. "Somewhere with the windows open. He'll have a head on him like a hammer for three days and he'll want to sleep. Don't let him sleep till noon." She looked up at the watch captain. "Who went in?"

He told her.

"Them too. Both of them. Sit them down somewhere open. If either of them gets a headache or feels sick, I want to know."

Ward came down from the gate at a run with a hand from the works behind him, and went straight past all of it to the bridge, to the valve Bram had put where the pipe came onto the island, and shut it with his own hands. By noon the lamps were out. By evening the chandlery's cellar had been aired for six hours and Bram was down in it with a lantern well away from the joint and his nose to the lead, looking at a crack you could not have put a hair into.

"The frost," he said, at the long-room table that night. "It'll do it again. Every thaw. Anywhere the pipe goes under a wall."

"Then it doesn't go under walls," Ferra said.

"It has to go somewhere."

"Then it goes somewhere with a vent in it. And someone walks the line every week with a pot of soap water on the joints and looks for bubbles. And every cellar along the street has a grating cut to the air, at the barony's cost." She wrote as she said it. "And Ward's rule stays in the garrison book. And the lamps stay lit."

Armand, in the chair, looked at her over his steepled fingers.

"You don't want to take them out?"

"Nobody died," Ferra said. "Kit Hallam will have a headache. The watch did what the rule said and the rule worked and Imani did the rest. If we take the lamps out, the next thing anybody tries in this barony while he's away, they'll wonder whether we'll take that out too." She did not look up. "Somebody nearly died and didn't. That's what a trial is for."

Nobody argued. Bram looked, for a moment, as though somebody had given him something.

She wrote it on the page. The gas. Cellar under Pruett's, the frost at a joint. K. Hallam brought out by the second watch on Ward's rule, brought back by I. in a count of 141. Lamps out one day and relit. Vents, soap, the weekly walk. Under it, after a moment, smaller: Not his decision. Ours.

The wall did not wait.

There had been a question, before he left, about the third season. Whether to begin it on the old date, with the first dry week after the thaw, or hold it until he was back to look at the ground. He had not answered it. He had been answering the summons and the licence and the road, and the third season had been a line at the bottom of a long list, and when the carriage left it was still there.

The first dry week came in the middle of the spring. Ferra rode out to the flat on the second morning of it, because the site's book had to be carried back and forth and she did not trust anyone else with it, and found Armand's mages already on the south arc.

They were working in pairs every two hundred paces as they had since the footing, the earth coming up out of the ground in front of them in long slow shoulders and settling into the line of the wall's core, and behind them the masons and the gangs setting stone and lime. It looked like a thing that had been going for years, because it had. The only difference from the autumn was the height. The wall stood at half its full height all the way round the circuit now, grey and raw, with scaffold on its inner face, and from the south arc you could see it curve away east and west across the flat until it was hidden by its own bend.

Tessaly Rook was at the gap by the south gate footing, with the boy Rafe beside her holding a lamp stone in his fist for no reason Ferra could see.

"Who gave the order?" Ferra asked her.

Tessaly looked at her with some surprise.

"Nobody gave an order. It was the date. The drawing says the third season begins the first dry week after the thaw." She shrugged. "It's dry."

Ferra wrote it on the page that evening. The third season. Begun on the drawing's date without an order. Nobody asked for one.

Hull three flew on the night of the dark moon, off the lower water, with Ward at the levers and Fletcher beside him because Fletcher had built her and would not be left on the ground.

Ferra was at the marsh relay post with the book. There was nothing to see. There never was. The lower water was black and the sky over the sea was black, and somewhere out there in the dark a hull half again the length of hull two, which was itself three times the length of the Hod, went up off the water with no sound at all and was gone. The post keeper and Ferra sat by the shuttered lamp and did not talk. Ferra wrote out and the hour.

She had asked Ward, before, what hull three was for, and Ward had said reach. Hull two carried. Hull two was a barn with a keel, and she flew low and slow and full, and could lift thirty-one casks of powder to a ford and come home. Hull three was long and narrow and her racks were built for staying up, not for lifting, and she carried little. Fletcher had drawn her lines for a long night and a longer way.

She came back a little before first light. The post keeper saw the signal first, three short and one long from a lamp far out over the marsh, and uncovered his own to answer, and a quarter hour later the lamp went out over the water and there was the faintest sound, like a sheet being shaken out a long way off, and then nothing. Ward walked up to the post an hour after that with his boots wet to the knee and his face grey with tiredness and something under the grey that Ferra had not seen on it for a long time.

"How far?" she asked.

"We saw the Emberfell headland light," Ward said. "And came back. On one night's racks. With something over."

She wrote it. The headland light was six days down the coast at a coaster's pace. She wrote six days in the margin beside the hour, and looked at it, and did not write anything else.

On the blank page: Hull three, first flight. W. and Fletcher. The headland and back in a night.

The Grayrock line reached the hill in the fifth week.

The slip came in on the relay from the hill post in Bryda's clerk's hand, three words: Rail at hill. B. Ferra read it at the long-room table and set it down and sat for a while with her hand on it.

Six summers, it had been, the day he first drew the line. She had written six summers in the works book in her own hand and underlined it, because it was a sum that had come out of the old pace of iron, and the old pace of iron had been all there was. Then the rail works, and steel instead of iron, and thirty-foot lengths with a head and a web and a foot, and a harvest bounty to bring the men back every summer, and the figure had gone to two summers and some, and then to the autumn after next.

The rail was at the hill in the spring, half a season early, because the gangs had laid through the winter on the Grayrock end where the ground did not freeze, and because Bryda had put her own men on the last mile to see it done.

Two summers, near enough. Not six.

She crossed out the autumn after next in the works book and wrote the date beside it, and then turned to the blank page and wrote The Grayrock line. At the hill. and nothing else, because there was nothing to add to it.

The Crown's bill came with Fenner's clerk, the week after the spring raising.

Fenner had not come himself this time. He had written to say he would attend the autumn raising in person and sent his senior clerk to the spring one with the weigher, and the clerk was a quiet young man called Lusk who had done everything by the book and had not looked at the spoil heaps at all. When the stone was weighed and entered he brought out the Crown's bill of purchase, sealed, and laid it on the table in the Grayrock counting house, and Ferra opened it there in front of him and Bryda.

It was a purchase of stone under the covenant. So many pounds from the spring raising, to be carried north on the Crown's carts at the Crown's cost. That was in order. It had been in order every season since the covenant was sealed.

The price was not in order.

The covenant's price stood at the top of the gravity page in Ferra's book, where she had written it the first year. Nine Gold Rand the pound. The Crown had paid nine Gold Rand the pound for every pound it had ever bought out of Grayrock, because that was the price the Crown had set when it sealed the covenant, and a sovereign does not change a price it set.

The bill said eleven Gold, four Silver.

Ferra read it twice. Then she looked up at Lusk, who was sanding the ink on his own copy and did not appear to have noticed anything.

"This is the Crown's figure?" she said.

"It's the treasury's, ma'am. It came down sealed with the order." He looked at it. "Is it wrong?"

"It's higher than the covenant."

He blinked at her. "I'm sure the treasury knows the covenant, ma'am."

"I'm sure it does," Ferra said.

She did not argue with it. There was nothing to argue. A seller does not write to a buyer to complain that she has been paid too much. She countersigned the bill, and took her copy, and rode home, and entered the purchase in the book on the gravity page under the covenant's price, pound for pound, at the figure the Crown had paid.

Then she took the pen and drew a ring round the figure. A small neat ring, in the same ink.

She looked at it for a long time.

A sovereign does not overpay. Not for bread, not for horses, not for stone. A sovereign that overpays is either being robbed by its own treasury or paying for something that is not written in the bill. The Crown's treasury was not a careless one. She had dealt with it for years. It sent back a bill for a single Copper if the Copper was wrong.

She did not ask Saban. Saban was in the capital. She did not ask Vantic, who would be down for the quarter day and would see the line when she copied the book, and would look at it, and would ask nothing, because Vantic never asked about the gravity page. She did not ask Armand in the chair or Bram at the works or Ward at the gate. She did not write it on the blank page, because she did not know yet whether it was a thing that had been decided while he was away, or a thing that had been decided somewhere else entirely, by somebody who was not in Kaldo and never had been.

She left the ring where it was, and closed the book.

Old Tam Brewer would not move.

The island was emptying. That was on the blank page too, in several lines: the second draw of households to the first quarter, the third, the day the first quarter's tower was filled and the taps turned on in the new streets and a crowd of children stood round the troughs in the new square and drank because they had been told they could. By late spring two households in five on the island had gone to the flat, and the rooms behind the market that had been three families deep were one family deep, and in some of them nobody at all.

Tam Brewer was on the list for the third draw. He was a cooper, past seventy, with a house on the lane behind the water steps that had been his father's and his grandfather's, and his grandfather was in the churchyard by the east wall, eleven paces from the gate.

"I'll not sleep over a sewer," Tam said, at his own door, with Ferra on the step and Ilsa behind her with the census book. "I've heard what's under those houses. Pipes. Pipes going down into the dark with every man's slops in them. I'll not."

"It's cleaner than what's under this one," Ilsa said.

"This one's got my grandfather under the hill at the end of it," Tam said, "and that's all it's got under it that I care about. When I go, I'll go next to him. I'll not go from out there."

Ferra looked at him for a moment. He was a small man, bent, with a cooper's hands, and he was only certain, and not angry about it.

"Take him off the draw," she said to Ilsa.

"Ma'am?"

"Take him off. Write stays." She nodded to Tam. "Nobody's going to make you, Master Brewer. The house is yours."

He looked at her with some suspicion, as though waiting to be told the price, and when it did not come he nodded once and shut his door.

On the blank page, that night: T. Brewer, the water steps. Stays.

The carriage came in by the Western Gate on a warm afternoon at the end of the spring, with Rylan on the box and the horses blown, and half the town knew it was coming because the relay had told the gate and the gate had told everybody.

Ferra did not go to the gate. She had gone to the gate the morning he left, in daylight, with the carriage waiting and people about, and said what she had said about the book, and she did not think she needed to go twice. She was in the long room with the day book open when they came up the stair: Emeric first, then Saban with his arm out of the sling at last and his wrist wrapped, then Rylan. Hobb was at the side table with the post. Armand was in his chair, which he left at once. Ward came in from the yard a moment after, and Bram a moment after that with iron dust on his forearms, and Imani last, from the ward, drying her hands on a cloth.

He looked tired. He looked, Ferra thought, like a man who had been wearing a coat that did not fit him for six weeks and had only just taken it off. His hair was cut in a way she did not like.

"Well," he said, to all of them.

"Sit down," Ferra said.

He sat, at his place at the table's head, which Armand had left for him. She turned the day book round and pushed it across to him open at the last page.

He read it.

He read it slowly, all nineteen lines, and she watched him. She had known she would watch him. It was the reason she had kept the page. The lamps. The gas, and Kit Hallam, and Imani's count of a hundred and forty-one. The third season on the drawing's date. Hull three to the Emberfell light and back. The Grayrock line at the hill. The draws, and the tower filled, and the children at the troughs. Tam Brewer, stays. And others, smaller: a new boiler in the paper mill, a quarrel at the rope walk that Ward had settled, a price for lime.

When he got to the bottom he turned back to the top and read it again.

He was glad. She could see him be glad. It came up into his face the way it did when a thing he had drawn stood up and worked, the look that Bram had and Fletcher had and she had never had, because nothing she made ever stood up; it only added up. He was glad of every line. And underneath it, behind it, the way you can sometimes see the second hand on a page through the paper, there was something else, and she knew what it was because she had seen it once before. The summer Bram opened his own room. He had come home from Emberfell to a Kaldo that had buried Rudd and written a new rule and opened a door without him, and he had been glad, and he had not entirely wanted to be.

He did not entirely want to be now. It was there for about the time it takes to read nineteen lines twice. Then he put it away, the way she had seen him put away the anger, and the grief, and the tiredness, and every other thing that did not help. She did not think he knew she had seen it.

"Tam Brewer," he said.

"Stays."

"Good." He looked at the line under it for a while. "Good," he said again.

Ferra looked at him across the long-room table, with the whole of the table round him, and Hobb at the side table, and the late sun coming in over the lake. She had been deciding a thing about him for a long time. Longer than she would have admitted to anyone, including the book. She had decided it and then put it away, and taken it out and looked at it, and decided it again, the way you check a sum you already know is right because the number is large and you would rather be sure. She had decided it at the gate, the morning he left. She had decided it every evening she wrote on this page and he was not here to read it.

She decided it now, one more time, watching him read a list of things that had been done without him and be glad of them and not want to be, and put the not wanting away where nobody could see it but her.

This time she did not put it back.

She took the day book back across the table, and turned to the day's page, and found his name in the column where she had written, the morning the carriage left, gone north, on the summons. Beside it, in the same small square hand, she wrote returned, and the date, and blotted it.
