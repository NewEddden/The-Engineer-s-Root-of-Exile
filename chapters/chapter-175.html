Chapter 175 - In Front of Someone

Tessaly Rook had come north with the column to raise a bank, and she had raised it, and she had not expected to be asked to do anything else.

There were three of them: herself and Paskin and a young earth mage from the south coast called Dorren, who had asked to come. The prince had asked for volunteers at the handcart on the flat the week before the column marched, and said plainly what for: a day's work at the end of a five days' walk, a bank the height of a man's waist and a quarter mile long, raised on ground the prince's surveyors would mark, and then back to the carts with the physician's people, well behind the line and well out of whatever the duke meant to do. Nobody would ask them to fight. Nobody would ask them to go into the dead ground. He had said the words dead ground at the handcart, in front of all of them, and a good many faces had changed, and Tessaly's had been one of them.

She had raised the bank on the afternoon before the fight, on the south edge of the stream ground, where the Tarrant nephew had put his pegs, with Paskin and Dorren taking a third each. It had taken them the afternoon. It had been nothing, beside the wall. Then they had gone back to the carts with the red cloths, and in the morning she had sat on the tail of Imani's second cart a quarter mile behind the line and felt the ground in front of her die.

She would not forget that. She had broken a stone on purpose once, as a girl at the tower, the one time every student was made to, to learn what it did, and had felt the little patch round her go dead for an hour and been sick. This was that, a thousand times. A long band of nothing across the whole stream ground, spreading, cold, the medium drawn out of it like breath out of a drowned man, and she had sat on the cart with her hands in her lap and felt the edge of it come to within fifty paces of the carts and stop. She had felt the prince feel it. She did not know how she knew that. She had seen him on his grey horse at the edge, and seen him put his hand to his head, and sit still.

Then the noise had started, and gone on for a long time, and she had not watched. She had helped the physician's people with the carts.

The column went north the next morning, after the duke.

It went slowly. The duke's host had broken in the fight and gone back north across the stream ground and round the town's east side and up the road toward his own border in no order at all, leaving horses and carts and tents and dead men behind it, and his foot had gone first and fastest, and his rearguard had come last. The prince's line went after it at a walk. Not to catch it, Paskin said; to be seen going after it. To be on the road north of Wayford, on the duke's side of the line, the day after, so that everybody in the three baronies would know who had held the field.

The mages went with the carts. There was nothing else to do with them.

It was past noon when they came to the tree.

It was an oak, a big one, standing alone by the road where the road came over a low rise two miles north of Wayford's last fields, with a stone trough under it for horses. The duke's rearguard had stopped there in the night. Tessaly could see where: the ashes of their fires in the grass by the trough, and the ruts where their carts had stood.

There were five men hanging from the oak.

She did not understand at first what she was seeing. The column had stopped ahead of the carts, and she stood up on the tail of the cart to see why, and saw the tree over the heads of the line, and the shapes under its lowest bough, hanging still in the windless afternoon, and thought for a moment that they were coats. Blue coats. Somebody had hung five blue coats on a tree to dry.

Then the line opened to let the prince's horse through, and she saw.

They had been hanged with cart rope, from the lowest bough, at a height where their feet were a yard off the ground. Their hands were tied behind them. They were in the blue coats of the levy. There was a board nailed to the trunk of the oak below them, a piece of a cart's tailboard, and somebody had written on it with a coal, in big uneven letters: OUTLAWS. COMMON MEN WHO SLEW KNIGHTS.

She knew, the way everybody on the column knew within the hour, who they were. A picket of eight from the east end of the line, put out by the woods to watch the flank, who had been overrun at the end of the fight when a band of the duke's men-at-arms came round behind the woods looking for a way off the field. Three had been killed there. Five had been taken, and nobody had known where. They had been carried north in the rearguard's carts, and at the oak in the night the rearguard had stopped, and done this, and gone on.

Doctrine on a rope, Paskin said afterward. He said it very quietly, as if he were translating it from another language. A commoner who kills a knight is not a soldier. He is a man who has killed his betters, and there is only one thing the law has ever done with those.

The prince got down off his horse.

He walked to the tree. Nobody went with him. He walked up the low rise in the grass to the foot of the oak and stood under the five men and looked up at them, one after another, the way Tessaly had seen him look at the wall's courses on the flat, as if he were checking each one.

He stopped at the second.

Tessaly did not know the boy. She would learn his name that evening: Piers, son of Sela, of the island, nineteen. She would learn that he had been in the fire company's second watch, and had carried buckets up from the water steps the night of the tannery lane, and had signed the levy book in a joined hand on its eleventh day. She did not know any of that yet. She saw only that the prince had stopped at the second man and was standing very still and looking up at his face.

Then she felt it.

It began as a pressure. She had no other word. A pressure in the air on the skin of her face and hands, the way the air presses before a storm, only it was not the air; the air was perfectly still. It was the medium. It was the medium all round them, on the rise and on the road and in the grass and in the oak's branches, the ordinary thin living medium of an ordinary afternoon that every mage in the world swims in all day and never notices any more than a fish notices water, and it was moving.

It moved toward him. All of it. It came in off the road and the fields and the rise and went toward the man standing under the tree the way water goes toward a drain, not fast, but all of it, from everywhere, a slow deep pull that she felt in her hands and her teeth and the soles of her feet.

Then it turned.

It did not stop moving toward him. It went on coming in. But round him, round the oak, it began to go round, the way water goes round above something very large turning under it, slow and heavy and wide. The dust on the road lifted. She saw it lift. A fine grey haze of it, rising off the dry ruts in a sheet a foot high, and hanging there, and turning. The ashes of the rearguard's fires lifted out of the grass and turned. The blue coats of the five men on the bough stirred, all together, the tails of them lifting and swinging, though there was no wind, none, not a breath; the leaves of the oak above them were perfectly still. The hanged men's hair moved.

Behind her the horses in the cart traces began to scream.

Not all at once. One, and then the next, and then every horse on the column, a high terrible sound, rearing in the shafts, the carters hauling on the reins and shouting, the line breaking up ahead as the officers' horses went round and round in the road.

And Tessaly stood on the tail of the physician's cart with her hands out in front of her, not knowing she had put them out, and felt it with every part of her that the tower had spent thirty years teaching to feel, and knew what it was not.

It was not a working.

There was no shape in it. There was no cast. A working had a shape, always; every mage in every tower learned that in the first month: you shaped the medium to your intent, fire or water or earth or air, you gave it a form and a direction and an end, and it went where you sent it and did what you shaped it for and stopped. Every working she had ever felt in her life, from her own first flame as a child to the high mages' great sieges, had had a shape. You could feel the shape from across a field. You could name it.

This had none. It was not going anywhere. It was not for anything. There was no intent in it at all, except that there was, and the intent was not a working's. It was the medium doing what a man was feeling. As if the medium were an animal, and it had heard him.

She had no word for it. Nobody in any tower had a word for it. She thought, standing on the cart with her hands out and the horses screaming, that she had been told all her life that it could not happen, and that she was feeling it happen, and that there was nobody within a hundred miles but her who knew enough to know that.

Then she saw the captain.

The old knight, Ward, the prince's captain, had got down off his horse at the head of the line and was walking up the rise.

He walked straight into it. She saw the dust hanging in the air part round his legs as he went through it. She saw his hair lift off his forehead. He did not hurry, and he did not stop, and he did not put his hands up in front of his face as she had; he walked up the rise through the turning medium with his arms at his sides the way a man walks into a cold sea, steadily, not looking at anything but the man under the tree.

He came up behind the prince and put his hand on his shoulder.

He did not say anything that Tessaly could hear. Perhaps he did not say anything at all. He stood with his hand on the prince's shoulder, behind him, under the five men on the bough, and waited.

It stopped.

Not all at once. It slowed first, the great slow turning, the way a wheel slows when the water is taken off it. The dust began to settle. The coats on the bough swung, and swung less, and hung still. The pull toward him eased, and went, and the medium lay back down on the rise and the road and the fields as if it had never moved, thin and ordinary and quiet. The horses stopped screaming, one by one, and stood trembling in the traces with the carters at their heads.

The prince stood where he was. Ward's hand stayed on his shoulder.

He put his own hand up to his face. When he took it away there was blood on it, and Tessaly saw a dark line of it running from his nose over his lip, bright in the afternoon sun, and saw him look at his fingers as if he did not know whose they were.

Ward took his hand off his shoulder and said something to him, low. The prince nodded. Then Ward turned round and called down the rise, in the voice he used on the flat, for knives, and a cart, and six men, and the physician.

They cut them down and laid them in the cart on straw, the five of them, and covered them with a cloth. The column camped at the oak that night; it was not going to go on.

Tessaly was on the rise when they cut the second one down, the boy, because she had not been able to make herself go back to the carts. She stood at the edge of it and watched two of the levy hold him while a third cut the rope, and lower him, and as they lowered him something fell out of the inside of his coat onto the grass at her feet.

It was a folded paper. Small. Good paper, she saw as she picked it up; white and close and heavy, Kaldo paper, the kind they printed the notices on at the flat.

She did not open it then. She put it in her sleeve without thinking, the way one picks up a thing a stranger has dropped, meaning to give it back.

That night she sat by the mages' fire at the edge of the camp with her book on her knee.

Paskin had gone to sleep. Dorren was sitting across the fire from her, awake, with his arms round his knees, not talking. The camp was very quiet. There was a guard on the prince's tent, and a lamp in it, and the captain sitting outside it on a saddle with his sword across his knees.

She opened the book.

It was her working book. Every mage had one; the tower gave it to you in your first week and you kept it all your life, and she was on her fourth. It was where you wrote what you had felt. Every working you did, and every working you saw done, and anything you felt in the medium that you did not understand. Where, and when, and how long, and how far, and what it felt like, in the plainest words you could find. The tower had taught her that before it taught her anything else. The masters said it was the only thing that made a mage different from a hedge-witch: that a hedge-witch felt things and a mage wrote them down.

She had written in it every day for thirty years. She had written the wall at Carrow Ford in it, and the dead patch from the stone she broke as a girl, and every high mage's working she had ever stood near. She had written the first day on the flat in it, and the printer's head on the island that had turned Paskin grey, and the knights carrying the oak beam.

She wrote the date at the top of a clean page, and the place: the Wayford road, two miles north of the last fields, at the oak.

Then she sat for a long time with the pen.

She was not his enemy. She thought that clearly, sitting by the fire, as if somebody had asked her. She had come south for three Gold a month and a founding stone, and stayed because the wall was the best work she had ever done, and she had watched him on the flat for a year, and she knew he had stood over Bastian at the cutting and said it's good work and walked on, and she was not his enemy. She did not think she ever would be.

She wrote it anyway.

She wrote it the way the tower had taught her: plainly, in order, with nothing left out and nothing added. What she had felt first, and then after. The pull. The turning. That it had no shape and no cast and was not for anything. How far it reached: she had felt it at the carts, which she paced out in the morning at a hundred and forty paces from the oak, and she thought it had reached further. How long: she had not counted, but she had said the morning office in her head once through, without meaning to, from the pull to the stopping, and that was about the time of forty breaths. What it did: the dust, the coats, the hair, the horses. That the leaves of the oak had not moved. That it had stopped when the captain put his hand on him, and not before. That he had bled from the nose.

Then she wrote the last line. She wrote it slowly. She read it back when she had written it, and it was true, and there was nothing in it that she could take out.

It was not a working. The medium moved because he was angry, and for no other cause I could find.

She held the book open on her knee to let it dry.

She remembered the paper in her sleeve while she was waiting.

She took it out and unfolded it by the firelight. It was a prayer, printed small on the good paper, a single page, folded twice to go in a coat. A Prayer for the Men of Kaldo in the Field, it said at the top, and under that, in smaller letters, by leave of the parish. She read it. It was short and plain and she liked it. It asked to be kept in the field and brought home, and to be forgiven what had to be done there, and for their people to be kept while they were gone.

She read the fourth line twice.

Keep us, lord, in the hour of the fight.

She looked at it for a long time. She had grown up in a tower that printed nothing but had copied the offices by hand for three hundred years, and she had copied them herself as a student, a thousand pages, and been beaten for a wrong capital more than once. She knew how the word was always written in a prayer. She knew what it meant when it was not.

It did not ask God. It asked him.

It was a mistake. She was nearly sure it was a mistake. It was one letter on one line of a cheap print, and anybody might have set it wrong. A boy had carried it into the field in his coat against his chest, and it had not kept him.

She folded it again, carefully, along its creases, and put it between the pages of her book, at the page she had just written, and looked at the page.

The ink had dried. The fire had burned down. The last line lay there in her own hand, in the plain words the tower had taught her to use for things she could not name, and she found, sitting by the fire with the book on her knee and the prince's lamp still lit in the tent across the camp, that there was nothing she could do now that would make her not have written it.
