Chapter 175 - Dense

Brother Orme asked to see the still on the ninth day, because everybody on the road had told him about the spirit, and Emeric took him.

It was the obvious thing to show him. The still was the oldest of Kaldo's works and the least secret, a long stone shed on the river bank below the brickfield with its copper and its worm and its casks, and every carrier who had ever drunk a cup of Kaldo spirit at a posting house knew it ran on fire stones, because the casks said so, stencilled on the head: STONE-FIRED. It was half the reason the spirit sold. Ferra had put the words on the casks herself in the second year.

The still crew were at work when they came in, eight of them, in their leather aprons, at the fire end and the cask end, with the heat coming off the copper in a wall. Brother Orme stood inside the door with his staff and looked at it all with his mild interest: the long copper belly of the still on its stone bed, the worm going away into its tub of river water, the run of clear spirit into the receiver, the smell. Then he walked down the shed, slowly, and shook hands with every man in it.

Emeric had watched him do it at the church door and in the students' room and in the gate tower, and it was always the same, and he had come to dread it. A priest's handshake. Friendly, unhurried, one hand round the other man's hand and the second hand laid over the back of it, a blessing almost, the way a good priest shook hands with a parishioner at the end of the office. And while he held it, the quiet humming note in him went down his arms into his hands and into the other man's, a little way, and listened.

He shook the bookkeeper's hand first, at the desk by the door. A young man with ink on his fingers who had never carried anything heavier than a ledger. Brother Orme held it a breath, and smiled, and let it go.

Then the cask men. Then the fire end.

Mott was at the fire end. He was the big slow quiet man from the dock quarter who had come to the still in the second summer, when the still was new, and had worked the fire end ever since, where the recharged stones went into the firebox in their iron cradles and came out again when they were spent; and for five years he had carried them in his bare hands, because the leather gloves made him clumsy and a dropped stone cracked. He wiped his palm on his apron and held it out, a little shy of a priest, and Brother Orme took it in both of his.

He held it.

Emeric felt the note go into Mott's hand, and stop, and stay. He saw the Visitor's face. It did not change. It did not do anything at all, which was how Emeric knew. The man's face had been mild and pleasant and slightly amused at everything for nine days, and now it was only still, the way Armand's went still over a reading he did not like.

"How long have you worked the fire, son?" said Brother Orme.

"Five years, Father. Since the second summer."

"Carrying the stones?"

"Somebody's got to." Mott looked at his own hand, held. "They're not hot, Father. Not to hold. Only in the box."

"No," said Brother Orme. "I don't suppose they are." He let go. He patted the back of the big hand once, kindly, as he let go of it, and went on to the next man, and the next; and he held the hand of every man at the fire end a little longer than he had held the cask men's, and the bookkeeper's not at all.

At the door, going out, he stopped and looked back down the long hot shed at the copper and the men round it, for a little while. Then he said that it was very fine spirit and he could see why they were proud of it, and went out into the sunlight.



On the tenth day it was the market.

Emeric did not go with him. He did not need to. He had a thread on him from the keep, thin, in the back of his mind, as he had all week; and Saban was in the market, at his own stall, selling nothing in particular and watching the Visitor over a tray of buttons; and Saban came up the hill at noon and told him.

"He takes hands," said Saban. "At the cross. Anybody who wants a blessing, he gives them one and holds their hand while he does it. Half the town's been." He sat down. "Old women. Children. The fishwives. Sela, twice. He blesses them all and he's very nice to them and he holds every one of their hands for the time it takes to say the words." Saban looked at his own hands. "And then sometimes he holds one a little longer."

"Whose?"

"The drying floor girls. Three of them. Every one of them has turned the stones on the drying racks for three years or more." Saban folded his hands. "A cooper from the cask yard who hands the stones in at the still. The stone bank's porter. One of Vesk's boys who carries for him." He paused. "Not Sela. Not the fishwives. Not the baker, and the baker's been to him twice for his chest." He looked at Emeric. "I sell for a living. I know what a man looks like when he's found the thing he came in for and doesn't want the stallholder to know it. He looks at everything else on the stall, one thing after another, very carefully. That's what he's doing."



The next day was Sunday, and the Visitor said the office in the parish church, as he had said he would at the long table on the first morning, and the town came to hear him.

Emeric went. He did not often. Doran knew why and had never said a word about it. Today it was politic. He sat in the lord's pew at the front with Ward beside him and Ferra and Imani behind, and listened to a sermon about a steward who kept honest accounts for an absent master, which was well made and short, and watched the Visitor's face while he gave it, and could not tell whether the steward had been chosen for him.

Afterward Brother Orme stood at the church door beside Doran in the April sun and shook hands with the parish as it went out. Every hand. Doran introduced every name.

Rylan went out with Nessa and the children. He shook the Visitor's hand, as a knight did a priest's, briskly. Brother Orme held it.

"You're a knight," he said.

"Yes, Father."

Brother Orme nodded. He looked at Rylan's wrist, where the brown wool cuff came down from the sleeve, and then at the back of his hand, where the tracery showed, faint and silver, over the knuckles, the knight's mark that the rite left, that told anybody who could read it how much the rite had made of him: so many lines, so fine. Whatever rank it showed, it was the rank he would die with. Nobody went past the mark the rite left. Everybody knew that.

The Visitor held his hand a breath longer. Then he let go, and said the blessing over the children, and Rylan went out.

Declan. Osric, with his red flowers on his cuffs. The Visitor held both of their hands, and looked at both of their tracery, and said nothing at all to either of them except the blessing; and Osric, going out, looked back over his shoulder at the door with his face screwed up, as if somebody had trodden on his foot and he could not see who.

Then Ward.

Ward's hand went into the Visitor's, and Ward's face did what it always did, which was nothing; and Brother Orme held it and looked at the tracery on Ward's knuckles, which was a knight's tracery like any other, the mark of what the rite had made of him, once, and left there. And he looked up at Ward's face, and down again at the lines, and held his hand for a long breath, as a man holds a coin he has been given in change that is the right size and the wrong weight.

"The Light keep you," said Brother Orme.

"Father," said Ward, and went out.

Then it was Emeric's turn.

He had known it would come since the morning in the long room. He had thought about not coming to the office at all, and had come, because a prince who stayed away the one Sunday a Visitor said it would be noticed by every soul in the parish. He had thought about it all through the sermon. There was nothing to be done about it but the one thing, so he did that. He held his note down. All of it, as far down as it would go, the way he had at the receiver's table, the way he had in the gate yard on the first day: the whole of what he was, pulled in and shut, quiet, a closed hand round a stone, until what was left on the surface was a thin plain hum like any man's who had never had a lamp stone in his life.

The Visitor's hand closed round his. The other hand came over the back of it.

The note came in. Emeric felt it. Light, polite, listening, down into his hand and up his wrist toward the cuff, and the band under the wool, and the bead in the band, which Emeric had put to sleep with all the others on the night before the man came, as he had put the ship's to sleep; and passed over it, as it had passed over the ship, and found nothing it knew what to do with, and came back to the hand.

Thin, Emeric thought, as hard as he could. Thin and ordinary and a prince.

The Visitor held his hand exactly as long as he had held the bookkeeper's.

"Highness," he said, and smiled, and let go; and turned to Ferra behind him, and took her hand, and held it, and Emeric walked out into the sun with the back of his neck wet and did not look round.



Imani had a visit on the twelfth of April.

She told him about it that evening, in the counting room, standing by the window with her arms folded, while Ferra wrote.

"He asked for my register," she said. "The ward's. Who came in, with what, how long they stayed. He said the chamber collects them, for the parishes that keep them, and most don't, and he'd heard I did." She looked out at the river. "I gave it to him."

"All of it?"

"It's a register. It's all of it or it's not a register." She did not turn round. "He sat in my office for four hours with it and a pencil. He read every page from the plague on. He asked me one question, at the end."

Emeric waited.

"He asked me why the men from the still lost fewer days in a winter than the men from the dock," said Imani. "When they work in the same heat and drink from the same well and half of them were dock men before they were still men." She turned round then. "I told him I didn't know. Which is true. I've never written down why. I've only written down that they do. Since the third year." She unfolded her arms. "I didn't lie to him. He didn't need me to say anything. He'd already read it. He only wanted to see whether I'd noticed."

"What did he say?"

"He said I keep a very good register." She went to the door. "And then he said that in his experience the people who keep the best registers are generally the last to read them."



He came to the keep on the morning of the fourteenth to take his leave.

He came alone, with his staff and his satchel, ready for the road, as he had arrived. Doran was not with him. Doran was at the church, Brother Orme said, writing a letter to a cousin in the chamber that Brother Orme had promised to carry, and was not to be hurried. They sat in the long room again with the shutters open and Maren's tea, and Ward by the window.

"I've had a very good fortnight," said Brother Orme. "Your town's been kind to me. Father Doran's a good man. I'll say so to the chamber." He drank his tea. "I'll say a good many things, Highness. Most of them about your drains and your school and your lamps and your very fine spirit. And your mages, twenty-one, as you said, every one of them where you said they'd be, doing what you said they did."

"Thank you."

"I'll say one other thing, and I'd rather you heard it from me first, at your own table." He set his cup down. "It's only a thing I felt. I don't know what it means. I'll write down what I felt and other men will decide what it means, which is how the chamber works, and I've never much liked it." He looked at his hands, folded on his staff. "I've read hands on the southern round for nineteen years. I can feel a stone across a room, and a mage across a street, and a knight's rank in his grip, as well as I can see the lines on him. It's a small gift. The chamber finds it useful."

He was quiet.

"I've never felt a town like yours," he said. "Your still men read like knights' squires. Men who've never been near a rite. Your drying floor girls. Your stone porter. The men who've carried your stones for years. They're dense, Highness, where common men aren't, and they're denser the longer they've carried. And your knights." He looked at Ward, by the window, and back. "Your knights read heavier than their lines. Every one. A knight's mark tells you what the rite made of him, and a knight's mark doesn't change, and a knight doesn't change after it either. Everybody knows that. It's the first thing we're taught." He paused. "Yours have."

Emeric did not say anything. He could not think of anything to say that would not be worse than saying nothing.

"I don't know what you've done here," said Brother Orme. "Perhaps nothing. Perhaps it's your water. Perhaps it's the stones, carried bare, day after day, which nobody does anywhere else, because stones are dear and men are careless and lords don't let them. Perhaps it's something I haven't thought of." He stood, and took up his satchel. "But whatever it is, it isn't one man in a cellar. It's a town. It's slow, and it's all of them, and it's been going on for years." He settled the strap across his chest. "I don't think it can be stopped. I'm not sure anybody ought to try. That isn't my decision either."

He bowed. The same exact bow.

"Good morning, Highness," he said. "Mend the cross in your undercroft."



He went out at the gate a little before noon, on foot, north, up the capital road, among the carts.

Emeric watched him go from the wall walk over the gate, with Ward. The small grey figure with the staff went up the road toward the first ridge without hurrying and without looking back, and past the railhead, where Fennick's crews were at the cutting and the dust was going up off the crest; and he stopped there and watched them blast, a while, as he had on the fourth day, and then went on, up over the ridge, and was gone.

"He'll write it," said Ward.

"He'll write exactly what he told me."

"That's worse." Ward watched the ridge. "A liar you can catch. He didn't lie once in a fortnight. Not to you, not to Doran, not to the eel men." He was quiet. "The chamber'll believe every word, because it's him."

Emeric looked down from the wall at the town.

They had hidden a ship. They had hidden seven suits in crates marked STOVE PARTS, and ten silver bands under wool, and a chair that flew, and a printer under a sack, and a forge behind a wall, and four mages off a hill. It had taken a week and cost a season and every one of them had worked, more or less. A man with a gift had walked through all of it for a fortnight and found nothing.

And had shaken hands with the town.

He could see the still from here, on the bank below the brickfield, with its smoke going up. The drying floor. The stone bank's roof. The church. The square where the cross was, and the market, and the people crossing it in the noon sun, the cooper and the porter and the girls from the drying floor, who were heavier than they had been five years ago and would be heavier again next year, and did not know it, and could not have been put in a crate. You could hide a ship. You could not hide what living beside her did to the people who built her.

Armand had told him so, in a way, every spring for four years, and had written it down every spring, and called it noise.

"What do we do?" said Ward.

"Nothing." Emeric turned from the parapet. "Open the shed. Take the suits out of the crates. Take the sack off the printer." He started down the stair. "And somebody go to the gate tower and tell Tess she can go home."

Ward went down behind him, and across the gate yard, and they went in at the keep door together out of the noon light.

The hall was empty. The keep stair went up from the far end of it, round and round, stone, worn hollow in the middle of every step by three hundred years of feet; and at the foot of it, where she had stopped a moment before on her wheels, having come across the yard ahead of them while they were on the wall, there was a hum Emeric knew.

Tess came up the stair on the air. The stones open, the lattice humming, the chair a hand's breadth off the steps and rising past them, round the first turn, not touching, with her book in her lap and her back very straight; like a leaf going up a chimney. She did not stop when she passed them. She did not look down.

"Thirty-one," she said, going by, to Emeric. "In three weeks. I counted." Round the turn. Her voice came back down the stair after her, getting smaller. "Ferra came twice, with sums, in case anybody thought she'd come for nothing. Imani came every day. Bram came once and stayed six hours and said about ten words. Ward didn't come at all."

"I was on the wall," said Ward, to the stair.

"I know," said the stair, from a long way up. "I could see you from the window."
