Chapter 284 - The Landing

They flew the morning in the Band, over cloud.

The weather had closed in behind them an hour out of Kaldo, the way it did in the last weeks of the year: a long grey deck of cloud coming in off the sea from the south and lying flat over the water a mile down, unbroken, from one edge of the world to the other. Ned took her up through it at eight. They came out of the top of it into the hard winter sun at a mile and a half, and went on up, and at a little after nine the Hod was at the Band's foot, four miles over the sea, with the cloud deck far below her like a field of snow and nothing above her but the dark blue and the cold.

And then Ned shut the scrubber.

That was the test. Imani's paper lay on the chart table under its weight with the nine names on it and the hours across the top; and every half hour, from nine, Emeric went round the Hod's house with it and his watch and took every pulse, and looked at every face, and wrote down what he found. Ned, at the helm under the cupola, nothing. Cade at the racks, nothing. Arthur, nothing, as Imani had said; he sat on the bench by the after window with his long legs stretched across the deck and his eyes half shut, and let Emeric count him, and said it was like sitting in a hot bath with your clothes on. Declan and Osric, who had flown the Band in their suits for years and had never sat in it with the scrubber shut, a little flushed, a little loud, nothing else. Emeric himself, nothing. He had not bled in the Band since the pod.

Sully bled at ten past ten.

He was seventeen, a port boy, on his fourth run. He had been quiet all morning, and pale, and at ten past ten he put the back of his hand to his nose at the racks and looked at it, and said, "Oh," in a small surprised voice; and Cade sat him down on the deck with his head forward and a cloth, and Emeric wrote it down, the time and how much and how he looked, and opened the scrubber's bypass a quarter for his corner of the house, the way Imani had shown him, and in twenty minutes it had stopped.

Mira bled at a quarter past eleven, at two hours and a quarter. She did not say anything. She took her handkerchief out of her sleeve and held it to her nose and went on reading the rack gauges to Cade with it there, in a slightly thick voice, until Emeric took it out of her hand to look at it and wrote it down.

Bren bled at ten to twelve, at nearly three. He was very cross about it. He had wanted to beat three.

"You've beaten a summer hand by two hours," said Emeric, writing.

"I wanted to beat three."

"Next year."

"Arthur doesn't bleed at all," said Bren, through his handkerchief, with deep resentment.

"Arthur," said Arthur, from the after bench, with his eyes shut, "has been up here since he was twenty. Arthur's been up here since you were mucking out Ned's horses."

"I never mucked out Ned's horses."

"You did once," said Ned, from the helm, without turning round. "Badly."

Emeric wrote it all down. He wrote everything down that morning: every pulse, every face, every handkerchief; and when he looked at Imani's paper at noon, at the columns going down it in his own hand, he saw what she had seen in her two years of numbers on the drawing office table, and it was the same. The ones who had been up longest did not bleed. The ones who had been up a year bled late. The ones who had been up a month bled early. A body could be brought up to it. Slowly.

He thought about the K Amara's figure, in Ferra's book, with a box round it.

Ned was supposed to turn at noon.

At noon Ned said, without turning round, "One more hour."

"Ferra said noon," said Emeric.

"Ferra's in Kaldo." Ned looked at the gauges. "Charge is up. She's been filling her racks in the Band all morning with the necks open, she's fuller than when we left. Water's fine. We're nobody's going hungry." He looked south-east, at the cloud deck going on and on under them to the edge of the world. "I've flown this ship for two years and never once been let see what she'll do. One more hour."

Emeric thought about it.

"One," he said.

"One," said Ned, satisfied; and at one o'clock exactly, because Ned did keep his word when he gave it, if not when Ferra gave it for him, he put her about.



They came home low.

That was the rule. Ships went east high, in the Band, with the westerlies behind them; and came west low, under the wind, because up high the westerlies blew in their teeth at fifty miles an hour and more in winter, and down by the water they hardly blew at all. So Ned put her about at one o'clock and took her down, out of the Band and down the long slope of the sky toward the cloud deck, and into it, into the grey, and through it, a long time, with the cupola's glass running with wet and nothing outside but white; and came out of the bottom of it at a little after two over the sea, low, a few hundred feet up, and set her course north-west by the compass for home.

It was a different world under the cloud.

Above it, the sun and the cold and the blue. Under it, grey: grey water, long and slow and heaving, going away on every side into a grey haze that was not quite fog and not quite rain; and the grey cloud lying on top of it all like a lid, so low that the Hod's cupola seemed to scrape it; and the light, which was afternoon light, already going. The sea was empty. It had been empty all day. Emeric stood under the cupola with his sense open, as he always kept it, and felt the water go by under them, cold and deep and thin, with nothing in it; and the Hod's racks behind him, full and bright; and his eight people round him in the house, nine notes he knew as well as his own. Ned at the helm. Bren asleep on the forward bench, with his handkerchief still in his fist. Mira at the gauges. Sully, better, eating an oatcake.

At twenty to four, the helm dragged.

Ned felt it first. Emeric saw his hands change on the wheel: a small adjustment, the kind a pilot makes without thinking for a gust; and then a bigger one; and then Ned said, "Hm," and looked at the compass, and at the trim gauges, and put the wheel over a spoke to bring her head back north-west; and the wheel did not want to go.

And then the whole ship leaned.

Not a roll. Not the wind. The whole Hod, both hulls and her fins and her house and everything in it, leaned over to the south-east and down, all at once, smoothly, as if the sea had tilted under her; and everything in the house that was not tied down slid. Sully's oatcake went across the deck. Bren came off the forward bench and woke on the floor. Mira caught the gauge panel's rail with one hand and the back of Cade's collar with the other.

"Ned," said Emeric.

"I've got it," said Ned, through his teeth, with both hands on the wheel and his shoulder in it, hauling her head back up and round to the north-west. "It's a wind. It's a down wind off something. I've got it."

It was not a wind.

Emeric knew it was not a wind before Ned had finished saying it. He felt it.

It was a pull. Not on the air round them; on the ship. On her stones. On every one of them, the lift stones in her racks and the trim stones in her fins and the canisters behind the iron in her hold; and on the people in her, a little, the way a bigger pull tugs at everything in its line; on Declan and Osric most, with their suits' stones on their backs, and on him. A long steady pull, from astern and below. From the south-east, low, along a line. He could feel the line. It came in out of the haze ahead, straight as a ruled stroke, at a slant, from somewhere down on the water a long way off, and went through the Hod from end to end, and pulled.

Gravity. He knew what it was the way he knew his own hand. A gravity stone pulled like that, along its axis, one way. An anchored one. A very big one.

And it was not getting stronger. That was the second thing he felt, a breath after the first. It had come on all at once, at full, and it was holding there, steady, a tow and not a blow; the way a horse leans into a collar and keeps leaning.

"Ned," he said again. "Stop."

"I've got her."

"You haven't. You're fighting it. Stop fighting it."

Ned did not stop. He had the wheel hard over and the trim stones in her fins working against the lean so hard that Emeric could feel them heating in their cradles, and the Hod's head was coming back round to the north-west, slowly, a point, two points; and the pull did not care. It leaned on her all the same, south-east and down, and the harder Ned fought it with her fins, the harder her fins pulled against her own frame. Emeric felt the starboard fin's root take the strain and begin, very faintly, to complain.

"Ned. Listen." He put his hand on Ned's shoulder, hard. "It's a stone. On the water, ahead. A big one, anchored. It's pulling her stones along a line. If you fight it with the fins, you'll tear a fin off her before you beat it, and you won't beat it. If you cut the lift to get out of its line, we go in the sea, a long way from home, at dusk, in December."

Ned's hands did not move on the wheel.

"Then what," he said.

"Ride it." Emeric kept his hand on Ned's shoulder. "Ease the fins off. Let her go where it's taking her. Keep just enough lift under her to keep her off the water and land her soft when it lets go. It's steady. It isn't getting stronger. It's a tow." He felt along the line with his sense, as far as he could reach, and further, until it hurt behind his eyes. "It's spending as it pulls. It's draining. It'll run dry."

"When?"

"I don't know." He did not. "Before it gets us anywhere we can't come back from. It hasn't got that much in it. It's big, but it's pulling a ship."

Ned looked at him, sideways, for one breath.

Then he eased the wheel. He did it the way he did everything with the Hod, slowly, by feel; he let the fins come off their hard lean a little at a time, so that she did not snap round, and as the fins eased the Hod's head swung, gently, away from the north-west, round to the south-east, into the line, and the lean came off her deck. She came upright. Sully's oatcake stopped sliding. Bren got up off the floor.

And she went.

South-east, low over the water, along the line, at a steady pace, faster than the stones in her racks would have taken her and not as fast as Emeric had feared; with Ned at the wheel keeping her trim and her lift and nothing else, his face like a man's who has been told to let go of a horse's reins and is doing it against every instinct he has; and the grey water going by under her faster, and the grey haze ahead, and the light going.

"Everybody sit down," said Emeric. "Hold on to something. Declan, Osric, suits on and sealed, now, in case. Cade, the hatches. Mira, call me the racks every minute."

They did it without a word. That was the yard; that was Ward's yard and Ned's ship, and two years of it. Declan and Osric were into their suits in the after space before he had finished the sentence, sealing each other's collars by feel. Cade went down the house dogging the hatches. Mira stood at the gauges with her handkerchief tucked in her sleeve and called the racks: full, full, full, a hair down, full. Arthur stood by Emeric at the cupola with his hand on the rail and his face perfectly calm and said nothing at all, and Emeric was glad of him.

"What is it," said Arthur, very quietly, after a while.

"I don't know," said Emeric. "A stone. A worked one. Nothing raw pulls like that."

"Out here."

"Out here."

Arthur thought about that, looking at the haze.

"Somebody put it there," he said.

"Somebody put it there."



They saw the surf first.

A long white line in the grey ahead, low on the water, coming out of the haze; a line of broken water, breaking on something. A reef. And beyond it, through the murk, flat and dark and low, so low it hardly rose off the sea at all: land.

Nobody said anything. There was no land out here. There was no land on any chart in Kaldo south-east of the bar but the drowned land in old Wicket's story at the inn, there one tide and gone the next.

The fog was in over it. It came off the land as they came in, low and white and thick, rolling out over the reef to meet them; and the light was nearly gone, the last grey of a December afternoon under a lid of cloud; and the Hod went into the fog at a few hundred feet, and the world went white.

Emeric could still feel. That was the one thing. He stood under the cupola with the white pressed against the glass and his sense pushed out through the house's iron as wide as it would go, with the pull behind his eyes at every reach, and drew the place for Ned in his head as they went, the way he drew a cave or a passage: the reef under them, a long ragged ridge of rock with the surf going over it, and the water behind it, a lagoon, shallow and still, and something in the lagoon that he could not make out, a round dense shape in the water, very dense, that the line came out of; and past the lagoon, the land. Dunes. Low ridges of sand, one behind another. And behind them, flat, soft, wet: marsh. Reed and water and mud, a great deal of it, going away inland further than he could feel.

"Ned," he said. "There's sand ahead. Dunes. Then marsh behind them. Soft. Flat." He put his hand on Ned's arm. "That's where it'll put us down, if it's going to. Over the dunes. Not on the reef. Keep her lift up over the reef. She'll scrape. Let her scrape."

"Over the reef," said Ned.

"Over the reef. Then she's over sand, then marsh."

"Blind."

"I can see it," said Emeric. "I'll call it. Just hold her level."

Ned held her level.

The reef came up under them in the fog. Emeric felt it come, the great ragged back of rock and the surf pouring over it; and the Hod went over it lower than he liked, much lower, so low that the port hull's keel touched. Not hard. A long grinding scrape that came up through the whole ship and into everybody's teeth, and a jolt, and the starboard fin, the one that had been complaining, caught something on the reef's crest and bent, with a sound like a wet bough breaking; and then the reef was behind them and they were over the lagoon, still and flat and black, and the round dense thing in the water went by under them on the left, close, so close that Emeric felt it like heat on that side of his face, huge, and pulling, and pulling.

And then less.

He felt it go. Not all at once. The way a lamp goes down when its oil runs out: a little less, and a little less, and a little less again; and the Hod, as the pull came off her, began to slow, and to settle, and to drift.

"It's letting go," he said. "Ned. It's letting go. Bring her lift up. Gently."

Ned brought her lift up, gently. The Hod rose a little off the lagoon's water, slowing; and the line ran out of her the way water runs out of a sieve, and was gone; and she drifted on, in the fog, under her own stones, on her own way, over the inner shore of the lagoon, over a beach, over the first dune, the second, the third, low and slow, with Ned feeling for her trim and Emeric calling the ground under her in a low steady voice like a man calling soundings; and over the last dune, there, flat, the marsh. Reeds. Black water between them. Mud.

"Down," said Emeric. "Now. There's a firm patch, right under us. Sand under the mud. Down."

The Hod came down on the marsh in the fog at ten past four on the twenty-second of December, as softly as Ned had ever put her down anywhere, on her two hulls, with a long sighing squelch, and settled into the mud to her waterline, and was still.

Nobody moved for a long moment.

Then Ned took his hands off the wheel, and looked at them, and put them in his lap.

"Well," he said. "I turned when I said."



Emeric went out on the dunes alone first, before anybody, with his sense open.

He went up the last dune from the marsh in the near dark, through coarse grey grass that hissed against his boots, and stood on its crest in the fog and let his sense go out as far as it would, in every direction, slowly, the way Armand had taught him, anchored on the rope burn across his palm from the mast's stay, coming down that afternoon; and listened.

Nobody.

No people. No notes. Nothing for as far as he could reach, which in that fog over that thin ground was a long way, a mile, more: no man, no woman, no child, no house, no fire. Birds, on the shore, a great many of them, small bright sparks crowded together on the sand and on the rocks, asleep. Something larger along the beach to the south, a dozen of something, heavy and slow, lying in a heap on the sand by the water and breathing; seals, he thought, from the size and the smell that came up the dune on the wind. Small things in the grass round his feet. The marsh full of little lives, frogs and fish and creeping things. And out on the lagoon, behind the dunes, low on the water, the great round dense shape that had pulled them in, very quiet now; nearly empty; a stone, he was sure of it now, a gravity stone, bigger than any he had ever felt, bigger than the Grayrock crater's biggest, lying in the water like a sleeping animal with its strength spent.

He did not go nearer it. It was dark. It was low tide or high tide, he did not know which. It had pulled a ship miles across the sea and over a reef and it was spent, he could feel that it was spent, the way you can feel that a man is asleep; and he did not know what it would do if somebody went up to it in the dark and touched it, and he did not intend to find out tonight. He had learned that on a hill in November.

He went back down to the Hod.

They had the hatches open and the lamps lit, the battery lamps, because nobody wanted a stone lamp tonight; and they were standing in the mud round the Hod's hulls in a circle, the eight of them, in the white, looking at her. She was all right. Mostly. The port hull's keel had a long bright scar along its iron where the reef had scraped it, and a dent the length of a man; the starboard fin was bent at its root, a quarter of the way round, like a bird's wing broken; and the mast at her stern, the tall thin mast that carried the wireless's aerial, had snapped clean off at its foot when she came down on the marsh and lay across the reeds behind her with its wires trailing in the black water.

"The set," said Cade, from the house's door. He was holding up a valve, one of Ivo's, its glass cracked across. "Three of them. The jolt on the reef. And the aerial's in the bog." He looked at Emeric. "We've no spares. They were in the locker that came open."

"Can you mend it?"

"Not without valves."

Emeric looked at the mast in the reeds, and the cracked valve, and the bent fin, and thought about Ferra on the dock that morning with the frost on the boards and her book open: back by midnight, if Ned turns when he said.

"Ned," he said. "The fin."

Ned had his hand on it already, up to his elbows in the cold, feeling the root where it met the hull. "I can straighten it," he said. "Cade and me, and Bren on the jack. Two days. It'll fly. It'll fly crooked, but it'll fly." He looked at Emeric. "Not tonight. Not in this. And not with a bent fin over two hundred miles of black water."

"Two days."

"Two days. Three if the jack's bad."

Emeric stood in the mud by the Hod's bent fin and thought about it the way he would have thought about a column of Ferra's. They had three days of water and food and four of charge, by Ferra's figures; and they had come down full, and the Band had filled her racks that morning, so call it more. They were dry, or would be, under Abloh's canvas. They were nine, and well. And the only way to get word home tonight was for somebody to fly it: one of the two suits, or him, bare, two hundred miles north-west over the sea in the dark in December with no stars and no coast and nothing under him but water if he got it wrong.

Declan was looking at him. Declan, in his suit with his helmet off under his arm, had worked out the same sum at the same speed, and was waiting to be asked.

"No," said Emeric.

"I could..."

"Nobody flies two hundred miles of night sea alone," said Emeric. "Ward would have my head, and he'd be right." He looked at Osric too, so that Osric would know it was for him as well. "Not you, not Osric, not me. We go home together, in her, when she'll fly. Ferra knows our water and our food and our charge to the hour. She'll know we're down, not dead. She'll know we've got three days." He was quiet for a moment. "She'll hate it."

"And Imani," said Arthur.

"And Imani."

Nobody said anything for a while. The fog dripped off the Hod's house. Somewhere out on the beach, beyond the dunes, a seal coughed.

"Right," said Ned. "Then we'd best get the tents up before we can't see our own feet."



Abloh's tents went up on the firm ground behind the last dune, in the lee of it, in the dark, by battery lamp.

Eight of them. They came out of the Hod's hold in their canvas bags, each one the size of a sack of flour, each with its frame in it in steel tubes, pinned, and its canvas folded round the tubes, and its groundsheet and its fly and its liner and its little tin stove; and Ward's order, since the Proving class had gone north, was that every man aboard a ship of Kaldo's could put one up alone, in the dark, in a quarter of an hour. Ward had timed them, in the castle yard. Ward had made them do it blindfold.

So they did it in the dark, in the fog, by feel, more or less. Ned and Cade, who had done it fifty times, had theirs up in ten minutes and their stoves going. Declan and Osric, in their suits, did theirs together and quickly and without a word, like knights doing anything. Arthur did his alone and then went and did Sully's for him, because Sully had never put one up anywhere but the castle yard and stood looking at the bag in his hands as if it might bite. Mira did hers with her teeth set and her handkerchief still in her sleeve, and it was perfect, and she stood back and looked at it, and said, "There," to nobody.

And Bren fought his.

Emeric would remember it afterward, of that whole night, more clearly than nearly anything else. Bren, on the sand behind the dune, in the light of one battery lamp stuck upright in the sand, with the steel frame of his tent in pieces all round him and the canvas over his head. The frame went together in a ridge and two ends and four legs, each tube pinned into the next by a little steel pin on a chain; and Bren had got the ridge in, and one end, and both legs at that end, and was holding the whole thing upright on one side with his shoulder while he tried to pin the second end with his free hand; and the second end, every time he got its pin near its hole, swung away from him in the wind off the sea and took the canvas with it, and the canvas came down over his head, and the first end, unsupported, folded quietly over sideways onto the sand.

He did it four times.

The fourth time, the whole tent came down over him at once, frame and canvas and fly together, and lay on the sand in a long low heap with Bren under it, and did not move; and from under it, after a long moment, in a voice of the most perfect calm, Bren said, "Ned."

"Yes," said Ned, from his own tent's door, with his pipe.

"I've decided to sleep like this."

Mira laughed. She did not mean to. She had been holding it in since the second time, standing by her own perfect tent with her arms folded, and it came out of her all at once, a great helpless bark of a laugh across the dark sand; and then Arthur was laughing, and Sully, and Cade, and Osric was making a noise inside his suit's collar like a kettle; and Emeric found that he had sat down on the sand with his back against the dune and could not stop.

The heap on the sand did not move.

"It's quite warm," said Bren's voice from under it. "Abloh's very good. It's quite dry. I can see why he fought Ward over the liner."

Arthur went and pulled him out by his boots, and then the four of them, Arthur and Mira and Bren and Sully, put Bren's tent up together in four minutes flat with Mira giving the orders, and it was perfect too; and Bren stood back and looked at it with his hands on his hips and his hair full of sand and said it had been a very unusual tent, and that he would be telling Abloh so.



The stoves were lit by six. They burned anything, Abloh's stoves: wood, coal, peat, dung. There was a great deal of driftwood on the beach beyond the dunes, grey and salt-white and dry at its tops, old trees from somewhere carried a long way by the sea; and Osric and Sully went down for it with a lamp while Cade unpacked the Hod's stores, and Declan, who could cook, cooked.

That was a thing about Declan Emeric had not known for years. Declan cooked the way he did everything, close and neat, without fuss. He took the Hod's salt pork and her oatmeal and an onion and a tin of Maren's preserved beans and a handful of something green he had cut off the top of the dune in the dark and smelled before he put it in, and made, in a pot on the stove in Ned's tent, which was the biggest, a thick hot stew that smelled better than anything had any right to smell on a marsh at the end of the world; and they sat in Ned's tent, all nine of them, crammed in on the folding cots and the groundsheet with their knees up and their bowls in their hands, with the little stove ticking in its jack and the lamp on its hook and the fog dripping on the canvas, and ate it.

Nobody said much for a while.

Then Sully said, "Where are we?"

Nobody answered him at once.

"Somewhere nobody's been," said Ned, at last. "Or nobody's come back from." He looked at his bowl. "Old Wicket. Fletcher's village. Everybody on the quay's heard him. The drowned land. Out past the bar, south-east, where the bad water takes you." He was quiet. "His brother."

"Davin," said Emeric.

"Davin. Forty-one years gone." Ned ate a spoonful of stew. "Never came back."

Sully looked at the canvas wall of the tent as though Davin might be on the other side of it.

"We'll come back," said Emeric. "We've a ship. He had a boat."

"What pulled us," said Mira.

She had not said anything since the tent. She sat on the end of Ned's cot with her bowl on her knees and her handkerchief, finally, put away, and looked at Emeric across the stove, steadily, the way she had looked at Ward on the morning of her first cold water in the river, years ago, as if she would sooner know a hard thing than not know it.

"A stone," said Emeric. "A gravity stone. In the lagoon behind the reef. A very big one. Bigger than anything at Grayrock."

"A wild one?"

"No." He was sure of that. "A wild stone doesn't do anything. It sits in the ground. Nobody's ever found one that pulls on its own. This one pulled along a line, one way, steady, like an anchored stone with somebody's hand on it." He turned his bowl in his hands. "Somebody set it there. Somebody made it to do that."

"Who?"

"I don't know."

"Why?"

"I don't know that either." He looked at the stove. "It pulled the densest thing it could feel. That's what it felt like. We were the densest thing on the sea for two hundred miles. Our stones, our canisters. You two in your suits." He nodded at Declan and Osric. "It pulled us like a fish."

"Is it going to do it again?" said Sully.

"No. Not for a long while." Emeric was sure of that, too. "It spent itself on us. I felt it go dry, over the lagoon, like a lamp going out. It's nearly empty. It'll fill again, I think, slowly, out of the sea. But not for a long while."

"Then we could go now," said Cade. "If the fin was right. We could put the stones down. Go thin. Stow the canisters behind the iron and go out over it quiet, and it'd not know we were there."

"We could."

"But we're not."

"The fin's not right."

"And if it was," said Arthur, from the tent's door, where he sat with his long legs stuck out into the fog, "we still wouldn't be." He looked at Emeric. "Would we."

Emeric did not answer him for a while.

"No," he said. "I want to know what it is."

Arthur nodded, as if that were the answer he had expected, and went back to his stew.



Ned set the watch: two at a time, two hours each, in the Hod's house, with a lamp shuttered and the hatches dogged, and one of the two always awake and listening. Declan and Emeric took the first.

The others went to their tents. Emeric heard them going, one after another, in the fog: Sully's stove door clanging, Cade's voice saying something to Ned, Bren's tent creaking as he got into it, and then Bren's voice, quite clearly, through two walls of Abloh's waxed linen: "It's still standing." And Mira's, from the next tent: "Go to sleep, Bren."

Then it was quiet.

He sat in the Hod's house with Declan, with the lamp shuttered to a slit on the chart table between them, on the marsh in the dark at the end of the world, and kept his sense open, wide, all the way out to the dunes' edges and the beach and the reef; and the place lay round him quiet in it, the birds asleep on the shore and the seals breathing on the sand and the marsh full of its small lives, and out on the lagoon the great stone, spent and still. Nobody. Nothing moving that should not.

Declan did not talk. Declan never talked on a watch. He sat across the chart table with his helmet in his lap and his eyes on the shuttered lamp, and after an hour took out a little knife and a bit of the driftwood from the beach and began to whittle something, slowly, with small exact strokes, the shavings curling into his lap.

Emeric took Imani's paper out of his coat and looked at it.

Nine names, in a column, in her square hand. The hours across the top. His own writing in the boxes, from nine that morning to two: pulses, faces, handkerchiefs. And at two, nothing, because at two they had been coming down through the cloud. And at the bottom, under Bring them home. All of them, smaller: Bring yourself.

He thought about her at the ward, at four in the morning, with a child's fever; and coming out at noon, and asking the line room if there was anything from the Hod, and being told no, not since the morning, they were low, they would be in range by dark. And at dark, no. And at midnight.

And Ferra on the dock. He could see it as plainly as if he were there: Ferra on the dock at midnight in her grey coat, in the frost, with her book, alone, because she would have sent everybody else home; doing the sum. Three days' water and food. Four of charge. She would be doing it now. She would have done it a dozen times by now, at the dock's end in the cold with the lamp, standing very straight. She would know exactly how long they had, to the hour, and she would know it was enough, and she would know that knowing it was enough did not help at all.

He had told Ned to ride it. He had told Declan no. Both had been right. He would do both again. It did not help at all either.

"She'll know," said Declan, from across the table, quietly, without looking up from his whittling.

Emeric looked at him.

"Ferra," said Declan. "She'll have done the sum. She'll know we've got three days and we're not using them yet." He took a shaving off the driftwood. "She'll be angry. That's all right. Angry's better than the other thing." He held up what he was making, in the slit of the lamp, and looked at it: a small fat bird, a sea bird, with its head tucked under its wing, asleep. "Osric thinks Tess'll be angry," he said. "He said so in the tent. He said she'll have done the sum too, and she'll be angry, and he's going to tell her he was on watch the whole time and couldn't have flown it if he'd wanted to." He blew the shavings off the bird. "He wasn't on watch. I'm going to tell her that."

Emeric laughed, very quietly, in the dark.

"You're a cruel man, Declan."

"I'm a fair one," said Declan; and went back to his bird.

At two Ned and Cade came in to take the watch, stamping the cold out of their boots; and Emeric went out across the sand to the tent that was his, behind the last dune, in the fog, and lay down in it on Abloh's folding cot under Abloh's felt liner, with the little stove ticking in its jack and the waxed linen dripping softly overhead, and found that it was warm, and dry, and that it smelled faintly of Abloh's sail loft and of the sea; and that Abloh had done what he had been asked to do, which was make something that felt like home when you were a very long way from it.

He did not sleep for a long time. He lay with his sense open, out over the dunes to the lagoon, to the great quiet shape in the water; and thought about who could have put a thing like that out here, and why, and how long ago, and what else they had left.

And the stone lay in its lagoon in the dark, empty, and did nothing, and did not tell him.
