Chapter 20 - The Crown's Judgment

The chamber was not large.

He had expected spectacle, which he recognized afterward as the last piece of the capital still operating inside him. What he got was a long table, hard chairs, and windows set too high in the wall to see out of.

Built for work. Built by somebody who had decided that a room where a man is sentenced should not have a view in it.

That told him something about Keller before Keller said a word.

He was older than the name had suggested, grey at the temples, with his papers already squared at his elbow in the manner of a man who has read every page twice and does not intend to look at them again unless somebody forces him to.

He did not stand when they came in.

He did not need to, and both of the men in front of him understood that immediately and separately.

Petram was three streets away in the judiciary's own holding, where Emeric had put him inside an hour of arriving.

He had worked it out on the last day of the road and had not discussed it with anyone. A confessed conspirator's word against a baron is worth nothing. It is worth less than nothing, because it hands the other side a coerced witness to talk about for an afternoon instead of a ledger.

It was the first genuinely lawyerly instinct he had ever had and he did not enjoy discovering he had it.

Wexler spoke first and did not waste the room's time.

He had petitioned the hearing himself because rumor becomes record if it is left to sit, and he would rather answer a thing directly than spend a year being spoken about.

Keller did not respond to any of that. He turned to Emeric.

"You have evidence or you do not. I am not interested in which of you says it louder."

Ferra stepped forward before Emeric could answer for her, which he preferred and which she had not asked about.

She put three pages on the table.

Barony accounts, in the barony's own registers. All three scraped and written over, and the scraping visible under a candle held at the angle she held it at.

What the original entries recorded was a debt discharge routed through a waypoint marker instead of a merchant house. The marker belonged to a dispatch route. The route belonged to a courier named Cobb.

Then the second paper, which had not existed nine days earlier.

Cobb's payment record, assembled out of waypoint registries she had written ahead for at every stop on the road, cross-referenced against the coin marks in the barony's own remittance log.

The coin had come out of Wexler's treasury under Wexler's seal. Not through a house. Not laundered. Direct, and timed to within a week of the road.

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when something has just become harder to argue with.

The same pages carried the rest of it, because the pages had never been separated.

Repairs invoiced and never done, including the outer wall and the drawbridge chain, which had been paid for twice and replaced with rope. A grain contract at a price no grain has ever cost, running eleven years. Sixteen names drawing garrison pay who had not existed since the war.

And three petitions from the barony's healer, entered into the castle's outgoing register and never forwarded to anybody.

Emeric had not known that last one was going in.

Ferra had found it on the road and had not told him, because she wanted Keller's reaction to be to a document rather than to a prince's grievance, and she had been right to, and he understood that about two seconds after he understood that she had kept it from him.

It was not treason. It was the only item on the table that changed how the room looked at the man.

"Explain the seal."

"I do not control every payment that leaves my treasury."

"You control the seal. That is what a seal is for. Try again."

"Then the pages are forged."

"Both hands on them are yours," Ferra said. "The one underneath and the one that came back over it. If you would like, I will show the court which letters you close and which you leave open, in nine years of registers you kept yourself."

Keller did not ask who she was.

He asked whether the clerk who had prepared the second document would swear to the method by which it had been built, and she said yes, and did, and he entered it in his own hand as the barony's clerk and moved on, because he was not interested in persons.

Nobody in that city ever learned her name.

Then he turned the grievance on Emeric, which Emeric had been waiting for since the door.

"You have an interest here."

"I have a grievance. He paid for the road I nearly died on." He did not dress any part of it. "I would have brought this whether or not I did. Rule on a barony's accounts disagreeing with themselves in the baron's own hand, and a coin trail that ends in his treasury. Not on me."

Keller looked at him for a moment longer than the answer required and then ruled.

Sufficient evidence of treasonous conspiracy, specifically the financing of an attempt upon the life of a prince of the blood.

Death, by crown order. Not baronial discretion.

Then, with a glance at Emeric that sat very close to a warning.

"This is the crown's judgment. Not yours. Remember the difference."

Wexler's face stopped moving.

That was all that happened. No protest, no colour, nothing that would have been visible from the far end of the table. A man in his sixties heard a sentence and the animation went out of his face, and Emeric watched it go and found himself examining a thing he had been carrying for three months without once looking at it.

He had wanted to do it himself.

Not the trial. The other part. He had been carrying a want with a shape on it and had never taken it out of the drawer, and he took it out now, in a small room with windows too high to see out of, and did not care for what it was made of.

It was not for Tam. Tam was not in the ruling anywhere and had never been going to be.

It was for a fire on a road, where he had held every piece of it an hour before it happened and had sat there pointing the whole apparatus at his own chest, and had moved only when two words were the last thing left in him.

No amount of anybody else's blood was ever going to be a payment against that.

The choice had just been taken off him by a man who had never met him.

He found, somewhat to his own disgust, that he was relieved.

At the door Wexler turned and spoke only to him.

Nine years ago he had ridden eleven days to this city, because his town was dying and he had exhausted every other thing a man in his position is able to do. He had sold a horse to make the journey. He had stood in a corridor in this building for two days.

On the third a clerk came out and told him the matter had been noted.

He went home. He stopped writing letters, because a man who keeps asking is a man who has agreed to keep being told no, and he had decided he would rather be a man who had stopped.

He buried a third of them and kept the rest breathing on what was left. Nobody from this city ever came. Nobody from this city ever asked.

And then a rider arrived with one line on it, and the line said the crown was giving four generations of his family's work to its own embarrassment, in order to get him out of the capital.

So he paid for it.

He wanted that plain. He was not asking to be thought better of.

"The debt cleared the same week as the road. Before I ever reached Kaldo."

"Yes."

"So you never saw me. You paid for what you were told was coming."

"I paid for a drunk."

And then the part that did the damage.

He had spent four months watching what had actually got off that carriage. A town with clean water in it for the first time in eleven years. Men on a payroll. A kiln lit. A trade that would outlive the boy who started it.

More in four months than he had managed in thirty years, and he had sat at his own table every night of it knowing both of those things at once.

That was not a defense. Keller's ruling was correct and he would have made the same one from the same chair.

It was only the thing he would rather Emeric carried than the version Emeric would have built for himself.

Then he went through the door and Emeric stood in a small room with nothing whatsoever to say.

He asked about the family afterward, and Keller's clerk gave it to him in about forty seconds, because it was on a page.

A wife, dead before the plague. A son who took a merchant clerkship in Wayford eleven years ago and did not come back, because his father was pouring four generations into a town that was visibly ending and the son could do arithmetic.

The letters stopped being about coming home somewhere around year six.

So there was no widow in Kaldo and no heir in the lower ward, and the thing Wexler had paid to keep was a name his own son had already declined.

Emeric wrote to the son once, from the inn, that night.

He did not answer.

Petram got labor, and Keller stated it flatly and did not invite discussion.

A confessed man who testifies is worth more alive than a corpse that deters nobody, and the crown does not reward cooperation with a rope, and any court that does it once will never be given a witness again.

Emeric asked for him to serve it in Kaldo and got it, because nobody else wanted him.

Ward found him in the courtyard afterward and had two things.

The first was Cobb, who was still out there, which Emeric filed next to the other things he had stopped expecting to resolve cleanly.

The second was the one that had actually brought him out.

"He kept a garrison. Whatever else he was, he paid armed men to answer to him, and now there's no him for them to answer to."

"How many."

"Enough to matter. Not enough to feel safe about." Ward looked at the wall rather than at him, which is what he did when he intended to be disagreed with. "I'd want to know which of them were soldiers and which of them were his friends before I put a blade in any of their hands."

"After the farm. The farm eats first. The garrison keeps."

Ward accepted it the way he accepted most of Emeric's ordering of the world, which is to say that he put his disagreement somewhere Emeric would find it later if it turned out he had been wrong.

Armand looked older than the memory, and the memory was fifteen years out of date, which was hardly fair to either of them.

"Your Highness."

Then, quieter, after a look at the courtyard to see who was in it.

"Emeric."

"I read your notebook."

"All of it?"

"Including the page at the back. Eleven tests. Nine years."

"Then you know I found nothing."

"I know you were looking correctly and stopped too soon, and I'm not going to explain that standing in a courtyard. I need you to see it. Not hear about it. Put your hands on it."

"That isn't how you used to talk to me."

"No. It isn't."

The old man stood there with his hands folded and did not say yes, and did not say no, and what he said instead was that the tower did not run itself and that he had obligations in it that could not simply be set down in an afternoon.

Settle what needs settling here first. Then he would come.

Emeric took that as a yes and went to find his horse.

On the way out he looked up at the gallery one last time.

It hung clear of the tower's spine with nothing under it but air, the way it had when he was seven and had thought it the finest thing he had ever seen. Ten men at the rail at that hour, each with a crystal the size of a melon in both hands and their eyes half shut. Ten more behind them, standing, waiting to step into the gap the instant a grip went.

Twenty a day. Twenty the day before. Twenty every day for four hundred years.

Three nights ago, at a waystation past Wayford, he had gone into a room that had been exactly where he left it, with nobody in it, holding its own shape for a month with no attention on it of any kind.

He stood in a courtyard doing arithmetic he had no business being able to do and arrived, without wanting to, at the fact that every one of those men was standing there because an entire order of mages had never once considered that a stone might be able to remember something on its own.

He did not say so.

There are things you let a man arrive at himself, and there are things you do not say out loud in the courtyard of the Circle.

The dispatch came three days after they got back, secondhand, in a list of other business.

Sentence carried out.

Nothing else. Four words in a hand that had written eleven other lines that morning.

He read it twice anyway, which was a habit now.

It should have felt like something closing.

The coin trail had held. The route had held. The timing had held, exactly the way Ferra had built them to hold, clean and provable and undeniable in front of a man as procedurally cold as any in the kingdom.

He had done every part of it correctly and the system had worked precisely as designed.

And Tam's name was still sitting where he had left it, and no ruling anywhere had touched it.

Nobody had asked about the guard on the duty roster. Nobody could prove Wexler had ordered that specifically, only that he had paid for the attempt in general, and general is sufficient for a treason charge and nowhere near sufficient for the thing Emeric had actually wanted answered.

Which left the possibility he had been carrying for three days without putting into words.

That the hand which reached Tam might not have been Wexler's at all. That it might still be in his castle. That it had watched him ride out and had watched him ride back.

And underneath that, refusing to settle in any column he had, a man who had ridden eleven days for a dying town and been told that the matter had been noted.

Both of those were true.

He could not get them to sit in the same place and he could not make either one go away.
