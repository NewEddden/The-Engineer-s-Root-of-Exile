Chapter 20 - Still Bleeding

The number would not die.

The census had been honest for a season, corrected down to the eighteen hundred and forty-seven Kaldo actually held, the false two thousand three hundred stripped out of the books before the wall ever went up around the true count. Honest headcount, honest tithe, honest collection.

He had expected the shortfall to close the way a wound closes once you stop reopening it. Most of it had. The great muddy gap between what Wexler reported and what the barony earned had shrunk to almost nothing.

Almost. A thin residual sat at the bottom of every quarter's reckoning, the same small wrongness Ferra had flagged in the delving space before the war and set aside behind a wall and a harvest. Small enough that a tired clerk would round it away. Stubborn enough that it came back every time he squared the figures.

He had assumed, the first three times, that the error was his. He had spent a life trusting that when a system misbehaves, the operator is usually at fault before the machine is. So he checked his own arithmetic, and his arithmetic held, and the residual sat there anyway, patient as rust.

He brought it back to Ferra, because Ferra was the one person in Kaldo who could look at a column of numbers and see the shape moving underneath it, and because she had already told him it would keep, and it had, and now there was room to look at it.

"It's not a rounding error," she said, before he had finished laying it out. She had the quarterly sheets spread across the trestle in the counting room, corners pinned with river stones. "I told you that much in the spring. A rounding error scatters. This clusters."

"Clusters how?"

"Same weeks. Every year. Let me lay it flat."

He knew what she meant by flat. On paper the pattern hid inside the ordinary churn of a barony's money, in and out, owed and paid, the residual one small wrongness lost among a hundred honest ones. Flat meant the other thing. The space.

He wanted her there when he thought about it, and that was enough. The counting room thinned around them, the trestle and the pinned sheets and the grey afternoon light going quiet and distant, and the workspace opened the way it had been opening since Bram stumbled into it by accident.

Ferra's data came with her. It always did. The figures lifted off the flat sheets and arranged themselves in the air the way her mind already held them, not because the space was clever but because she was, and the space only gave her room to spread out what she already knew how to read.

She spread it out. Four years of quarters, stacked and aligned, the residual picked out in a colour she chose without thinking.

And there it was. Not scattered across the calendar. Gathered. Four bright knots, one per year, each landing in the same stretch of weeks, late in the trading season when the roads were still good and the river still high enough to move goods down toward the coast.

"There," Ferra said, and there was no triumph in it. "It isn't old. It comes back."

That was the answer to the question he had been carrying since the day he found the false headcount. He had framed it plainly to himself then, the only way he knew how to frame anything. Old rot or new. Was the bleed a dead thing, a scar left by dead barons that would fade now that he collected honestly, or a live wound with something still feeding on it?

He had wanted it to be old. Old could be healed by time and clean books. Old asked nothing of him.

It was new. It was live. Wexler's fraud had died with Wexler under the crown's blade, and the barony was still bleeding on a schedule.

"Wexler's books hid it," Ferra said, following his silence to the same place. "Not caused it. His lie was so big it swallowed a smaller one. Clean the big lie away and the small one's just sitting there. In the open. It was always in the open. We couldn't see it for the mud."

"The season," Emeric said. "The clustering. It isn't the harvest cycle. It's a route cycle. Something moves through here on a schedule and takes a cut on the way."

She was already reaching for it. He watched her overlay the one other thing she had carried into the space with her, a thing she had built long before this, back when she first traced the barony's official correspondence to satisfy herself she understood how word got in and out of Kaldo. The dispatch route. The line the crown's courier ran, up through Wayford and on to the capital and back, the same line that had carried Wexler's fat false reports to a crown too busy to weigh them against the truth.

She set the courier's passes against the residual and the two patterns lay down together as though they had been cut from the same template. The knots of missing coin sat exactly where the route ran.

"Cobb," she said.

The name landed with the small ugly weight of something long suspected finally holding still enough to see. Cobb, the courier who serviced Kaldo's official dispatch line. Cobb, whose route she had confirmed back in the winter, when she could prove the man passed through and could prove nothing else. Cobb, who had been a name attached to Wexler's dealings and had gone right on being a name after Wexler stopped breathing.

"He's the conduit," Emeric said. "The money leaves Kaldo clean and arrives short, and it goes short on his pass. He's carrying it, or he's clipping it, or he's telling someone when to clip it. He's the mechanism."

"He's the mechanism," Ferra agreed. And then, because she was honest even inside a space that would have let her lie to herself comfortably, she said the rest. "And that's all he is. That's the whole floor and the whole ceiling of what I can show you."

He came out of the space too fast, the way you surface too fast from deep water, the counting room slapping back into place around him.

The residual, in his head, had already become a problem with an obvious next move. Find the mechanism, remove the mechanism. Take Cobb. Squeeze him. Follow the money back up its own channel to whatever hand was waiting at the top of it. He had the shape of the whole operation now, and a shape you can see is a shape you can dismantle.

"We take him," he said. "Quietly. Ward can have him off the road before his next pass, and once he understands what waits for him if he stays silent, he gives us the man paying him, and we walk that thread all the way home."

"You walk it as far as Cobb," Ferra said. "Then it stops."

"He knows who pays him."

"Maybe. He knows who pays him to pass through here." She said it slowly, making him hear the word. "This is one road, and Kaldo is one stop on it, and all I can see is our own gate. I can show a magistrate that coin goes out of Kaldo and comes back short on the weeks Cobb runs. I cannot show who takes it, or why, or whether it's one hand or ten, because everything past our own wall happens on ground I can't put a number to."

She squared the sheets under her hand. "You want the man at the top of the channel. I can prove the channel has a man in it. That's a different thing, and no court alive treats them the same. We already learned that once, and it cost us Tam."

The name came into the room and sat down.

Ward was at the door by then. Emeric had not heard him arrive and did not know how much he had caught, and did not need him to have caught much. Ward had a way of arriving on the edge of exactly this kind of conversation, drawn to the shape of it the way a man keeps pressing a tooth that aches.

"Say it plain," Ward said.

"The barony's still being robbed," Emeric said. "We found the door the money leaves by. We can't reach the room it goes to."

Ward took that in without any change to his face, which was its own kind of answer. "Same as before," he said.

"Not the same."

"Same wall." Ward came a step into the room. "You proved Wexler skimmed and you put Wexler in the ground and it didn't touch Tam, because the thing that got Tam killed lived on the far side of what you could prove, and you never reached it. You'll prove Cobb carries. You won't reach the far side of Cobb. Same wall. New coat of paint on it."

He was right, and the worst part was the arithmetic of it.

Take Cobb, and you get one courier and a warning that travels up the channel faster than any confession could travel down it. Whoever waited at the top would go quiet, change couriers, change seasons, and the one strand Kaldo could see would go dark, leaving the web behind it more hidden than before.

To follow the whole thing you would need to stand at more gates than Kaldo's. You would need to see the route from Wayford's end and Milford's and every stop between here and the capital, and lay all of their missing coin down together until the pattern of one man paid by many hands finally stood up where a court could look at it.

Kaldo could not do that. Kaldo was one gate. From one gate the thing was invisible past its own threshold, for the plainest possible reason, which is that a single strand looks like nothing at all until you can see the rest of the web it belongs to.

And there was a colder thought under it now, one the war had put there. The neighbours had tried to take Kaldo with a hundred men and failed. Men who fail at the loud way do not simply stop. They find the quiet way.

A barony strangled on its own dispatch road, bled a little every season, kept just poor enough that it can never quite arm or store or grow past reach, was exactly the quiet way. It was already running. It had been running before the war, and would run better now the loud way had been tried and lost.

He could not prove that either. He could only feel the shape of it, the same cold hand he had felt at the edge of the battle, lighting fuses and standing back, and note that whoever it was had more than one kind of match.

So the move his engineer's mind had already made, find it and fix it, was not a move. It was a category error, the same one he had walked into with the labor and the fields. He had confused identified with actionable. He had treated the discovery as the end of the problem when it was only the discovery of how much larger the problem was than Kaldo.

"So we do nothing," he said. It came out flatter than he meant.

"We do nothing yet," Ferra said. "There's a difference, even if it doesn't feel like one. We know the route bleeds. We know the season. We know the name of the man who carries it. That's not nothing. It's a held card. You don't throw a held card because your hand itches."

"It's knowledge we can't spend," Emeric said.

"Most knowledge is, until it isn't." She began, methodically, to unpin the sheets. "You wanted this to close. I understand why. But we don't get to close it from here. Whatever Cobb is a thread of, we only pull it apart from the outside, from more gates than ours, and we don't have those gates. Not yet."

Not yet. He turned the two words over and did not like how much of his life had started ending in them lately.

He had bought the barony time it could not afford, and now a creditor's factor sat in a rented room three streets away with a lawful right to read these very books, and somewhere in the honest ledgers he was so proud of there was a small clean wrong figure that came back every year like a tide, and he could name the man who carried it out the door and do absolutely nothing to the hand that caught it on the other side.

"Log it," he said finally. "Everything. The residual, the season, the route, the name. Sealed. Ferra keeps it, nobody else reads it, and we do not so much as look at Cobb sideways. He runs his passes. He stays comfortable. He stays exactly as visible as he is now, which is the only advantage we have and the one we'd throw away the second we moved."

Ferra nodded. She had already known that was the answer. She had only wanted him to arrive at it himself, and he understood, dimly, that this was a thing she did, and that it was a kindness, and that he was not always good at seeing her kindnesses for what they were.

Ward stayed a moment after she had gone, the sheets under her arm, the room emptying of the pattern that had been so bright half an hour before.

"You wanted it to be Wexler," Ward said. "The whole of it. One rotten baron, one clean blade, done."

"Yes."

"It's never one man." Ward looked at the door she had gone through, or past it, at something on the far side of a wall neither of them could reach. "The one man is just the part that holds still long enough to kill. The rest of it keeps moving. Tam taught me that, and I keep having to learn it."

He left. Emeric sat with the squared, honest, quietly bleeding books, and the small figure that would come back next quarter and the quarter after that, faithful as the season, carried out his own front gate by a comfortable man he had just decided to protect.

He had asked whether the rot was old or new. He had his answer. It was new, and it was live, and it was so much bigger than Kaldo that the only thing to do with the truth of it was to sit very still and hold it, and wait for a day, still far off, when he would finally be standing at enough gates to see the whole of the thing at once.
