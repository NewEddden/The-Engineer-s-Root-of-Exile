Chapter 20 - The Mill

Wire needed a pull.

A steady one, the same weight hour after hour, that didn't tire and didn't jerk and didn't stop to eat. Emeric had known that since September, standing in the foundry with Bram arguing about bays. He'd known where the pull was, too. He'd walked past it twice a week since April.

The mill stood on the river below the east bridge, on the town side, where the current came hard round the bend. Stone below, timber above, a wheel as tall as two men turning in a race cut into the bank. It turned whether anyone was using it or not. Most of the time, that winter, nobody was. Half the fields in the barony had stood empty since the plague, and a town with half its fields empty didn't need much flour ground.

The town priced it accordingly. An old wheel and a tired building, a living for one family and not a very good one. When its lease of the ground came up on the pile that had landed on Emeric's desk in September, nobody had thought it worth a second line.

He'd thought it worth a great deal more than that. He didn't say why.

Wire now. That was the reason he could say. And underneath it, the one he didn't: the river went past Kaldo all day and all night with more strength in it than every mage in the capital, and one day the power for everything Kaldo built would come off that bank. Not from stones. Not from a man feeding a crystal till he dropped. From water that didn't care who the king was.

He wanted to be standing on that bank when the day came. He wanted to already own a piece of it.

"What's the wire for?" Bram asked, when Emeric told him he'd want a draw plate by midwinter.

"Later," Emeric said.

Bram looked at him. Then he shrugged and went back to his anvil. The work was paid; the question could wait.



The miller was called Harl. So was his father, and his father's father; the name was cut deep into the stone lintel over the mill door, with a date under it that was nearly a hundred years old. He was a stooped, floury man past fifty who lived alone in the rooms above the stones and ground what little the town brought him.

He was also short of money. Everyone in Kaldo was short of money, but Harl had been short since the plague, and the mill's books, which Ferra had because the barony owned the ground it stood on, said so.

So Emeric went to him and bought a tenth of it.

He kept the baron out of it, and made that matter. The ground lease was the barony's business, and he renewed it in the same week at the old rate, and told Harl so before he said a word about anything else: the lease was his, whatever he decided, and nothing about it depended on the prince's coin.

Then, with his own coin, out of his own purse, he offered to buy a tenth share in the mill itself. The stones, the gear, the wheel and the takings. At the price the town would have put on it: an old wheel and a tired building.

Harl took it. He needed the money, and a tenth was nothing, and a prince who wanted to own a tenth of a flour mill was plainly a fool, and Harl had heard about the drunk like everybody else.

Ferra wrote it in her book. She wrote the price. Then she studied it.



After that, Emeric let himself be seen.

He went down to the mill often. He stood on the bank and looked at the wheel. He brought Bram, and Bram measured the wheel shaft with a cord and his thumb and stood in the race in his boots for an hour, frowning at the paddles. The prince's carriage stood on the mill road twice in one week, and the second time, half the lower market saw it go by.

Nothing about the mill changed. Not a board. Harl ground the same three sacks a day he'd always ground. But the prince was interested, and in a town that had learned in one summer that what the prince was interested in tended to end up paying wages, that was enough.

Within a fortnight, people were asking.

Pell, who had the biggest cloth business left on the high street and had once had a much bigger one, asked Harl whether he'd sell a share. The widow Brannock, who owned more of the west farms than anyone but the barony, sent her steward to ask the same, and then came herself. A third man, who lent money at a rate Ferra described only as illegal anywhere with a magistrate, asked through his nephew.

Harl came up to the keep to ask Emeric what was going on.

Emeric told him the truth, which was that nothing was going on, and Harl went away more puzzled than before.

Ferra put it to him that night, flatly, at her table.

"Nothing about that mill is different," she said, "except who's standing in it."

"I know."

"You're making it worth more by looking at it."

"I know."

She put the pen down.

"I know," he said a third time, quieter. "It's not honest. It isn't dishonest either. Nobody's been told a lie." He thought about it. "Everybody's just been told who I am."

Ferra looked at him. She didn't write anything.

He saw that he wanted her to, and that she wouldn't, and he did it anyway.



Then he went to buy the rest of it, and Harl wouldn't sell.

Emeric had the numbers. He'd worked them three times and Ferra had worked them once, and they were good: a fair price, more than fair, for an old wheel and a tired building that three people in the town now wanted to buy. A price that would let Harl stop grinding three sacks a day for a town that didn't need them.

He set the figure down on Harl's table in the rooms above the stones.

Harl looked at it for some time. Then he pushed it back across the table with one finger.

"No," he said.

Emeric raised it. Not much. Enough.

"No."

He didn't raise it again. He looked at the old man instead, properly, the way he should have looked at him the first time.

"My wife went in the first spring," Harl said. He wasn't looking at Emeric. He was looking at the floor, where the flour dust lay in the cracks between the boards. "The plague. My two boys in the summer after. I buried all three of them in the rows, because there wasn't anywhere else. And the mill kept going. I kept it going. I ground for the town all the way through, when there was hardly a town."

He looked up.

"Everything I had went in the ground except this. So you can't frighten me with losing it. I've lost. And you can't buy it. It isn't for sale." He nodded toward the door, toward the lintel with his grandfather's name on it. "Nor is that."

Emeric sat still.

His numbers had been right. Every one of them. And his read of the man had been wrong from the first day, because he'd read a poor man who needed money, and Harl was a man who'd already lost everything money could replace.

He thought of Corliss in the garrison yard, waiting to be asked a second question. He thought of Ferra's dated line in her book. He put both thoughts away for later and made himself think about the mill.

"Then don't sell it," he said.

Harl waited.

"Keep it. Your name on it. Your stones. Your trade." Emeric put his hands flat on the table. "I keep my tenth. And I want the wheel's idle hours. At night, and in the winter, when the stones aren't running and the wheel's turning for nothing. A belt off the shaft, a drum in the back room, nothing that touches your stones. I'll pay for every hour of it. And I'll pay for the fittings."

Harl looked at the floor again.

"My name stays on it," he said.

"Your name stays on it."

Harl put out his hand.

The three who'd asked to buy in got a share in the end, because Emeric sold them one: half of his own tenth, split three ways, at the price his own carriage on the mill road had set. When Ferra added it up, the half he'd sold had brought in more than the whole tenth had cost him, and the difference paid for the copper.

Ward had been at the door of the steward's room the evening the last of it was signed. He looked at Emeric, and then at the ledger on the table, and then he went back to looking at the corridor.

That was how Ward said something.



The draw plate, they built in the room.

At Ferra's table, on a short grey afternoon, with Bram's hand on Emeric's shoulder and Ferra on the other side of the table with the timing book open and her pen ready. Inside, Emeric put up the plate as he wanted it: a slab of the hardest steel Bram could make, with a row of holes bored through it, each one tapered like a funnel and each one a hair smaller than the last, so that copper rod pulled through the first came out thinner and longer, and pulled through the next came out thinner again, and so on down the row until it was a wire.

Bram turned it over in his hands in there and asked how big each hole was meant to be.

Emeric didn't have an answer. There was still no measure. So they numbered the holes instead, one to fourteen, by order, and Bram filed them to fit by eye and by feel.

Outside, the next week, Tess turned up at the forge with something she'd made in the evenings. A thin plate of scrap iron with fourteen holes drilled in it, one for each hole in the draw plate, so you could push the wire through the matching hole and see if the draw plate had worn.

"So we know if it's going," she said. "Since nobody knows how big they are."

She'd written thumb at the top of it, scratched into the iron with a nail, and then crossed it out.



The copper came off the Grayrock barge in December, with the iron, in rough bars.

Bram cast it into rod. They rigged a drum in the back room of the mill, as promised, with a belt off the wheel shaft that ran only when Harl had stopped grinding for the night. The first rod went into the first hole and came out the other side a little longer and a little thinner. Then the second. Then the third.

Then it snapped.

Emeric knew why. He'd known before it happened, the way he'd known the third batch of soap would burn somebody. Copper hardened as you worked it. Every pull through the plate made it stiffer and more brittle, and after three pulls it was too stiff to stretch any further, and it broke. You had to heat it between pulls, bring it up red and let it cool, to soften it again. Annealing. He knew the word, and the reason, and the right heat, and how long.

He could have done it properly himself. Slowly. Thinking through every heat, every colour, every minute.

Bram did it by eye. He took the snapped rod to the forge and brought it up in the fire and pulled it out at a colour Emeric couldn't have named, and laid it down, and said, "There." It took him about a tenth of the time it would have taken Emeric. Then he picked up the broken end of the first rod, turned it to the light, and said this copper was dirty.

"Dirty?"

"There's grey in it. See there." Bram showed him a faint streak in the break. "That'll snap wherever it is. Doesn't matter how soft you get it." He put it down. "Grayrock's sending us their sweepings."

Emeric looked at the streak. Nothing in any book he'd ever read had said anything about Grayrock's sweepings.

They melted the rest of the bars again and skimmed them, which took two more days and cost a fifth of the copper. Then they drew it again, with a heat in the forge between every third hole.

It was Tess who drew the first length that ran the whole row. All fourteen holes, end to end, on a night in the second week of January, with the wheel turning in the dark outside and Harl asleep upstairs. It came off the drum in a long bright coil, thinner than a bootlace, and didn't break.

She stood holding the end of it, not saying anything. Bram put a hand on the back of her neck, once, and took it away again.



That same week, at night in his own room, Emeric held himself a hand's width off the floor with the candle in his hand.

He could do it for a count of forty now. Gravity and fire both, the hover and the bent flame, two glyphs flickering side by side, and the headache afterward no worse than a long day.

On the fortieth count he held the flame bent away from him and felt it, the same faint shove against his palm he'd felt in August, stronger now that he could hold it longer. He knew exactly what he'd do with it. Push harder, and hold the hover, and see which way the shove sent him.

He didn't. He was alone, in the dark, with nobody in the room to see what happened if the hover went while he was moving. Ferra would have had a rule for that. He could hear her saying it. He let the flame stand up, and came down on his heels, and sat on the bed.

In the back of the notebook he wrote the count, and under it Not yet, and underlined it.



Abloh came to see the coil the next morning, because Bram had told him there was something at the forge that wanted a sailmaker.

He turned the end of it over in his fingers. Then, without being asked, he took a spool of linen thread out of his coat, ran it through a cake of beeswax, and wound it round and round the wire in a tight spiral, close as the hairs on a dog, for the length of his forearm. He held it up. Not a scrap of copper showed.

"That what you wanted?"

"That's what I wanted."

"What's it for?"

"Later," Emeric said.

Abloh looked at him for a moment, the same look Bram had given him in November. Then he laughed, shortly, and put the thread back in his coat.

Ferra priced it at her table that evening. Copper by the length, the fifth they'd lost to skimming, the forge time, the belt, the hours of the wheel, the thread and the wax. She worked it out to the copper and then corrected his estimate of it, which had been low.

"What does it bring in?" she asked.

"Nothing yet."

She wrote it in the book on its own line, and in the column where the return went she wrote one word.

Unpriced.



The coil hung on a nail on the forge wall, by the door, where everyone who came in could see it. It had no use yet. Nobody but Emeric knew what it might be for, and he wasn't saying.

On her next forge morning, Tess took it down and measured it.

There was no foot to measure it by. So she took a length of cord and tied a knot in it at every stretch of her own two arms, and laid the coil out along the forge floor, and walked the cord down it, counting knots.

Then she wrote the number on the wall beside the nail in chalk, and the word knots after it, and hung the coil back up.
