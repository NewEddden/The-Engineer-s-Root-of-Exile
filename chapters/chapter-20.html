Chapter 20 - The Venture

He fixed the franchise first, because he had written down that he would fix it before the harvest and in a way that cost him something, and because he had learned by now that the order of two things was usually the whole argument.

If he asked the field for money and then repaired the voting rule, the repair would read as the price of the money. If he repaired it first, standing there with nothing in his hands, it read as a man keeping a note he had made to himself in the dark.

He put it to them on a hot morning with the ring three weeks off closing.

"I wrote down who owns what and I never wrote down how you decide," he said. "That's mine. Nobody asked me for it and nobody caught it, and it cost Maren a vote she should have had, and it cost the eleven other households in this room who wanted the coin in spring and got outweighed by ground they don't stand on."

Dunnel, on the second bench with the tally-book on his knee, said, "You ruled parts."

"I ruled parts because a share that votes by heads isn't a share, and I still think that's right, and it's half of a right answer, which is the most dangerous kind." He had the sheet in his hand and did not look at it. "So here's the other half. Anything the field decides needs both. A majority of the parts, and a majority of the households. Both, or it doesn't carry."

He gave them a moment to find the objection, and Halloway found it, because Halloway always found the procedural one.

"Then nothing carries," he said. "Not on anything hard. I've a hundred and forty acres and Maren's eleven and we'll disagree about half of what comes up, and you've just built a thing where either of us can stop the other."

"Yes."

"That's not a virtue, my lord, that's a stuck cart."

"It's a stuck cart on anything where the men with the land and the men with the hands want opposite things, and I'd rather be stuck than have one of them able to sell the other." Emeric leaned on the table. "You've all been on the wrong end of a decision made by people whose interests weren't yours. Every one of you. That's the whole history of this floodplain. If the price of never doing that to each other again is that some things don't get decided in one morning, I'll pay it and so will you."

"And your four parts?" said Dunnel.

Which was the question he had come in wanting, and he had a small ignoble moment of pleasure at being asked it rather than having to volunteer it.

"My four parts don't vote," he said. "Not on this, not on anything the field decides. They pay out and they take their share last, and they have no voice at all."

The room went quiet in the particular way it did when he had said something they had to check twice.

"That's a fifth of the field silent," Halloway said.

"That's a fifth of the field belonging to a man who has a garrison, a wall, a foundry going up, and the only working relationship with the merchant who'll be moving your grain. I don't need a vote. I've got every other kind of leverage a man can have and pretending my four parts are just four parts among yours would be the most dishonest thing on this page." He shrugged. "Also I intend to ask you for something in about ten minutes and I'd rather not be voting on it."

Dunnel laughed. It was short and surprised and it was, Emeric was fairly sure, the first time he had heard the man do it.

It carried, both ways, twenty-nine households and fourteen and a half parts, and the chandler ruled the amendment onto the page under the original terms with the date, and Emeric watched him do it and felt the specific relief of a thing being made harder to undo.

Then he asked.

"Say the number first," Dunnel said, before he had got three sentences out.

"I don't have one number. I have a shape and then a number, and the shape matters more, so bear with me for a quarter of an hour and then shout."

The shape was this.

The foundry needed ore. Ore came from Grayrock down the river for nothing, because water ran downhill out of the hills and a barge cost less than a cart, and Grayrock knew that and priced accordingly. Ore came from Duvrenne by road at four times the carting cost. Either way it needed paying for, and paying for it needed something that was not coin, because there was no coin, and the only thing Kaldo was about to have in quantity was grain.

And the grain was not his.

"Four parts in twenty are mine and I'm spending every one of them on ore," he said. "That's already decided and it's already spent, most of it, because Milford paid me a third of it in advance and the third went into the shaft you can see from the road. Four parts in twenty buys me a furnace running at a bit under half. It doesn't buy me three furnaces, and it doesn't buy tools for you, and it doesn't buy the pipe for the spray heads I promised you in the spring."

"So you want ours," said Halloway.

"I want to be able to ask for some of yours, and I want the asking to be a thing you can say no to household by household, and I want the arrangement written somewhere that has nothing to do with the field's twenty parts, because if I bolt this onto the field and it fails, it takes the field down with it."

That was the part he had been turning over for eleven days, and Bram had handed him the answer in about nine words without knowing he was doing it.

They had been at the forge, and Emeric had been complaining, and Bram had said: "That's not the boat. That's the voyage."

The Marrow was held in sixteen parts and had been for sixty years, and those sixteen parts were the hull. They were not the cargo. When the boat went down to the coast with a load, whoever wanted a share of that particular trip put in for that particular trip, and the money and the risk and the takings of it were reckoned on their own and closed out at the end, and the man who owned four parts of the hull might put in nothing at all and the cooper who owned none of it might put in half the cargo. One boat. Sixty years. Hundreds of voyages, each one its own small separate life, and if a voyage was lost the men who had funded that voyage lost their money and the boat was still a boat.

"So the foundry is a voyage," Emeric said.

He laid it out. Its own page in the chandler's book, after the field's, with its own name on it and no connection whatever to the twenty parts. A stated purpose: ore, coal, and the tools and castings that came out of the far end. A stated term of four years. A stated number of parts, two hundred and forty of them, so that a household with eleven acres could put in a small amount and get a real number of parts rather than a fraction that had to be argued about.

Grain in, valued at the ford price on the day it was handed over and written down at that price by the man who took it. Parts out, in proportion. Paid from the foundry's own takings, from the sale of what it made, and not out of the field's harvest, ever, under any circumstances.

"And when the four years are up?" Ferra said, on cue, because they had agreed she would ask it.

"The page closes. The parts pay out whatever the venture made. If it made nothing, they pay out nothing and the page closes anyway, and nobody carries it forward, and nobody's grandchildren are still arguing about a foundry venture in ninety years. A voyage ends."

"And if it made a great deal?"

"Then the men who put grain in a hole in the ground in a year when nobody sane would have get paid for it, and I hope they're insufferable about it."

Then Dunnel stood up, and did the thing Emeric had been hoping for and had been careful not to arrange.

"Two terms," he said, "and I'll not sign without either."

"Say them."

"First. A cap, per household. No man puts in more than a quarter of what his own parts bring him." He said it to the room rather than to Emeric. "I'll not pretend that's for the prince's protection. It's for Tarrow's, and Ashe's, and mine, because I've seen what a man does when he's watched his neighbour make money on something and he's got a chance to make it back double." He turned his hand over. "You give a man leave to put in everything and one of us will, and he'll be the one who can least afford it, and he'll do it because he's the one who most needs it to work. A cap stops me being a fool at my own request."

Emeric had, in fact, written a cap into his own draft and taken it out again on the grounds that it was patronising.

"Agreed," he said. "A quarter, and it's a quarter for me as well on my four parts, which means I can't quietly hold the whole venture myself in a bad year."

"Second." Dunnel's voice changed slightly. "The seed store's outside it. Not capped. Outside. It cannot be put in, it cannot be valued, it cannot be pledged against anything, not for the foundry, not for a war, not if the prince asks nicely with the whole town watching. It's written on the page as a thing that isn't on the page."

"Why the seed specifically?"

Dunnel looked at him as though he were slightly slow, which Emeric had learned to take as a good sign.

"Because the harvest is this year and the seed is every year after it." He sat back down. "You can lose a harvest. Everyone in this room has lost a harvest. You lose the seed and you're not a farmer any more, you're a man with a field and nothing to put in it, and then you're buying seed off Grayrock's road at whatever they name, which is where my father spent the last nine years of his life."

The room made a low sound of agreement, which it had not made for anything Emeric had said all morning.

"Write it in," Emeric said. "In the strongest words the chandler's got. And write it in as a term, not a favour, so I can't undo it and neither can whoever comes after me."

Nineteen households of the thirty-seven came in.

Ferra had guessed twenty-two and Emeric had guessed twelve and they were both wrong in the directions that told them something about themselves.

The nineteen were, almost exactly, the households with the fewest ground parts and the most work parts. Maren put in her full quarter within the hour and brought two of her sons in behind her. The cooper's widow put in, and a man named Tarrow who held nine acres and six work parts, and Widow Corbell put in a quantity so small that the chandler had to invent a line for it and she watched him write it with her arms folded.

The big ground-holders mostly stayed out. Ashe stayed out. Two of the three men who had signed the rotation first stayed out.

Halloway abstained, which he did in a way that made clear he had thought about it for three days.

"It's not doubt in the furnace," he said afterward, when Emeric asked, which he did straight out because he had decided some time ago that he would rather ask a question and be told to mind his business. "I've stood next to it. It'll work."

"Then what?"

"I'm the reeve." Halloway rubbed the back of his neck. "The day that venture pays or doesn't, there'll be nineteen households who put grain in and eighteen who didn't, and they'll all be looking at the man who holds the count. If I'm in it, I'm in it with the nineteen, and the eighteen will know it, and every tally I read out for the rest of my life gets read through that." He shrugged. "I'd sooner be poor and believed."

Emeric thought about that for the rest of the afternoon and eventually wrote it in the list under the space, in the form he thought he would need it in later:

Halloway understands the cost of an office better than I do, and nobody taught him. I keep expecting to be the only one in the room who has thought about the second-order consequence, and I keep being wrong about that, and I have not yet stopped expecting it.

Dunnel put in nothing at all.

He negotiated both terms, argued the cap into being, got the seed store walled off in language that would outlive all of them, signed as a witness in his capacity as tally-holder, and then declined to commit a single bushel of his own.

"You wrote the terms," Emeric said.

"I wrote the terms so that the men who wanted in could get in without ruining themselves. That's my job now, apparently." Dunnel closed the tally-book. "It's not my money's job."

"You think it'll fail."

"I think you'll build the furnace and it'll pour and the iron will be better than anything on this road, and I think there's four years between here and the payout and I've no idea at all what's in them." He put his hat on. "You've never once been wrong about a thing you built, my lord. You've been wrong about nearly everything that happened around it. That's not an insult. It's just where I'd put my grain if I had any confidence, and I haven't, so I'm keeping it."

Emeric stood in the doorway of the candle shop and watched him go up the street, and then went back in and read the venture's page over the chandler's shoulder while the ink dried, all of it, the two hundred and forty parts and the four-year term and the cap and the clause about the seed store that Dunnel had insisted be written twice in two different ways so that no clerk could ever claim to have misread it.

It was, he thought, a considerably better document than the one he had brought in.

"That'll be a penny," the chandler said.

"For the whole page?"

"For the copy." The chandler did not look up. "The page is free. The page was always going to be free. You keep paying me pennies for things and I keep writing them down, and one day some clerk in the capital is going to go through this book and conclude the barony of Kaldo ran its entire affairs out of a candle shop for three pence."

"It did," Emeric said. "That's rather the point of it."
