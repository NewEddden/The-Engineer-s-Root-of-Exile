Chapter 96 - Fifty-Five Tons

The last plates of the winter's order went down to the lower water on the steamboat in the week before midwinter, stacked flat in her waist under sacking, and the next morning Bram opened the mill stand and took the rolls out.

It took the whole of a day. The rolls were each as thick as a man's thigh and they had sat in their bearings for more than a year, turning out sheet for Fletcher and strip for the pens, and they did not want to come. Bram rigged a tackle to the beam over the stand and took the top screws off and lifted the upper roll out an inch at a time, with Neta on the chain and Jorrin at the bearing with a bar and a pot of tallow, and nobody spoke except to count. The lower roll came out easier. They laid the pair on trestles by the wall, wrapped, oiled, with their journals capped, because the hull would want them back, and everybody in the mill knew it.

Then Jorrin brought in the others.

He had turned them in the evenings through the autumn, as Bram had said he could, on the second lathe, after the day's work was off it. They were the same length as the old pair and the same thickness, and they were not smooth. Round each roll ran a row of grooves, eight of them, side by side, and every groove was a different shape from the one beside it. The first was nearly square. The last was the rail: a thick rounded head for the wheel to run on, a thin web under it, and a broad flat foot to stand on, cut into the upper roll and the lower roll so that when the two were closed the shapes met and made one. Between the first groove and the last, six others, each a little nearer the rail than the one before, so that a square bar put through the first would come out of the eighth a rail, having been squeezed a little and turned and squeezed again, eight times, and never asked to become anything in one pass that it could not become.

Emeric had drawn the last groove. Jorrin had drawn the six between, over a month, on a slate he wiped every night, because nobody could tell him how much a bar would stand in one pass and he had not wanted to guess. He had heated strips of lead and pressed them in a vice and measured them, and then done it again with iron, and then gone back to the slate.

"I might have it wrong at the fourth," he said, when the rolls were in. "The fourth's the one I'm least sure of."

"Then we'll find out at the fourth," said Bram.

They found out at the second. Neta brought the first bar out of the forge at a yellow heat and Bram fed it into the first groove and it went through, and she caught it on the far side with the tongs and brought it round and fed it back into the second, and it went through that too, and came out with a thin wing of iron standing up along one edge of it the whole of its length, where the groove had been too full and the metal had gone the only way it could.

"A fin," Jorrin said. "That's the second, not the fourth." He sounded almost pleased. "It's too tight. I'll take it out."

He took it out that night, on the lathe, a hair at a time, with the roll turning slow. The second bar went through all eight grooves and came out the far side a rail, the right shape, straight and glowing, fifteen feet of it, and lay on the iron floor of the mill and cooled, and as it cooled it bent.

It bent like a bow. The head was thicker than the foot and it cooled slower, and the foot shrank first and pulled the rail round with it, so that by the time it had gone from red to black it lay on the floor in a long curve with its ends a hand's width off the iron and its middle down hard.

Nobody said anything for some time.

"Hit it," said Bram.

They hit it. They laid the next one on a bed of flat iron plates while it was still dull red and six men went down the length of it with wooden mallets and a straightedge, striking where the straightedge showed light, and it came out of that nearly straight, and nearly was not enough for a thing a wheel was going to run on at the pace of a horse. Neta said, on the third day, while the mallets were going, that they could lay them on the hold bench to cool. The bench would hold anything dead level. It held a billet at a red heat without a mark on it.

"The bench spends stone," Emeric said. "Every hour it holds a rail, it's burning a thing we can't buy more of. A rail can lie on iron and be hit."

"It's a great deal of hitting," said Neta.

"It is."

It was Jorrin who found the answer, and he found it by accident, the way most things in that mill were found. He had a rail cooling on the plates on the fifth day and was called away before the mallets came, and when he came back it had bent the way they all bent, and he stood looking at it with his hands on his hips and then went and got another bar and fed it through the rolls and laid it down on the plates curved the other way. Bent against the bend, by eye, the width of a hand, with the head on the outside of the curve. It cooled. The foot pulled, as the foot always pulled, and it pulled the rail straight.

Not quite. Near enough that the mallets had two blows to strike and not two hundred. He tried it at two hands' width, and it went too far. He tried it at a hand and a half, and it lay down straight on the plates at the end as though it had never been anything else, and the straightedge showed no light under it anywhere along its length.

Bram put the straightedge down on the rail and stood looking at it.

"Write it on the wall," he said.

Jorrin wrote it on the wall, over the plates, in chalk: A hand and a half, head out. Somebody went over it in paint the next week.

Emeric had watched every day of it and said almost nothing. He had known the rail would bend. He had not known which way until he saw it, and he had not known the answer, and he had not needed to, because the answer was a hand and a half and it was on the wall in Jorrin's writing. The first rail was the drawing. Every one after it was the wall.

Ferra came down to the mill on the last night of the winter's first month, late, with her sheet.

It was warm in the mill. It was the only warm place in Kaldo at that hour except the kitchens, and she stood inside the door for a moment with her hands under her arms before she came over to the plates, where he was watching a rail cool. She stood beside him and watched it too. It went from red to dull red to the colour of a plum, and lay down straight.

"Six tons," she said.

"Of bar?"

"Of bar, in a week, off the forge, in a week when the hull wants nothing. Bram's number. He gave it to me twice and I checked it against the charcoal and the coal and it's right." She did not look at the sheet. She had it by heart. "A length is fifteen feet and weighs a hundred and seventy-five pounds by our weight. A mile of line is two rails, so seven hundred and some lengths, so fifty-five tons of iron before you've cast a chair or cut a block. Forty miles to the hill." She turned the sheet over, finally, and held it out, and he took it. "Two thousand two hundred tons. At six a week, if the forge does nothing else, which it won't, a little over seven years."

"Seven," he said.

"Seven." She did not smile. "Everything in this barony comes out at seven. I've started to think it's the only number I know how to make."

He read the sheet. She had done the other column as well, because she always did the other column. Every mile of iron laid was a mile the carts did not go. She had priced the carriage on a mile, off Ilsa's tallies and Saban's rates, against the wages of the gang to lay it and the wagons to run on it, and each mile paid its own wages inside a season and did nothing else. It did not pay for the iron. The iron was the forge's time, and the forge's time was not in her book as coin because it had never been sold to anybody.

"It's not seven," he said. "The forge isn't going to stay one forge."

"I know. You wrote it on the column in the autumn." She looked at him. "I read it upside down. Second forge, and a line under it, the same night as the survey."

"I should have told you."

"You should have told me at the ford," Ferra said. "Not when I found the sheet. I'd rather be told than find. You know that about me. You've known it for ten years."

"I know."

"Then do it." She said it without any heat at all, the way she said a clause, and looked back at the rail. "It's very straight."

"It's Jorrin's."

"I know whose it is. It's on the wall." She was quiet for a moment. "Bram told me about the first plate. It came out wrong in three places and you were pleased, he said, and you didn't say so, and you stood with your hands behind your back like a man at a burial." She took the sheet back out of his hand and folded it. "You're standing like that now."

He looked down. He was.

"Goodnight," said Ferra, and went home, and he stood by the plates until the next rail came through.

Bryda's letter came down the river road in the frost, with the ore tallies and Ilsa's column, which had not stopped growing all winter, because the carts went slower in the frost and the hill did not.

It was about the blocks. Every rail sat in a chair of cast iron, and every chair sat on a block of stone a foot and a half square and a foot deep, one under every joint and one under the middle of every length, and a mile of line wanted better than fourteen hundred of them. Hask's spoil was grey and hard and came out of the hill in slabs, and there were masons at Grayrock who had nothing to do from the first frost to the thaw. Bryda had asked them. She had asked Hask. She had written the price at the foot of the letter: a silver the dozen, cut, dressed square and drilled for the pins, stacked at the pithead, the masons to be paid by the dozen on the day. Below that she had written the number the masons thought they could cut before the thaw, and below that, in the same hand, one line more.

The masons ask whether the iron will come as far as the hill in their lifetimes.

Emeric read that line for some time. Then he wrote back that it would, and that the blocks for the last mile would be theirs as well, and he did not say which lifetimes, because he did not know.

Bram cast the chairs through the rest of the winter, in the foundry, a hundred to a pour, off a pattern he had carved himself in pear wood and would not let anybody else touch. He took four of the barony's old carts out of the yard and knocked their wheels off and fitted them with iron ones cast with a lip round the inside edge, and lettered them one to four in white on their tailboards, so that nobody would put a cart on the road by mistake that could not leave the iron. They sat in the yard under snow with nowhere yet to go.

The rails went into a stack beside them. They were laid in layers with laths between, so that the air could get round them, and counted every evening, and the count went into Ferra's book in her hand under a heading she had ruled for it in the first week and never changed: Iron, laid, none. Iron, rolled, and then the number.

By the thaw the number was seven hundred and forty.

A little over a mile. Emeric walked round the stack on the last evening of the winter with the lamp, while the snow went to water in the yard around him. A mile of line, in a heap, that had taken a whole winter and the whole of a forge that was also needed for everything else. Forty of these to the hill. He knew exactly what that meant and he had known it since the night at the ford, and it was a different thing to walk round it.

Out at the corner of the yard, where the ore carts came in, Bram's stake stood in the thawing ground with its notch cut in the top, where it had stood since the autumn. It had nothing beside it yet. It had the rest of the spring for that.
