Chapter 96 - The Lease

Bryda's letter came down on the coal barge in the third week of October, folded small and sealed with plain wax and no device, and the bargeman carried it inside his shirt and gave it to Ferra at the exchange window as though he were glad to be rid of it.

It was short. Ferra read it first, because it was addressed to the barony and the barony's letters came to her, and then she gave it to Emeric without a word and went back to the column she had been adding, and he read it standing at the end of her trestle.

To the prince of Kaldo, from the holder of Grayrock. Notice, under the seventh line of the terms. A factor acting for a house in the capital had come up the river road in the first week of the month and offered to lease the east working at Grayrock, the one that stood under boards, for twelve years, at eighteen Gold Rand a year, paid each year in advance. The offer had been refused. The holder of Grayrock gave notice of it as the terms required. And her name, in a hand Emeric had seen once, at the foot of a sheet of terms in June: square, heavy, pressed hard into the paper. Bryda Renner.

That was all of it.

He read it again.

He read it a third time for what was not in it, which was most things. She did not say who the factor was, beyond the house he acted for, and the name of the house meant nothing to Emeric. She did not say why she had refused. She did not say what the factor wanted the working for, or what he thought was in it, or whether she had asked him. She did not say whether he had gone away quietly. She did not ask Kaldo for anything at all.

"Eighteen Gold," he said.

"A year," said Ferra, without looking up. "For twelve years. In advance."

"Is that a lot?"

She put her pen down. She got up and went to the shelf behind her and took down one of the oldest books in the exchange, a tall brown one with the old baron's mark on the spine, that had come out of the keep's chest the first winter and never gone back, and opened it at a page she seemed to know without looking, and turned it round on the trestle for him.

It was Kaldo's ore book, from before the plague. Every barge that had come down from Grayrock in the old baron's time, and what was on it, and which working it had come out of, because the old baron's clerk had been a careful man. Ferra put her finger on a column. The east working. Year after year, in the clerk's tidy hand, until a little over ten years back, when it stopped.

"Best year it ever had," she said, and moved her finger. "Three Gold and some Silver. Of ore. Sold to us." She took her finger away. "He's offering six times the best year, every year, for twelve years. For a hole with boards across it that nobody's been down in a decade, where a lamp won't stay lit."

"She said no."

"She said no." Ferra sat down again. "And then she told us, because the terms say she must. Seventh line. Any lease or sale of a working, notice to the other party inside a month." She picked her pen up. "I put it in so that if Grayrock ever sold the pits out from under the ore, we'd know before the barges stopped. Not for this."

"Why the figure, then? She didn't have to give us the figure. The terms say notice. They don't say how much."

Ferra thought about that, with her pen over the column.

"So that we'd know it was worth telling," she said.

He looked at the letter again. And then, because something about it had been sitting wrong since the first reading and he had not been able to say what, he looked at the hand.

The signature was Bryda's. The letter was not. The letter was written in a tall, careful, upright hand, the letters standing up off the line like a row of pikes, every one the same height, and he had seen that hand before, on a tally that had come down with the third ore barge in July, and on the slip tied to Bram's rod. And under Bryda's square heavy signature, small, at the left, where a clerk put his mark to say he had made the copy, there were two letters.

I. R.

"Who's I. R.?" he said.

Ferra did not even look.

"Ilsa," she said. "The daughter. Ilsa Renner. She copied the terms for us in June, at the side table, while you were looking at her mother." She added something at the foot of her column and wrote it in. "She's kept their book since the spring. Every tally that's come down the river has been hers."

"You knew that."

"I knew it the first tally." She did look up then, briefly. "It's a good hand. She rules her own lines." It was, from Ferra, a very great deal of praise, and she seemed to hear it, and went back to her column.



Saban came in from his autumn round that same afternoon, wet to the knees and smelling of other people's horses, and sat down on the bench by the door with his hat in his hands, turning it, which meant he had priced something on the road and was about to tell them what it cost.

He gave them the coast first. Spirit holding its price. Soap up a little. A man in the second coast town who wanted Kaldo's soap so badly he had tried to buy the recipe from a carter, and had been sold, by the carter, a recipe for a pudding. Then the roads, and who was on them, and who was not.

Then the halls.

"They're still talking about Paxton," he said.

"Still?"

"Halls don't stop. They change the words." He turned his hat. "In the summer it was the coward who stood at the back. Now it's the man who marched and ran. Same man. Same day. Better story." He looked at the hat. "Wayford's hall tells it best."

"Sethe."

"Sethe tells it at his own table, to anybody who's eating. And then he says a thing after it, every time, the same thing, so that I heard it from three men who'd heard it from him." Saban put the hat on his knee. "He says that when the crown gets round to the south, and it will, Milford will want friends who can write to it."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That's kind of him," said Ferra, to her column.

"It's a leash," said Saban. "That's what it is. The law says a man who takes the field in a private war forfeits his land. Paxton took the field. Everybody saw him do it. He stood at the back, but he stood there in his colours, and the law doesn't care where in the field." He shrugged. "So Paxton's land is the crown's the day the crown hears. Unless somebody the crown listens to tells it a better story first. And Sethe is telling every hall in the south that he's the man the crown listens to."

"Paxton knows that."

"Paxton knows exactly that. It's why he answers Sethe's letters about the weather."

Emeric sat on the edge of the trestle and looked at the floor. He thought about Paxton in the gate yard in August, with his square face and his hands behind his back, saying that a man with no wall does what the man with the army asks. Paxton had no wall still. He had a lot of cows and a letter in Emeric's locked drawer, and a neighbour who held his land on a string and told the whole south so over supper.

He did not say anything. But Ferra, adding, had looked up once while Saban was talking, and once again after, and both times she had looked at him and not at Saban.



Armand came down to the water gate on the last Saturday of the month, because he had been told that it worked, and had been waiting since August to be shown.

He came slowly. He had a stick now, which he had begun to use in the summer and did not mention, and Arthur walked beside him carrying a three-legged stool from the long room, and they stopped at every corner going down through the town so that Armand could stand and look at something, a door or a gutter or a dog, for exactly as long as it took him to get his breath back, which he did not mention either. By the time they reached the bridge foot and the water gate it was the middle of the afternoon, and Ambrose had attached himself to them in the market square, and Osric was on the gate.

Tess had the board. She had asked for it, as she had asked for it every time anybody tested anything, and Arthur ran.

Emeric had not walked a mage past one of the stones. He had said so to Rylan, and written it on the card as a guess. Ambrose went first, because Ambrose wanted to, and because Ambrose had been told by Osric that the card had a line on it about mages, and could not bear not to know what his own line looked like. He walked along the inside of the water gate at a stroll, past the place in the masonry where the stone lay behind the face of the wall, and turned round at the far end, pleased with himself, and waited.

Arthur came back from the line room at a run.

"It hit the pin," he said. "It hit the pin so hard Tess said a word."

"What word?" said Ambrose, delighted.

"She said not to tell you."

Osric, at the gate with his crossbow on his shoulder, had been watching all this with the expression of a man who has been on gate duty since dawn and has finally been sent some entertainment.

"A cup," he said, "says you can't get past it quiet."

"Quiet?"

"So the needle doesn't go over. Like it's a cat going by. The prince says a man would have to pour out what's in him, to get under the line." Osric shrugged. "You're a mage. Pour."

Emeric opened his mouth to say that nobody was going to pour anything, and Ambrose had already taken his coat off.

He was a lightning mage. What was in him did not go out as a flame or a gust; it went out in a snap, a burst, the thing he had spent his life wishing would run and never had. He went down the river stair to the bottom step and squatted at the edge of the water, and put both his hands flat on the surface of the river, and let go.

There was a crack like a whip. A web of thin blue light ran out from his hands across the water, a yard every way, and was gone, and a little fish came up white and stunned on the surface and floated off downstream. Ambrose let go again. Another crack, and another. Five, six. His back went stiff. On the seventh his hands came off the water by themselves and he sat down on the bottom step, quite suddenly, as if somebody had kicked the step out from under him, and stayed there with his elbows on his knees and his face the colour of ash.

Emeric was down the stair to him by then, and Osric behind him, all the entertainment gone out of Osric's face.

"I'm fine," said Ambrose. His voice was not fine. "Walk me past it."

"No."

"Walk me past it. I paid for it."

They walked him past it between them, one under each arm, because he would not sit still until they did, with his feet going where they liked. Arthur ran. He came back slower this time, and looked at Ambrose with something careful in his face.

"It went back from the pin," he said. "A little. It's still nearly all the way over. Tess says it's still more than Ward."

Ambrose took that in. Then he put his head down on his knees, on the walk where they had set him, and laughed, weakly, into his own trousers, for some time.

Armand had watched the whole of it from his stool at the head of the stair, with both hands on his stick and nothing on his face at all.

"I could have told you that," he said, when Ambrose had stopped laughing, "for nothing."

"Tell me now," said Ambrose, into his knees.

"Capacity is blood." Armand said it the way he said everything he had said to a room of students many times before, plainly, so that the plainness would stick. "Your body knows how much of it it carries. It has known since you were small. It's set to it, the way a house is set to its fire. Let a little out for a spell and the body takes it back from the air in an hour and never minds. Let a great deal out at once, and your body thinks you are bleeding to death, and does what a body does when it thinks that. It sits you down." He looked at Ambrose. "And the more you carry, the further you have to fall to get under a line set for a child. You fell a fifth of the way and you're on the floor. Another fifth, and you'd be in the river."

"So nobody gets past it," said Osric.

Armand thought about it, honestly, as he thought about everything.

"A hedge mage," he said. "One of the small ones, with very little in him to start with. If he spent years learning to sit empty, a little lower every day, until his body stopped minding. Perhaps. For a few minutes." He moved his stick. "I've never met one. I've never heard of one. I can't think why anybody would want to be one. And he would come along your wall like a drunk man, Osric, and you would see him with your own eyes."

"The rest can't do it at all," Emeric said. Everyone looked at him. "A commoner can't move his own at all. He doesn't know it's there. A knight's only works inside him. The only people who can pour any out are the ones with the most to pour." He looked at Ambrose on the walk. "And it sits them down."

"It sits them down," said Ambrose, who had lifted his head. "I want to try again."

"No," said Armand and Osric, together.

Ambrose paid the cup that evening, at the inn on the market square, and two more after it, and told the story himself, very well, with the fish.



Before they went back up through the town, Armand asked to see it.

The stone itself. Emeric took him to the place in the water gate's masonry, a little above the height of a man's knee, where there was nothing on the face of the wall at all but the old dressed stone, grey and green with river damp, exactly like the stone on either side of it. Vesk had closed it over so well that Emeric had to measure from the corner with his hand to be sure of the place.

"Here," he said.

Armand gave Arthur his stick, and put his hand flat on the wall where Emeric showed him, and closed his eyes.

He stood like that for some time. Emeric felt him do it, faintly: the same thing he had felt in Armand's room in July, a door opened somewhere in a house and the draught not yet arrived. A little of the sense, through the palm, into the wall.

Armand opened his eyes.

"It's asleep," he said.

"Yes."

"A watch stone is never asleep." He kept his hand where it was. "It can't be. It's open all the time, that's what the sign on its face does to it, it holds it open, and it spends while it's open, every hour, whether anything comes near it or not." He took his hand away and looked at it, as though it might have something written on it. "This is shut. Shut tight. It's not spending anything at all. And there's nothing on the face of it. And when the boy walked past it just now it woke." He looked at Emeric. "That isn't showing me how."

"It's showing you it works."

Armand looked at him a long while. Then he laughed. It was a real laugh, sudden and pleased, and it cost him; he had to put his hand back on the wall for a different reason and stand with his head down until his breath came back, and Arthur moved close and did not touch him, which Sela had taught him.

"You were always like that," Armand said, when he could. "At twelve. You'd answer the question exactly and not the one I meant." He took his stick back from Arthur. Then, more quietly, so that Arthur, who was listening with his whole body, would have to strain: "You haven't tried the other thing."

"No."

Armand nodded slowly. He looked at the stone in the wall that nobody could see.

"Good," he said.



That night, behind the shutters, with the cloak over them, he lit the candle without his hand.

He put it on the stool on the far side of the room, against the wall, and sat on the edge of his bed with his hands flat on his knees and looked at it. He had done it once by accident, the winter before last, and once in the room in March, where everything came easier, and never outside it since. Fire was his. It was cheap. But fire without a hand to point it was fire without a road, and every time he had tried it the flame had come out of him at about the strength of a breath, and died in the air halfway, and left him a headache.

Line of sight. That was the road, if there was no hand. He looked at the wick and nothing else.

On the third try it caught.

A small blue bead of light at the black tip of the wick, the size of a pea, there and not there, and then the wick took it and it stood up yellow, a whole candle flame, across the width of his room from his hands, which had not moved off his knees. He sat and looked at it burning. Then the headache came, a nail behind one eye, as it always did, and he lay back on the bed with his boots on and let it.

The dog was watching him.

It was the guardhouse dog, the big brindled one of no particular kind, which had walked along the river stretch on a rope in September and put its nose on the top step of the river stair and rung the board's bell for nothing. Since the afternoon Emeric had re-cut the stone so that it would not ring for it, it had attached itself to him. Nobody knew why. The garrison said it was because he was the only man in Kaldo who had never called it by any of its four names. It lay across his door most nights now, and tonight, because it was cold, it had come in and lain down on the hearth by the candle's stool, and was watching him with its chin on its paws.

He looked at it for some time.

Then he sat up, and looked across the room at the wood basket by the hearth, which was full, and which he had carried up the stair that evening and knew the weight of. About the dog's weight. A little more. He took it off the floor. Not with his hands; with the other thing, the one that was his and nobody's, pointing at it with two fingers. The basket rose a hand's width off the boards and hung there, and it cost him about what lifting a full basket of wood with one hand would have cost him, and he set it down again.

Then the dog.

He pointed at it with the same two fingers and lifted, the same, a hand's width.

It was like lifting the basket with a man standing in it. The dog came up off the hearth by perhaps a finger's breadth, all four feet off the boards and its legs stiff, and something in it pushed back against him. It was not the dog's weight. He knew the dog's weight now, he had just lifted it in firewood. This was something else, on top of the weight, an unwillingness, as if the dog were leaning on him from the inside. The dog did not like it. It whined, high and puzzled, and looked at him with its ears flat, and he put it down at once.

It stood on the hearth and shook itself all over, as if it had come out of the river.

He sat on the edge of the bed and looked at it.

He had read it somewhere in his first year, in the notebook, in Armand's hand: that living things resisted. That a mage could pull a stone across a room and not pull a man, and the stronger the man the less. He had read it and believed it and never once tried it, because there had never been anyone he could try it on.

The board in the line room read how much a thing carried. The dog carried less than a boy, though it weighed more. And the dog had pushed back against him by more than its weight, and the basket by nothing at all. It was the same thing. It had to be the same thing. What the stones felt when a body came into their field was what had leaned on him from inside the dog. The board was measuring, every night, how hard every man on the wall would push back against him if he tried to lift him.

A man harder than the dog. Ward or Rylan like a wall. A boy like Finn, less than a man.

He got up and found his book and wrote it down, by the light of the candle he had lit without his hands, and dated it, and closed the book.

The dog was still standing on the hearth looking at him reproachfully. He went to the table, where the end of his supper was, a heel of bread and the rind of a cheese, and held them out. It considered him for a while. Then it came across and took them both, very gently, and went back to the hearth, and lay down with its back to him.
