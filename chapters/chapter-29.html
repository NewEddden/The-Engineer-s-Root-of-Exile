Chapter 29 - The Trove

The frost came in the second week of December and did not go away again.

It took the works first. Nothing with mortar in it could be built in that frost, so nothing was, and the stone span stayed where it was, waiting for spring and twenty men. The trough over the gap ran on under a skin of ice that thickened at the edges every night and was broken off every morning by a boy with a pole, who was paid in bread for it and regarded it as the best work in the barony. The water still came out of the tap at the top of the lower ward, slower, and so cold it hurt the teeth.

Then the snow came, and took everything else.

For the first time since April there was nothing for him to do that could not wait until it thawed. The petitions slowed to a handful a day. The roads shut. Across the east bridge the old works had snow on its footings and the wheel locked in ice. Bram took the forge down to one fire, and Ferra's book went from a ledger to a list of what was owed and when, and the town went indoors and stayed there, and so did he.



He counted what he had.

He had not done it properly since the capital. The chest his father had given him had been opened once, in his room in the keep, in April, for about as long as it took to see that it was full and to shut it again. He opened it now on the floor of the same room with the shutters closed and the lamp lit, and took the crystals out one at a time, and laid them on a cloth in rows by element.

Fire, fourteen. Water, nine. Air, eight. Earth, seven. Thirty-eight uncut crystals, the largest the size of a hen's egg and the smallest no bigger than a thumbnail, none of them marked, none of them recorded anywhere in the kingdom except in his own head and now on the back of a page in Armand's notebook. He counted them twice and got the same answer both times, which pleased him more than it should have.

The two he had promised Bram's furnace in July were in there, a fire crystal and an air crystal, and he put them to one side by themselves. He looked at the rest. Every one of them was something he was going to need, for something he had not built yet, and could not buy again at any price if he spent it now.

He put them back in the order he had taken them out.

The trunk was quicker. Varek's coat, which he had worn every day since the frost and which deserved every word the old soldier had not said about it. Armand's notebook, its back pages full of his own hand now, Kaldo's problems and Kaldo's counts and the list of the unrecorded dead. The stylus, in its case. The practice slate, three seasons of dry strokes on it, the newest ones straight. The case with Corbin's nine stones in it, which had done nothing in the summer and did nothing now. His mother's was in his pocket, where it always was, soft at the edges from handling.

And the pouch. He tipped it out onto the cloth and counted that too.

It did not take long. In April the pouch had been heavy in the hand. It was not now, and Ferra had a month written at the foot of a page somewhere, face down, which he had not asked to see.

He put the coins back and tied the pouch, and sat on the floor among his possessions with the lamp burning down, taking a kind of stock he had not taken before. Fifty Gold, most of it spent. Thirty-eight crystals he would not sell. Ten stones, one of which worked. A coat. A notebook. A stylus. A town of two thousand eight hundred and seventy-six people who had, at last count, started to come up the hill with their business instead of waiting for him to go down it.

It was not nothing. He went to bed.



With the works frozen he went into the room most afternoons, with Ward in the chair by the door, because Ferra's rules said nobody went in alone and never at night, and he had never yet found a reason good enough to break them.

The drills had not changed much since the summer. The coin, lifted and held. The candle, bent and held. The stylus, dry, across the slate, until the stroke came out the same every time. The ink had been in them since September, and in the room he could wrap a drop and push it and hold it a hundred times in an afternoon without spending more than an afternoon. It got easier, a little.

At night, behind the shutters, with Ward's cloak hung over the gap where they did not meet, he did the one thing the room could not teach him, because the room could not teach him what his own body weighed.

He took the weight off his feet and hung a hand's width above the floor. That was easy now. Then, with the hover held, he put his hand out to the candle and bent the flame toward him, two elements side by side, a ring for each, and held both, and counted.

On the last night of the frost's first week he held them both to forty.

At thirty-one he felt it again: the small shove against his palm when he pushed the flame away from him, the same shove he had felt and written down in the summer and had not tried since. Hot gas one way, the hand the other. Hanging off the floor as he was, there was nothing holding him in place sideways at all. If he pushed harder, it would move him.

He did not push harder.

He came down at forty, and sat on the edge of the bed with a headache arriving, and looked at the candle. Alone, at night, in a stone room, with nobody who knew what to do if a man set himself on fire in the air: that was exactly the wrong way to find out. He wrote two words under the summer's question in the back of the notebook, Not yet, and pinched the candle out.

Ward put him on the ground in the snow the next morning, and the morning after that, and every morning of the frost. He still lost every time. He was beginning to lose more slowly.



The school lost half its children to the snow.

It had been sixty-two at the opening and nearer seventy by November. In the second week of December it was thirty, and on the worst mornings fewer. The dock quarter's children had the bridge to cross in the wind off the river, and the west farms' had a mile of drifted lane, and the ones who did come up the street came up it blue at the lips and sat in a room built for a trading crowd in August, under high windows, with eighty-year-old trusses holding up a roof that creaked exactly as the carpenter had promised.

Sela fixed it without asking anybody.

There was an iron stove in one of the houses the barony held on the lane behind the north well, a big black box of riveted iron plate that some richer family had bought before the war and nobody had used since the plague. Bram had passed it in the autumn with the grates on his cart and left it standing, because it was a good stove and he did not break good things for scrap. She found it. She had the husbands of two of the north-well women carry it down the hill on a handcart in the snow, and Bram ran its pipe out through one of the high windows on her instruction, and grumbled about it the whole time. Then she had the children bring a stick of wood each, every morning, as the price of a place near it.

The roll did not go back up. But the thirty who came stayed until noon, and the bread went further, and the room smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke instead of nothing.

He went down most mornings and stood across the lane, as he had since September. He had worked out where to stand: at the corner where the lane met the bridge road, where he could see the door and the step and the bread going in, and could be round the corner and out of sight in one step if she looked up.

She looked up on the third Tuesday of December.

He was round the corner before he had decided to move. He stood there with his back against the wall of the end house and the snow coming down past his face, and felt like a boy hiding from a tutor, and did not move.

Footsteps came across the lane. Sela stopped at the corner, in her shawl, with the empty bread basket on her hip.

"You can stop that."

He looked at her.

"Round the corner. Every morning. Since September." She did not raise her voice. "Half the lane's seen you."

"I thought you'd sooner not have to look at me."

"I've been looking at you since September. Round corners." She shifted the basket. Snow had settled on the shawl across her shoulders and she did not brush it off. "It was the wrong place. The wrong hour. It wasn't your hand." She said it the way she said the price of a bar of soap: once, flat, not to be argued with. "I've been angry at you since the summer. I'm not, now. I'm tired of it, and it wasn't yours to begin with."

He did not argue. He did not believe her either. He could see, quite clearly, the boy on the gate in April with his bad shave, going red at a prince's eyes, and a fortnight on a door in the east corridor, and a name nobody had asked. It had been his failure, whatever she said, if not his hand. But it was her son, and it was hers to say, and he did not say anything.

"My eldest's a different thing," Sela said.

The snow came down between them.

"The crown came for him, when they were choosing men, and the crown took him, and the crown never once said where he went or how, or if there was anything left to bury." She looked at his eyes, the crown's, as she had at her counter in July. "I don't forgive that. I won't. I want you to know which is which."

"I know which is which," he said.

She nodded, once, as if a sum had come out the way she had expected, and went back across the lane to the door and the children and the stove.

He stayed at the corner a little longer, in plain sight. Then he went up the hill.



On the last night of the year Ferra closed the year's books, and he kept her company while she did it, because she had asked him to be there for the totals and then had not reached them, and outside the snow had come on again so thickly that the lane behind the forge had disappeared.

Bram had gone to sleep in his chair by the stove an hour ago with his boots on and his hands folded on his stomach, and was snoring in a slow, considered way, as he did most things.

The table was covered. The shop's columns, the ash, the kiln, the school's bread and the households' pay, the ward's crates, the trough and the pipe and the cisterns, the iron, the wages, every line of the year that had gone through her hands since she had taken Kaldo's books at the end of the summer. She went down each column in turn, carrying each total to the sheet for the year, and checked it, and checked it again, and wrote it in.

He had his own copy of two of the columns in front of him, and was doing them the long way, in his head, to see whether he got the same answer as she did, and somewhere in the middle of the ash he found he was humming.

He stopped. She had looked up.

She looked at him, and said nothing about it, and looked down again.

A strand of red had come loose from where she had pinned her hair up for the work, and kept falling forward across her face as she bent over the page. She pushed it back with the end of her pen, without looking up. A column later it fell again, and she pushed it back again. She did it six times in an hour.

He did not say anything. There was nothing in it to say anything about. He went back to the ash, and got the same answer as she did, a little after she had.

The stove ticked. Bram snored. The snow went on.

A little before midnight she drew a line under the last total, and wrote the year beneath it, and blotted it, and closed the book.

"There," she said. "That's a year."

At the door the latch had stuck with the cold. It lifted halfway and stopped, and he was still forcing it when she came over and took his wrist. "You'll have it off the door." She knocked the bar up with the heel of her other hand, and the door came open on the snow, and she let go of him and went back to her table.

He went home through the snow.

It was up to his shins in the lane and over his boots on the hill, and coming down so thick that the lamps of the gate yard were only a brightness in it until he was nearly at the gate. Ward was in the gate passage with his back to the wall, where he had evidently been waiting.

Emeric was halfway across the yard before he noticed that his hands had stopped feeling like hands somewhere around the canal ring, and that he had not felt cold once, the whole way up.
