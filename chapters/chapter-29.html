Chapter 29 - The Books

Ferra came to the long room on the tenth day with three books and did not sit down, which he had learned meant she intended to be finished quickly and to have it hurt while it happened.

"The eleven sacks are gone," she said.

"I know where they went."

"I want it said properly, because you will otherwise carry it as a rounding." She put the first book down. "Eleven sacks came out of a burning granary because two men who are now in that church went in for them. Nine and a half of those eleven went up the Wayford road in the hands of three hundred and forty men, forty at a time, as two days of bread. What is left in this barony out of a harvest of four hundred acres is a sack and a half of wheat, a seed bin I am not counting, and whatever is in the houses."

"And in the houses."

"Thirty-seven households have between four days and three weeks in them, and the four hundred who came here in the spring have nothing at all, because a man who arrives in April on a promise of eight parts in twenty does not arrive with a winter behind him."

Emeric had it in front of him already. He had done the arithmetic on the sixth day, twice, and had been hoping in a way he did not care to look at directly that she would come up with a different answer.

"Give me the date," he said.

"The first household with nothing in it is somewhere in the last week of September. The bulk of it is the first fortnight of November. By the middle of December there is no version of this where anybody in Kaldo is eating out of Kaldo." She closed the book. "That is not the worst line in these three books and I would rather you heard the worst one from me than found it."

"Say it."

"Your four parts are sold. Fixed price, one third taken in advance in June, and the advance is in the foundry, in brick, in a third furnace standing cold for people who are not born yet." She said it without any edge on it, which was worse. "You owe Baron Paxton of Milford four parts in twenty of a harvest that does not exist, and he is the only baron on that plain who has not lost a man, a coin, or an hour."

Paxton's rider came on the twelfth day.

He carried a letter which Emeric read twice standing up and then a third time sitting down, and it contained three sentences about the weather on the Milford road, one about the health of Paxton's wife, and nothing whatsoever about a contract, a delivery, an advance, or a grain harvest.

"That's a demand," Ferra said.

"That's better than a demand. A demand I could answer." Emeric put it on the table. "He's telling me he knows, and that he isn't going to say it, and that the not saying it is his to spend whenever he chooses. I've got a hundred and fifty men who stood on a shoulder of ground for nine hours and a letter about his wife's chest."

"Do you write back?"

"I write back about his wife's chest."

The steel was the thing he had been wrong about in the direction he had not expected.

He had spent the sixth and seventh days telling himself, in the part of his head that did arithmetic while the rest of it was elsewhere, that there was one clean line in the whole disaster: five hundred and twenty men had come up his hill and four hundred and ninety of them had gone away or been buried, and every scrap of iron they had brought with them was stacked in the lower ward.

Bram priced it in an afternoon and came up to the long room himself rather than send word.

"It's quilt," he said.

"Say the whole of it."

"Two hundred and fifty men off a mining hill in August. About one in five in mail, and half of that mail is older than me and has been mended by men who mend carts. Leather. Quilt. Caps. Spearheads, and the spearheads are the only honest metal in the pile." Bram put a scrap of it on the table, a mail skirt with a rust bloom through it. "There's a hundred-odd mail shirts, four decent Wayford harnesses off the four they left, about nine hundredweight of spearheads and axe heads, and one suit of enamelled plate off a dead baron that's the best steel that's ever come into this town including ours."

"And in feedstock."

"Six weeks of charges for one furnace. Maybe seven." Bram shrugged. "I'd not sneeze at it. It's not what you've been telling yourself since Tuesday."

"No."

"You costed a bolt through a Grayrock harness in March off a sample Saban brought you." Bram was not being unkind about it and that was somehow the trouble. "There wasn't a harness on that hill. You've been wrong about the same fact twice now in two directions and both times it was because you had a piece of Grayrock plate on a table and never asked how many men on that hill would ever get to stand inside one."

Renner's armour went into the middle furnace on the fifteenth day and Emeric watched it go, and did not make a speech about the king's steel or anybody else's, and afterward could not have said what he had been thinking about while it went grey and then orange.

Saban came on the sixteenth.

"There's no ore," he said, before he had sat down. "I'll not spend your morning on it. Grayrock's got no baron, no steward answering, and two hundred and fifty men who went up that hill and a hundred and forty-nine who didn't come off it, and the workings are standing empty because there's nobody on the hill who can say who owns what's under it. There'll not be a cart down that river before the spring and I'd not swear to the spring."

"And Duvrenne."

"Duvrenne has ore and Duvrenne is eleven days by road and six by water if you can get a barge onto the western arm, which you can, because I've looked, which is the one piece of good news I've carried into this room in a year." He sat down. "It's twice the price at the far end and it's four times the carriage and it wants paying for before it moves, because a Duvrenne factor has never heard of you and has heard of Grayrock. I can open the line. I cannot open it on your credit and I'll not open it on mine."

"How much before it moves."

Saban told him, and Ferra wrote it down, and the number was about eleven times what was in the box.

The forward-selling took nine days and failed in three different ways, and Emeric made himself write down which way each one failed.

He offered a season of the foundry's output at a discount against payment now. He offered it to a Wayford ironmonger who had bought from Bram for eleven years, and the man wrote back that his cousin's boy had come home from the Kaldo road on a hurdle and that he would trade with Kaldo again in a year and not before, which Emeric read as the only honest refusal of the three.

He offered it to two houses in the capital who dealt in contracts of that kind, and the first did not answer, and the second answered in four lines that came down to the observation that a barony which had recently been assaulted by three of its neighbours was not a barony against which one bought a year of future iron.

He offered it to Paxton, because Paxton had money and a grudge he had not yet cashed, and Paxton wrote back about the harvest on the Milford grass and did not mention it.

"That's the whole of the market," Ferra said. "That is not a run of bad luck. There are four buyers of iron within eleven days of this town and you have fought two of them, you owe the third, and the fourth has read a letter about you."

"What letter."

"I do not know that there is a letter. I know that a man in the capital who has never met you declined in four lines and did not ask a single question about the iron." She looked up. "You will want to build something out of that and I would rather you did not, because I have nothing to put under it."

Ward said the thing about the crystals on the nineteenth evening, and he said it because Emeric brought it up.

That was the part Emeric thought about for a long while afterward, that he had gone looking for it. He had come down to the armoury with a lamp at about eleven at night to look at the four housings on their racks, which he had told himself was because he wanted to check the breaker springs after Declan's had thrown, and it was not.

Seven bins. Six feet by four by five, stone floor, timber walls, slate roof, and a Stasis working in the frame of each lid, and the whole barony's harvest sitting where no single fire and no single roof and no single bad week could empty it. He had said that to Dunnel in July standing next to the one he had built. He had said the answer to the granary out loud, in daylight, three weeks before the granary burned, and had not built it because he did not understand a working he had read off a wall at eight years old.

There were four workings in that room, doing nothing at all, eleven months of the year.

"No," Ward said, from the doorway.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've come down here at eleven at night with a lamp to look at four stones you've no reason to look at, and I've been waiting on this since the morning of the fire." Ward came in and did not raise his voice. "You want it said in your own words or mine?"

"Mine."

"Then say them."

Emeric stood there for a moment. "Nothing comes out of the four suits. Not in a bad month, not in a bad week, not if I'm standing in the yard with the thing half built and one stone short and a good reason. Not ever."

"And the rest of it."

"That a suit a man knows can be taken apart for parts isn't a suit. And that a store I've promised not to open gets opened, because I'll be the one deciding and I'll have a reason and the reason will be good." He put the lamp down. "I asked you and Bram in July to hold me to a thing I said when it was easy."

"Aye. And it's a fortnight since forty men walked off that wall and every one of them still had a mother, and Declan's is on that rack with a broken coupling because a stone that got cut off is a stone you've still got." Ward looked at the four housings. "It's a good reason, what you've got. It's the best reason anybody's ever going to bring in here. That's the whole of why I'm standing in this door."

"You could have let me look and said nothing."

"I could have. Then in five years you'd be a man who once took a stone out of a suit in a hard November." Ward went to the door. "Go to bed, Your Highness."

Maren came on the twenty-second of September, which was two months earlier than he had told her to, and she came with eleven households behind her and a proposal written out in a hand that was not hers.

Emeric had it in front of him and read the whole thing before he said anything, because he had promised in a field in June that he would write the arithmetic for it honestly whichever way it cut.

Eight parts in twenty, recut against households and not against days worked, with a floor under any household of four or fewer, for one season only, reviewed at the following harvest.

"There's no harvest to cut it out of," he said.

"There's next year's."

"There's next year's, and next year's is why you've come in September and not in November." He put the page down. "Say it properly. You want it settled before anybody's hungry and you know exactly how few weeks there are between now and the first house with nothing in it, and you're right, and I'd rather you told me that than let me work it out."

"I've eleven households and I want it voted while people can still think," Maren said. "You told me a thing decided in March is decided by whoever's most frightened. It's not March. It's the twenty-second of September and I've been counting the same weeks you have."

"And the twenty-six families? The ones your son says the machine took it off? They're the dilution. Your recut takes eight parts and spreads them over households instead of days, and every one of those twenty-six is a household."

"I know what it does. That's why I brought it in September." Maren did not look away. "It comes out for them and against my boys, and my boys have signed it, and I'd like you to write the arithmetic under it in your own hand and put your name on the honesty of it and not on the proposal, because it isn't yours and I don't want it to be."

He wrote it that night. Both columns, what it gave and what it took, with the twenty-six named and his own four parts shown separately at the bottom so that nobody could say the barony had been quiet about its own share.

Then he sat with the pen for some while, because a recut of eight parts in twenty of next year's harvest was a redistribution of a thing that did not exist, out of a barony that had a sack and a half of wheat and a seed bin at the edge of dry and a first empty house due in about five weeks.

Ferra came in at some point after midnight and stood on the other side of the table.

"You are going to have to borrow," she said.

"I know."

"I would like to be the one who says the number out loud rather than have you arrive at it privately and hand it to me finished, because I have watched you do that twice this year." She put her own sheet down. "Food for two thousand two hundred people from November to the cut. Seed we have and I thank Dunnel for it. Ore out of Duvrenne, paid before it moves, or the foundry is three cold furnaces and a stick on a wall. Wages for a garrison and a foundry through a winter in which neither of them earns a penny. And the second granary, which you will want, and which I have put at the bottom because it is the only line on this page that is not a thing somebody eats."

"Total."

She told him.

Emeric looked at it for a long time. It was a larger number than the entire assessed value of the barony of Kaldo had been in the ledger he had opened on his fourth night here, and he had built most of the difference himself, in a year, out of a pouch of nine stones and other people's labour.

"Who lends that," he said.

"Four houses in the kingdom. Two will not lend to a barony without a charter of its own. One is the crown's own and is therefore your brother's." Ferra's mouth did something unpleasant. "The fourth is Ducatis."

"Say what you know about Ducatis."

"That they lend to people nobody else will lend to. That they have never once, in ninety years, been the reason a debtor failed, which is a thing they say about themselves and which appears to be true." She closed the book. "And that they do not lend against a promise. They lend against a thing, and they put a man of their own in the room with the books for as long as the debt is live, and the man does not go home when you would like him to."
