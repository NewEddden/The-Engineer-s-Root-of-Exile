Chapter 29 - Three Machines, Three Failures, One Week

The buildings all worked.

That was the thing nobody expected and nobody remarked on, and it was worth remarking on, because a foundry, a pump-house, a drying shed and eleven sealed bins had gone up in one season built by people who had mostly never built anything. The roofs held. The wheel turned. The bins were dry.

What failed that week was every single working inside them.

The threshing drum went in on the Monday.

Wooden beater bars in an oak body, iron pins set in a pattern Bram had argued about for three days, belt-driven off the mill wheel that had been turning in that spot since before the war. One plain water stone at the axle, set to hold a fixed speed, because a fixed speed is what a machine is for.

It threshed about four times what a floor crew with flails could do and it did it badly in two directions at once.

The dry sheaves went through too hard and cracked, and cracked grain does not keep and does not sell and turns into pig feed at a quarter of the price. The damp ones came out the far end still holding half their heads, and had to be pulled out by hand and sent round again, which choked the whole line.

Emeric stood in the mill yard on the Monday afternoon watching a machine be wrong in both directions and could not see it, and the reason he could not see it was that he was looking at the drum.

Farris had been feeding it all morning. He fed it the way he had fed a hand-flail in a cold barn in February, which was steadily, and Emeric had watched him do that too and had thought nothing about it either time.

He watched him for half a morning on the Tuesday before he understood what he was looking at.

Farris's arm was slowing down. Not on a rule. Not on a count. He picked up a sheaf, and somewhere between the pile and the drum his arm decided what that sheaf was, and a wet one went in slower and a dry one went in lighter, and he had been doing it since Monday and had no idea he was doing it and could not have told anybody how.

The correction was already in the yard. It was attached to a person and it had been there the whole time.

Emeric asked him about it, and Farris said that the wet ones want a bit more time, and looked mildly worried about having been asked, in case he had been doing it wrong.

The fix took two days and it was the rule out of the frozen courtyard with a name and a face on it at last. A second stone bedded against the axle housing, doing no work at all except straining, reading how hard the drum was having to turn. A wet sheaf loads the drum. A dry one does not. And the draw off that second stone went straight to the driver.

The machine rode the load the way the man's arm had ridden it.

Emeric wrote the principle out properly that evening, in general terms rather than as a note about a drum, because he could already see three other places it went, and one of them was a furnace.

And the redundancy was the same as everything else on the site. Pull the stone and the drum still turned off the mill wheel at a plain fixed speed. Slower, more cracked grain, hand-sorting of the wet sheaves and a longer week. The crop still comes in.

The drying shed nearly killed somebody on the Wednesday.

The shed itself had gone up in the spring and had been sitting there all summer doing its job on draft alone. The stone went in for the harvest, in the brick channel under the slatted floor, because draft alone is fine in August and useless in a wet September.

One fire stone. Bedded at a fixed heat. With no stopping condition on it anywhere, because Emeric had written the drum's conditional logic and the spreader's route working and had put the shed in as the simple one.

The near batch scorched some time after midday. Somebody smelled it and came out, and the floor slats above the channel were black and going, and there was a wind up off the water that afternoon doing exactly what it had been sited to do, and the bins stood forty feet downwind of it.

Eleven men put it out with river water and wet sacking in about twenty minutes and nobody was hurt and the barony lost a quarter of one batch of barley.

That was not what made Emeric sit down on the bank afterward.

He went and read the stone. He did it before he did anything else, before he looked at the floor or spoke to anyone, with his hands shaking slightly in a way he hoped was from the running.

It had been holding at its set point for six hours. A fixed heat in a sealed brick channel with nothing drawing the excess off is not a fixed anything. The channel had been climbing all morning and the stone had been working harder every hour to hold a number against a rising floor, and the draw had gone up and kept going up, and it was sitting at something he could put a figure to.

He had read Armand's page in February. He had done the demonstration himself in a frozen field with Ward thirty feet away.

A stone that lets go in a brick channel under a slatted floor, in a shed, in a yard with eleven men in it and a granary downwind, does not scorch a batch of barley.

He sat on the bank for a while.

The fix went in on the Friday and it was the same fix as the drum wearing different clothes. A splinter set in the exhaust path, straining against the moisture coming off the grain, because wet air and dry air do not resist the same. A target mark. And a cutoff, hard, above the mark, which stopped the stone whether or not the grain was done.

And the channel drew a plain draft with or without any stone in it at all, the way it had all summer, so a wood fire under it and a man's judgment still dried a batch.

He added a line to the standing rules that night. Nothing holds a number in this barony without something watching what it is costing to hold it.

The seed spreader was the one he took worst and it was the one that did the least damage.

A cart hopper over a spinning brass disc, one plain stone with a route working cut into it, timing the feed so the disc got a constant measure whether the ox was walking fast or slow. He was rather pleased with it. It was the neatest thing he had built all year.

It laid the autumn field in bands. Visible from the slope. Heavy grain thrown wide by the disc and light grain and chaff dropping short, over and over, in stripes, down the whole length of a division that had been surveyed to a common foot in March by men who had walked up a hill to check the rule.

The machine had done exactly what it had been told to do, to the letter, all afternoon. The input was mixed seed. Nobody had told it to sort, and it had sorted, because that is what a spinning disc does to objects of different weights and always has.

Emeric was genuinely unpleasant about it for an entire evening.

Not shouting. Worse than shouting. Cold, and precise, and going back over the route working three times in front of two people who had nothing to do with any of it.

Ferra let it run for about an hour and then pointed out that he was angry at a rock.

He stopped.

The fix had no stone in it anywhere. A graded sieve upstream of the hopper, a wooden frame and three meshes, built in a day and a half by a man off the kiln crew for the price of the timber.

And it turned out to be worth more than the spreader. The by-catch went to feed, the through-fall was the heaviest and best of the seed stock, and Kaldo spent the following spring sowing graded grain for the first time in its history, which did more for the yield in year three than any of the machines did in year two.

The bins were the one that did not fail, and he noticed that it had not failed, which was new.

He caught it on paper on the Thursday evening, going through the sealing plan with a stasis stone in his hand and the eleven bins written out in front of him.

There were not enough stones for eleven bins. There were four. He had been about to put four stones in the four largest bins and treat the other seven as a problem to solve later, which is exactly the shape of decision he had made in three other places that week.

So he did the arithmetic instead. Grain dried to the mark and sealed into a limed, damp-proofed bin keeps most of a year on its own, with no stone in it at all, which Kaldo had known for centuries and had stopped doing because nobody had a reason to seal anything. Stasis is margin. Stasis is not the floor.

Four stones went in as margin, spread across the eleven rather than concentrated, and the bins stayed distributed, so a dead stone cost one bin and never a winter.

And a standing rule went on the wall with the rest of them, because he had thought of it two minutes after he thought of everything else and it was the one that mattered. No bin gets sealed until it has been walked and checked. Stasis preserves the weevil exactly as faithfully as it preserves the grain.

The plow heads came out of the foundry in the middle of the week and were the first useful thing it made.

Cast off a master mold. No stones in them, no working, nothing clever. Grey iron, cast, dressed, and handed out to the west farms in a crate.

The redundancy in a cast plow head is the casting. That is the whole of it and it took a farmer standing in a yard to make Emeric understand how large it was.

Geddes broke one on the Thursday, on something under that sour ground of his that he had been meaning to dig out for a decade. He brought the pieces up to the foundry expecting to lose a week, because a broken plow head means a smith, and a smith means a queue, and a queue in harvest week means you do not plow.

Bram handed him another one out of the crate.

Geddes fitted it in the yard in under four minutes with the same pins and the same beam and no filing, and then stood there holding his own plow and did not move for a bit.

"It's the same."

"It's the same," Bram agreed.

"No, it's the same."

He worked it out standing there, out loud, in stages, and by the end of it he had got most of the way to a thing that nobody in this kingdom had ever met. If it is the same, he does not need a smith. If it is the same, he can keep one in the cart. If it is the same, his son can fit it. If it is the same, then the thing that breaks stops being a disaster and becomes a Thursday.

He asked Bram how many there were. Bram said as many as they cared to pour.

The harvest came in good and it was not miraculous and the honest number mattered.

The rotation had not had time to do anything. It had been in the ground for one season. Clover in a third division does nothing to a yield in its first year and Emeric had said so in a barn in February to three men who had not believed him and had turned out to be right for the wrong reasons.

What improved was the getting-in.

Less grain lost to weather because it came in faster. Less lost to damp because it dried properly. Less cracked, less spoiled in store, and a field sown with graded seed waiting for next year. The growing had not changed at all.

Ferra said that plainly in the market, twice, to people who asked her.

Emeric agreed with her, twice, to people who asked him.

Several people in Kaldo did not believe either of them and credited the machines, because a machine is a thing you can stand in front of and a rotation is a thing somebody tells you about, and by the end of the month the drum in the mill yard had been credited with about a third more grain than existed.

He let it stand.

Then he noticed himself letting it stand, and sat with that for a while, because a year ago he would not have noticed and two years ago he would not have cared, and he was not sure which of those made this worse.

He wrote it down. He was accumulating rather a lot of things he had written down and done nothing about.

The real cost of the week was not the barley.

Every working in all three machines had come out of Emeric's own hands, inside the delve, because that was the only method that produced anything reliable and it was not transferable to a living soul.

The drum's load sense. The shed's reader and its cutoff, which he cut twice because the first one was not deep enough and he could feel that it was not. The spreader's route working. Four stasis settings. And the second reader for the shed, which he did on the Saturday when he should not have done anything at all.

By the end of the week he was worse company than he had been since the road. Short with people who had done nothing, slow to follow a sentence, and twice unable to remember whether a thing had been decided or only discussed.

Ferra's log said so in her own handwriting, in the column she had started keeping in the winter for exactly this and had never mentioned to him.

Bram said it to his face on the Sunday, in the foundry, with nobody else in the building.

"A barony with one carver in it is a barony with one carver in it."

"I know what it is."

"Then do something about it, or stop looking surprised in September."

He thought about Armand that night for the first time in months.

He had written twice in the autumn after the trial. Once properly, with the substance of it, as much as he was prepared to put on paper going north. Once more in the winter, shorter.

Nothing had come back.

The charitable reading was sitting right there and he took it, because it was also the likely one. The man had students mid-course and a bench and a tower and the Circle standing over all three of it, and he had said he would come once he had settled what needed settling, and an old man settling things does not stop to write.

The other reading sat underneath it and did not go away.

And underneath both of them, the practical objection, which was the one he actually acted on. A third letter is not a letter any more. A man who writes three times is asking. And an old teacher who crosses a kingdom because his former student kept asking arrives as a favor rather than as a decision, and Emeric had a very clear idea of what he would eventually have to ask that man to do, and none of it survived being a favor.

So he waited. He did not write, and he did not mention any of it to anyone, and on the Monday he went into a room on his own and carved the next thing himself.
