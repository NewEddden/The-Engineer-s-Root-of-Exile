Chapter 45 - Lightning in a Jar

Armand spent the first week of September in the dyehouse, and for most of it he did not touch anything.

He sat on Abloh's stool by the door, which Abloh had surrendered to him without being asked and then stood beside for a day, glowering, before giving it up as lost. He watched the jars on their bench. He watched Tess come in each morning and lift the lid of every brown pot and look at the zinc, and write in her bench book, and watch the needle. He watched the needle. He asked Tess, on the second day, what she wrote, and she showed him the jar pages, with her thumb keeping the rest of the book shut, and he read those slowly and gave the book back to her and thanked her, and after that she let him watch her write.

On the third day he got up off the stool and went to the end of the bench and held his hand flat over the last jar, an inch above the lid.

He held it there. Then he moved it to the next jar, and the next, all the way down the bench, and came back to the stool and sat down.

"There is nothing in them," he said.

"Nothing you can feel."

"Nothing at all. A stone with a hundredth of that needle's push in it, I would feel from the door." He looked at the needle, lying at the end of its swing under Abloh's compass glass, where it had lain since June. "Something is pushing it, and there is nothing in the jars. I have sat here three days and I do not like either half of that sentence."

Emeric showed him. Not in a sentence, as he had promised. He took him through it at the bench over the next two days, the copper and the zinc and the two waters and the clay wall between, and the wire, and the coil round the compass, and the needle; and Armand asked the questions he had always asked, the short ones that found where the answer was thin, and in two places it was thin, because Emeric knew why from books that did not exist in this world, and could not show it on this bench, and Armand found both places and put his finger on them, and was not satisfied, and said so.

"You do not know what it is," he said, at the end of the second day.

"I know what it does."

"That is what every apprentice in the tower says about a stone in his first year." Armand looked at the jars. "And they are all of them right, and all of them wrong."



On the eighth day of September a storm came up the river from the sea.

It came in the late afternoon, a long black wall of cloud over the salt runs with the light gone green under it, and the swifts went down low over the canal ring and then vanished, and the wind dropped to nothing, and the whole town went indoors and shut its shutters. Emeric went up to the line room at the top of the keep, because the line was half a mile of iron on poles, and he wanted to see what a storm did to it, and Armand came up the stair behind him, slowly, with a hand on the wall, and would not be sent back down.

The first flash was a long way off, over the sea. They counted, both of them, out of a habit Emeric had not known they shared, and got to twenty before the thunder came, low and rolling.

The needle on the table had kicked.

A twitch, off north and back, the way it had twitched in the dyehouse in May on its first day. Nobody had touched the key. Down at the gate the gate crew were in their tower with the shutters closed and Ward among them, and nobody there had touched the key either. Emeric had asked Ward, before the storm, to make sure of that.

Another flash. The needle twitched with it, at the same instant, before the thunder had started on its way.

Armand had stopped counting.

The storm came up the river toward them. The flashes came closer and the count got shorter, fifteen, then ten, then six, and every time, at the moment the sky went white, the needle on the table jumped, further each time, until at the last one before the rain, a great split crack of light that came down somewhere across the river beyond the dock quarter with no count at all, only the noise, the needle swung right over to the end of its travel and hung there trembling and then fell back.

Then the rain came, all at once, like a dropped tray, and the light went, and the flashes moved off inland over the farms.

Armand sat down in the chair at the table, which Emeric had not noticed him reach for. He was looking at the needle.

"The same," he said.

"The same as in the jars. A great deal of it at once, instead of a little at a time."

"The tower holds," said Armand, "and has held for four hundred years, and I have held, and taught to boys a good deal brighter than you were, that lightning is the sky's mana breaking. A storm is a stone forced past its capacity a mile wide. That is in every book in the tower's library." He did not take his eyes off the needle. "And the sky has just reached down half a mile of iron wire and pushed your needle with the same hand as your jars. And there is no mana in your jars."

"No."

Armand sat in the chair while the rain hammered the window.

"I am seventy-eight," he said at last, mildly, to nobody. "I had hoped to be finished being wrong about large things."



The mage came through in the middle of the month, going south.

He came down the south road with a servant and two good horses, and stopped at the inn for a night, because Kaldo had only the one, and heard in the inn's yard within the hour that the old master from the tower was living in the prince's keep. He was young, perhaps thirty, and he wore the tower's cloth with the tower's marks on it, and he came up the hill the next morning to pay his respects, with every appearance of a man doing a kindness to somebody who would remember it.

Armand received him in the dyehouse, because that was where Armand was. He did not get up off Abloh's stool. The young man stood in the doorway with his hand over his nose, because of the vats, and looked at the old man on a stool among the jars and the brown pots and the copperas, and Emeric, at the bench, watched him decide what he thought of it.

He was polite. He asked after Armand's health, and was told it was what it was. He gave the tower's news, which was mostly who had been made what, and Armand listened to it with every appearance of interest. Then he looked along the bench, at the jars and the wire and the compass, and asked what it was for, and Emeric told him, plainly, as he had told Armand: the jars, the current, the needle at the end of the wire, the line to the gate.

The young man listened all the way to the end, which Emeric gave him credit for.

"A jar of acid," he said, "that makes a needle twitch half a mile away." He smiled at Armand, kindly. "Master, a first-year boy in the tower could light this whole room with a lamp stone and a thought. This is a great deal of copper and stink to do what a stone does better."

"It is," Armand agreed.

The young man seemed not to know what to do with being agreed with. He looked at Emeric, and at the golden eyes, and then back at the old man, and his smile became a little kinder still, as people's smiles do toward furniture.

"You'll have heard," Armand said, from the stool, "of dead zones."

"Of course."

"Tell me what happens in one. As you were taught it."

The young man looked faintly put out to be examined, at his age, in a dyehouse, but he answered, because it was Armand, and the habit went deep. A stone forced past its capacity bursts. Its mana goes out of it all at once, like a blast of wind, except that nothing moves: not a leaf, not a candle flame. And the blast drives the ambient mana out of the place, for a day at most, until it seeps back out of the ground and the stones. While it lasts, nothing outside a body works. No glyph will form. No stone will do what it was told. A mage inside one is a man with his hands in his pockets.

"And a knight?"

"A knight's mana is inside him. It goes on working." The young man shrugged. "Which is why the tower has always held that dead zones are of no use to anyone who has anything to lose. You'd be handing the field to the knights."

"Just so," said Armand. "And your lamp stone, in a dead zone?"

"A pebble."

"And your glyphs?"

"They won't form."

Armand turned on the stool and looked along the bench at the needle, lying at the end of its swing, as it had lain all summer.

"And this?" he said.

The young man looked at it. He opened his mouth, and then he closed it again.

"The same as it does now," Emeric said. "That's the point."

The young man looked at him longer than courtesy needed, and then he smiled again, differently, and said that he was sure it was all very interesting, and that he had a long road and must not keep the master from his work. He bowed to Armand, properly, and to Emeric, a little less properly, and went out holding his nose, and was gone down the south road before noon.

"Nobody makes dead zones," Emeric said, when the horses had gone. "Not on purpose. He's right about that."

"Nobody has had a reason to," Armand said. He looked at the door the young man had gone out of. "You have just shown one to a boy who will be telling it at the high table in the tower by the first frost. As a joke." He got up off the stool, slowly. "They will laugh. I would rather they laughed."



His first morning at the school was the last Wednesday of September, and he brought his own chair.

He also brought a cup of water and the wooden case from his bench, and he sat in the chair at the front of the long room with the case on his knees and a hundred and thirty-one children on the benches in front of him, and Sela at the back with her arms folded, and Emeric beside her, and he did not frighten anybody, though he did not try not to.

He talked to them about stones for most of an hour. Where they came from, which was out of the ground, like iron. What was in them, which was mana, which was in everything, a little, the way there was water in bread. What a mage did with it, and a knight, and how few of either there were, and why. He made them laugh twice, which nobody had expected, the second time by describing the tower's stair.

Then he opened the case and took out a lamp stone.

It was spent. It was grey all through, the size of a hen's egg, with no light in it at all. He held it up so that the back benches could see.

"Anybody can wake a stone that has something in it," he said. "You close your hand on it and you want it, and it does what it was made to do. This one has nothing in it. It is empty. You can want it as hard as you like and nothing will happen, because there is nothing in it to do anything." He held it out. "So I would like each of you to hold it, and want it to light, and see what happens."

They came up in rows. Every child in the room held the grey stone in two hands and screwed up their face and wanted it, and nothing happened, and they gave it back to him and went to their places. Some of them were disappointed. Most were not. A boy at the end of the third row held it to his forehead, and was laughed at, and laughed too. Tess, who came most school mornings, held it in her two hands and turned it over, and gave it back without wanting it to do anything at all, and Armand looked at her with interest.

Arthur came up in the seventh row.

He took the stone the way he did everything, quickly, without looking at anybody, and held it in his two cupped hands in front of his chest, and looked down at it.

Emeric felt it before he saw it.

A small warmth from across the room, the way a fire is felt through a wall, somewhere behind his own breastbone, faint and quite unmistakable: the warmth anybody with the faculty felt when mana moved close by without a glyph to shape it. He had felt it from his own hands, at night, pushing a drop of ink across a page. He had never felt it from anybody else's.

Then the stone lit.

Barely. A faint pale light down in the middle of the grey, like a coal under ash, so faint that in the bright room it could hardly be seen at all.

Arthur stared at his hands.

Armand did not say anything. He reached over to the floor beside his chair and picked up the cup of water and held it out, and nodded at Arthur to put a finger in it, and Arthur, not looking away from the stone, put the first finger of his right hand into the water.

The surface of the water shivered all over at once, from the rim inward, like a cup set down on a table where somebody was dancing.

Armand set the cup down. He took the stone out of Arthur's hands, gently, and closed his own hand over it, and the light went out.

"Thank you," he said, as he had said it to all the others, and held out his hand for the next.

Arthur went back to his bench. The children on either side of him moved along a little, without meaning to, and he sat down in the gap and looked at his hands.



Sela was waiting at the door when Armand came out at the end of the morning with his chair under his arm. She had her shawl on and her arms folded inside it, and Arthur behind her, and her face was the face she had worn at her counter in July of last year, when a prince with the crown's eyes had come into her shop.

"Who has to know?" she said.

Armand looked at her, and then at the boy behind her, and then back.

"I do," he said. "Now you do. And the boy."

Sela's eyes went past him to Emeric, standing behind him in the doorway.

"And him?"

"He felt it," Armand said. "From across the room. I watched him."

Sela looked at Emeric, and he did not look away, and did not say anything, because there was nothing he could say to her about the crown that she did not already know better than he did.

"Then that's three too many," Sela said, and took Arthur by the shoulder, and went home.
