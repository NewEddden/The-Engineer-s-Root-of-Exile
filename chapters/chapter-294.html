Chapter 294 - The Stair

The castle's Long Table went down the great hall from the hearth to the door, as it had for three years, and this year it was not long enough.

Maren had said it would not be. She had said so in November, with her list, and been told by Ferra that the hall was the length it was and that if there were more people than table the people would have to sit closer; and Maren had said that the people in question were knights, and their children, and sitting closer did not make a knight any narrower. So there were two tables, the long one and a short one across its foot for the children, who had been told it was a punishment and had taken it as a promotion, because nobody at it could be made to pass the salt.

Emeric sat in the middle of the long one with Ferra on his left and Imani on his right, and could not have moved his elbows if the keep had caught fire.

It was warm. The hall was always warm now, on the steam from the river, and with a hundred people in it and the hearth lit for the look of the thing it was warmer than it had any right to be in the last week of December. Half the men at the table had their coats off on the backs of their chairs. Ward's hung on his, at the hearth end, beside Armand's wheeled chair and Armand in it under a rug; and Ward sat in his shirt with his sleeves buttoned at the wrist, because Ward did.

The goose came in at eight, nine of them. Bram said it was one for every man Maren had ever been afraid of. Maren said she had never been afraid of any man in her life and he could carve his own, and then carved his for him, because she always had.

"She's been at it since four," said Ferra, at his ear. "In the morning. I saw the kitchen lamp when I went to the dome."

"Did you send her back to bed?"

"I'm not a fool." Ferra took a roast parsnip off his plate, because it was nearer than the dish. "I took her a cup of tea and went away again."

On his other side Imani was not eating. She had her fork in her hand with a slice of goose going cold on it, and she was watching the hearth end, where Armand sat with a glass of Kaldo spirit he had been allowed a finger of and had made into two, holding forth.

He told stories at the table now, more than he used to. Wren had come up the hall with a plate of pudding on the pretext of the salt and was sitting on a stool at his knee, sixteen and too old for it and not caring; Arthur stood behind her with his arms folded, and Finn beside him, and Mira and Bren a little off, pretending not to listen.

"A drain net," Wren was saying, in Rylan's voice, which carried. "What's a drain net?"

"A net," said Armand. "Of iron cord, with a stone at every knot, like the stones in a cell's cuffs. The temple guard carries them. It's the only thing in the kingdom made to catch a mage, and they're proud of it, and they're right to be." He turned his glass on the arm of his chair. "When I was a young man in the tower there was a mage two floors up from me. An earth mage. Cleverer than I was, and he'd said something at dinner about a cardinal's nephew that he oughtn't. One morning the guard came for him on the stair. Six of them. He threw two of them down it with his hands. Then the net came over him from the landing above, and he put his hand up to cast, the way you'd put your hand up against the rain, and nothing came."

"Nothing?"

"It all went into the net. Every stone in it lit at once, like a string of lamps." Armand looked at the fire. "He was as strong as ever. He broke a guard's arm inside it. But he couldn't have lit a candle. They took him down the stair, and I stood aside on the turn to let them by, because I was young and wanted to be old." He drank. "I never saw him again."

Wren looked at him with her spoon in her mouth. "That's horrible."

"It was. Remember it. A knight in a net is still a knight. A mage in a net is a man in a net." He looked past her at Arthur. "Ward knows that. It's why he puts you lot in the cold water at six."

"Did the other mages do anything?" said Finn.

"The other mages went to dinner." Armand set the glass down. "And sixty years later, when I wrote to every student who'd ever sat in front of me to say there was work in Kaldo for a mage who'd like to be of use, most of them wrote back in very good hands to say they were needed where they were." He smiled a little, at the fire. "Halric Venn wrote back in the best hand of any of them. The cleverest gravity mage I ever taught, after one. He's still needed where he is, I'm told. The crown has him."

Emeric saw, without wanting to, an iron tube on a carriage on the crown's proving field, a thin young man with ink on his fingers at its breech, and a hulk's side caving in at three hundred yards without a sound.

"She's watching him," said Ferra. "She's been watching him since the soup."

Imani put her fork down.

"He had it at five," she said, to her plate. "When the boys were putting the tables up. He said it was the goose fat in the air. There wasn't any goose fat in the air at five." She turned her water glass round. "I counted him at six. Eighty-eight, and out of step, which is what he is, which isn't news. He's had his pill. I gave it him at seven with the foxglove and he made the face."

The pill was the aspirin. Bram's acid works had made it in November out of the willow bark Imani had always used, small and white and bitter, one at night with the foxglove; and every night the old man said he had lived eighty-seven years without being made to swallow chalk, and every night Imani said he had lived eighty-seven years without being made to do anything, and that was the trouble.

"Leave him be," said Ferra. "It's Long Table."

"I am leaving him be. I'm watching him be. That's allowed."

At the hearth end Armand had stopped talking. His right hand had gone inside his coat, flat, high, the way Emeric had seen it go a hundred times, and his face went a little grey at the edges, the way a page goes near a lamp. Wren had not noticed. She was asking Arthur whether a net would hold him, and Mira was saying a net would hold Bren because Bren would walk into it to see what it was.

Emeric had his sense in the hall. He felt the old man's note at the hearth end, the great still lake with very little water left in it, go uneven; and he did what Armand had told him to do the first winter and every winter since, and did not look at the middle. He looked at the old man's mouth instead. The lips were moving very slightly over the rim of the glass.

Imani had half risen. Without turning his head Armand lifted his free hand a finger's height off the chair's arm and put it down again, and she sat.

"He's counting," she said.

They watched him count. It went on longer than a hundred. Then the hand came out of the coat and picked up the glass, and he said something to Wren that made her laugh; and Imani let out a breath Emeric had not known she was holding, and ate her goose cold.



Bram made the toast at a quarter past nine.

Nobody had asked him to. Nobody ever had; he had made it the first year because nobody else stood up, and every year since because he had made it the year before. He got up the way he got up off a forge stool, in two goes, with a hand flat on the table, and the hall went quiet the way a yard goes quiet when a cart starts down a hill, and every face at both tables turned up the hall to him. That was the point of Bram standing up. Even Ward turned in his chair, very straight, as he sat for any toast; and at his elbow Armand leaned out of his own to see past the lamp, with his arm along the back of Ward's chair in the folds of Ward's coat.

"Decadays," said Bram.

He said the names in the order they came to him, which was not the order of anything: the farrier's boy, ten, who went scarlet at the children's table; Maren's sister in the dock quarter, who was not there and was fifty; the old man who kept the lamps on the wall walk, seventy, who stood up to be looked at and sat down before anybody could; two of the knights' wives; a carter's girl at the base; a boy from the line room. Fourteen. He drank to each with a mouthful and did not sit down after the last.

He looked down the long table, slowly, and past it, at the dark windows over the ward.

"And them as isn't here," he said, "that ought to be."

He drank and sat. Rylan said it after him, and lifted his glass, and the hall said it, the children with their water; and Emeric said it and did not know whose face he was seeing, and found Ferra's hand under the table holding his knee very hard.

"Last year it was nine names," she said. "The Hod's." She let go. "He's stopped naming them."

"He's had a year."

"We've all had a year. He's had a cracked head." She looked at her father, who was being told by Maren that he had spilled spirit on her cloth and was saying it was his spirit. "I'm going to have to go and kiss him. In front of everybody. He'll hate it."

She went. He hated it, loudly, to Maren, and did not let go of her wrist until she let go of him.



Armand left at half past nine.

Nobody asked why. He was an old man and it was a long night. When Ward began to rise to push his chair he put a hand on Ward's arm and said, "Sit. It's Long Table. I've the girl's machine."

The girl's machine was Tess's spare chair, which lived at the foot of the hall ramp for the weeks when hers was in pieces on the Motion Shop's bench, which since the autumn had been most weeks: black, with the bronze hub drum on its right wheel and the dial by the right hand. Armand had taken to borrowing it in October on the evenings his arms would not do his own rims. Tess had shown him the dial with a face like a schoolmistress's and walked behind him to his door saying ease it, sir, ease it, it's not a horse; and he had said that if it were a horse he would know what to do with it, and run it into the door frame, and the castle had heard by supper.

So Ward sat, and Arthur gave the old man an arm across, and Armand turned the dial with two fingers and went off down the passage toward the east range at a man's walking pace with the motor whining in its drum. Wren called after him to ease it. He lifted a hand without turning.

Imani watched him to the passage's turn.

"He's going to bed," said Emeric.

"I know where he's going. I'll count him at seven. Before he's had his breakfast and told me it's nothing." She looked down at last at the pudding Maren had set in front of her without asking, a wedge the size of a brick. "Seven," she said again, and ate it.



They went up at half past ten.

It was not late; Rylan had only just started singing. But Ferra had been up since half past four and Imani at the ward since six, and Emeric had been in the yard with the K Amara's fitting gang until two the night before, and the three of them had come to the end of something that was not the evening. Ferra went first, with her hand on his shoulder going past. Imani went a quarter of an hour later, to look in at the ward. Emeric finished his glass, was told by Rylan that he was missing the best of it, and went up the keep stair.

Ferra's room was dark but for one lamp turned low, and the floor was warm under his boots in the door, wall to wall, the pipes ticking under the oak. The quilts were already down in front of the cold hearth. Ferra lay on them with her back to the bed and her eyes shut.

"Boots," she said.

He took his boots off.

Imani came up ten minutes after him in her scrubs, her shoes in her hand, because the ward had wanted her for a boy with a scald; and dropped the shoes in the door and lay down on his other side and put her cold feet on his shins.

"Was it bad?"

"A kettle. He'll have a blister like a sixpence and tell everybody it was a dragon." She pressed her feet harder. "Warm these."

He warmed them, a little heat in his palms, the inward thing. Ferra found his other hand without opening her eyes and put it on her hip and held it there, which was what she did instead of asking for anything. Far down through the keep's walls Rylan was still singing, and somebody had joined him, badly.

He drew his sense in.

He did it without thinking now. For most of his life in Kaldo he had lain at night with half of himself out on the town, every note he knew going about its business in the dark, the way a man who sleeps beside a road hears every cart. Then one night last winter, in the snow, on this floor, he had drawn it all in to the edges of the quilts because there was nothing out there he wanted to hear; and every night since that the three of them were in this room he had done the same. He did it now. Off the hall, the ward, the dome and the tree humming in it, the east range, the passages; in, until there was nothing in it but two notes very close and warm, and his own between them.

"There," said Imani into his shoulder. She always felt him do it. "You've stopped listening."

"You can't tell that."

"Everybody in the castle can tell," said Ferra, with her eyes shut. "The kitchen dog goes and lies across Ward's door instead of yours." She turned her face into his arm. "It's the day for another hour and a half. I don't want to spend it talking."

They did not spend it talking.



Armand sat in his room at the end of the east range, in the dark, in Tess's chair, with his coat on, and waited for the prince's sense to go.

He had felt it on him for years. Not as a weight; as a man feels a blanket in his sleep, and would notice only if it were pulled off. A net laid light over the whole town every night from somewhere in the keep, with the boy at the middle of it not knowing he was casting it; and Armand had lain under it in this room and been comforted by it, absurdly, and had never once said so.

Since last winter, some nights, it went. The first time he had wondered whether the boy was ill. By the next week he had worked out which nights they were, and had been, for an old man alone in the dark, a good deal more amused than he would ever have admitted: they were the nights the prince went up the keep stair after the Chancellor, and the healer after him.

He had chosen the night in October, when Imani said no. Long Table, after a year like this one: nothing in the world would keep those three out of that warm room, and nothing would bring them out of it before the Chancellor's first reading at half past five, at which she had not been late once in all the years he had known her. Seven hours. By the boy's own week, seven hours was more than enough to be past the place where anybody could stop it.

He had only not known which year. The heart had told him that at the table, under the child's chatter. It had not let go at a hundred. He had counted it to two hundred and forty with a glass in his hand and a girl asking whether a net would hold Arthur, and at two hundred and forty it had let him go, and he had known that it would not wait for another Long Table. It was a courteous heart, in its way. It had picked the night he would have picked.

At a quarter to eleven the net came up off the town, the way a fisherman draws a net hand over hand into a boat, off the dome and the ward and his own walls and off him; and the room was only a room, cold at the window.

He turned the dial.



The east stair to the counting room had twenty-two steps. He had never counted them before. He climbed them with the chair at the foot and his stick in one hand and the rail in the other, and rested on the landing for a count of a hundred, which seemed fitting; and at the top he stood in Ferra's door with his hand inside his coat and thought that if he died on her threshold she would never forgive him, which was a good reason not to.

The stone drawer took two keys and had two holders. Ferra had said, the morning the pod fell, that a stone with one key was a stone one person could lose, and that the stones' secretary would hold the other. He had never used his. It turned in the lock as if it had been waiting to be asked.

Three long grey stones in their hollows in the felt, and two hollows empty, the boy's and the girl's. Beside the box, the plain wooden one with nothing written on it, which was the king's, and which he did not touch. He took the middle one of the three. It seemed the least rude.

Then, because he had been a scholar for seventy years and could not take a thing out of a collection without leaving a slip, he opened Ferra's stone book at its last page, under Inventory, sundry, and wrote in pencil, in the small square hand that shook now on the downstrokes: 25 December. One. Taken by A., for the reason in the gauge book. And under it, because she would want it: Two remain.

He locked the drawer and went down the twenty-two steps backward, which was easier on the knees and more frightening, and sat in the chair at the bottom until he could see properly, and went out into the ward.



It was clear and very cold. The great dome stood up over the middle of the ward against the stars, its iron ribs going up from the colonnade with most of their glass in and the last of it in crates under tarpaulins along the colonnade's foot; and under it, at its heart, the old glass room glowed faintly violet through all its little triangles, like a lantern left burning inside a cathedral. The singing came from the hall's windows across the frost. The chair's wheels went over the flags with a sound like a page turning, and nobody came to any door.

The pass-box's outer room was warm from the glass room's warmth through its iron. He lit the lamp.

The gauge was on the wall, and under it the brass wheel on the feed pipe that came in under the floor from the plant's reservoir: a quarter turn, as it was every night, a trickle to hold the room at its line until the morning's cartridge. The needle sat on Ferra's red line. He had given her that figure himself, the night he came down from the Band. He looked at it and remembered Bram on his crate through the whole of the boy's first night, counting cartridges into the collar aloud until his voice went, and every leaf on the tree folding shut at three minutes past midnight because the boy had drunk the room down under its line faster than one smith could put it back. A room that went under its line in the first hours gave its sleeper the tree next. He did not want the tree. He wanted the room.

He opened the valve wide. It took both hands and most of what was in them. The pipe knocked once under the floor and began to sing, a thin high note in the iron, and the needle stirred and lifted a hair above the line and stayed; and he sat with his heart going like a moth on a window until it let him go on.

Then he took the feed gauge book off its shelf, turned past Ferra's nights to the clean pages at the back that she would not reach for a year, and wrote his note. He had composed it in October. It came out shorter than he had composed it, and he left it shorter, because it would be read at half past five in the morning by a young woman who would not want it long.

The cuffs were in the iron press on the far wall, where Ward had put them two springs ago after carrying them out of the dome in his arms: two sets, wrists and ankles, the flat stones in their bands, grey and dry. Ward kept the press's key on a cord in the inside pocket of his coat, as a man keeps the key to a thing he hopes not to open again. At a quarter past nine, with every face in the hall turned to a smith on his feet, Armand had leaned out of his chair to see past the lamp with his arm in the folds of that coat, and found the cord with two fingers, and drawn it out, and sat back with the key in his fist under the rug and said them as isn't here with everybody else.

He was not proud of it. He was, if he was honest, a little proud of it.

Inside the press door, on a nail, hung Ward's book, because nothing came out of a store of Ward's that did not go into Ward's book. Armand wrote in that too: 25 December. Stone cuffs, two sets, out. A. Key in the last. He took the four bands down into his lap, and they were cold through the rug.

He did not take a mask. A man going into a room to let the room into him had no use for a thing that kept it out; he had heard the boy say very nearly that, in this room. He drove the chair into the pass-box, which it fitted with a finger to spare each side, shut the outer door, and opened the inner one.



The room came at him the way the sea comes at a man who walks into it in winter.

He had been in it before, in a mask, for a quarter of an hour, and it had been a hand on the skin then. It was more now: the tree's line, held all year, quiet and green-smelling and dense, pressing on him everywhere at once, gently, enormously, like a great soft weight that wanted to be let in. He sat inside the door and let it press until he could breathe in it.

The tree stood in its violet night with the blue light going along under its branches as slow as sap. Bram's boards were still by the trunk, on the side away from the glass, with a straw mattress and a folded blanket and a jug, because Bram had said in the spring that they would be wanted again and had meant the girl. Armand drove the chair to them and turned the dial off. The motor's whine stopped, and the tree's note came up through the chair's frame into him instead, low, like a bowed string.

He sat with the stone in his hand.

He had done the sum in October, properly, the way he would have made a student do it, with the boy's sheets beside his own. Eating one did not kill: that was the first line, and nobody living had known it before the boy's week. The boy had come out wider, a great deal wider; and his knee had stopped clicking on the stair; and nothing else in him was mended, not one old scar. The stone had cut its roads through him and closed each one behind it with no seam, and mended what it tore and very little it had not torn, because it had not known what else in him wanted mending. It had come in a stranger and done a stranger's work: a good day's work at the job in front of it, and nothing past it. A heart was not the job in front of anything. A stone that was to mend a heart forty years scarred would have to know the heart.

So the stone would have to know him. The girl's had taken six weeks, because she had never been into a stone before and did not know the way. He had learned the way years ago, at the long table under the hall. A man who knows the way into one house does not need a map for the next; and if he was wrong about that, he would find it out in the first hour, sitting in a chair, and could go back to bed.

He had taken the sum to Imani in her surgery in October and she had heard him out with her hands folded and said no. Not no to the stone; no to him. He was eighty-seven, with a scar in his heart the size of a thumb's end and a beat that would not hold step for a minute together, and nobody anywhere had ever eaten a stone that knew them, so far as anybody could read; and she would not find out what it did by letting the oldest man in Kaldo try it on her watch. She had said it kindly. She had said, if you were sixty, and stopped, and said, no, not even then. He had thanked her and meant it. It was exactly what he would have said to anybody else.

That was the selfish half, and he did not pretend otherwise: he was dying, slowly, of a heart that had been dying for twenty years, and a dying man skips the line, and wants to. The other half was that somebody had to go first who knew the stones and could tell them afterward what it had been like from inside; and if he did not come back, they would know that too, and would not put the girl on these boards on a guess. Either way the table would know something by the new year that nobody knew tonight. He sat in the girl's chair in the green dark and found that he was smiling, which surprised him.

He held the stone in both hands and went into it.



It was bare, as they always were new: a room with the plaster still wet, nothing on its walls, nobody in it. He stood in its door a while. Then he brought his own room in after him.

It was a library, his room; his corner of the tower's, sixty years of shelves going away under a lamp that never moved. He carried it into the stone's bare room the way you carry a lamp into a dark house so the house can see what it is, and walked the stone down every shelf. Here is my hand, the shake in it. Here is my chest, the dead place, low on the left. My knees. My back. The boy at six lighting every candle in his mother's room. His mother at fourteen asking the porter for the kitchens. The floor of the tower and the forty-one men and the six who got up. Here is what I am, the bad drawing and the good. Learn it.

It learned. It took an hour by the watch, and felt like a long morning with a patient student, and at the end of it the bare room had shelves, and they were his.

He came up in the chair with the stone warm in his hands. He wrote nothing down. There was nobody to write it for until morning.



The ankles first, because they were furthest away and it would only get harder.

He did them as Ward had done the boy's, over the stockings, so the skin would not rub; bent over his own knees in the chair with his breath going short and the key stiff in each lock. Then he got out of the chair, which was the part he had been most afraid of, and lay down on Bram's boards with the trunk by his head and the blanket over him.

The left wrist. The flat stone settled on the inside of the joint and began, coldly, to drink, though there was nothing to drink, a pull like a hand on a door you were not leaning on. The boy had said that, after. Armand had written it down.

The right was hardest, with the left hand already cold. He did it with the band on the board and his wrist laid into it, and when the key turned he left it where it was, in the lock, because whoever came in the morning should not have to send for a knight.

He drank from Bram's jug. He held the stone up between thumb and finger against the tree's violet, and could not see through it, and had not expected to.

The figure in Vey's drawing had its hand to its mouth. The boy had said you could not tell from the drawing whether it was afraid. You could tell, Armand thought. You only had to be the figure.

He swallowed it dry.

It went down like a cold pebble out of a river, too big, all the way, and stopped. He lay with his cuffed hands on his chest and the pipe in the floor singing, and waited; and after a while, from habit, because it was what he did on the nights he could not sleep, he opened his room.

The lamp. The shelves going away. He walked down them a little way, as he had every night for sixty years, meaning to come back.

He did not come back. The shelves went on further than he had ever gone, and he was not tired, and the floor was warm under his feet, and the books on either side were his and then were older, in hands he did not know, and then were not books at all; and at the end of the shelves, where there had always been a wall, there was no wall.

There was a stair. Stone, each step worn hollow in the middle by feet, going down into a dark that was not dark the way night is, but the way deep water is.

He stood at the top of it a while, an old scholar in his slippers, with his hand on the cold rail.

Then he went down.



Ferra got up at twenty past five.

Emeric half woke when she went; he always did. Her weight went off the quilts and the cold came in where she had been, and he heard her dress in the dark and pin her hair by feel, and the door, and her step on the stair, quick, and gone. Imani did not wake. Imani never woke for Ferra. She lay against his back with her forehead between his shoulder blades and breathed slow and deep.

He let his sense out, as he did waking. The castle came back into it piece by piece, the way morning comes into a window. Maren in the kitchen with the range lit. Ward awake, as Ward was at five every morning of the world. Bram asleep in the house by the forge like a banked fire. The ward and its sleepers, the boy with the scald. Ferra crossing the castle ward in the dark toward the dome, small and quick and bright. The tree, humming, its note filling the glass room to the brim, fuller than last night, he thought. And on the boards by its trunk a note he knew better than almost any in the world, so drawn down and so cold and so faint that for the space of a breath his sense slid over it the way an eye slides over a stone on a path.

He was up before he knew he was up. Imani said something into the quilts behind him. He was in his trousers and his shirt and on the stair barefoot, three steps at a time, then lightened and down the well of it with one hand on the newel at each turn, and out into the frost of the ward with the stars still out.

Ferra was at the gauge in the outer room with her pencil stopped above the page. She turned in the lamplight. Her face was white. She had the feed gauge book open at the back in her other hand, and she lifted it an inch toward him, and said nothing.

He went past her, through the box, into the room.

It was as thick as it had been the week he lay in it. He was through it before it finished touching him, down the path under the tree to the boards.

Armand lay on his back on Bram's straw with the blanket to his chest and his cuffed hands on it, very straight, as though somebody had laid him out. Tess's chair stood by the boards with its dial off. His eyes were shut and his mouth a little open, and his face was grey, not the grey of sleep but the grey of a wall, and wet; and when Emeric went down on his knees and put a hand on the old man's forehead it was as cold as a cellar step.

He knew what he was looking at. He had sat by enough of the dead.

He knew what to do about it, too, which was a different thing, and it did not come. It was all there, in the order it had always been in, as plain as a page: the breath, the pulse, the eyes. Watch the chest for ten seconds. Two fingers in the groove beside the windpipe, light, never the thumb. Lift a lid to the lamp. He had taught it to Imani's ward himself, the first winter, to women who had laid out the plague dead by the hundred and thought there was nothing left to learn about it. He knelt with his hand on the cold forehead and did none of it.

Nor did he look with his sense. The old man's note was a hand's breadth away, if it was anywhere. He had only to turn toward it. He did not turn. As long as he did not look, it had not gone.

What he did instead was think, quite clearly, as though reading it off a page: he's dead. He came here on Long Table night and lay down by the tree and died. And then: I drew my sense in at a quarter to eleven to lie on a warm floor, and I felt him go down the passage in the girl's chair at half past nine, and I thought, he's going to bed.

He made himself move. He watched the blanket over the old man's chest and counted, and lost the count at four, and could not have said whether it had risen. He put two fingers to the throat. There was a beat under them, hard and quick, and it took him far too long to understand that it was his own, in his fingertips, the way it was in every frightened student's; so he pressed harder to get under it, and found nothing at all.

Then the old man's hand moved.

Not under his own; the right hand on the blanket, in its cuff. The fingers closed on the blanket's edge, slowly, and opened. And on the inside of the wrist, just above the iron, a thin silver line came up under the skin like a hair under wet paper, and dulled, and sank, and was gone.

Emeric stared at it. He did not take his fingers off the throat. He did not seem able to.

Behind him the inner door banged on its stops. Imani came down the path in her scrubs with her ward coat over them and a scrubber mask buckled crooked over her face, barefoot, and went down on the boards' other side; and took his wrist, and lifted his hand off the old man's neck the way she would lift a tool somebody had left on her bench.

"You're pressing it shut," she said.

She put her own two fingers in the groove, lightly, and counted. Her eyes were on the trunk over Armand's head, unfocused, the way they went when all of her was in two fingertips.

"Sixty," she said.

She took her fingers away and put them back and counted again, and he watched her get to sixty a second time and look up at him over the mask with her eyes wide and furious and wet.

"Sixty. In step. Every one. He was eighty-eight at six last night and couldn't hold it for twenty." She put her hand flat on the old man's chest, over his cuffed hands. "He's breathing from the bottom of his lungs. I've never once heard him breathe from the bottom of his lungs." Her voice went. "He's cold as a fish and he's better than he was yesterday afternoon."

Emeric put his hand beside hers. It was shaking. He watched it shake on the blanket as though it belonged to somebody at the far end of the table, and Imani, without looking, put hers over it and pressed it flat and held it there; and he went in.

He did not have to go far. The lake was not still. The room was going into the old man everywhere at once, through his skin, slow and steady and enormous, the way a dry field drinks a flood, so that he lay at the bottom of the room like a stone at the bottom of a pool with the pool pouring into it; that was the cold, Emeric thought, every scrap of him drawn in to the middle where the work was. Low on the left under the ribs, a knot, the shape of a long small egg, quiet, and not still: unrolling, threads going out of it fine as hair into the arms and the neck and the great vessels and the heart. In the heart's wall, round the old dead place, working.

And under all of it, where a delver's door was, the old man's door stood open. Not ajar, as a sleeping delver's was. Open all the way, and deeper behind it than any door Emeric had ever stood at, his own included: a long way down, and going on.

"It's in the heart," he said.

"I know it's in the heart." Imani had her eyes shut. He felt her hollow view going through the old man beside his own, cool and thin. "It's tearing round the scar and mending behind and tearing again a finger's width on." Her eyes opened. "Get him out. Bleed the room down through the box. Get Bram and two men in masks and drag him to the door on these boards the way we drilled it for you, and let it stop in thin air. I can work with half done and alive. I said it for you."

"Look at the wall again," said Emeric. "Look how many."

She looked at him; then shut her eyes, and he went in with her, and they looked at it together: an old heart beating in step for the first time in twenty years, its wall torn open in a hundred places at once and a hundred threads running along the tears, each a finger behind the next tear, as fast as the room came in.

It was the work she had written down in his arms, two springs ago. It was in the one place in a body where it could not be stopped halfway. Coming out meant bleeding the room down; the room would stop coming in, and the tearing would stop, and the threads would stop with it, because the threads ran on the room as a mill runs on its race; and every tear open at that moment would stay open, all at once, in a wall that had been dead in one place for forty years and was holed now in a hundred. In his arm that would have been a scar. In this heart it would be the next beat, or the one after. There was no board and no door and no two men in masks that led anywhere but that. And the old man had made sure that by the time anybody found him, it was the middle.

Imani came out first. He felt her go. She knelt with her hands flat on the old man's chest and did not speak, and he watched it go into her face and set there.

"If I take him out now, I kill him," she said.

"Yes."

"Now. In the first hours. Later, when the heart's past the worst of it, there's my rule. A line that stays an hour." She stopped. "Now there's no road back at all. Is there."

"No."

"He knew that. He sat in his room and timed it so that by the time any of us found him there'd be no road back."

"Yes," said Armand.



His eyes were open. They came down off the tree slowly, as if from a long way off, which Emeric thought afterward they had, and found Imani in her crooked mask, and found Emeric; and the grey wet face did a thing Emeric had seen it do perhaps a dozen times in twelve years, when a student's sum came out.

"Half past five," he said. His voice was a thread. "She's never once been late."

"You," said Imani.

"Good morning, Imani."

"Don't. Don't you good morning me. I said no. In my surgery, in October, to your face, sitting down, and you said thank you."

"I did. I meant it. You were quite right."

"Then what are you doing on these boards with a stone in you?"

"You said no to me." He breathed, from the bottom, slowly. "You'd have said it to anybody. It was the only answer you could give. So I didn't ask again."

Imani said a word Emeric had not known she knew, and then another.

Emeric had not said anything. He was still on his knees with his hand where Imani had pressed it, and something was coming up through him at last, all at once and from nowhere, and it was not grief. It was hot. He was glad of it. It was better than the forehead.

"You waited for me," he said.

Armand's eyes moved to him.

"You sat in your room and waited for me to go up. You knew I'd draw in. You knew which nights." His voice was not doing what he told it. "If I'd been listening I'd have felt you on the stair. I'd have been down here in a minute."

"Yes," said Armand. "That was rather the difficulty."

"You could have asked me."

"You'd have said yes." The old man breathed, from the bottom, slowly. "You did it yourself. You'd have said yes, and sat with me, and if it had gone the other way you'd have carried it about with you for the rest of your life, the way you carry Sela's boy." His eyes went back to the tree. "Imani's no was hers. I could take it from her. I couldn't take a yes from you."

Emeric opened his mouth to answer that, and nothing came; and the old man watched it not come with a look that was very like an apology and was not one.

The inner door went again. It was Ward, dressed, every button, his mask properly buckled, coming down the path at a pace Emeric had only seen on him in a yard with a blade. Ferra had gone for him. Ferra, Emeric understood, had gone for everybody.

Ward stopped at the foot of the boards. He looked at the old man; at the four cuffs, the ankles over the stockings and the wrists on the blanket; at the right one; at the key standing in its lock, on its cord, with the cord's end still in the knot Ward had tied in it himself.

He put his hand to the inside of his coat, and kept it there. He did not look surprised. Emeric had never in his life seen Ward look surprised. He stood with his hand flat over the empty pocket and looked at his own key in the lock on the old man's wrist, and something went across his face under the mask that on another man might have been a laugh.

"During the toast," he said.

"During the toast," said Armand. "You sat very straight. You always do. I've never known a man so easy to rob as a knight listening to a smith." He breathed. "I wrote it in your book. Two sets, out, A. Key in the last. I left it in the last so you wouldn't have to look for it."

Ward looked at him a while longer. Then he knelt at the boards' foot and put a hand on the old man's ankle over the cuff, the way Emeric had seen him put a hand on a horse's leg he was not sure of, and felt how full the stone in the band was, and left his hand there.

"You did it as I did," he said. "Over the stockings."

"I watched you." Armand's eyes closed. "You were very good with the ankles. I thought you'd like to know."

Ferra came in last, masked, in her grey with every pin in, the gauge book under one arm and the felt box open in her other hand. She did not come down the path. She stood at the foot of the inner door at the edge of the room, as though she had come to the end of a column and did not want to start the next.

"Two," she said.

Nobody spoke.

"I went up. I counted. Two. The drawer locked. And a line in my stone book, in pencil." Her voice through the mask was very level, which was how he knew. "You wrote in my book."

"I left a slip," said Armand, with his eyes shut. "One takes a thing out of a collection, one leaves a slip. I couldn't not."

"You wrote two remain."

"Two do remain."

"I know two remain. I counted them." Her voice cracked straight across, and she stopped, and stood there. "It's my book. Nobody writes in it but me."

"Then you'll have to rule a line under me and write that I did. You'll like that."

Ferra made a sound behind her mask that might have been anything.

"You opened the feed," she said. "Wide, at a quarter past eleven. Forty-one cartridges' worth out of the reservoir since, by the gauge. Ned will have to fly the Hod today. On the twenty-sixth." She drew a breath. "I read the night's fall first. I always read it first. I thought it was a leak. I went to the back of the book to start a page for the leak."

"I know. I counted on it." His mouth moved at one corner. "Don't shut that valve. Shut it and the room's under the line by noon and the leaves fold, and then I don't know what it does, and nor does anybody living." He breathed. "Will you read it to them? I'd like to hear how it sounds."

She opened the gauge book at the back, against her arm, in the violet light, and read it in the voice she used for the quarter's sums, every word, because that was how a page was read.

"Ferra. You will have read the night's fall first and wondered. It was me. I opened the line, because a room that goes under the tree's line on the first night gives its sleeper the tree, and we have learned that once already. Leave it open. I have taken one of your three, the middle one, and bonded it; I sat with it an hour and showed it everything, and it is mine. That is the only thing I have done tonight that he did not do, and it is the thing I want to know about. The cuffs are Ward's and the key is in the last of them. I have written both in their books. Imani said no to me, and she was right, and I am not sorry. If I am dead, bury me beside the tree, and build a house of windows over me when the duchy can afford the glass, and tell the tower my heart failed, which will be true. If I am not dead, I expect you will all be very cross with me, and I find I am looking forward to it. Either way, by the new year, you will know a thing that nobody knew tonight. A."

She closed the book.

Over their heads, beyond the old glass room's triangles, the great dome's ribs went up into the first grey of the morning with their glass in, the light coming into them.

"A house of windows," said Ward.

Nobody looked up.

"I wrote it in October," said Armand faintly, "before the glazing. I'm not going to change it now."

Imani had not moved. She was crying behind her mask without a sound, and very angry, and her hands did not leave his chest.

"You're eighty-seven," she said.

"Near enough."

"You've a scar in your heart I could put my thumb in."

"Not for long, I think." His eyes opened once more and found her. "You'll sit with me. At the box. In your hours."

"Every night. Every hour. Arms and neck." Her voice was not steady at all. "And once you're past the first of it, if one line stays an hour, I'm bringing you out on these boards whatever you say, and you can lie in my ward cross with me for the rest of your life."

"That seems fair."

"It's not fair. None of it's fair. It's my rule."

"Then it's the right one." He breathed, deep. "I'd like to sleep now. I've been down a very long stair, and I'm told I'm an old man."

"What stair?" said Emeric.

The old man looked at him; the look across a tower table at the end of a lesson, when the boy had asked the question that was next and not this one.

"Later," said Armand. "When I've had a look round."

His eyes closed, and did not open again. Emeric felt him go: not out, not away, but down, the way a stone goes down through clear water, all at once and quite slowly, with the room still pouring into him from every side. The cuffed hand with Ward's key in its lock closed on the blanket's edge and held on, the knuckles coming up white in the violet dark, the way a man holds the side of a boat in a sea.

Above the iron on his wrist a silver line came up, and went, and another came up beside it.

Ward stood, and looked at the key, and did not take it out.

"Leave it," he said. "He wants it there." He looked at Imani. "Five minutes. You've had six. Out. I'll send for Bram. He'll want the collar."

"I'll give Ned the figure," said Ferra. She stood aside to let Ward by and did not follow him. She stood at the foot of the door with the box under her arm, two stones in it, and looked at the boards, and the old man holding on, and Emeric kneeling barefoot on the path in his shirt; and he watched her draw a breath behind the mask, all the way down, and let it go.

"I'll rule the line," she said. "Under him. I'll write that he did."

She went out through the box. Imani did not go. Emeric did not tell her to.

When the outer door had shut, Imani said, without turning her head, "You had your thumb on the pipe."

"I know."

"You stood in my ward the first winter and told my girls a frightened nurse feels her own heart and calls it the patient's."

"I know."

"And you didn't look. With the other thing. I felt you not look."

He did not answer. It had come now, what had not come at the forehead. It came up from wherever it had been waiting and took him by the hands first, the way it took the old women in the plague year, so that he sat back on his heels on the cold path and put his hands over his face and could feel them going against his teeth. He did not make a sound. He did not think he could have.

Imani took one hand off the old man's chest, for the first time since she had put it there, and laid it on the back of Emeric's neck, and left it there until his hands were still.

"All these years in my ward," she said, "and that's the first time I've seen your hands not know what to do." She took her hand back. "Don't make a habit of it."

He knelt by Bram's boards with the tree humming over him and the pipe singing under the floor and the old man's cold hand holding on beside his own, and let his sense out over the castle and the town and the walls in the grey of the twenty-sixth; and then drew it in again, slowly, off all of it, until there was nothing in it but the boards, and the tree, and the knot under the old man's ribs, not still, unrolling; and held it there.
