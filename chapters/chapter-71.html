Chapter 71 - Two Rings

The guardroom under the gate had been built to hold a dozen men for a night, and it had held thirty-one for most of a week in May, and it still smelled of it.

It was low enough that Emeric took his hat off without thinking, and damp enough that the lamp on the shelf by the door burned with a ring of mist round it. There was a bench along the back wall and a drain in the floor and nothing else. The two men on the bench had their hands tied in front of them now, not behind, which was Ward's one kindness since June, and they had a blanket each, and a bucket, and a garrison man on a stool in the doorway with a crossbow across his knees and his back against the frame.

The one who talked began as soon as the door opened.

He was younger than Emeric had thought from the yard, thirty perhaps, with a coast accent that had been sanded down by years of being somewhere else, and he had a list. He had evidently been keeping it since the moorings. Renner's steward had promised him four silver for the month and a silver for every week after, half on signing and half on the day, and he had been paid the half, and he had sent the half home to his sister on the coast, so he had nothing, and the day had not come, and now it would not, because Renner was dead and his steward had not so much as written.

"Do you know what a year costs?" he said. "For a man with a ring and no tower? A room in a town that'll have you. Stones, if you want to keep your hand in, and you've got to buy those from somebody who knows what they're worth, so they cheat you. Boots. You walk everywhere. Nobody hires a hedge mage who's come on a horse." He held up his tied hands. "I've not had my boots off since July."

"Why not?"

"Because I'd never get them back on." He looked at the man in the doorway. "And because he'd have them."

The garrison man did not look up.

The other one had not moved. He sat at the far end of the bench with his back against the wall and his tied hands in his lap, a broad, heavy, quiet man with a farmer's neck and a burn scar across the back of one wrist, and he was looking at Emeric's hands. Not his face. When Emeric put his hat down on the shelf, the quiet man watched the hat go down and watched the hand come back. When Emeric folded his arms, he watched the arms fold.

Emeric did not know what to make of it, and so he made nothing of it, and asked the talker his name.

"Orrin." He jerked his head down the bench. "That's Vesk. He doesn't talk."

"Can't, or doesn't?"

"Ask him."

Emeric looked down the bench. Vesk looked at his hands.

"Where are your rings?" Emeric said.

"Your sergeant has them." Orrin said it with some bitterness. "On a nail. I can see it from here when he opens the door."



Ward and Ferra were at the top of the gate stair, where the wind came straight off the river and nobody lingered long enough to overhear anything.

Ferra had her book under her arm and her shawl over her head. Ward had nothing, and stood in the wind as if it had been put there for somebody else.

"Keep them as we have," Emeric said, "and it's bread, and a man at the door, for as long as they live. That's the garrison's bread and the garrison's man."

"Yes," said Ward.

"Hang them, and it's two men who sat down in the mud with their hands open. I'd have to explain that to the whole town, and then to myself."

Ward did not say yes to that.

"Send them home, and they're two mages walking up the river to the next baron who'll pay, with no love for us and a very good idea of where the wall's low." He looked at Ward. "So I want to hire them."

Ward looked out over the gate yard.

"They shot at the wall," he said.

"They shot at the lamps."

Ward said nothing. He had a way of saying nothing that was more exact than most men's arguments, and Emeric had learned to hear it as a line written in the margin: noted, objected to, not withdrawn. The wind took Ferra's shawl and she put her hand up to it.

"Never on the wall," Ward said at last. "Never armed. Not a knife. A man of mine at the door of wherever they work, for the winter at least. And every morning I see their hands."

"Their hands?"

"Burns. Cuts. Anything new." Ward turned his own hand over in the wind and looked at the palm of it. "A mage practising where he shouldn't has hands that show it."

"Done."

Ferra had not said anything. When Emeric looked at her she was not looking at either of them but at the wet roofs of the lower ward, and she had the face she wore when she was adding.

"What does a hedge mage cost?" she said.

"The talker says four silver a month."

"The talker would." She took the shawl off her head, since the wind would only take it again. "And is it running or new? A wage the barony didn't pay on the day of signing."

He had not thought of that. Ward, who had never asked a question about money in his life, looked at Ferra with what might have been respect.

"I'll write it," she said.

She wrote it, and sent it up the hill, and the answer came back down before the light went, on a half-sheet, under the House's form and the date.

A wage is a wage.

Ferra read it, turned it over to see if there was more on the back, and there was not.



Armand had not been up the keep stair since April. He had a room off the hall now, on the ground floor, that had been a steward's office once, and it held his bed and his bench and his case of stones and more books than a room that size had been built for, and he received everybody there as though he had chosen it.

They brought the two mages up through the lower ward under guard in the middle of the afternoon, with their hands untied for the walk and the garrison man a pace behind them, and people in the lanes stopped what they were doing to watch them go by. Orrin looked at all of it. Vesk looked at the ground.

Armand sat them across his table from him like boys in a schoolroom.

He did not ask them anything. He opened the case on the bench at his elbow and took out a lamp stone, one of the plain white ones from the wall, and set it on the table between them, dark. Then a fire stone, the red-brown kind, smaller than a hen's egg, worn smooth at one end. He pushed the lamp stone across to Orrin and said, "Light it," in the voice of a man who has said the same thing to a great many people across a great many tables.

Orrin put a finger on it and it lit. Quick, bright, too bright, and then settling.

"Again. Half that."

Orrin lit it at half. Armand watched his hand rather than the stone. Then he pushed the fire stone across, and Orrin closed his hand round it and held it, and after a while the room was warmer, and after a while longer Orrin put it down and wiped his palm on his trousers and did not look at anybody.

"Vesk," said Armand.

Vesk lit the lamp stone dimmer than Orrin had, and held it there, and did not make it brighter or let it fade, for as long as Armand let him. Armand gave him the fire stone and he barely warmed it. Armand took it back, and reached into the case again, and came out with a lump of what looked like plain grey slate, and put it in front of Vesk without a word.

Vesk looked at it. Then he looked, once, at Armand, and put two fingers on it, and the slate grew warm under them in a way that had nothing to do with fire, and a line ran across its face as fine as a hair and then closed again, and was gone.

Armand put the slate away.

When the garrison man had taken them back down, he sat with his hands flat on the table and waited for his breath. Emeric waited with him. He had learned not to fill the time.

"The talker's fire," Armand said. "A shaper. Quick hands, a small cup. He empties himself in a quarter hour and tells you about it for the rest of the afternoon." He breathed. "The quiet one's earth. Slow. He let me see about half of what he's got, and he's not a fool, so I'd guess half is what he meant me to see."

"Would the tower have taken them?"

"The tower wouldn't have let them up the step." Armand smiled at that, not pleasantly, at something a long way off. "No. No family, no letter, wrong accents. They learned it from whoever would teach them and paid for every lesson twice. Hedge mages." He looked at the door they had gone out of. "They're the two best tools in this barony, and I include myself, and I include you."

"They were trying to kill us in May."

"They were paid to." Armand shrugged, which cost him something. "A tool doesn't care who swung it last. You'll learn that about people eventually, I expect, and you'll hate it." He laid one hand on the closed case. "One thing. If they're to put anything back into a stone, any stone, they do it in this room. Where I can watch the work."

"You don't trust them."

"I don't trust anyone with a stone I haven't watched them fill." He looked at Emeric over the case. "I include you."



Bram did not like mages.

He said so on the first morning, in bay three, to Emeric, in front of both of them and the garrison man at the door, and then he set Orrin to the one job in the forge that he could not do himself without a second pair of hands, which was holding a bar end at heat while he drew it under the hammer.

The roof of Harl's mill wanted irons: straps and knees and long bolts to tie the new rafters into the old wall, nothing difficult and a great deal of it, and every piece of it had to come off the same few bars of good iron Bram had been hoarding since the barges stopped. Drawing them down was slow because the iron cooled under the hammer and had to go back to the fire, and back, and back, and every time it went back the scale came off it and there was less.

Orrin stood at the anvil with his hands a foot above the bar end and held it at a cherry red while Bram drew it, and drew it, and turned it, and drew it, and it did not go back to the fire at all.

"That's cheating," said Bram.

"It's four silver a month." Orrin's face was wet. "Cheating's dearer."

Bram finished the strap and quenched it and looked at it. Then he took another bar out of the rack and put it in the fire.

"Do it again," he said. "Slower. So Tess can see where it changes colour."

Tess was a quarter of a mile away, lying on her side in Imani's ward. Bram heard what he had said after he had said it, and stood with the tongs in his hand looking at the fire, and nobody in the bay said anything at all, and then he took the bar out and laid it on the anvil and Orrin put his hands over it.

Vesk was not given a job. He stood by the bellows where the garrison man could see him, with his tied hands in front of him, and he watched the work. About the middle of the morning he went down on his heels by the forge and looked at the hearth stone underneath it, which had had a crack across it since the winter, a crack Bram had packed with clay twice and which had opened twice. He looked at it for some time. Then, without asking anybody, he put his tied hands down flat on either side of the crack, and Emeric felt something under his boots, very slight, the way you feel a cart go by in the street through the floor of an upstairs room.

When Vesk took his hands away, there was no crack.

Bram looked at the hearth stone. He looked at Vesk. He went on looking at Vesk while the iron in the fire went from orange to yellow, which it should not have been allowed to do, and then he swore, and took it out, and went back to work, and did not say anything about it then or afterward.



Emeric went up to the ward that evening, as he did most evenings, and stood at the end of Tess's bed while she read him the river's height from Bram's figures, and the lamps, and what Bram had said about the new man's hands, which Bram had evidently told her at length on his way home and had apparently not called cheating at all.

He listened to her. He looked, while he listened, at the chair beside the bed. It was the chair Imani's helpers sat in when they sat with her, a plain kitchen chair with a rush seat, and on the days Tess sat up at all, two of them lifted her into it and she sat in it by the bed, and then two of them lifted her back. She sat wherever she was put. She had sat by the window once, in August, because somebody had put the chair by the window.

He had been in the powerhouse with his hands on her back. He had built the powerhouse. He had chosen where to put the machine and where to put the door, and it had been his door that the bolt came through, and he did not know, and would never know, whether any other door would have been any different. He thought about that most nights. He had stopped telling himself not to.

On his other world they had made chairs for this. He had never needed one and never thought about one, and he found, sitting up that night at Armand's spare table with a lamp and the back of an old survey, that he could see one all the same, very clearly, as if it were parked at the end of a corridor he had walked down every day of his life.

Two big wheels at the back, nearly as high as the seat, set close to the sitter's hips. A second rim on each wheel, a little smaller, a little out from it, for the hands, so that she would push the rim and the wheel would go, and nobody would have to stand behind her. Two little wheels at the front, on pivots, that turned whichever way they were pulled. A light frame. A seat of leather or canvas slung between two rails, a back that came up to the shoulder blades and no higher, and a bar to rest her feet on. Brakes she could reach.

He drew it from three sides. Then he drew the wheel on its own, larger, and looked at it, and understood why nobody in Kaldo had one.

A wheelwright's wheel was ash and oak and an iron tyre shrunk on hot, and it was built for a cart and weighed as much as a child. A wheel for this had to be light enough for a girl to turn with her hands all day, and true enough that it did not wander, and strong enough to take her weight sideways when she leaned. On his other world that was thin steel spokes, laced and tensioned, and a rim rolled to a section, and a hub on bearings. He could draw every piece. A good wheelwright, starting tomorrow, with the iron to hand and nothing else to do, would take a month to make one wheel round, and it would not be round.

He looked at the drawing for a while. Then he went to bed, and in the morning he took it to bay three.



Bram looked at it on the bench with his hands behind his back, the way he looked at anything he expected to be asked to do.

"For Tess," he said.

"Yes."

Bram looked at it a while longer. He put his thumb on the hand rim, on the paper.

"She'd push it herself."

"That's the point of it."

"She'd like that," Bram said, and then, because that was more than he generally said about anything, he picked up the drawing and turned it round and began to find things wrong with it, and Emeric let him. The frame wanted to be ash, not iron, Bram said, ash steamed and bent, there was no iron to spare and ash would do. The seat wanted to come lower. The little wheels at the front were too little; they would catch in every crack in the ward floor, and the ward floor was nothing but cracks.

"The big wheels," Emeric said. "I can't make the big wheels."

"Nobody can make those." Bram tapped the spokes. "Thirty of them. Thin as a nail. Pulling against each other. You'd spend a winter."

Emeric looked across the bay to the anvil, where Orrin was standing with his hands over another bar end and the sweat running off his chin, and to the bellows, where Vesk was standing with his tied hands in front of him, watching them.

Bram followed his look and said nothing.

It took the rest of that day and most of the next. Orrin heated the rim, a single long strip of Bram's good iron, and held it at heat along its whole length while Bram bent it round the form he had built on the floor out of pegs, and it did not crack, and it did not go back to the fire once. Then they cut and drew the spokes, thirty-two of them in the end, because Bram had counted again. Then Bram and Emeric laced them, hub to rim, and the wheel lay on the bench like a bent thing that wanted to be round and was not.

Emeric spun it on a spindle. It wobbled at the top like a drunk man.

Vesk had been watching from the bellows the whole of both days. He came over now without being called and stopped at the bench, and looked at the wheel, and then at Emeric.

"May I," he said.

It was the first thing anybody in Kaldo had heard him say. His voice was low and dry and a little hoarse from not being used, with the same far-coast accent as Orrin's but heavier, and he said it to Emeric and not to Bram, and waited.

Bram opened his mouth and shut it again.

"Go on," said Emeric.

Vesk put his tied hands on the rim, one on each side of the bad place, and stood very still. Nothing happened that anybody could see. After a long breath he moved his hands a hand's width round, and stood still again, and then again, all the way round the rim, and once round the hub, and then he stepped back.

Emeric spun it. It ran true. Not near true, not true enough for a ward floor: true, the rim going round past his eye without a flicker, as if it had been turned on a lathe that did not yet exist.

Bram bent down and sighted along the rim with one eye shut. He straightened.

"How," he said.

Vesk looked at his hands. "It wanted to be round," he said, which was evidently all the explanation he intended to give, and went back to the bellows.

They made the second wheel by the end of the afternoon. Vesk trued it without being asked. Bram began on the ash for the frame that evening, and said he would have the whole thing together in a week, and then said five days, and then said nothing and went on working after Emeric had left.



Ferra was at her table late, with her book open to the wage lines and the lamp pulled close.

To reach the wage lines she had to turn the pages, and he was standing at her shoulder when she did it, and so he saw it go past. The page for the night of the terms. The date at the top in her hand. Nothing under it. She had not written anything on it since July and she had not cut it out, and she turned past it now at the same speed as all the others, neither faster nor slower, and he knew that she knew he had seen it, and neither of them said a word.

She found the wage lines and dipped her pen.

"Orrin's rate," she said. "What did you settle with him?"

He had settled it at the anvil, that afternoon, over the second wheel, and had nothing to write on, and had written it on his cuff in chalk. He held out his arm. She looked at the cuff, and then at him, and then she took the edge of it between her finger and thumb and turned it toward the lamp, his wrist going with it, and read the figure off it, and wrote it down.

Then she let go.

"You can't keep a book on a sleeve," she said.

"It was the only thing I had."

"Then have something else." She wrote Vesk's rate under Orrin's, the same figure, because he had told her it was the same, and blotted both lines, and ruled under them. Then she wrote, below the rule, on a line of their own, the way she had written the three dead mages on a line of their own in May, two names.

Orrin. Vesk.
