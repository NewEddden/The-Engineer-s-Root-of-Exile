Chapter 71 - The Box

Bryda gave him the box on the last morning, in the yard, with the horses already saddled.

She came out of the hall carrying it in both hands, a plain oak thing the size of a bread loaf with iron at the corners and a lock that had been painted over, and she set it on the mounting block beside him and stepped back from it.

"Renner's," she said. "He kept it in the press behind the bed, under the winter blankets. I've known it was there for eleven years." She looked at it without any expression at all. "I took it for his private papers. Letters from his mother. Old debts. Whatever a man keeps where his wife won't look."

"You've not opened it."

"No."

He waited. She did not make him ask.

"I don't want to be part of his work," Bryda said. "I never did. He never let me be, and now he's dead, and I find I don't want to start. I want to begin from something that's mine." She said it plainly, once, the way she said everything, and did not add to it. "If there's anything in there that touches Grayrock, you hold Grayrock. It's yours to know. If there isn't, burn it."

He took it down the river road across his saddle and did not open it until he was in the guest house at the ford that night, with Ward outside the door and Saban asleep, and the lock gave to the point of a knife the way painted locks do.

It was not letters from Renner's mother.

It was a survey. Eleven sheets of good paper, far better paper than anybody in the south made, drawn in a trained hand in an ink that had not faded: a section through the Grayrock hills, with the old workings marked in, and one working in particular drawn much larger than the rest, the east shaft that had been sunk deepest, with its galleries followed down and down into the foot of the hill. There were figures in the margins in the same trained hand. There were samples described, and where they had been taken, and what they had assayed at.

Under the survey were payment records. A sum had been paid, several years before the war, for the survey to be made by outside men. He read the sum twice, because the first time he assumed he had misread it. It was more than Grayrock's revenue for three years together.

Under the payment records there was a correspondence, a bundle of letters tied with tape, which he set aside. And under the correspondence there were two last things. A closure order on the east working, in Renner's own hand, dated the season after the survey. And a note, also in Renner's hand, shorter and much less steady, recording that the men would not go back in, and that two who had gone in against orders had not come out, and that the working was to be called unsafe and sealed and the men told what they liked.

He sat back in the guest-house chair with the lamp between him and the papers, and read the note again, and then went back to the survey and read the margins of the east shaft, and found what the survey had been paid to find.

Gravity crystal. Not a seam. A body of it, running down past the deepest gallery into the foot of the hill, sampled at three depths and assayed at every one.

The curse took him about a page.

Everyone in Grayrock knew the east working was cursed. He had heard it on his first visit, four years ago, from a groom and a cook and one of the captains, and he had filed it with every other mine curse he had ever heard, which was to say not at all. Men went into the east working and did not come out. That was the curse, and it was true, and the men telling it believed every word.

He read Renner's note again, the part about the two who had gone in against orders. Then he read the closure order, and saw what Renner had specified in it: the working to be sealed at the head with timber and earth, and the galleries below to be left as they stood, with their packing in.

The packing was wood. Every gallery in a mine that deep had its roof held up with timber, and every void behind the timber packed with more of it, split and stacked, because wood was cheap and the hill was heavy. Sealed in the dark, in the damp, with no air moving, that wood had done what wood does. It had rotted. And rot is a slow fire. It eats the part of the air that a man breathes, and gives back the part that he does not, and in a sealed working with nothing to stir it, that spent air settles to the bottom and lies there, heavy and still and without any smell at all.

There was no gas seeping from the rock. There was no curse. There was a sealed hole full of rotting wood, and at the bottom of it air that could kill a man in two breaths, and every mine had too much wood and too many holes.

Renner had not discovered a curse. He had built one. He had sealed a working that did not need sealing and left the timber in it, and let his own men's fear do the rest, and the working had been guarding itself for years at the cost of nothing but two lives and some planks.

Emeric sat in the guest house at the ford until the lamp burned out, and then sat in the dark.

He did not go back up the hill for it. He sent Saban on to Kaldo with a message for Ferra, and took Ward, and rode back to Grayrock in the morning, and asked Bryda for a captain who was not Tolley, and was given an old man named Hask who had worked the east side before it was closed and had not been down it since.

Hask took them to the sealed mouth. It was a head of timber and packed earth grown over with grass, set in the hillside above the old spoil, with the rotted stub of a windlass beside it and nothing else. Nobody came near it. The path up to it had closed over.

Emeric stood at the seal for a long time.

He did not go in. He had been an engineer in two lives, and he was not going to break open an oxygen-starved shaft with his own hands to confirm a thing that a survey already told him, drawn by men who had been paid more than a barony earned in three years to draw it correctly. He stood on the grass and felt the hill under his boots and thought about what was at the bottom of it, and about the air sitting in the dark above it like water in a well, and about how you would get it out.

You did not fight dead air. You moved it.

He took the notebook out and sketched it against his knee, while Ward watched the path and Hask watched him. The sealed shaft, drawn in section. A second way into its foot, a short drive from the nearest live working, which the survey showed running less than a stone's throw off at the bottom. And at the head of the sealed shaft a fire, burning in its own chimney, drawing. The fire would pull, and the live air would come in along the drive from the working next door and push the dead air ahead of it, up the old shaft and out into the chimney and the sky. No crystal. No mage. A fire, two holes, and somebody to feed it.

It was not a ventilation plan. He knew the difference. A sketch on a knee was a thing a man could argue about in a warm room, and a ventilation plan was a thing a man could stake another man's life on, and between them lay a season of measuring and a great deal of money. He wrote the cost beside it, roughly, as a range, and then he turned to the back of the notebook, where the covenant clauses were numbered in the margin, and wrote the sketch into the second column beside the clause that forbade it.

"What is it?" Hask said. He had been looking at the drawing upside down.

"A way to make it safe," Emeric said. "One day."

Hask looked at the grass over the sealed mouth, and at the prince, and at the sketch, and did not say anything, and they went back down.

Ferra delivered the scale, the next evening, in the long room at Kaldo, and she delivered it as arithmetic, which was how it should be delivered and the only way he could have heard it.

She had the survey spread on the table under lamps, weighted at the corners with ledgers. She had spent the day on it and on the payment records, and she had three sheets of her own figures beside it.

"The vein alone," she said, and put a finger on the section, on the east shaft's deepest gallery, "at the survey's assay, at the price gravity crystal fetched the last time any was sold openly in this kingdom, would make Grayrock richer than all four of your baronies together. That's the vein. That's what a man could reasonably take out of it in a working lifetime." She moved her finger down, past the gallery, into the foot of the hill, where the survey's lines ran on and faded. "The body under it doesn't price in anything we use."

"Then what does it price in?"

"Platinum." She said the word as if it were the name of a foreign country, which in a sense it was. "It's a kingdom unit. Crowns and empires settle in it. Nobody below a duke has ever held any. At the survey's figure, the whole of it is worth roughly a decade of everything you hold. Gross. Four baronies, every harvest and toll and bar of soap and lift of ore, for ten years."

Ward, at the end of the table, said nothing at all, which was the most eloquent thing he could have said.

"And it's worth nothing," Ferra said.

He waited for the rest, because there was always a rest.

"It's a stock," she said. "Not a flow. There's that much of it and there will never be any more. There isn't a buyer in the world at that scale who isn't a kingdom, and any kingdom that buys it will want to know why a baron in the south had it to sell. And the moment anybody sells any real quantity of it, the price it's worth falls through the floor, because the price is only that high because there's none." She laid the sheets square. "Its cash value is the least interesting thing about it. What it actually is, is material. It's enough gravity crystal to build things nobody in this world has ever built. That's what it's worth. Not money. Things."

He looked at the survey for a long time.

It took him about four seconds to see the trap, and he saw it all at once, the way a man sees a step that is not there.

Clearing a sealed shaft. Ventilating it. Timbering it. Opening a working and bringing ore up out of it. There was no reading of the covenant under which that was repair, or maintenance, or the operation of anything that had existed before the loan. It was a new capital project. It was the plainest new capital project he had ever looked at. And the covenant said that he could not begin one, of any kind, for as long as a single coin of the Ducatis principal was outstanding.

He was sitting on the largest single asset in the southern half of the kingdom, and he was contractually forbidden to touch it, and the only thing that forbade him was a debt which that asset could have cleared ten times over.

He laughed. It was not a good laugh and he stopped it quickly, and Ferra looked at him with an expression that said she had seen the same thing some hours earlier and had been waiting for him to arrive.

"I have to say it to Vantic," he said.

"Yes," said Ferra. "You do."

Vantic heard him out in her own room, with the survey on her table, and did not hesitate for a moment.

"No," she said.

"I know."

"The covenant says what it says, Your Highness. A new working is a new working. I can't read it any other way and neither can you." She did not enjoy it. He could see that she did not, and he could see her decide that it made no difference. "I'll forward it."

"You don't have to."

"It's a request from the borrower concerning the security. I forward those. It's what I'm for." She was already reaching for paper. "They'll say no. You should know that before I send it. But I'll send it."

She sent it the next morning, and a copy of her covering note went into the Kaldo book, because every letter she wrote about Kaldo's affairs went into the Kaldo book, and Ferra read it that afternoon.

It was the longest thing Vantic had written since she arrived. It set out the survey and the deposit and the covenant clause, and argued, carefully and without once raising its voice, that a lender might consider an exception for an asset of this scale. And then, in its last paragraph, it said a second thing, and said it just as plainly: that even setting the covenant aside, a sealed working full of dead air with no ventilation plan was a liability and not a capital project, and a house did not lend against a liability, and the writer did not recommend that it should.

Ferra read that twice. Then she sat for some time with the copy in her hands.

She saw what Emeric did not. The note was honest, and its last paragraph was correct, and the house would refuse, and the house would be right to. But that was not why the house would refuse. A factor who argued an exception for a borrower, and argued it well, and was then proved wrong, had put a file in her house's hands that somebody in that house would be very glad to read. Ferra did not know who. She knew only, from the length of the note and the care in every line of it and the one paragraph that argued against the rest, that Vantic had known it too, and had sent it anyway.

She said nothing about it, to Vantic or to him. She filed the copy, and went back to the payment records.

The payment records were where she found the name, because payment records were where Ferra lived.

The money that had paid for the survey had not come from Renner. Renner had never had a sum like that in his life. It had come in from outside, through a factor, in three parts, over a season, by a route that went from a house in the north to a lender on the coast to a clerk in Grayrock's own counting house, each part a little smaller than the last and each a little later. Ferra laid the three payments out on a sheet side by side. Then she went to the shelf and took down a ledger from four years before, from the summer when a buyer nobody could trace had outbid Kaldo for Grayrock's high-grade ore, and laid that payment beside the other three.

It was the same shape. The same factor's distance, the same staging, the same way of arriving a little late and a little light. She had said so at the time, out loud, at this table, with the number in front of her, and he had overruled her, because there had been no name and he could not act on a shape.

This time there was a name. A survey is a service. Somebody has to commission it in writing, and pay for it against a signature, and the surveyors had kept their copy of the commission and put it in the box with their sheets, because surveyors are careful men and like to be able to prove who asked them.

The signature was Aldane's.

He sat with it for a long time, after Ferra had gone to bed.

Duke Aldane. Who had given him a box of carved wooden soldiers at his farewell banquet, eight years ago, and had seemed to find the gift very funny. Who had bought a survey of a baron's hill, found out what was under it, and then never reported a word of it to the Crown, which required every find of that kind reported to it and took the concealment of one extremely seriously.

He turned it over and over, and every way he turned it, it came out the same.

Aldane could never raise the mine against him. Not in court, not in a corridor, not in a whisper to the Crown, because to say he knew what was under Grayrock was to say he had known for years and had hidden it, and that was a charge that could finish even a duke. So the mine could never be a weapon in Aldane's hand, ever. And Emeric could not raise it against Aldane either, because to prove what Aldane had concealed he would have to open the working and bring up the stone, and the covenant said he could not open the working.

Two men holding the same knife by opposite ends, and neither of them able to pull.

He could see that there was more. He had the name now, for the money. And Keller had told him two years ago, in a plain room in the capital, that the road south had been kept dark before Kaldo was worth the trouble of silencing. And Ferra had told him long before that, in writing, with a date on it, that Wexler had paid people who were already on their way. Three facts, all of them his, all of them handed to him by people who had been right when he was wrong. He could see that they made a shape. He laid them side by side in his head the way Ferra had laid out the payments, and looked at them for an hour, and could not see what the shape was, and there was nothing to pull on.

Whether Aldane was the head of it, or only a hand, he could not tell. He did not let himself guess.

He found Ferra at her table in the morning, before the clerks came in, and he said it once, plainly, with nobody else in the room.

"You were right about the ore price. Four years ago. I should have listened."

She did not look up at once. She finished the line she was writing and set the quill down beside it.

"It's easier to see the second time," she said.

It was generous, and it was not true, and they both knew it was not true, and he let her have it.

She went back to the records. He stood in the doorway for a moment longer, and saw her glance, without seeming to, at the shelf where the older books were kept, where a ledger from his first year sat with a line in it in her hand and a date beside the line, about Wexler, and men who were already coming. That one was not paid. That one was still in the book.

He went down the stairs. He had a name, and a fortune that he could not dig, and the beginning of a shape with nothing to take hold of, and all three of them were in a box on his table with the lock broken, where anybody who came in could see them.
