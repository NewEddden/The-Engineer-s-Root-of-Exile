Chapter 330 - The Same Breath

He had put it behind the door at ten o'clock that morning, on the K Amara's after apron with the knights going off the rail below him in pairs, and he had stood on it for nearly four hours like a man standing on a trapdoor in a room full of people. Now he took his weight off it.

There was no trick to it. There never had been. For thirteen years the work had been the keeping: the mage's half out through his hands where a tower man would expect it, the knight's half into his arms only where nobody was looking, and never the two at once where anybody could feel them. The thing itself was nothing. It came up out of the middle of him the way warmth comes into a cold room when somebody opens the stove, into his legs and his back and both hands, the bound one and the empty one, and out of the empty one into the air of the square in the same instant, where it stood and waited for him to tell it something. It had never been two cups. It was one, and he had been drinking from the near side of it all day so that nobody would see how deep it went.

The cold went out of his feet. The ache went out of his arms, not all of it, enough. The water round his boots, which had been cold, was only wet.

Twenty yards up the slope Augustine's head came up a little, the way a dog's head comes up at a sound in another room.

Emeric did not draw anything.

Augustine tried him the way he had tried everything that day, small and quick, to hear where he rang. A fire out of his bare left hand at Emeric's face, glyphless, no wider than a plate; and Emeric felt it gather and turned it the other way with nothing in front of his hand at all, no ring, no light, only his open palm and the fire going out over the water a yard short of him with a sound like a sheet shaken once. And he was not standing where it had been aimed. While his hand was killing it his legs had taken him three yards to the left across the wet setts in one long step, a knight's step, the water going up off his boots on either side in two grey fans and coming down behind him.

The hand and the legs. One breath, and no seam in it anywhere.

Augustine sent two more, from either side, so close together that a man with a glyph to draw would have had time for one. Emeric killed the first by feel moving, and the second he did not trouble to kill; he stopped. He put his weight down into his left foot and planted it, all of it at once, so that he stopped dead on the wet stone in the middle of a stride, and the sett under his heel cracked across with a sound like a knuckle; and the fire went past in front of him through the place his stride would have carried him, and into the cookshop's wall, and blacked a yard of plaster.

The windows saw it. He could hear them see it. There was no indrawn sound this time from all those sills; a whole hill, which had been not making a sound for half an hour, went on not making it in a different way. On the chandler's lane steps the man who had said there wasn't a ring opened his mouth and shut it again.

His sword lay behind him among the plate, twenty feet off, in the running water on its belt where he had dropped it. He put his left hand out without looking round, and it came.

Up out of the water, off the setts, across twenty feet of air, hilt first, turning over once in the grey light, into his palm; and the belt slid off it in the air and fell back into the gutter's run behind him with a slap. Nothing had touched it. There had been no ring, no violet, nothing. A sword had got up out of a puddle and gone to a man's hand because he wanted it to.

He had kept that out of every room in the kingdom since his first year in Kaldo. It went across the fountain square in front of the whole hill in a count of one, and he found, closing his fingers on the grip, that he did not mind at all.

On the chandler's roof, in his sense, Ward stood up. Ward had told fifty knights not to move, and had said it once, and was standing up.

Augustine had stopped casting. He was not looking at the sword, nor at the cracked sett, nor at the hand. He was looking at Emeric, all of him, the way he had looked at nothing else all day; and his long pale face had gone very still, the eyes nearly white in it; and Emeric, who could feel him from twenty yards as plainly as a man can feel the heat off a stove with his eyes shut, felt something in that great cold note turn over like a page. Older than fear, and tireder: a sum the man had stopped working a long time ago, come out at last, in front of him, to the number he had been afraid of when he began it.

"Both," said Augustine. He said it quietly, to himself, the way a man reads a word off a stone. "Of course. Of course you are."

Then, more quietly still, so that only Emeric heard it: "Then we were right about her."

Emeric did not know all of what he meant. He knew that the man had understood something just now about his mother and about him, together, before anybody else on that hill had understood anything at all; and he knew he would not be told it.

Augustine turned his head, for the first time that day, and looked behind him at the line along the top of the square.

"Sir," he said, to the big fair man in plain mail. "If you'd be so kind."



The knight came.

He came the way the old ones came, made on the floor and twenty years at it: straight, and fast, and with no doubt in him at all. The silver lit on his jaw as he started, a hard white line up out of the mail's collar to the ear, and the setts went away under his boots ten yards at a time; a sword in his right hand that had cost somebody's grandfather a farm. He came down the dry high side of the square in three strides and was onto the wet in the fourth, and he did not look at the man in white and gold as he passed him. He looked at Emeric's bound hand. A knight goes for the hurt side. Everybody teaches it.

Emeric reached for him with nothing, the way he had reached for the sword, and took hold of the whole of him at once.

It went heavy in his hand the way a rope goes heavy when the load leaves the ground. Then heavier. The blank in the forge cellar on its fouled chain, a ton of steel, Bram's bread put into his palm on the step afterward without a word: that was what it was like, and then it was more than that, because the man felt himself taken and did what every knight in the world does at a shove, which is drive himself down into his own feet.

But he was in the middle of his stride, one boot in the air and the other just leaving the setts; and a man in the air has nothing under him to brace on.

Emeric pulled.

The knight came off his feet. He came down the last of the slope through the air at a man's height with his arms going out and his sword going away from his hand end over end, the silver blazing up his jaw as he fought it with everything he had and had nothing to fight it against; and the windows saw a Vein-silvered knight of the old blood picked up off a street by nobody and drawn through the air like a bucket on a rope. Emeric turned his hip into him as he arrived. It was the oldest thing on the knights' square, the throw Declan had put him on his back with three times in one morning on the knights' square before he learned it, the other man's weight taken onto the hip and given somewhere else to go; and he put the knight's weight over his hip and the whole of the pull with it, and let go, and the man went on past him and over the basin's lip into the basin.

The water went up in a grey sheet and came down over all of them.

When it cleared the knight was sitting up in the basin under the bronze pipe, with the water to his chest and the pipe's rope of water coming down on his head and shoulders, and the silver going out along his jaw, staring at nothing. He did not try to get up. He sat there, a big fair man with a face like a shut door that had just been opened by somebody else, and let the pipe rain on him.

Emeric's hands were shaking a little. Not his head. A morning's carrying, not a night's; he could feel the price of it in his arms and his thighs and the backs of his legs, and he let it be there.

Somewhere above the square a woman laughed, high and once and helplessly, the way people laugh when they have watched a cart go into a river.



The line broke first at its end, as lines do.

A man by the open garden gate fired a copied fire-tube. Emeric felt him decide to; a sour grey note, very young, that had just seen the strongest man in his lord's household thrown into a fountain and had to do something with his hands. The tube went off with a flat bang and a gout of brown smoke, and the ball went into the cookshop's shutter a long way to the right of anything; and on the bang, because a bang is what crossbowmen wait for, a dozen bolts came down the slope in a ragged flight, and he put a plate up in front of himself with nothing drawn on it and heard them ring off it and drop into the water.

Then the forty were coming. Not fast, not together, the way men come who have fired and now feel they must go after what they fired at, because standing still after it is worse.

"Hold," said Ward.

He said it off the chandler's roof into the open air with no set, in the voice he used on a whole yard at once, and every knight on every roof round the square heard it, and so did the forty, and some of them stopped. Not all. And the rest of the roofs had self-loaders on the leads under their eaves and forty men walking into the open below them, and Emeric could feel every one of those knights waiting for Ward to say the other thing.

He did not want forty of the capital's boys dead on the setts of the fountain square. He had come down to the square for one man.

The water was there. It lay over the lower half of the square where he had thrown it at noon, a shining sheet from the basin to the gutter, round his boots. It was not enough. So he took what was there and sent it back up the slope against its own run, low, a finger deep, as wide as the square, over the cracked sett and the dry high side, the way a tide comes up over a flat; and where there was not enough he made the rest. Out of the air, out of nothing, at ten times the price of moving it. He paid without counting. He had not had so much to pay with in his life.

It went up over the dry setts under the forty men's boots and round them, and up to the garden wall's foot, and in through the open gate a yard onto the first terrace's gravel, where two more men stood holding the gate. It stopped two steps short of the cookshop's steps, where Arthur sat on Brey with Bren over them. It stopped short of the mouth, where Mira stood. And it went round Augustine.

He left the man an island: a ring of dry stone two yards across, under the garden wall, with Augustine standing in the middle of it in his wet white and gold and the water going round him on either side like a stream round a rock. He did not want current within a yard of that right hand. He did not know what it would do to what was in it.

Then the part he had been taught on a cold morning on another world, by a man in a hard hat who had thought it too obvious to need saying twice.

He tore the sheet in two. Halfway up the square, across its whole width, he pulled the water back off a strip of stone a yard wide and held it open, so that the setts in that strip lay bare and wet and empty; and the top of the square was one sheet and the bottom, where he stood ankle deep, was another, and there was nothing at all between them to carry anything from one to the other. A break in the line. The man in the hard hat would have nodded at it and said nothing, which was what he did when he liked a thing.

Lightning was not his. It came at three times the cost, like water, and he did not need much of it. He laid it into the top sheet thin and even, the whole width at once, so that no stretch of it held more than any other; just enough to put a man on his back and keep him there a while, and not a grain over.

The forty went down.

They went down the way men go down on new ice, all at once and badly, the crossbows going out of their hands and the fire-tubes clattering on the setts; and they lay in a finger of water along the top of the square, and by the gate, and on the terrace gravel, twitching a little, like dogs dreaming. Nobody screamed. There was not enough left in any of them to scream with. The two at the gate sat down against the gateposts, one each side, as if they had arranged it.

He let the strip close. The top sheet came down the slope across the bare stone and joined the bottom one and ran on past his boots toward the gutter, as it had wanted to all along, carrying a crossbow bolt and somebody's hat.

He counted them, because he always counted. Forty along the top. Two at the gate. One in the basin, who had watched the whole of it with the pipe raining on his head. Every note there, every one beating, sour and frightened and very much alive.

When he breathed in, the square tasted thick.

He knew the taste. The dome at the end of a long day, by the tree, when the air under it lay heavy on the tongue like the smell of rain before it comes. What he had made out of nothing did not cost nothing; most of what he had paid for it went back into the air afterward, slow, and lay there the way smoke lies in a room after the fire is out. He had never made so much at once in his life. There was nobody else in that square who would know what it was.



Augustine came at him then with everything, because there was nothing left to keep it for.

No more tapping. The man let go of whatever he had been holding back for ninety years, and the fire came out of his left hand in a wall, and under the fire the setts heaved, a long ripple running down the slope through the sand under the stones like something swimming under a blanket, to throw Emeric's feet from under him; and on top of both a pressure in the air at the level of his head that he did not understand, and had no time to.

He did not stop to answer any of it. That was the whole of it, and he understood it as he did it. He killed the fire by feel with his hand out in front of him and his legs taking him up the slope at a run while he did it; and where the ripple came under him he planted, one foot, the sett splitting, and rode it the way a man rides a wave by standing still on it; and the thing at the level of his head he went under, low, a knight's duck, still killing the last of the fire, still running. There was no moment in it where the one half stopped and the other started. Every hybrid Augustine had ever fought or heard of had stumbled there, between the out and the in. He had been looking for the stumble all day. It was not there to find.

His Eminence goes for whatever's holding you up, Brey had said, on the cookshop's step, under Arthur. He'd watched him do it nine years.

Nothing was holding him up now but himself.

Augustine stooped as he came, his left hand going down to the stones at his feet to lay it flat on the water as he had laid it at twenty to two; and the stones at his feet were dry, because Emeric had made them dry. His hand found dry granite. There was a breath while he understood that too.

Then Emeric was on the island with him.

A yard. The skin was up over the man close as a coat, the one the charged edge had chewed through at noon; and the sword in Emeric's left hand was plain steel, and he did not light it. He hit the skin with the fist round the hilt instead, the weight from the ground up through the planted foot and the turning hip and into the knuckles, and at the last instant before the knuckles arrived the rings, down into the bone and the flesh over it, the outermost to give first and the next a hair after, the joints left free; three hundred a morning on the cart shed's post with Ward counting, and every one of them for this. The skin went like ice on a trough when a boot comes down on it. The second blow was short and came up under the ribs on the right side, where the low shoulder was, and he pulled it at the end, because he knew to the inch what was under there and how old it was.

Augustine sat down.

He sat down against the foot of the garden wall on his dry island with his breath going out of him in one long low note, like a pipe when the bellows stops, and his white and gold spread round him on the stones; and Emeric stood over him with the point of the sword an inch from the hollow of his throat.

Augustine looked down along the blade at it.

"It isn't lit," he said.

"No."



From a yard he was old.

The man of fifty was gone. Emeric had never been this close to him for this long, with the skin down and the breath out of him and nothing left over to hold his face up with; and what looked up at him out of it now was a man of more than ninety, the bones very near the surface, the skin round the pale eyes gone fine and soft and creased like a glove that has been worn a long time. The cut across his shoulder had stopped bleeding. His grey hair had dried in tufts. He looked at the sword, and at the bound hand, and at Emeric's face; and then he laughed, a little, without much breath, as a man laughs at the end of a long joke against himself.

Then he opened his right hand.

It took him a long time. The fingers came open as if they had forgotten how, one and then the next, stiff and pale and seamed across the palm with something old that had never healed right, and the hand lay open on his knee on the white and gold.

In it lay a stone. Small, grey-white, rounded, no bigger than a walnut; with far down inside it a little light, warm, the colour of a lamp seen through a hand.

The bead at Emeric's wrist rang against it like a glass answering another glass across a room.

"Your mother," said Augustine. "All of her that matters."

He said it low. The windows did not hear it. Nobody heard it but Emeric, who had known since the river road with his bare hand in the mud, and had not let himself know, and knew now.

"You'll want her," said Augustine. "You can have her. I'll leave her on the basin's lip, and walk up that road, and nobody on those roofs will shoot an old man in the back. That's the whole of it. That's all I want." He looked at the sword's point, and then past it, kindly. "You're not going to answer me. You never did answer anybody, did you, even at seven. You stood at your father's elbow and listened."

Emeric said nothing.

"Then I'll talk. I've talked to worse." Augustine shifted his back against the wall, slowly. "You think it was hate. Everybody does. It was an order, and it was right, and it covered her line. The whole of it." He let that sit a little. "It still does. It doesn't lapse because I'm tired, or because she's in my hand. Every one of you." The pale eyes came up. "Your brother. The little one. He's where I left him, on the shelf, within reach of my hand, for twenty-eight years. Nobody ever thought to move him."

Waylon was in Kaldo. He had been in Kaldo since the summer, behind Ferra's walls, holding the town with her and Armand. Emeric thought that, very clearly, as a man puts his hand on a pocket to feel a purse is still there; and it was not enough, and he knew it was not enough, and put it away.

"Who sent you the road?" he said.

It was the first thing he had said since the helm came off. Augustine smiled at it, very slightly, the way he had smiled once that day already.

"Ask your brother what the Light showed him."

And that was all. Emeric waited, and the man did not say anything more about it, and did not mean to, and Emeric put it beside Brey's nine years and the false tracks in the snow and did not look at it.

"You think we're afraid of your ships," said Augustine. "We're not. Ships are wood and stones, and stones are ours. Nobody at Ostrel lies awake over a ship." His eyes went down the square, to the mouth, where Mira stood in her black suit with her spear in her left hand. "It's her. Somebody's girl out of a back lane, who walks into a tower in the dark with no floor behind her, and knows she can. A world can stand a great many things. It can't stand a commoner who can and knows she can. Not for long. Nothing built on who can and who can't was ever built to stand that."

His left hand lay on his thigh, and he looked at it, and flexed it slowly, open and shut, the hand he blessed with and cast with and held his reins in.

"I aimed at you," he said. "Did anybody ever tell you that? Not at her. At the boy. Because she'd put herself in front of it, and she did." He looked at the open right hand on his knee, and the stone in it. "She took the arm for it. I've blessed with the other one since. A mind can be taken whole, and a body can be mended, if you know how. I was going to put her back. Afterward. Owned." He said the word without weight, as a clerk says a sum. "Then your father came up the road with every man in that castle who could sit a horse, and I felt him coming a mile off, and I had to leave her. I laid her straight. I had time for that and for nothing else." A small breath. "So I wanted the cocoon. The crystal your father sealed her in. The first queen wouldn't give it me. She's the only person in this city who ever refused me anything to my face."

"There were four more," said Emeric. "The year after."

"Four." Augustine's mouth moved, with contempt, not for him. "Lesser men, and your father's food taster. Nobody wanted you dead, child. You were worth more breathing."

He lifted his chin a little against the wall, toward the windows and the roofs and the hill, and his voice went up, not loud, never loud, only enough to carry, so that the square heard it again: the chancel voice.

"So. You've beaten an old man in a square. And tomorrow somebody has to decide who can and who can't, or everybody tries; and you know what everybody trying looks like, because you've read the same books I have. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me what's better."



He had lived in a world with no Light in it at all.

No mana, no stones, no floor in a Circle's cellar. He had been a boy and a man there, and it had had all of it anyway: the same wars, and the same men deciding who could and who couldn't, by skin and by birth and by what a man's grandfather had owned; the same floors of dead, under other gods and under none, and nobody upstairs had come down for any of them. People did not change from one world to the next. Hate did not need a Church; greed was older than any book. Whatever sat in the sky over either world had watched all of it, if it watched, and let it run.

He did not say any of that. He would not have said it to anybody in that square but two women who were not in it.

"You didn't stop war," he said. "You owned it."

He said it for the windows. He had never in his life had so many people listening to him, and he found he did not mind that either.

"Hate doesn't need your Church. It doesn't need anybody's. Greed's older than your Light and it'll outlive it. So I don't put my hope upstairs." He moved the sword's point an inch, toward the town below the square, toward everything south of it. "I put it in a school."

Augustine looked at him, interested.

"Those three." Emeric tipped his head at the mouth, and at the cookshop's steps, without taking the point off the man's throat. Mira across the road with her spear. Bren on the top step with his blade down. Arthur sitting on the narrow man, in black plate with nothing on the breast of it, no crest and no house, and his visor turned toward them. "Three commoners' kids. No rite. They came down on one of your towers in the dark this week on canvas wings and took it without a shot. Your Light needed a floor of dead every winter to make one of them. I needed a yard and patience."

On the cookshop's step Arthur, who could hear every word, sat up very straight, and Brey under him said something short.

Emeric lifted his eyes past the garden wall, past the gardens and the empty palace and the crown wall, to the summit, where the raw pale scar was.

"You built that to tell people where they stand. A priest wrote them a letter and they all walked out of it. Not one dead. That's what they think of your order when somebody gives them a choice."

Augustine was quiet for a while. When he spoke again it was to Emeric only, low, the voice for the square put away.

"You talk like a man who's watched the world end."

"Twice," said Emeric.

The man's mouth moved; a little dry acknowledgment, a teacher marking a pupil's flourish in the margin and letting it stand. He took it for what anybody would have taken it for. Emeric let him.

Then he leaned in, a few inches, over the sword, and said the last thing quietly, so that nobody on the whole hill heard it but the man under the wall.

"And when you go home, he reads you. Every room. Everything you failed at."



Augustine had thought it.

Emeric could see that he had. He had thought it in the house above the falls in the nights since the cathedral came down, and on the white hull in the summer, and very likely a great many times before that, for years, in rooms Emeric would never see; he had thought it the way a man thinks of the stair down to a cellar he will one day have to go down. He had never heard it said. Nobody had ever been in a position to say it to him.

His face did not move much. Something under it did. The great cold note that had come down the falls road at noon like weather down a valley went thin, all through, the way ice goes thin under a boot before it goes; and for the length of a breath, sitting against the garden wall with his ruined hand open on his knee, the cardinal of Randillia looked down at the stone in his palm as if he were not sure whose it was, or whose hand it was in.

"Yes," he said. Very low. "He will."

Then the pale eyes came up again, and there was nothing kind left in them anywhere.

"I can burn her," said Augustine. "Before you can cross that yard. One breath. She goes with it, and every room she has." He tipped the stone off his right palm into his left, slowly, so that Emeric could watch it go. "You'll have to choose, child. That's all that's ever been wrong with you. You never once in your life could choose."

He threw it.

Not at Emeric. Up. An underhand toss off the left palm, high, out over the basin, the way a man throws a coin into a well; and the left hand came round after it open, and the fire came out of it, glyphless, a long hot tongue going up after the small grey thing turning over in the air above the fountain square.

He had one breath.

In it he did two things, and they were one thing, as everything had been since twenty to two. He reached for the fire with his empty hand, the sword already falling from it, and turned it, by feel, all of it, the whole long tongue of it from the root in Augustine's palm to its tip a foot under the stone; and in the same breath, with nothing, with no hand at all, he reached past the fire for the stone and took hold of it.

It did not go heavy. It went light. It came down out of the air toward him as if it had been waiting a long time for somebody to ask, and the bead at his wrist rang out against it so loudly in his sense that he would have turned his head at it if there had been time to turn anything. The fire he had turned went out against itself above the basin, and not cleanly; nothing that big turns cleanly by feel. What was left burst over the water in a flat hot clap, and the basin and the lower square went up in steam all at once, white, rolling, thick as wool, from wall to wall.

The stone came into his left hand in the white, warm, and he closed his hand on it.

It was her.

He did not know how he knew. He had nothing of her before his seventh birthday but a wall he had stood at a thousand times; and the wall did not open now, and nothing came through it. He knew anyway, the way a man knows a voice in the next room before he knows whose it is. The bead he had cut from her stone had held the shape of a room somebody had walked out of; and this was the somebody. Small, and fine, and clear, and warm in his fist, against his chest, beside the bound hand.

In the steam, up the slope, the great cold note was moving.

Not toward him. Away, in through the open gate and up onto the first terrace, past the two men sitting against the gateposts, and up the steps to the second; slowly, at first, and then faster, an old man running. Emeric felt him go, one terrace and then the next, up through the gardens where he had played as a boy, toward the empty palace and the crown wall, and whatever lay beyond it.

He let him go. The stone was in his hand.

Only, before the man was out of reach, he laid the finest thread he had along after him, finer than the one Brey had laid down the falls road that morning; a measuring line with nothing on the end of it, that nobody but a delver would feel, and perhaps not one; and he let it pay out up the gardens behind the running note, and did not pull on it.

Then his knees went, and he sat down in the water.



Ward had seen it.

All of it, from the chandler's roof, with his blade across his knees and his ribs strapped, standing, which he had told fifty men not to do. The sword out of the water. The knight off his feet, a man Ward knew the look of, through the air like a sack on a crane's hook and into the fountain. Forty men going down in a line at the top of the square as if somebody had pulled a rug. And before any of it, the thing Ward had been watching for eight years in the yard at home and had never once seen him let go of in front of anybody: the boy casting and moving in the same breath, the hand out and the legs going, and no gap. Ward had sparred hybrids, two in his life, and each of them had a gap between the one thing and the other that you could put a blade in. There was no gap.

The roofs had seen it too. He could hear them on the set, the forty off the aprons, nobody saying anything, which was worse than talk.

And half the windows. Every face on that side of the hill.

The steam was going off the square now in long rags toward the lane. He had heard the boy's voice come up out of it, once, while it was thickest, quite level, not loud: "Let him go." And he had looked at the white figure going up the garden terraces at an old man's run, three terraces now, four, its robes dark to the knee with water, and at his own chalk on the K Amara's board at five that morning, NOT AT HIM, not while his right hand's shut. The hand was open now, and empty. Nobody had made a rule for after.

He had not given an order. Nobody had fired. That would have to do.

His mouth was wrong.

He noticed it when he breathed in to speak. His tongue was thick, as if he had held a copper on it, or eaten too much new bread too fast; a close heavy taste, like the air in a bakehouse at four in the morning with the ovens going, but no smell of bread. He spat over the parapet. It did not help. He thought it was the lightning, and then he thought it was not; lightning smelt of something sharp, and he knew that smell from a hundred storms and the fight in the cloud. This was not sharp. This lay on the tongue.

He did not know what it was. He put it away with the rest.

"Osric," he said, into the set. "The top of the square. Forty and two. Disarm them where they lie. Bind them if they can stand, leave them if they can't. Two on the fountain with self-loaders and don't let that one out of it till I say." He got up off his knee. "Roofs stay on the roofs. Watch the tower."

"The tower," said Osric.

"The tower."

He went down the chandler's back stair three steps at a time with his ribs going at every one and out into the lane and round into the square. The water was over his boots at the foot of it. The steam lay low here still, at knee height, rolling off the basin, and he went through it to the place where the black plate lay in the shape of a taken-apart man; and the boy was sitting beside it in the water in his padded undersuit, with his knees up and his back against nothing, his bound right hand against his chest and his left fist shut beside it.

He was grey. Ward had seen him grey before, after the black keel's night, on the dyer's roof. This was greyer.

"He's gone up the gardens," said Emeric.

"I know."

"I let him."

"I know."

"I've got her," said Emeric, and looked down at the left fist as if it surprised him; and Ward looked at it too and did not ask anything, because there would be time to ask, and because he had carried a boy of seven into a castle yard once and had not been told what he was carrying then either.

"Can you stand?"

Emeric thought about it honestly. "No."

Ward put his hand to the set's stud at his jaw. "Fletcher."

The answer came at once; Fletcher had been on the line since ten. "Here."

"The square. Now. In over the south lane, low, down below the basin, keel in the wet. Ramp down before you're down." A breath. "Guard up."

There was the smallest of pauses, the length of a carpenter deciding not to say that he knew where the guard was.

"Guard's up," said Fletcher. "Two minutes."



The tower on the crown wall stood above the gardens on the hill's east shoulder with its two bombards upright on the leads and its chest under them, cold and held; and Ward stood in the water below the basin with his hand on the boy's shoulder and watched it the whole two minutes.

A bombard did not care about a chest's ring. Its ball went where it was thrown. A ship down in that square, sitting still on her keel in the water while a man carried another man up her ramp, was the easiest shot that tower's crew would get all winter; and Ward knew it, and Fletcher knew it, and Ward had told him to come anyway, because the boy could not stand and the square had a hundred windows round it that would all have a story by tonight, and he would rather it was the end of this one.

The crew were at the parapet. He could see them. Eight or nine men along the leads in the grey, and the two grey mages; and the nearer bombard had begun, very slowly, to come round on its pivot, the way it would come round for a ship.

Then it stopped.

They were not looking at the square. Ward followed their faces. They were looking down into the gardens below their wall, at the terraces, where a man in white and gold was going up the last steps under them at a stumbling run with his wet robes in his left hand, past the box hedges and the dry fountains of the upper terraces, alone; with nobody after him. Nobody at all. And every man on that tower stood at the parapet and watched the cardinal of Randillia, who had blessed their mothers and their banners and every one of their feast days, run up past them through an empty palace's garden with his skirts in his fist.

The bombard did not move again.

The K Discovery came in over the roofs of the south lane so low that Ward felt her in his teeth, her lift stones in a violet smear under her, and put her keel down in the water below the basin with a long hiss and her ramp already coming down off her flank before she had stopped. Fletcher in the cockpit glass with his hand on something Ward could not see and did not need to.

Ward picked the boy up.

Over his shoulder, as he had once before, the whole weight of him, which was a great deal more than a boy of seven's and less than Ward had braced for; and his ribs said everything they had to say about it, and he let them, and said nothing. Emeric said, into his back, "I can walk," in a voice that did not believe it, and Ward said, "No," and went up the ramp.

"Mira," he said, on the set. "Aboard. Now."

She came across the square from the mouth at a run with her spear in her left hand, the first she had moved all day, and went up the ramp past him without a word.

"Arthur. Bring him."

Arthur got up off the narrow man. Bren took him under one arm and Arthur under the other, and they brought him across the wet setts between them with his feet going, like a man being helped out of an inn; and Matthias Brey looked at the K Discovery's flank as he came, and at the long iron can on her arm with its mouth toward the lane, and said, quite clearly, to nobody, "Is that going to be loud?"

"Yes," said Bren.

The king was at the head of the ramp.

He had come down from the rail. He stood in the hatchway with his collar up and his hands at his sides, and looked at his son over Ward's shoulder; and Ward, going past him with the boy, saw his face, and had known that face most of his life, and had not seen it look like that since a night in a castle yard.

"I felt it," said Harrold. "From the rail." He did not say what. "Both. At once." Then, after a step, as Ward laid the boy down on the bench under the cockpit glass: "How long have you known?"

"Eight years, sir."

The king took that. He took it the way he took everything, with no sign on his face that it had cost anything.

"They told me there was always a gap," he said. "The old men in the tower. Every man like me has it, they said, and always will."

"There is, sir," said Ward. "For the rest of us."

Behind them the ramp came up. Bren and Arthur put Brey down on the deck plates against the bulkhead, and Mira sat down beside him, carefully, on her good side, and put her spear across her knees; and up in the cockpit Fletcher said, without turning round, "Hold on to something," in the voice he used to apprentices when the beam was swinging.

Ward held on to the bench, and to the boy.

The noise was not like anything. It came up out of the iron can on her arm and through her frames and her deck and the soles of everybody's feet at once, a great tearing scream with a roar under it like the sea going into a cave; and she went. Out of the square low over the south lane with the steam blown flat behind her off the basin and every shutter on the lane banging, and down onto the boulevard at the lane's foot, the one broad straight street on that side of the hill, that the old kings had cut from the second wall up to their gardens for processions and that everybody had called the boulevard for two hundred years though it had never had a tree; and up the length of it a roof's height over the setts, screaming, the windows going by on both sides in a long grey smear and the second wall's gate coming at them, and then Fletcher's hand moved and she put her nose up and went into the climb under the burn, and the whole capital fell away under her like something dropped.

On the bench the boy was laughing.

Ward bent down to him, because he could not hear anything, and put his ear nearly to the boy's mouth.

"It's so loud," Emeric was saying. He was laughing the way a man laughs at a wedding when he has had too much and is very tired. "We built her to be quiet. I spent a year making her quiet. And the best thing on her is the loudest thing anybody's ever heard." He laughed again, with his eyes shut. "Ferra's going to want the cost per mile."

Then he was asleep, or something past it. The laugh went out of his face like a lamp being carried into another room. His left fist stayed shut on his chest.

The king had knelt down on the deck plates on the bench's other side. He put his hand out, toward his son's head, and Ward saw it stop in the air an inch above the boy's hair and stay there, as if it had been told not to and did not remember who had told it.

Then it went down, and rested there.

Fletcher brought her up under the K Amara at a quarter past two with the can glowing a dull red the length of it, and put her alongside the after apron, and nobody said a word about the burn rule then or later.



Imani was on the apron before the ramp was down.

She had come across from the K Endeavor on the mail boat the moment Saban told her the set had died in the middle of a word, and had been standing on the apron in the wind for twenty minutes with her bag and Bettany beside her and her hands in her sleeves; and she came up the ramp past Ward and the king without seeing either of them and knelt by the bench.

She did the face first, and the breath, and his pulse at the neck with two fingers, and then sat back on her heels and looked at him the way she looked, through the skin, into the room of him, for a long time. Ward had stopped asking what she saw when she did that. She came out of it with her mouth tight.

"Empty," she said. "Nearly. More than he's ever been in front of me." She looked up at Ward. "What did he do?"

"Everything," said Ward.

She did not argue with that either. She unwound Emeric's own strap off the right hand, which was black and stiff with blood from inside, and looked at the hand, and read it, with her thumb over the two knuckles and her eyes half shut; and Ward watched her face, because the hand was not a thing he needed to see twice.

"Bettany," said Imani. "Here. Hold the wrist. Now, while he's out, before it's begun to take." To the knuckles, or to herself: "Down and in. Both."

They set it there on the bench, the two of them, in the hold of the K Discovery with the can ticking as it cooled, Bettany's big hands on the wrist and the forearm and Imani's on the knuckles, drawing them up out of the back of the hand where they had been driven, one and then the other, slowly, with the boy's face not moving at all. Then the splint. Then a clean strap, soft, the long figure of eight. Then she sat back again on her heels and looked at it for a while.

"You told him," said Ward, "the third time it wouldn't knit straight."

"I told him that so he'd keep it out of things." She did not look up. "He didn't."

"Will it?"

She was quiet long enough that the king, on the bench's other side, looked at her.

"It went in in two pieces," she said. "Not twenty. It ought to have been twenty. Something packed the bone round it when he fell, hard, like a knight's goes when he's braced, and held it while it broke." She put her thumb very lightly on the strap. "He did that. Falling. Without meaning to. Without anything to do it with but himself." She breathed out. "It'll knit straight. He'll write like himself. Strapped till January. Tell him I said so when he wakes up, and tell him it was close, and don't tell him how close."

"Yes," said Ward.

"He's flat," said Imani. "Flat on his back. Four days. Five. No set, no table, no Saban, no anybody, and nobody wakes him for anything short of the ship coming down." She looked at the king then, and at Ward, one and then the other, as she looked at the families of men on her ward. "Nobody argues."

Nobody did.

She reached to open his left fist, to see what was in it, and the fist did not open. She tried once more, gently, and it did not open then either; and she looked at it a while with her fingers still round it, and did not try a third time.

"Leave it," she said, to nobody, and laid the fist back on his chest where it had been, and put his bound hand over it.

They carried him below at half past two. Ward went back down to the square at dusk on hull four's boat with his ribs freshly strapped against Imani's advice, and sat on the basin's lip with Osric and forty-two prisoners and a Vein-silvered knight in a borrowed blanket, and ate a cookshop's pie that the cookshop's owner brought out to them himself without being asked and would not take money for, and watched the tower on the crown wall all night.

The cookshop's owner stayed to see the dish come back. He had spent the afternoon under his own counter with a ball through his shutter, and had things to say about it. "Fire," he said, and put down a thumb. "That business with the knight, that's gravity, my wife says. Water in the fountain. Lightning on top of it. I've a nephew at the Circle's school in the capital, two years to get one spark to stay lit. How's a man get all that?"

"He doesn't," said the knight in the blanket. It was the first thing he had said since they fished him out. He did not look up from his pie. "Two of them are his. I felt the others go by. Slow. Dear as blood. No tower mage alive would spend that on a thing he could hire done."

"So he's no good at them."

"No."

The owner looked at the fountain, and at the forty men still sitting round it with their hands tied.

"Well," he said, and took the dish.



At first light the tower's door opened. Its crew came down through the gardens in a line in the grey, the eight and the two grey mages, with their hands empty and their coats buttoned against the cold, and the chest left on the leads behind them as cold as it had stood all week; and a little after them, up through the lower town from the gatehouse at the long bridge's town end, the gatehouse's crew too, who had not seen the square but had been told about it by midnight by everybody on the bridge. They came into the fountain square at its top, past the open gate where the forty had lain, and stopped in front of Ward on the basin's lip, and the oldest of them said that they had watched the Church's leader run up past their wall in the afternoon with his skirts in his fist and nobody after him, and that they had sat up all night after it and talked, and did not see what they were for any more. Ward took them without a shot. He sent Osric for breakfast, enough for everybody, and wrote down their names.
