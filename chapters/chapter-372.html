Chapter 372 - The Line Sits

The packet from Emberfell came down at the yard at a quarter past ten on the eleventh of November in a fine rain, and Rowena came down the gangway before they had it tied, as she came down every gangway, with one hand on the rope and the other holding her own bag, which she had never in her life let anybody carry.

She was in the green of Emberfell under an oilskin. Her hair was in the long braid with the steel rings in it, and she had a cut on the back of one hand that she'd got, she said, from a horse that thought it knew better.

"Where are they?"

"Zale's at the bay house," said Emeric. "Maeve's in the Works yard with a lightning rod. Waylon's on the afternoon train."

"Maeve's what?"

"With a lightning rod."

Rowena looked at him for a moment out of the oilskin hood. Then she looked at Ward, who had come down with him, because Emeric always went to Rowena with Ward; and Ward looked back at her, and something went between the two of them that Emeric had seen go between them before and had never been told about.

"She's teaching him," said Ward.

"Is she." Rowena put the bag down on the planking, which was a thing nobody had ever seen her do. "Well. Somebody ought to have."



The Stone Board sat at two the next afternoon in the records hall, with the doors open on the square as the Charter said they must be, and it rained the whole time.

It was the twelfth, a Wednesday. The board sat once a fortnight, and had since the spring of the crowning, and in all that time there had never been more than a dozen people on the benches at the back: the ones who came because it was free, and because it was warm, and because Prudence Fairley read the cases out and Prudence Fairley reading anything out was better than the theatre. Today there were a dozen on the benches by one o'clock. By two there were sixty, standing three deep along the back wall and in the doorway and out under the portico with their collars up, and Master Aubin had looked at them over the top of his spectacles when he came in and said to the clerk, quite audibly, "Is something happening?"

"The princes, master," said the clerk.

"Which princes?"

"Three of them."

"Ah," said Master Aubin, and sat down in the chair and took the two pencils out from behind his ear and laid them side by side on the book, parallel, and looked at nobody else for the rest of the afternoon.

Emeric sat on the back bench, where he always sat. He had taken off his coat because the hall was warm with sixty people in it, and the people on either side of him had moved along a little to give him room and then, after a while, moved back, because it was the back bench and there wasn't any room. Ferra was next to him with her own book on her knee. Rowena was on the front bench, on the far side, in the green, with her hands folded in her lap, very straight. Zale was beside her with his stick between his knees. Maeve was beside him, in a plain grey coat that Emeric had never seen her wear at court and that he suspected she had bought on Gate Avenue that morning for this, and her hair braided up out of the way, the way she wore it at six in the morning in the Works yard.

Waylon was late. He came in at five past with straw on his coat, from the train, and stood at the back by the door with his hat in his hands, until a woman on the bench in front of Emeric looked round at him and then at the bench and pushed her husband along with her elbow, and Waylon sat down beside her and said, "Thank you," and the woman said, "You're the one with the horses," and he said, "Yes," and she seemed satisfied.

There were four cases before theirs. The board took them in order, the way it took everything, and the benches, who had come for the princes, had to sit through a ropemaker's daughter's eyes, granted, healing; a Grayrock widow who wanted a stone for her son and had brought his school reports, refused, under eighteen, and Prudence had leaned over to say something and Master Aubin had written And she's to bring him back the week he turns it; and a man from Wayford who had come all the way on the train to ask whether the board would give him a stone if he paid for it in coal instead of coin, since the clause said coin, and Prudence had read the clause out to him twice, the second time slowly, and the benches had enjoyed it very much.

The fourth was Fletcher.

He hadn't entered himself. Cade had entered him, his second at the yard, three weeks ago, without telling him; and the board had had the record of service from the yard's books and the Hod's logs and the K Discovery's and the forty years before those, which Cade had copied out in a fair hand onto nineteen sheets. Prudence read the first two sheets aloud and then stopped and looked at the other seventeen and said, "It goes on like that," and the benches laughed. Fletcher was there, at the end of the front bench, because Cade had told him at noon that he'd be sacked if he wasn't. He sat with his big scarred hands on his knees and looked appalled.

"Master Fletcher," said Master Aubin. "Do you want it?"

Fletcher thought about it for a long time.

"I've a keel on the slip," he said at last, "that I'll not see finished, the way my back is. That's all I know." He looked at the floor. "Somebody'll finish it. Cade will. He's good." He looked up again. "I'd sooner finish it myself."

The four took six minutes. Granted, wrote Master Aubin. Service on the record. The hulls this town flies in. The keel on the slip. And Fletcher went out under the portico into the rain and stood there in it for a while without his hat, and Cade went out after him and stood beside him, and neither of them said anything that anybody heard.

Then Prudence turned the page.



She read all three of the entries first, one after another, before the board took any of them. That was new. She didn't ask. She just did it, in the same voice she used to tell a tram that it was full.

"Prince Zale of Randillia. Entered by the ward. Healing." She turned the sheet. "Waylon, Duke of Highcairn. Entered by himself. Service on the record." She turned it. "Princess Maeve of Randillia. Entered by herself. Service on the record." She put the three sheets down side by side in front of her, the way Master Aubin had put down his pencils, and squared them with her fingertips, and looked up, past the table and the benches, at the doors and the square and the rain.

"Before we start," she said.

Master Aubin took off his spectacles.

"I've read the clause," said Prudence. "I read it to the man from Wayford, twice. Not by coin. Not by the crown. Nobody under eighteen." She paused. "And not by birth." She turned her head and looked along the front bench, at Rowena in the green and the two beside her, and then back down the hall to the man with straw on his coat on the bench by the door, and last, without any change in her face at all, at the back bench. "That's three of the old king's children at one sitting. On one afternoon. One after the other." She looked at the steward, and at Armand, and at Master Aubin. "I'm not saying they're not owed. I don't know if they're owed. I'm saying if a carter came in here on a Wednesday with his brother and his sister and they all wanted one, I'd want to know why it wasn't the family. And I'd want it written down." She tapped the three sheets. "So I'd like the book to say, each one, by itself, why this isn't birth. Not the three together. Each."

The hall was very quiet. Emeric could hear the rain on the portico. He could hear, beside him, Ferra not writing.

"That's fair," said the steward. It was the first thing he'd said all afternoon. He was a stout man from the crown's holdings office with a cold, and he blew his nose, and wrote something down.

"It is fair," said Master Aubin. He put his spectacles back on. "Each. By itself. With its reason." He picked up one of the pencils. "And I would add that if the book cannot say why, it isn't granted."

Rowena, on the front bench, didn't move at all. Emeric saw the back of her neck under the braid, and that was all, and he knew from the back of her neck exactly what her face was doing, because he had been on his back in front of it on her practice ground at Emberfell, twice, with the whole of her household watching.



Zale's was first, because healing went first. Imani had entered it, so she got up from the table where she had a chair and went and sat on the front bench beside Rowena with her folder on her knee, and the chair at the board's end stood empty.

The healers' board had sat on him on the Monday. That was the rule: healing judged on the body, by healers, before any record. Imani read their finding out herself, because it was hers. The bleeding sickness, taken in June past the wall in thick water. The small vessels of both legs let go under the skin. The bleeding stopped since July; the vessels closed but not mended; the bones of both shins aching every day, in the healers' words, like an old tooth. Walks with a stick, a mile at most. Barred from the Band since June and from any water but a bath until August, when she'd cleared him for short dives in the bay because the bay was thin.

"Would you enter anybody with these legs?" said Prudence.

"I'd enter anybody with these legs." Imani didn't look at Zale. "I entered a ropemaker's daughter's eyes an hour ago. Same rule."

"What does it do? The stone. For legs."

"Bonded, eaten, it rebuilds him. The whole body. To what he'd be at his best." Imani turned a page in the folder. "We've seen what it does to a heart that was nearly gone. Master Armand's. And to a body that had twenty years of failing in it." She didn't say whose. Half the benches knew whose. "Vessels are easier than either."

"And then he goes in the spring," said Prudence, "with the king's ships. Past where it's thick." She said it plainly, not as a question. The convoy was no secret, only where it was going. "Where his legs went the first time."

"Yes."

"Convenient."

"It's not convenient," said Imani. "It's why he's got the legs." She closed the folder. "If he'd been a fisherman off Grayrock and done it on a herring boat, he'd be in that book today with the same legs and I'd be saying the same words. He'd be going back to sea in the spring as well, because that's what fishermen do." She looked at Prudence. "Healing first. Before any record. Your clause."

"It's not my clause."

"It's your book."

Prudence considered that. Then she looked down the front bench at Zale, who had been sitting the whole time with his hands on the head of his stick and his eyes on the floor, and said, "Do you want it?"

Zale looked up.

"I want my legs," he said.

The four took nine minutes. Imani stood in the doorway with her arms folded while they did, with the rain blowing in past her, and the clerk wrote that she had.

Granted, wrote Master Aubin. Healing first. Both legs, the vessels, from thick water. The healers' board's finding. The same for anyone. He looked at the last line a moment, and then underlined it.

Prudence read it out to the square.



Waylon's took longer, because Waylon's was the one with nothing wrong with him.

He had entered it himself, on a sheet in his own large untidy hand that Prudence held a little further from her face than the others. The record was the duchy's: two years of Highcairn held under the Charter's terms, the first duchy in the kingdom to be; the thirty-two hours kept, and the Hearing Rule; eleven parish schools open of nineteen, the rest by the date in the grant; the books of the duchy open to Ferra's office every quarter and never once late. Then the Isle. The treaty's half held with Duvrenne's people since the war; the works under the Isle to be rebuilt in the open next year, with the island families on wages and shares.

"It's a good record," said the steward, through his cold. "I'll say that. I've had the Highcairn books across my desk. They're better than ours." He blew his nose again. "But it's a record for holding land. I don't see what a stone does for holding land that a good steward doesn't."

"Time," said Waylon, from the bench.

Everybody looked at him. He hadn't stood up. He seemed to realise that he ought to have, and did, with his hat still in his hands.

"The schools," he said. "Eleven of nineteen. They'll be nineteen by the date, I can do that. But they'll want teachers that went to them, and that's twenty years. The works on the Isle. Bram says it's thirty years before they run the way he'd have them run, and I believe him." He turned the hat round. "And I'm thirty-four, and my father's been young again two years and more, and my brother's going to be twenty-five till he's a hundred." He didn't look at the back bench. "Everybody at the top of this kingdom's going to live a long time now. All of them. And whoever holds the Isle in thirty years, the people on the other side of it are going to be holding it too. Duvrenne's people. And they'll know whether the man across the table's going to be there next year." He stopped. "I'd like to be there next year. That's all. I'd like them to know I'll be there."

"And if it went to the next man?" said Armand. He had not spoken yet, all afternoon, and his voice from the end of the table was mild. "Not the king's brother. A steward. A good one."

"Then I'd want him to have it," said Waylon. "The same. And I'd want him in here asking, like me. He'd have the better case, mind. He'd not have a brother."

Prudence looked at him a long time over the top of the sheet.

"Nobody asked for you," she said. "Did they. You came in on the train and asked."

"Yes."

"Why not your brother? He's the king. He could've asked."

"Because he can't," said Waylon, quite simply. "He's the crown. You wrote it down."

The four took nineteen minutes, which was the longest the board had taken since the dyer's hip. Waylon stood in the doorway beside the steward's place while they did, because he hadn't known where else to stand, and the woman on the bench who had moved her husband for him watched him the whole nineteen minutes as if he were a horse at a sale she had a bid on.

Granted, wrote Master Aubin, at last. Service on the record. The Isle, and the duchy, held to the Charter's terms. The other side of the table should know who it is talking to in thirty years. The same for any holder who asks. And under it, in a different pencil, because the steward had asked: One against.

"Me," said the steward, to the room, and blew his nose. "Nothing against the duke. I'd have wanted a year's more books."

Prudence read it out to the square. All of it. The one against as well.



Maeve stood up before Prudence had finished turning the page.

"I'd like to say something first," she said.

"You can say it after I've read it," said Prudence. "That's the order."

Maeve sat down again.

Prudence read it. It was very short. Princess Maeve of Randillia. Entered by herself. Service on the record. Under that the record, which was three lines: the court at the capital; Emberfell in the summers; Kaldo since September, by her own choice, on the people's train, second class, paid. Prudence turned the sheet over to see if there was anything on the back. There wasn't.

"That's not a record," she said. Not unkindly. "That's an address."

"I know."

"Is there more?"

"Yes," said Maeve. "There's a great deal more. I'll not say it here."

The benches stirred. Somebody by the door said something to somebody else, and was shushed.

"The book's open," said Prudence. "That's the point of it. We wrote it on the door."

"I know that too." Maeve stood up again, and this time Prudence let her. She stood at the front bench with her hands at her sides in the plain grey coat. "I've read your book. All of it, from the first page. I came in on Monday and asked the clerk for it and sat over there and read it." She nodded at the table under the window. "And there's a case in it from the summer before the wedding, a healing case, for two stones. Not for eating. For a body. And the patient wasn't named, because the healer said she'd be named in her own hand when she had one. And the healer told the four of you who it was, in the side room, with the clerk there, and you put that in the book, and you granted it." She stopped. "I'd like the same. The side room. The four of you and the clerk. What I can do, I'll show you. And you can write in the book that I showed you, and that it was enough or it wasn't. And what it was, I'd like that to be mine to say. When I choose to say it."

Prudence looked at her.

"You read the whole book," she said.

"Yes."

"On a Monday."

"It's not very long," said Maeve. "Yet."

Emeric, on the back bench, watched his sister and thought: she found that on Monday and she's been sitting on it for two days. He looked sideways at Ferra. Ferra was looking at Maeve with an expression he'd seen on her once before, in the counting house, at a clerk who had found a mistake in her own figures and come and told her about it.

"There's a reason," said Master Aubin. It was the first thing he'd said to any of the three of them. "For the side room. In that case. The patient couldn't sign."

"There's a reason in mine," said Maeve. "I'll tell you that in the side room too."

Master Aubin looked at Armand. Armand looked at the ceiling, and then, after a moment, very slightly, nodded.

They went into the side room, the four of them and the clerk and Maeve, and shut the door. The hall waited. It was not quiet. Sixty people who had come for the princes and been made to wait an hour for them were not going to be quiet about a shut door, and they weren't; and Prudence's tram voice came through the door once, quite clearly, saying "Well, I didn't know that," and then nothing.

Emeric didn't feel anything through the wall. That was the point. Maeve would have made sure of that before she did anything. He thought about the fine humming thread at the end of her fingers in the Works yard in September, held for a breath, and the cat's fur and the curtains when she was four, and Rowena on a practice ground at Emberfell every summer for twenty years teaching a girl to throw lightning and then to never, ever do it where the court could see.

Rowena, on the front bench, sat with her hands folded and looked at the shut door, and Emeric couldn't see her face.

They were in there twenty minutes. When they came out Maeve's face was perfectly calm and her hair had come a little out of its braid at one side, and Prudence Fairley was walking in a way that Emeric had never seen her walk, which was carefully, as though she had been told the floor was wet.

The four sat down. They didn't go back into the side room to decide. They decided at the table, in eleven minutes, while Maeve stood in the doorway with her arms folded in the rain, the way everyone did.

Granted, wrote Master Aubin. Service on the record, shown to the board in the side room with the clerk present, and found sufficient by four. What was shown is hers to say. She goes with the king's ships in the spring if he takes her, and she can already do more than most who have one. He stopped. He looked at Prudence. Prudence said something to him, very low. He added a line.

Not birth. She has hidden what she is all her life because of it.

Prudence read it out to the square. She read the last line twice. The second time, she read it slowly.



Armand stayed behind afterwards. The benches went out into the rain talking, sixty of them, and the clerk sanded the book and shut it and carried it to its table under the window, and Master Aubin put his two pencils back behind his ear and went home without a word to anyone, as he always did. Armand stayed sitting at the end of the board's table with his hands round a cup of something the clerk had brought him, and Emeric came down the hall and sat in the chair next to him that Imani had left.

"She had a reason," said Armand.

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm telling you she gave it, and it was a good one, and the woman in the tram jacket understood it before I'd finished explaining." He drank. "But it's going to happen again. Not her. Somebody with nothing in the book and something real in them. Or somebody with nothing in them and a very good book." He turned the cup. "That record's an address, she said. She was right. Most people's are. The board can only weigh what's written down. It can't weigh what a person does when nobody's writing."

"No."

"The tower had a way." Armand didn't look at him. "For the novices. Before they let them near the senior stones. A master would take them in, in a delve, and build them a room, and something would happen in it. A fire. A man who'd stolen. A senior who was wrong. And the master would watch what the novice did." He was quiet. "The Church has it too. They call it a Seeing. They don't tell the novice it isn't real."

"You'd tell them."

"I'd tell them. I'd have Aubin define the room for an hour first." He put the cup down. "I'm not proposing it. I'm telling you the next one is coming, and that's what will be proposed, and someone will propose it who doesn't tell the novice. So think about it before then."



Imani asked him that night, in the long room, at nine, with her stockinged feet up on the fender and her folder on her knees and Rowena gone up to bed.

"Armand told me," she said. "About the tower. The room."

"He told me too."

"He wants to know what we'll do when somebody proposes it." She was looking at the fire. "Does it have to be an exam?"

He looked at her.

"I don't mean the board. I mean the room." She frowned at the fire, as if it had said something she didn't agree with. "If you can build a room in somebody's head, and they walk about in it, and something happens, and they choose what to do. Why does it have to be to catch them out? Why can't they just..." She lifted her hand off the folder and turned it over, looking for the word. "Pick what to do. Go where they like in it. Fall off something and start again. And it doesn't count."

He didn't say anything for a while.

He knew exactly what she was describing. He'd sat in one a thousand times as a boy, in a dark room in another life with a screen and a pair of handles, flying an aeroplane into the side of a mountain and starting again before his mother called him for tea. Later, grown, the training sessions at Meridian, which had been the same idea with a great deal more money behind it. He sat with both of those for a moment in the warm long room and said nothing about either, because there was nothing in either that he could say.

"Why?" he said instead.

"Because the first time my students cut," said Imani, "it's into somebody." She tapped the folder. "Ropemaker's girl. Grayrock widow's boy, the year he turns it. They learn on the cadaver and the bench, and the cadaver doesn't bleed, and then the first time it bleeds it's a person and their hands are shaking. I'd like their hands to have shaken somewhere first. Somewhere it didn't count."

"It can go wrong."

"I know."

"People who won't come out." He said it carefully. He'd seen it, on the other side of everything: not this, but near enough. "If it's better in there than out here. If they keep going back."

"I know," said Imani again, patiently, as if to a student. "I've thought about that. Not more than so often. A limit, and it's on their card, and the host keeps it, not them. And never anyone who doesn't know where they are. Not once. That's the whole difference from Armand's room." She looked at him. "I've been thinking about it since the summer. Since Zale's legs. Did you think I'd only just started?"

"No," said Emeric, which was not quite true.

She built the first one the following Sunday, in the dome, with Ferra as her learner, because Ferra had volunteered and because Ferra would tell her exactly what was wrong with it. It was an operating table with a lamp over it and a tray of instruments, all correct, every one, down to the scratches on the handles of the clamps. Imani had built it from her own hands' memory, and her hands remembered the instruments perfectly.

They hadn't remembered the room. There were no walls past the edge of the lamp's light, and no ceiling at all; where a ceiling should have been there was nothing, a soft grey nothing going up and up, and Ferra had stood at the table with the scalpel in her hand and looked straight up into it and said, "Oh, no," and come out.

"There's no ceiling," she said, opening her eyes on the dome's board.

"I know," said Imani, writing it down. "I never look at the ceiling."

"Everybody looks at the ceiling. Your patient's looking at the ceiling. That's all your patient does."

Imani stopped writing.

"Oh," she said.

She called it the Try-Again, because the first one had been so bad. Everybody did, after that.



Zale's stone was one of the ward's: a pod stone from the year before, small, grey, waiting in Imani's cabinet with a tag on it. She gave it to him in the bay house kitchen on the Friday, with Ilenne watching from the pool door and Rowena at the table with a cup of tea she wasn't drinking.

"Against your skin," said Imani. "All the time. Sleep with it. Bathe with it, I don't care, it doesn't mind water. Delve it every night with me or Armand for the first week, then on your own." She put it in his hand and closed his fingers round it. "Six weeks. Then we'll see."

"Six weeks," said Zale. He looked at his closed hand. "Then what?"

"Then you swallow it, and you sleep, and you wake up, and then we'll see." She said the last part slowly, so that it went in. "Not before. I'm not promising your legs. I'm promising I've seen it do harder."

Tess had come down with Paskin and a note plate in a padded box, because she wanted the figure of his note before the stone and after it, for the book, and Zale put his palm on the rim and held still for the count with the look of a man having his portrait painted against his will. The figure came out on the Kaldo paper long and windy, all fine branching lines going out to one side, like a gust drawn in pencil. Tess wrote Zale, before and the date, and put it in the folder.

Rowena watched all of it and said nothing.

She went home on the Saturday packet. Emeric and Ward walked her down to the yard. The rain had stopped in the night, and the slips were steaming in the thin sun, and the K Tam's keel was on the far slip in its first ribs, with Cade's men on it, and Fletcher on it too, on a trestle, swearing at something.

At the foot of the gangway she stopped, with the bag in her hand.

"That woman," she said. "In the tram jacket."

"Prudence Fairley."

"She said it was three of the old king's children." Rowena looked up at the packet's envelope, and the riggers on the lines. "In front of sixty people and a square. To my face, and to the back of yours." She was quiet. "I sat at your father's table for years, and at my own for nearly thirty, and watched men decide who got things. Nobody ever said that out loud. Not once."

"You didn't like it."

"I hated it," said Rowena. "I sat there and hated her, all the way through Waylon's, and halfway through Maeve's." She picked up her bag. "And then she read out the last line twice."

She went up the gangway with one hand on the rope. At the top she turned round.

"Tell your sister," she said, "I'll want to see what she showed them. At Emberfell. In the summer. Where nobody's looking."

"You'll be the only one," said Ward.

"I was always the only one," said Rowena, and went in.
