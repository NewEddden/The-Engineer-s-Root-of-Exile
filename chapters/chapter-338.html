Chapter 338 - Home Under Glass

The four in grey had died on the sixteenth of December, in the same hour, in the end bay of the K Endeavor's hold, a day after Augustine went down in the square; nobody had touched them, and Imani had been in the bay when it happened, counting their blinking against her pulse, and had got to three. So there were five bodies in the hospital's cold room on the morning of the eighteenth of February, under sheets, on five of the long zinc tables Bram had made for the plague year and nobody had wanted since.

The cold room was under the east ward, down eleven steps, with a cold stone in the ceiling and its exhaust plate run out through the wall to the yard. Imani had chalked the door the night before. NOT FOR VISITORS. Under it, smaller, because she knew who would try: OR THE KING.

Bram came down at seven with his tool bag and a pair of long-handled shears off the boiler shop wall. He looked at the five sheets and did not ask which one was first.

"Plate," said Imani. "All of it. I want them as they were made, not as they were dressed."

"It's riveted."

"Then unrivet it."

He did, for an hour and a half, with a cold chisel and a punch and the shears, setting each piece on the floor along the wall in the order it came off, greaves and cuisses and the breastplate with its small gold Eye, so that by nine there were five suits of dark grey plate laid out on the flags like men who had lain down and dissolved. Only once, lifting the breastplate off the second, he stopped, and turned it over in his black hands, and ran his thumb round the inside where a padded jack should have been and was not.

"No lining," he said. "They wore it on the skin."

"They didn't feel it," said Imani.

She worked from nine until a little after two with Emeric across the table from her, and she did most of it with her eyes shut.

He had asked to be there because he had been inside one of them at the gate, by accident, and had not been able since to put what he had felt into words. So he stood across the zinc with his sense open and watched her read, and from time to time she said a thing aloud, flatly, for Fennick's boy in the corner to write down, and Emeric would look where she was looking and find she was right and he had not seen it.

The evenness first. She had seen that in December: both sides of the body the same, to the bone. Now, with the time and the cold, she went down into it, and it went further than the bone. The two lungs were the same weight. The two kidneys lay at the same height, which Emeric had never in his life seen in a body and knew from the trial he would not have seen in a thousand. The same small flaw sat on the same rib on both sides, matched to a hair. "Made from a pattern," Imani said, "and the pattern had a mistake in it, and they copied the mistake." Fennick's boy wrote that down.

Then the pain. There was a way the pain went up, which she had traced in living patients for two years and drawn in her register in red, and in these five, high in the neck, it stopped. Nothing had been cut. It went up to a place and there was no more of it, like a road on a map somebody never finished drawing.

"They'd bleed," she said. "They'd tire. A knee would go the wrong way and they'd stand on it. They'd never know."

Then the spine. She had her hand flat on the third body's back, turned on its face, and her eyes shut, and she was quiet so long that Bram put down his chisel.

"There's something along it," she said. "Little bags. Eight. Nine. Down each side, between the muscle and the bone, like a string of beans." She opened her eyes and looked across at Emeric. "They're full."

He put his own hand beside hers. Under the skin, under the cold, small and tight and bright as a pocket of sun: liquid mana, held. He knew the feel of it the way he knew the feel of his own name, because he had drawn it off the Band in a cold-walled tank and watched it run like oil. He had never felt it inside a man.

"A reserve," he said. "They'd not need to eat for it. When they ran low they'd draw."

"Somebody fills it," said Imani. "Or it fills itself from what they eat. I can't tell which, dead." She took her hand away and wiped it on her apron, which it did not need. "I'll want one of these to keep. In spirit. In a jar."

"You can have the man," said Bram. "He's not using it."

The heads last. She did not open them; she did not need to. She sat on the stool at the head of the fifth table with her two hands on either side of the pale face, as she sat with every patient who was frightened, and read down into it, and her mouth went thin.

"It's all there," she said. "Everything a brain has. The parts are all the right size. But the middle." She stopped, and found the word. "It's blank. Smooth. Like a slate that's been washed. And round the edges of it, where it meets the rest, it's not smooth at all. It's written on. Close. Rows and rows." She opened her eyes. "Like the inside of your stones. Like Tess's book."

Emeric did not need her to say whose writing. He had been inside it at the gate, with his hand on a steel rail goad: a drill, perfect, a knight's first-year drill as Ward taught it, and two glyphs, and a rule about a door; and room around it for more of the same, and none at all for anyone to stand in. A file, he had thought then, in a language nobody in this world had a word for, and had put the thought away.

The last thing she found by accident. She was drawing the sheet back up over the fifth one's chest and stopped, and turned the sheet down again to the belly, and put one finger on a place to the left of the navel.

A scar. Small, white, a little puckered, no longer than the top joint of her thumb. She went to the fourth table. The same. The third, the second, the first. Five scars, one place, one size, as alike as the rest of them.

"That's old," she said. "That's older than anything else on them. That's from before they were born."

Nobody in the room said anything for some time.

Emeric stood by the zinc table with his hands at his sides and understood it all at once, as a man understands a machine he has been staring at for an hour when one part turns and the rest turns with it. A cut in the womb, to the child, mended. Somebody had gone in. Somebody had gone in, again and again, into the bellies of women who believed whatever they had been told, and touched the thing growing there at the size of a bean, and the size of a thumb, and the size of a hand; had laid out its two kidneys at one height and closed the road the pain came up and washed the middle of its brain clean before there was anything in it to wash, and written on the edges. And had known what to write on. Had known what a cell was, and where the nerves would go before they went there. Had done it often enough to make five that matched, and how many that had not, in how many women, before there were five.

It was engineering. That was what turned his stomach. Not that it was cruel; he had seen cruel. That it was good work. It was the best work on a body he had ever seen in this world, Imani's included, and it had been done on babies, by somebody who had had a very long time to get it right.

He went up the eleven steps quickly, and out through the yard door, and was sick in the yard, where the exhaust plate had melted the snow to the cobbles in a black square, and stood with his hand on the wall.

He had written to that, last night. He had sat at the long table by Ferra's lamp and written annihilate to whatever had done this, and been quite pleased with the word, and it was on a horse going north this morning and could not be called back. He thought about the five sent to Randillia, out of however many there were, and how lightly the Church had spent them. He had been fighting a Church that read old books and burned groves and kept women in stones, and he had thought he knew its size. He had been wrong about its size.

Imani came out after a while with a cup of water and did not say anything about the snow.

"The two in the cells," she said. "The handlers. I asked them. I didn't touch them. I asked."

"And?"

"One said nothing. He's said nothing since December." She gave him the cup. "The other one looked at me as if I was stupid, and said, They were given. That's all. They were given."



Waylon was in the long stable behind the keep, where he had been most evenings since the summer, in the end box with the grey mare and the filly. Ash was all legs now, and had learned to stand with her chin on the half door and watch the yard, which she did now, and moved her chin to Emeric's coat as soon as he was near enough.

"She remembers you," said Waylon, from the straw.

"She remembers the buttons."

"Same thing, to her."

Emeric let himself in and stood against the partition. The mare shifted her weight and went on with her hay. He had come to say a thing tonight and had been saying it to himself since the cold room, and it had come out a different shape each time, and now that he was here none of the shapes would do.

"Tomorrow," he said. "In the dome. You know what we're going to do."

"Put her back." Waylon was rubbing a rag over a bit with a great deal of attention. "Father told me. Ward told me. Imani told me what I'm to do with my hands, which is nothing." He looked up. "I'm coming."

"I know. I want you to know two things first. So you're not in there finding them out from somebody's face."

Waylon put the bit down on the straw.

So Emeric told him, standing against the partition with the filly working at his coat. That the man who had shot at their mother on the road had done it under an order; that the order covered her line, all of it, and had never been lifted, and had not lapsed because the man who carried it was dead. That Augustine had told him so in the square, with the stone in his open hand, and had named him, Waylon, the little one, on the shelf within reach, for twenty-eight years; and that there had been a knight on his door in the palace who was Corbin's knight, and a cardinal who had asked Corbin, kindly, as a man asks after a household, which room the little one slept in now.

"I slept in the mews," said Waylon.

"I know."

"Most nights. Since I was fourteen." He picked up the bit again and did not do anything with it. "I thought it was because I liked horses."

"It might have been."

"It might have been." Waylon turned the bit over. "What's the other thing?"

That was harder. Emeric told him what a Seeing was: a delver's work, done through a stone, on a man who did not know there were such things. That Corbin had been shown his family in a chapel, over years, by a kind old man with a hand on his head, and had believed it because it came the way holy things come; and that some of what Corbin had done to all of them, he had done with his eyes full of pictures somebody else had painted.

Waylon listened to the end without moving.

"Does Corbin know?"

"He found it himself. In the yard, in January. I didn't tell him."

"Why not?"

"Because if I'd told him, it would have been one more man telling him what he'd seen."

Waylon thought about that for a long time, with the bit in his hands. The mare blew out through her nose. The filly gave up on the button and put her head over Emeric's shoulder and breathed in his ear.

"I've hated him since I could walk," said Waylon, and hung the bit up on its nail. "I'll have to think about that."

"You don't have to decide tonight."

"No." Waylon stood, and brushed the straw off his knees, and came and took the filly's headcollar and turned her gently away from Emeric's ear. "She's going to be awake? Tomorrow. Mother."

"Once. Imani thinks once. Then a long time asleep."

"And she'll know us."

"She'll know Father," said Emeric. "I don't know about us. We were small."

Waylon nodded at that as if it were a fair price, and went out to fill the water.



There was a hum.

It came before anything else. Before light or weight or the knowledge that she had a back and was lying on it. It came out of the dark where she had been for a very long time, three notes stepping down and one long one that climbed and stopped, and then the three again, patient, as somebody hums who is thinking about something else; and she knew it. She did not know where from. She went toward it the way you go toward a door with the lamp lit behind it.

Then there was everything at once, and it hurt.

She had a body. It was hers; she knew the shape of it from the inside as you know your own teeth with your tongue; and it was wrong all through. Her chest was full of water. Her heart went and stopped and went again, like a horse that has been asked too much. There was a great cold under her, and a dry sweet smell she did not know, and a light coming down through something high and green and moving, leaves and glass; and her mouth was dry as a road.

"Don't sit up," said a woman's voice, close, very calm. "You can't. Don't try. Open your mouth."

She opened it.

Something was laid on her tongue, small and smooth and the size of a walnut, and her whole mouth knew it. She had had it in her hand at nine, in a cold room in a tower, when Jerome had put it in her palm and closed her fingers on it and said keep this, it will learn you, and she had kept it ever after in the pocket of every dress she owned. It was warm. It was hers. It wanted to go down.

"Water," said the woman's voice, and a cup touched her lip, and Amara swallowed the stone, and felt it go, and felt it begin.

A mind in a stone is not asleep; it hears. She had heard a great deal, for a great many years, in a voice she hated more than she had ever hated anything: a man talking to his clerks, to his brothers in grey, to himself in the night. She had heard her sons' names in it. She knew that the man was dead, and that a long time had gone by, and that she was in the south, in her elder son's house, under glass. She could feel the tree from where she lay, behind her head, like a hearth.

She turned her eyes, since she could not turn her head.

A young woman was kneeling by her, brown, with a great deal of curly hair tied up out of the way and her two hands flat on Amara's ribs, and her eyes shut. Behind her a young man with white curls and round spectacles that had nothing in them stood holding a small oak box, open and empty, against his chest. A very big man with grey in his beard stood at her feet with his arms folded, the way he had always stood, and she knew him, and could not find his name, and was frightened by that for a breath until it came. Ward. Ward, with grey in his beard.

And on the boards, a little way off, under the tree, with a drawing board on his knee and a pencil in his hand that had stopped, was a man she had never seen.

Tall. Long dark ropes of hair tied back with a bit of cord. A face she knew better than any face in the world and had never once seen, because it was her own face, made over into a man's and lived in; and his father's gold eyes on her, very still. He had been humming. He had stopped when she opened her eyes, and she wanted him to go on.

"You did that in your cradle," she said.

It came out as almost nothing, a breath with the shape of words in it. He put the drawing board down and came and knelt by her head, so she would not have to make it louder, and she said it again.

"That tune. Before you could talk. I used to stand by the cradle and you'd be lying there on your back humming it at the ceiling, and I'd think, where did he get that. Nobody sang it to you. I never knew it." Her mouth wanted to smile, and she let it, though it hurt. "I hum like a crow. Your father says. I couldn't have."

"How does it end?" said her son.

She thought about it. It was a strange thing to want to know, at a time like this; she thought it was exactly like him, and she had no idea how she knew that.

"I don't know," she said. "You never got there. You'd stop, as if you'd lost your place."

Something went over his face and was gone. Then he looked away from her, along the boards, toward the trunk, and she looked where he was looking, because it was easier than looking at him.

Harrold was sitting on the floor.

On the boards, with his back against a crate, in a plain brown coat with straw on one sleeve, as if he had come straight from a stable; his long legs out in front of him and his big hands open on his knees. There was no chair in the dome. Nobody had thought to bring one, and the king of Randillia had not asked, and had sat down where he was. His hair had gone grey, and he had a beard she had never seen. The lines by his mouth were deeper than the lines she remembered, and there were others she did not know at all. His eyes were the same. The gold was the same.

Beside him, a little behind, sat a bearded man with a horseman's hands, who had his father's shoulders and his father's way of sitting, and who was looking at her with a face she knew from the inside of a nursery, three years old and furious about a sock.

"Harrold," she said.

He did not get up. She thought afterward that he could not have, and that he knew it. He looked at her across the boards as he had looked at her at the foot of Armand's stair at eighteen, when she told him not yet, and why, and that she would tell him when.

"Well," he said. His voice was not steady and he did not try to make it steady. "You took your time."

She had a great deal to say to him. She had had twenty-eight years to put it in order, in the dark, and it was all there, in rows; and she found she could not get to any of it, because the stone was going down through her like warm water into dry ground, and it was pulling her down after it.

"Not yet," she said, which was what she had said to him at the foot of the stair; and she saw that he understood it, and that he laughed, once, the way he had laughed then; and she looked once more at the bearded one and the tall one, her two boys, and went to sleep under the tree.



"Hours," Imani had said in the morning. In the end it had been a little under three, from the moment Bram took the crystal lid off the cocoon in the dome's green light to the moment Amara's breathing went long and slow under Imani's hands and stayed that way. She was laid on the boards by the trunk, where Armand had lain, on the same mattress, with the same oiled sheet under it; and Imani went home at midnight and slept fourteen hours, and Ward stood in the dome's door until dawn because nobody could think of a reason to make him stop.

Emeric found his drawing board in the morning where he had left it on the boards. On it was the sling frame he had been drawing while he waited, for lifting a body that could not be bent, and in the margin, beside the last line of figures, a ring with one stroke through it that stopped short of the far side, which he had made without noticing, as he always did. He looked at the drawing. He tried the tune, under his breath, and got to the top of the long note, and stopped, as he always did, and could not think what came next.



He took the knights in on the twenty-second, one at a time, at night, in the dome, on the boards' far side from where his mother lay.

He told them first what Saban had said at the long table, because he had said he would. He told it to the three of them standing together by the door, in Saban's words as near as he could get them: that every head that knew was a head that could be taken and opened; that knights went where the fighting was, and men who went where the fighting was got taken; that the table had voted six to one, and the one was Saban, and Saban had not been wrong to say it.

Rylan heard it with his burned arm folded over his good one.

"He's right," he said.

"He is."

"Then why are we standing here?"

"Because you're eating a stone in April either way," said Emeric, "and Imani won't let you near the dome with one that doesn't know you. And because the six thought that three more heads who'd gone down a cliff for us were worth the risk. Saban's minuted. You can read it. He'd want you to."

Declan said, "I'd like to read it," in the tone of a man asking for the salt, and Osric said nothing, and looked at the tree.

He took Declan first, because Declan's sense was the neatest of the three, close and tidy as a folded shirt. He sat him on a crate with his year-twelve stone in his two hands and put his own hand over both of them, and said, as he had said to Tess on a stool in Bram's shop the winter she bonded, you don't, the first time; I take you; and went down, and brought Declan with him.

Declan was through the door before Emeric had properly opened it. He stood in his own room, a small square plain place with one window and a bench and a hook for a coat, everything put away, and looked round it with no expression at all; and then he said, in the room, without moving his mouth, oh, it's here, as a man says it who has found the knife he put down somewhere sensible and forgot where; and that was all. When Emeric let go, the room stayed. It held for the count of forty, and went, and Declan opened his eyes on the crate and said, "Again," and was in again in a quarter of an hour by himself.

Rylan balked at the door.

Rylan's sense was quick, a half beat and a stop, the way he fought; and at the door the stop came and stayed. Emeric could feel him not going through the way you feel a horse that has decided about a ditch.

They came out. Rylan sat on the crate and looked at his arm.

"It's a door," he said. "I've been through a door."

"Then go through it."

The second time he got a hand on it and took the hand off. The third time, which was a little before one in the morning, Emeric said nothing at all, and did not push, and stood beside him in the dark with his hand over Rylan's; and after a long time Rylan said, inside, not to him, it's only a bloody door, and went through it as if it had insulted him.

Osric spilled.

There was no other word for it. Osric's sense was a broad, warm, careless thing, like his fire; the door was open before either of them touched it, and Osric went through it and out the other side of himself into everything, the tree and the boards and the sleeping queen, like water out of a burst bucket. The room was there for a breath, a great untidy barn of a place with a fire in it, and then it was everywhere and nowhere. Emeric got him back by the collar. When he let go, Osric lost it at once.

"Was that it?" said Osric, on the crate, breathing hard.

"That was it."

"It was enormous."

"It was," said Emeric. "You need it smaller."



Ward took the nights Emeric could not.

He had been the last of the six to go in alone, after months of sitting on a stool coming up red and furious; and he had done it in the end, he told the three of them on the second night, by flying. Not in the room. In his head. "I put the suit on," he said. "The MK2. In there. I knew where every strap went. It was the only thing I was sure enough of, so I stood in it, and the room came round it." He looked at the three of them. "Find yours. Something you know so well you don't have to think about it. Stand in that."

Declan's was the river below the castle, the stretch by the mill race where he had swum every summer since he came to Kaldo, every stone of it. Rylan, after two nights of trying a sword and a horse and a forge and finding none of them would hold, found the wall walk: the castle's east wall at Kaldo, between the second tower and the gate, which he had walked at night every night of his first year in Kaldo's service and could have walked blind, counting the merlons. Osric found his on the fourth night, with Ward sitting on the boards beside him saying nothing whatever for an hour: the bout ring in Ward's yard, the chalk square on the packed earth, where he had been knocked down more times than any man living, and had always known exactly where the edge was.

"You put a fire in a box," said Ward. "Good. That's what a ring's for."

Nobody's room held all night yet. But they came every night at nine and went in, and Armand sat with them on the nights Ward slept, with a book he did not read, and Imani came at eleven to put her hand on the sleeping queen, and on her way out looked at the three of them and wrote something in her book. "Four weeks," she said to Emeric, on the stair. "If they keep this up. I'll want five."



The supply hull came down from the Well on the twenty-third with Armand's crates of instrument glass and a letter in Tess's square hand for the stone book, folded twice, which Ferra opened at the breakfast table and read, and passed across without a word.

Captured stone, hull two's, the starboard deciding stone. Delved with A. in the station lab, three sittings. Not a join anywhere. We looked for joins for two days because that's what we'd make. It's seams. They go straight through, all of it, like the grain in a board. It was grown that way. Not cut, not set: grown. The keywork's the same as I wrote at 324, the hand nobody has. A. says it isn't the Church's. A. says it isn't the tower's. A. says it isn't the old races' either, from what we've got off the island, and he's crosser about that than about anything since his knees. Whose?

And underneath, in Armand's hand, very small: She's asked me that four times. I don't know. Coming down Thursday. A.

Emeric read it twice and gave it back. Ferra copied it into the stone book's back pages under SEAMS, beside Tess's line from December, and left a space under the question, as she had left a space under it then.



Bram opened the ball mill on the twenty-eighth.

He had been running it since the week after the survey, in the end bay of the forge cellar: an iron drum the size of a beer barrel on two shafts, half full of hard steel balls, turning day and night off the line shaft with a noise like a hailstorm in a church. Into it had gone a measured weight of iron powder off the reduction furnace, fine as flour, and a measured weight of the charging room's bucket, the grey dead sweepings Ferra had written off in the first summer and restored to stores in February at the Secretary of Works' request. Value: to be found. Three days and nights of it. He had stopped the shaft at noon.

He had told nobody he was opening it, not even Tess, who was back from the Well that week and would have wanted to watch.

He knocked the drum's hatch dogs off with a hammer at half past twelve, while it was still warm under his hand, and swung the hatch back to look in, and the powder inside met the air and went up.

It did not bang. Fennick said afterward that if it had banged Bram would have been a smear on the cellar wall. It went up in a soft orange sheet, with a sound like somebody shaking out a wet blanket, and rolled out of the hatch and across the front of Bram from his knees to his hair, and was gone, and left a smell of struck matches and a slow sparkle of red flecks settling on the cellar floor, going black one by one.

Bram stood with the hammer in his hand.

The front of his leather apron was scorched brown in a fan. His eyebrows were gone. His beard had come through, being damp with sweat, but was shorter at the front, crisped at the ends like the edge of a burned pie, and he could smell it.

"Right," said Bram.

Imani was there in eleven minutes, which was how long it took the cellar lad to run to the hospital and her to walk back at the speed she walked when she was angry, which was not fast. She put Bram on a stool by the cellar door, and looked at his face, and his hands, and his eyes with a lamp, and found a blister the size of a farthing on the back of his left wrist where the apron did not reach. She dressed it. Then she stood in front of him with her arms folded.

"Iron that fine burns in air," she said. "By itself. Warm, it burns faster. Did you know that?"

"I knew it," said Bram. "I forgot it."

"Three rules," said Imani. "You'll paint them on the drum. It runs wet, under oil, from now on. Nobody opens it till it's stood a full day cold, and you'll put your hand flat on it before you touch the dogs. And masks, on everybody in this bay, for the fines, the same as the dust shed, because I'm not having iron lungs on top of everything." She looked at his face. "And you'll grow those back. You look like a boiled egg."

He painted the rules on the drum that afternoon. He pressed what was left of the powder into a mould in the big screw press, heated the cake white, and forged it down under the steam hammer in three heats to a bar a foot long and two fingers thick, dark and plain. Beside it he forged a second bar of ordinary mild steel, the same size.

Emeric came down at five with Armand, because Bram sent the lad to say there was a thing to see and not what.

Bram had two firebrick posts set up in the gas furnace's mouth, and he laid the bars across them side by side, hung a fifty-pound cheese of cast iron off the middle of each on a wire hook, closed the door to a crack and brought the furnace up. They stood and watched through the crack. The bars went dull red, and cherry, and the bright orange-red where a smith stops trusting his steel.

The mild steel bar sagged. Slowly at first, then less slowly, bowing down between the posts under its cheese the way a candle bows in a hot window, until its middle was a thumb's width lower than its ends, and then two.

The other bar lay straight across the posts with its cheese hanging off it, red as the other, and did not move.

They watched it for half an hour.

"Bucket steel," said Bram.

"It's called dispersion steel," said Emeric. "Where the dust's milled in so fine it holds the iron still from inside. You can do it with any fine hard grit. The alumina off Vantic's pot room would do it, if the bucket ever runs dry."

"It's called bucket steel," said Bram. "I made it out of a bucket."

Armand had bent down to the crack with his spectacles in his hand, his young face red in the furnace light. He straightened up slowly.

"The old races never did that," he said. "Everything we've had out of the island is dust in clay. Fired, like pots. They never once put it in iron." He looked at the straight red bar. "That's ours."

Emeric thought of turbine blades for the river dam, and the grate at the shaft's foot that had sagged a finger's width in a month, and said nothing, and looked at Bram's face, which had no eyebrows on it and was perfectly happy.



Ferra kept the log because somebody had to, and because when Emeric had asked Ivo in February for the year's film, every foot of it, Ivo had said yes, and then, after a moment, "Who's counting it?" in the voice of a man who has seen what happens to film nobody counts.

So she counted it. A foot of film was sixteen pictures, Ivo said; she had checked with a rule and a lamp and he was right, which had disappointed her slightly. A thousand feet made one reel, about eleven minutes, and Ivo's year since September was a little under nine thousand feet in forty-one tins. Out of that Emeric was making one reel. She had said it was like making one page out of a ledger, and he had said yes, exactly, and she had stopped arguing.

They worked at night, from nine until one or two, in Ivo's darkroom over the printing works, because it was the only room in Kaldo with a viewer in it: a box on the bench with a lamp inside, a ground glass on top, and the film drawn across under a lens by two cranks, so that you could wind it past at any speed and stop it on any one picture. Emeric sat at the viewer. Beside it was the splicing block, a little brass jig Ottoline had made with pins to hold the film's holes and a blade to cut it square, and a pot of cement that smelled of pear drops and gave Ferra a headache by eleven. She sat on Ivo's high stool at the end of the bench with the log book and wrote down every cut: which tin, which foot, which picture, kept or out. Out went in a basket. Kept went on a big reel on the wall, a little thicker every night.

It was dull work most of the time, and she liked it. He talked while he did it, and not about anything. The express went first, coming straight at the camera down the station line, because, he said, the front rows would duck and then the children would want it again. Abloh glaring at the lens over the Clothing Works' cutting table for nine seconds by her count stayed in, all nine. Maren's ladle raised at the camera he played four times and laughed at every time.

And then the quay in November.

She saw him slow the crank before he got there. She knew the tin; she had written it in. He knew what was on it. He wound it on anyway, more slowly, and the ground glass filled with the grey November light off the river and the side of escort four and the gangway, and men going up it.

A heavy man at the gangway's foot, counting his swimmers up by their heads. One, two, three. His hand going out, on each head, and back.

Emeric's hand stopped on the crank.

The picture stopped with it, and stayed, a broad man with his hand out and his mouth open, half a word into something, on the top of a boy's head. Ferra could see it upside down over Emeric's shoulder. Behind Fenn on the gangway, small and out of the light, a thin dark boy with his hands in his pockets.

Emeric sat and looked at it and did not wind on.

She did not stop. She wrote it down, Tin 32. Ft 410. Fenn at the gangway, frame 6,561, because it was the only thing she knew how to do that would be any use to anyone in that room; and then she sat with the pen and waited, and the lamp in the viewer ticked as it heated.

After a long time he wound on. Fenn's hand came down on the next head. Robin Sennet went up the gangway, and took his hands out of his pockets because somebody below told him to, and put them back in at the top. Then two knights stopped at the camera, a big fair boy in a grey scarf and a little dark girl, and the boy put his face to Merrin's horn.

"There's sound with this one," said Emeric. His voice was quite ordinary. "On Merrin's stripe. I've not heard it."

"Then you'd better hear it," said Ferra, "before you decide."

They heard it the next night, on the bench, on Merrin's player with the photocell reading the stripe and the little horn turned toward the wall: gulls, and boots on a gangway, and women laughing along the rope, and Wilf Abery as loud as he could be, Is it on? And under all of it, if you knew where to listen, and once you had heard it you could not stop hearing it, a man's voice like a gull, going steadily, one, two, three, four.

"That's going in," said Ferra.

"It's not mine to put in."

"No," she agreed. "So you'll ask."



He went with Sela, because Sela had read the names at Commencement and the town knew her voice, and because Sela had asked to. Ferra did not go. She stayed in the darkroom with the log and the pear drops and the last tin, the February one, which she had not let him touch yet because it was the end of the reel and she wanted to see it first.

She was there, at the bench, when they came back on the second evening and Emeric said, from the door, that both had said yes. Fenn's mother had sat in her own kitchen in ward two, the heavy grey woman with the red neck, and listened to all of it without a word, and at the end had said, he'd have hated it, he'd have stood at the back with his arms folded and complained all the way through and he'd have come three times. Mistress Sennet had said yes before Sela finished asking, and then asked if she could have the picture of Robin's hands.

"And Fenn's mother wants to hear it first," said Emeric. "Before the town. Alone. Here."

She came the next night at nine in her good shawl. Ivo set the player for her and the horn, and showed her which lever, and went out; and Emeric went out; and Ferra, who had meant to go out, found that the woman had sat down at the bench and put her hand on the stool beside her without looking, as you put your hand on a chair for a daughter, and so she sat on it.

They heard the quay through twice. The second time, at the place, Fenn's mother put her hand over Ferra's on the bench and held it very hard, and said nothing. When it was done she stood, and settled the shawl, and said to the dark, "That's him counting," in a voice of perfect satisfaction, and went home.

Ferra sat for a while afterward with her hand on the bench where it had been held.

Then she wound on the last tin, by herself, because it was the end of the reel and she was the one counting.

February, the sixteenth: the fleet coming down over the river, ship after ship, the quay black with people. Ivo had been high up, on the crane's cab, and then down. The crane's foot. A woman in a blue coat with a board and a list, reading. She had not known he was filming that close. She had not known he was filming her at all.

She watched herself read the names, very small and straight, and rule a line, and read another. She watched a tall man come down the ramp last of all with his hair tied back and his arm out of its sling and stop in front of her. She watched herself look up at him.

She had not known what her face did. She had thought she had been perfectly composed. She stopped the crank and looked at the picture for a long time, at the woman on the ground glass, upside down, looking up at him with her whole face, in front of the whole town and Ivo's lens, and did not know whether to laugh.

The door opened. He came in with two cups of Maren's chocolate and saw what was on the viewer, and stood with them.

"Is that going in?" said Ferra.

He put the cups down on the bench, carefully, and came and looked over her shoulder at the picture, a long while.

"You said you'd count me in," he said. "That's you counting."

"That's me making a fool of myself in front of nine thousand people."

"It's eleven frames," he said. "Ivo cut the crank. Half a second. Nobody will see it but you."

"And you."

"And me," he said, and she felt him smile against her hair, and wrote down, very neatly, Tin 41. Ft 790. The quay, Feb. Kept; and drank the chocolate, which had gone a little cold.



The lantern was proved on the long room wall on the fourth of March at ten at night, with the shutters closed and the six on the benches and a sheet off Abloh's sail loft pinned up where the stone map usually hung.

The film projector stood on the long table, a black box a yard long that the crews had already started calling the lantern: Ottoline's Maltese cross clicking the film down a picture at a time behind Ivo's lens, the Forge's sprockets drawing it through, the sound stripe running past Merrin's photocell to two horns stood on chairs. The lamp was a light stone in a mirrored housing, cool, so the film could not scorch; behind it sat the floor, as there was always a floor, a carbon arc behind a cell of water to drink the heat. Ottoline had lit the arc once that afternoon to be sure. It had boiled the water cell in four minutes, and she had pronounced herself satisfied.

Ivo threw the switch. The express came down the wall at them out of the dark.

Saban ducked, and said afterward that he had been reaching for something under the bench. Ward did not duck, and was seen the next morning on the platform's end at the station, watching the express come in.

The Kaldo News for the eighth printed, at the foot of its back page, in Ferra's sentence because nobody else could get it short enough: On Lantern Night, in Lantern Square, after dark, on the records hall wall: Kaldo, year thirteen, in pictures that move and talk. Three showings. No charge. Bring the children. Do not sit at the front if you are frightened of trains.



Vantic came south on the ninth, in her own hull, with the two ledgers in a dispatch case and a cold she said she had caught off the council; and on the night of the eleventh Emeric and Ferra climbed the stair of her glass tower on the civic ring to the room at the top, the same room, with the city's lights laid out round three sides of it like a dinner service.

The lamp stone on her desk was guttering. It did that in the evenings, when the tower's feed ran low; it was a cheap stone and she had never changed it.

She looked at it as they came in, and put out two fingers, and touched the side of it; and it steadied, and burned clean and white and quiet, the way it had on a night two winters ago when she had sat at this desk and told him her name. She did not hurry. She let him watch her do it.

"I've known since then," said Emeric.

"I know you've known." Vantic took her fingers away. The lamp stayed steady. "I'm saying it."

That was the trade, and all three of them knew it, and none of them said anything more about it.

Ferra gave her the buy-back fix first, because it was business and Vantic would not settle until the business was done: the Fairhythe widow, the factor's third, the difference to be paid on what the buyer had actually paid and not the House's price. Vantic read the sheet twice with her handkerchief at her nose.

"It'll cost me more," she said.

"It will."

"And it's right." She set the sheet down. "I said you'd find something."

"You did," said Ferra. "You'll want the widow's name. I've put it at the bottom."

Then Emeric told her what the table had voted.

He did it as Ferra had said to do it at the long table: offer what the table knew, all of it, and let her choose what she took. He began with the thing that was hardest to say, because it was the thing all the rest hung on. There was a way into a stone that was not a mage's way. There was a way into a mind. The Church had it, and had buried it, and used it; and Kaldo had it; and had not buried it.

He took out a card-one chip, a square of grey glass no bigger than a fingernail, and put it in her palm, where it lay grey and dead. Then he laid his own hand over hers, and went in, a little, down through the glass, and the cut inside it answered him: his own mark, laid down in its heart, that nobody in the world could read or copy. She could not read it either. But she was a mage, and he felt her feel it, the shape of a thing answering from inside the glass like a bell struck under water, and her hand went quite still under his.

"Ah," said Vantic.

He told her about the Forge, its rule and not its room; the island to the south-east and the Cradle on it; liquid mana, and why every hull of Kaldo's could refill a dead sky.

He began on the dome.

"Don't," said Vantic.

He stopped.

"Not that." She had put her handkerchief down. "Whatever's under that glass on the hill, that you all look at when you think nobody's looking. Whatever keeps an old man young. Don't." She looked at him, and then at Ferra. "Don't tell me anything I'd have to lie about. I'm the head of a House with factors in every port the Church can reach. Your Saban's right about that; Ferra told me he said it, and he's right. I'll ask when I can afford to know."

"He'll be glad you said that," said Ferra.

"He'll be insufferable," said Vantic.

So he told her the one other thing that he had to, which was that in April the six would be gone for some weeks, all at once, and the town would be held by others, and the bank's account with the House would be kept by Ferra's clerks and signed by the king.

Vantic was quiet a long time. Outside, a tram went down the civic ring with its pole spitting blue at the wire.

"Tell me they'll wake," she said. "Not why."

"They'll wake."

She nodded once.

She was still holding the chip. She turned it over in her fingers, and he watched her see it, because she saw things in figures before other people saw them in words. A mark nobody could forge. A key that was the man who held it and nobody else. What that would be in a bank. He watched every ledger in the kingdom reorder itself behind her eyes round a fingernail of glass, in about the time it took to breathe out.

She put it down on the desk, carefully, between them, and took her hand off it.

"Don't show me what that's for," said Vantic. "I'll want it. And I can't want it yet."

Ferra, beside him, said nothing at all, and he knew without looking that she was very nearly smiling, because it was her own rule, said in a different voice: not till the Charter said who read the House's book.

On the stair going down, Ferra said, "She was right about the dome. I'll have to tell Saban. He'll be impossible."



The tree let its pods go in the second week of March.

They came down over four days, the way they did, one and then a gap and then two; and the drops' mana went out through the dome in a slow soft push that every delver in Kaldo felt in their back teeth and made the knights on their crates open their eyes. Under the canopy the flowers opened for the partner tree's answer, and somebody had to go up the ladder and do them by hand, a hundred and sixty or so, one at a time, before they closed.

Emeric and Ferra were in the darkroom. Bram had a mill to watch, Imani had her hands on a sleeping queen, and the knights were in their rooms, Declan's holding now for an hour, Rylan's for most of one, Osric's for a quarter and then gone, and then back.

So Tess did the flowers.

Emeric came up the hill at one in the morning, done with the night's cutting and the reel very nearly whole, and found the dome's door open a crack and green light coming out of it, and went in quietly.

The ladder was up against the canopy's lowest limb. Osric was at its foot, out of his room for the night, with both hands on the uprights and his feet braced and his eyes on the top of it, not on anything else, the way Emeric had seen him watch a wall for a gunner. At the top of it, with her stick hooked over a rung beside her and one arm round the limb, was Tess, in a pair of Abloh's grey working trousers and one of Bram's old shirts with the sleeves rolled four times, holding her hand flat over a flower.

It shut. She moved her hand to the next.

She had stood up for the first time last summer, and she could walk across a room now, and down a ward, and on a good day along the quay as far as the crane; she could not stand on a ladder for an hour. She had been on it for two. Emeric could see it in the way she had her hip against the rail, and the set of her mouth, which was Ferra's mouth when Ferra was adding something that would not come right.

He did not offer. Osric looked round at him once, and the look said that if he did, Osric would put him out of the dome himself.

Under the tree, on the mattress on the boards, Amara slept with her hands on the sheet and the green light on her face. Her breathing was long and slow. On the far side Declan and Rylan sat on their crates with their eyes shut. Ward was asleep against the wall by the door with his arms folded, as he had slept against walls on watch for thirty years, and did not wake.

Tess did the last flower at twenty past one.

She came down the ladder a rung at a time with Osric's hand on her back and her stick in her other hand, and sat down on Bram's crate by the collar of the trunk, and found that she could not hold a pen; and laughed at that, quietly, because it was exactly what Ferra had said about herself a year ago, word for word. So Osric fetched the stone book from its shelf by the door and held it open on his knee, and she put her hand on his to steady it, and wrote in it in the shape of her own square letters, slowly, with his hand under hers.
