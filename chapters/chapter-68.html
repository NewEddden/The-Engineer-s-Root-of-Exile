Chapter 68 - Terms

The House's rider came in at the South Gate on the Thursday before the last Saturday in August, on a good grey horse that had been ridden hard and fed well, with a leather case strapped behind his saddle and a bag in front of it that he did not let out of his hand.

He was a quiet, square man in a plain coat, and he would say nothing at all in the gate yard but that he carried the House's answer and was to wait for the barony's. He gave the case to Emeric with his own hands. The bag he gave to Ferra, at her table, after he had looked at her book open on it and then at her, and asked, very civilly, whether she was the clerk. She said she was. He said the bag was a first draw, against signature, and that if the barony did not sign, he was to take it back in the morning, and he would be obliged if she would count it in front of him.

She counted it in front of him. It was enough for three Saturdays.

Then the rider went up the hill to sleep at the keep, and Emeric sat down across the table from her and unbuckled the case. Through the window, over the roofs, the lamps on the wall had come on for the night, a shade yellower than they had been in the spring, and neither of them remarked on it.



The terms ran to ten pages in a clerk's hand so even it might have been pressed, and Ferra read them aloud, all of them, line by line, with her finger under each line as she went, as she had read the barony's count. Emeric listened. Twice he asked her to read a line again, and she read it again without looking up.

The sum came first, at the top of the first page. It was more money than had been in Kaldo at one time since before the plague, and Ferra read it out without any change in her voice at all.

The interest came after it. A sixth part a year, on whatever was owed, reckoned at each quarter day. Ferra read it, and stopped, and read it again, and then went on without saying anything, and he knew from how she went on that it was worse than she had feared and about what she had expected.

"A sixth," he said.

"A borrower with no history isn't refused," Ferra said. "He's priced. That's in here too." She turned the page and found it and read it to him: the House's own words, set down plainly, as if it were a courtesy to tell him. "They've priced the plague, and the war, and you."

She did the sum in the margin while he watched, a twenty-fourth at every quarter day, forty-eight quarter days, and the sum itself at the end, and wrote it under the line.

"If it runs the whole term," she said, "we pay them back their money three times over. If we let the quarters run on unpaid, seven."

"Then it won't run the whole term."

She did not answer that. She did not need to. He had known the shape of that sum since before he could do it, in another life, from a bank's letters on somebody's kitchen table, and he knew what a sixth did to a debt that was paid slowly.

The security came next. The barony's revenues, all of them, the spirit and the soap and the arch bar and the dues of the moorings and whatever else Kaldo should come to make or take, pledged to the House until the debt was paid. The works of the barony, named one by one, the still and the powerhouse and the dyehouse and bay three. And the barony's grain, of whatever kind.

"Of whatever kind," Emeric said.

Ferra looked up.

"That's the seed." He held out his hand for the page and she gave it to him, and he read the line himself, and then he took the pen out of the inkstand and drew a line through the last three words, carefully, with a ruler, and wrote above it, in his stiff hand: the seed grain of the barony stands outside this security, or there is no loan. And initialed it.

"They'll say no."

"Then they'll say no. I'm not pledging next year's harvest against this year's." He gave her back the page. "They lend against what a place will be. Saban said so. Without the seed it won't be anything."

She looked at the struck line. Then she went on reading.



The term was twelve years. Ferra did a sum in the margin of her own book without being asked, and said that at the revenue Kaldo had made last year, before the war, it would be paid off in three, if nothing else went wrong, and that if it was not paid off in three or four there would be nothing left to pay it off with by twelve. Emeric believed her.

Then the factor.

The House would send a factor with the money, to live in Kaldo for the life of the debt, in the keep, at the barony's charge. The factor would have the right to see every book the barony kept that touched the security, which was every book the barony kept. The factor would sit at any spending above a sum the terms named, which was not a large sum. The factor would report to the House.

"A lodger," Ferra said, "who reads my book."

"Who reads every book."

"Mine is the one that matters." She said it as a fact.

He thought about that more than about the sixth. For two years he had been the only person in the world who could read what Kaldo was: what it made, what it owed, what it was going to become if nobody stopped it. Ferra could read the numbers. Ward could read the walls. Nobody else had all of it. Now there would be a second reader, in his own house, eating at his own table, with every book open, and reading for somebody else.

Ferra was waiting for him. Then she turned to the last page but one, and her finger stopped under the first line of it.

"The covenant," she said.

He had been waiting for it: the page that said what he could not do while he owed them.

Ferra read it. Then she read it again, slower, and he heard her voice change on the second reading, very slightly, as it did when a column she had expected to be wrong came out right.

The barony would maintain and repair and operate everything it had: every building, every work, every wage and every service it paid for on the day the terms were signed. The House had no objection to any of it. But each year, while any of the principal was owed, the barony's spending on new works, things that had not existed on the day of signing, was not to exceed a share of the year before's net revenue. The share was named. The barony's clerk would reckon the figure each year, and the House's factor would check the reckoning. When the principal was repaid the covenant ended. If it was repaid early, the House kept the right to read the barony's books, in perpetuity, for its trouble.

"New works," Emeric said.

"New works. Not running ones." Ferra put her finger on the line. "The school's paid for. Shut or open, the barony pays its bread and its wage on the day you sign, so it's running." She looked up at him. "The households' pay is running. The ward. The lamps. The machine. Harl's mill, when it's roofed: that's repair." She was going faster now, down the clause, her finger moving. "They'll let all of it run. What they're stopping is you building the next thing faster than Kaldo earns the money to build it."

"Which is sense," Emeric said.

"It's the first sensible thing in ten pages." She sat back. "And it's the only thing in ten pages that's going to hurt."



Imani came in while Ferra was reckoning what the share would come to.

She knocked on the open door, which nobody in the lane ever did, and stood in it with something folded over her arm, in her ward apron, with her sleeves rolled down for once and her hair tied up for the evening. She looked at the two of them at the table with the pages spread between them and the lamp lit, and at the bag on the corner of the table, and did not sit down.

"I won't keep you," she said.

She came to the table and laid the thing on her arm down on the end of it, away from the pages. It was his coat.

He had not seen it since the end of May. He had not missed it, because it was summer. It had been boiled. Everything that came through Imani's ward was boiled, and his coat had come through it, and it had come out a shade lighter and very slightly smaller and smelling of lye and spirit, folded into a square as exact as one of Imani's cloths.

"Thank you," he said.

Imani looked at him across the table. She did not look at Ferra.

"How did I get into that bed?" she said.

It was the voice she used for asking which way a bandage had been wound. Quite level. Quite pleasant. She had waited the whole of the summer to ask it, he understood, until the last of the war's wounded but Tess had gone home and the ward was hers again, and she asked it here, at this table, with the lamp lit.

"You walked," he said.

Imani held his eyes. She did not believe him. She did not say so. She had been asleep on the floor against the wall with her head on her knees and she had woken in a bed under his coat, and she knew exactly how far it was from the wall to that bed, and that she had not walked it. He watched her put that beside the other things she had put away, the dog that was always sick, and the knot, and the room, and close the lid on all of them.

"Then I'm a better walker than I thought," she said. "Goodnight."

She went out. Her steps went away down the lane.

Ferra had not moved. She had not looked up when Imani came in, and she had not looked up while Imani spoke, and she did not look up now. She was looking at the page in front of her, at the covenant, at the share, at the sum she had been reckoning in the margin, with her pen in her hand.

She had heard all of it. He could see that she had heard all of it and understood none of it: why Imani had had his coat, and since when; what bed; whether he had been in it. He could see her not asking. She had no right to ask. They were nothing to each other that either of them had said aloud, and she was not a woman who asked for what she had no right to, and she did not ask.

She went on with the sum.

She got it wrong. He saw it, upside down, before she did: a figure set down a column short, a tenth of what it should have been. Then she saw it. She looked at it. Then she drew her pen through it so hard that the nib went through the paper and into the page beneath, and wrote the right figure above it, and underlined it twice.

Her finger went back to the line that named the share, and at the same time his went to it from across the table. Hers stopped before it reached his, and came away. She put her hand flat on the table beside the page, and read the line without her finger under it.

"The share's a fifth," she said. "Of the year before's net. From the day you sign, that's this one, with the war in it and the summer after. And next year won't be much better." Her voice was very even. "So you'll build nothing new for two years. Nothing. Not a house, not a pipe, not one of your machines. Unless you mean to tell me a new pipe is a repair."

"I hadn't said anything about pipes."

"You were going to." She turned the page. "You always are."

It was not fair, and they both knew it was not fair, and she did not take it back, and he did not ask her to.



He signed at the bottom of the tenth page, a little before midnight, under the struck line about the seed, with a hand that had healed tight across the palm, which made his name look like somebody else's. Ferra witnessed it as she signed everything that went up the hill: the clerk, and the date, small and plain and perfectly steady, and blotted it, and put the pages back in the case in their order, and buckled it.

He walked up the hill afterward with his coat over his arm, and halfway up, because the night had turned cold the way August nights did at the end, he put it on. It was tight across the shoulders, and short in the sleeve, and it smelled of lye and spirit and very faintly of the ward, and he walked the rest of the way up to the keep in it.

Hale read the case at first light, before the rider took it, every page, standing at the window of his office with the light on the paper, and said that he could find no fault in the form of any clause, which from Hale was a long speech. Then he said that the House's factor would need rooms in the keep, and that the east tower had stood empty since the master went downstairs in April, and that he would see to it.

The rider took the case out through the South Gate an hour later.

That Saturday, the last in August, the trestle at the exchange door opened at the usual hour, and the households stood in their line, and Ferra sat behind the trestle with the bag and her book, and paid every one of them to the copper. Hollis took his grandchildren's pay from her hand and counted it, coin by coin, and put it in his pocket, and looked across at Emeric, standing in the exchange door, and nodded once.

Emeric went to the table that evening, and Ferra was not there. Her book was. It lay open on the table under the lamp at the page for the night of the terms, with the date written at the top in her small hand, as she wrote it every night, and nothing under it at all.
