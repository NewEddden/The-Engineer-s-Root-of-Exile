Chapter 68 - Turned Over

The summons came up the Wayford road in the fourth week after the judgment, in the crown post, with its seal intact, which was still new enough that Ferra turned it over twice before she opened it.

It asked the Baron of Kaldo to attend the chancery at his earliest convenience on a matter arising. It did not say what matter. It did not need to. Three baronies had lost their holders in one afternoon, one to a sentence, one to a grave four years old, and one to having stood on a plain and done nothing, and somewhere in the capital men whose names Emeric did not know had been deciding what to do with the pieces. He had not thought about it on the road home. Nobody in that corridor had. He thought about it now, standing in the long room with the letter in his hand, and found that he already knew the answer and did not like it.

"They'll give them to you," Ward said.

"They'll give them to somebody."

"They'll give them to you," Ward said again, in the same voice, and went to see about horses.

Twelve days down the road again, with Ward, and Ferra, because she had been promised her index back and did not trust anybody else to collect it. They did not talk about it much. On the ninth day, at an inn with a bad roof, Ferra asked him what he would do with a junction, and he said he did not know, and she said that was the first time in eight years she had heard him say that about anything with a toll on it.

The chancery was a long grey building behind the court, and its business was done in rooms with low ceilings and good light, by men who had been doing it for thirty years and would be doing it for thirty more. Keller was not in the room. Keller's recommendation was, in a leather folder on the table, and a crown clerk with ink on his cuffs read the three instruments aloud one after the other, at the same pace, as though they were one thing. They were not one thing. That was the first thing Emeric understood, and it was the whole of the morning.

Wayford went by judgment. A living offender, tried in person on the war, the suppression of the post and the attempt on a prince of the blood, and condemned, and his holding forfeit to the Crown by operation of the sentence. The sentence would be carried out in Wayford, in the square, in the second week of summer. The clerk read the date as he read everything else. It was the cleanest of the three instruments and it needed no explaining, and nobody explained it.

Grayrock went by attainder. Renner had raised and led a private war against a crown-granted barony and died in the field doing it, and the Crown had now reached into the grave and taken his line out of the succession, which it was entitled to do and did not often bother with. The trust by which his widow had held the hill for their daughter was voided with the line. The widow herself was not the offender. She was confirmed in the hall, the clerk read, as resident in the Crown's interest, pending disposition. Emeric turned that phrase over and could not find a floor in it anywhere.

Milford went by presence. Paxton had brought a hundred men to a plain on the morning of the battle, stood them there from first light, and taken them home again at dusk having spent nothing and struck nobody. The instrument did not call that innocence. It called it mitigation. Paxton kept his life and his name and his title, loosely held, and his rights in his own lands, and became steward of Milford under the new holder, with no power of refusal in anything that holder chose to order.

Then the clerk read the disposition, which was a single sentence in each instrument and the same sentence in all three. The forfeit holdings were granted to the Baron of Kaldo, to hold of the Crown on the same terms as Kaldo.

Ferra, beside him, did not move. Ward, behind him, did not move either. Emeric signed where the clerk showed him, three times, and the clerk sanded the three signatures and blotted them and put the instruments into three folders, and that was all.

It was said in the corridor afterward, as Emeric had half known it would be, by a man he had never met.

He was pleasant. He was perhaps fifty, with good gloves and a chancery seal on a ribbon, and he fell into step beside Emeric on the way to the stair as though they had arranged it.

"You'll forgive me, Your Highness. I only wanted to say that it was very neatly done." He smiled. "Three forfeit territories on a frontier, a season after a private war. The Crown can't leave them empty, and it doesn't care to arm anybody new down there, and it certainly isn't going to hand three baronies to three new men who'd owe their seats to whichever faction put them forward. So it gives them to the man who's already standing there. Already governing. Already fed." He paused at the head of the stair. "It is very like the arrangement that sent you south in the first place, when you think about it. The cheapest place to put a thing is where it already is."

"I'd thought that," Emeric said.

"I'm sure you had." The man looked genuinely pleased, the way a teacher is pleased with a slow pupil. "And three baronies is a great deal to hold. A great deal of room. A man can do almost anything with that much room." He inclined his head. "Good day, Your Highness."

He went down the stair. Emeric watched him go.

"Who was that?" he said.

"Chancery," Ward said. "Doesn't matter who." He was watching the stair too. "He meant it as rope."

"I know what he meant it as."

"Good," Ward said.

His father saw him that afternoon, in the long audience hall, at carrying volume, in front of a court.

It lasted perhaps four minutes. Harrold sat where he always sat and Emeric stood where a son stood, and around them the hall went on with its own business, clerks and petitioners and a bishop's secretary and forty people who were pretending not to listen and were listening to every word. The King congratulated the Baron of Kaldo on the resolution of a long and troublesome matter. The Baron of Kaldo thanked His Majesty for the Crown's confidence. The King observed that the south was a great responsibility. The Baron agreed that it was.

That was all. Every word of it carried to the doors, and every word of it was for whoever was standing at the doors, and there was not one sentence in it meant for him.

He had known there would not be. There was nothing to whisper. Nothing in that hall was close enough to whisper to, and his father had not come down from where he sat, and would not. Keller had recommended. The Crown had ratified. Harrold had visibly not been in the room where it was decided, and Emeric had checked, carefully, and had not been able to find one trace of his father's hand anywhere in the three instruments. He could not tell whether that was a father protecting an appearance or a king who had genuinely had nothing to do with it. He found, standing there at carrying volume in front of forty people, that he could not tell which he wanted it to be.

It was worse than a refusal. A refusal would at least have been addressed to him.

He bowed. The King inclined his head. Emeric walked back down the length of the hall with his face arranged the way it had been arranged in that hall for fifteen years, and not one person watching him could have said that anything had happened, and nothing had.

Corbin found him in the gallery outside, as Corbin always did, with at least three people in earshot.

"Brother." The same warm voice, the same open face, the same hand on the shoulder that he had been putting on Emeric's shoulder for twenty years in front of people. "I heard. Three baronies. I couldn't be happier for you." He smiled round at the three people in earshot, including them. "It's the most fortunate disaster I've ever heard of."

"It was very fortunate," Emeric agreed.

"You must be exhausted. All those books." Corbin squeezed the shoulder and let it go. "We must dine before you go south. I'll send."

He would not send. He had never sent. He went away down the gallery with his three people, pleasant and unhurried, and Emeric stood where he had been left and noticed, with a kind of distant interest, that he felt nothing at all.

He had spent seven years running a barony. He had learned in that time to recognise the shape of maintenance, a man tending an arrangement by small regular deposits so that it never had to be examined, and he had seen it in Wexler's accounts and in Sethe's and in the way a factor keeps a slow payer sweet. It took him about four seconds to see it in his brother. Every warm word in that gallery had been a deposit, paid in public, into an account whose balance was that Corbin had always been kind to his poor wastrel brother, in front of everybody, for twenty years, and could not possibly be anything but glad for him.

What was new was that it did not hurt. He noticed that. He turned it over and looked at it and found that it interested him a good deal more than anything his brother had said.

Waylon came to the inn that evening, and nobody had arranged it.

Ward came up to say there was a young man downstairs asking for him, and Emeric went down expecting a clerk, and found his youngest brother standing in the common room in a good coat with his hat in his hands, grown, and a head taller than the last time Emeric had seen him, and looking as though he had very nearly not come in.

They sat at a table by the fire. Neither of them knew how to do it.

Emeric was better at people than he had been. Eight years of the long room and the table and Ferra's arguments had seen to that, and it did not help him at all. The thing sitting between them was fifteen years of a mask, the drunk and the wastrel and the brother nobody could rely on, which he had worn on purpose and for good reasons and which had had victims, and one of them was sitting across the table from him turning his hat round and round in his hands.

They talked about horses. Waylon had a grey mare he was pleased with. Emeric asked about her feet. Waylon said they were good. They talked about the road south and whether it was as bad as people said, and it was. They talked about the weather in Kaldo, which Waylon had apparently wondered about.

After ten minutes Waylon stood up and said he had to go, and Emeric stood up too, and they shook hands like two men who had met at a fair, and Waylon went out.

Neither of them had said any of it. Emeric sat back down by the fire and stayed there until Ward came and told him the fire was out.

Paxton was at the chancery the next morning, to receive his own instrument, and Emeric paid him before he could open it.

He had worked it out on the road with Ferra and had it written up on separate books, so that it would not be tangled with anything the Crown was doing. Kaldo owed Milford for cattle and for feed, a running account four years old, and the price on that account had been fixed before Sethe's men put fire to Milford. The price of everything on the plains had climbed since the fire. Emeric paid the whole account at the old price, in full, in coin, before anybody raised it, so that the debt could be seen to survive the change of banner.

Ferra had pointed out, on the ninth day of the road, that he could make exactly the same point on a tenth of the money. She was right. He paid it anyway.

Paxton received the payment in the chancery anteroom. He was a quiet man and a careful one, and he had stood in the court five weeks ago and given the four days that condemned a baron, and he had known when he gave them what they would cost him. He read the sum. He read it again. Then he opened the Crown's instrument, and read that too, and Emeric watched him understand that as of the moment he had broken its seal he was steward of Milford under the Baron of Kaldo, and that the payment he had just received from the Baron of Kaldo was therefore a payment he would now have to administer, on the Baron of Kaldo's behalf, into the Baron of Kaldo's barony.

He noticed. Emeric saw him notice. He folded both papers together and put them inside his coat and said nothing whatever about it.

It was Ward who said it, outside, when Paxton had gone down the steps to his horse.

"That's four times."

Emeric waited.

"He warned you before the war, when he didn't have to. He stood on that plain and didn't move, and the men who wanted him to move are dead or ruined. He came to the court and gave the four days, and he knew what they'd cost him before he opened his mouth." Ward watched the big man mount. "Four times he's paid for standing near you. This one was his barony."

Emeric did not answer. There was nothing to answer. It was true.

There was one more thing that morning, and he did not notice it.

When the clerk had read Paxton's instrument and the anteroom was emptying, the Duke of Aldane had been standing by the far window with two other men of his rank, as men of his rank did on mornings when the chancery disposed of land. Emeric had seen him and nodded, and Aldane had nodded back. He had not changed much from the man who had given a disgraced prince a box of carved wooden soldiers at a farewell banquet eight years before and had looked, at the time, as though he found the gift very funny.

He did not look as though he found anything funny now. He did not look as though he found anything at all. When the clerk read the Grayrock instrument aloud, the attainder, the line extinguished, Aldane stood by the window with his hands behind his back and did not move in any way, for rather longer than a man stands still by accident.

Emeric noticed that he was there. He did not notice anything else.

They went home by the Wayford road, because it was the only road.

The sentence had been carried out by then. They came through Wayford in the second week of summer, three days after it, and the square was swept and empty and there was nothing in it to show, and the town went about its business round the edges of the empty square without looking at it, and did not look at the three riders coming through either. Ferra had her index on her knees in its case. She had got it back from the exhibits office on the last afternoon after a conversation Emeric had not been present for, and she would not tell him what it had cost her, and she had not let go of it since.

He was glad.

That was the uncomfortable thing, and he made himself look straight at it on the road, because he had learned what happened to men who did not look at things. He was glad of the baronies. He had done nothing to earn them and he was glad of them, because he had plans that needed land and money and men, and had been forbidden from acting on any of them for nearly four years, and now he had three times the land and something like four times the revenue and a junction that every road in the south ran through. He knew exactly how that sounded. He did not soften it in his own head.

He had fought a war he did not start, against men who came up his road. He had prosecuted a suppression he did not invent, against a man who had been burning his letters for three years. And he had come out the other end of both of them holding four times the land he had arrived with, and there was no version of that story that he could tell which did not sound precisely like the version his enemies would tell. The pleasant man in the chancery corridor would tell it one way. Corbin would tell it another. Both of them would be telling it by the autumn, in rooms he would never see.

Two months after they came home he wrote to Waylon.

It took him an evening. He wrote it eleven times and burned ten of them, and the eleventh was four lines long and said that he had been glad to see him, that Kaldo was colder than the capital and wetter, that the grey mare sounded like a good horse, and that his brother was welcome in the south whenever he cared to come. He read it over and saw everything it did not say, and sealed it anyway, and it was the hardest thing he wrote all year.

Then he went back to the long room, where Ferra had laid out the books of three baronies on the table beside the books of the fourth, and the covenant in its folder beside all of them.

He was now the largest holder on the southern frontier of the kingdom. He had a junction, a mine, a plain full of cattle and close on nine thousand people. And he had signed his name under a clause that said he could build nothing new on any of it, not a furnace, not a works, not a single manufactory, for as long as one coin of the Ducatis principal remained unpaid, and the principal had years to run.

Ferra looked at him across the table, and then down at the covenant, and did not say anything. She had written down the date she expected it to become intolerable four years ago, on the day he signed it, and she was not going to be the one to say it had come.
