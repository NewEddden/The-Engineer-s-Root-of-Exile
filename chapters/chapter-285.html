Chapter 285 - South Sentinel

The fog had gone by morning.

It went the way sea fog goes, all at once, in the half hour before the sun: the white thinning over the marsh and lifting off the dunes and going up in rags, and the cloud deck above it breaking with it, so that by eight the sky over the place they had come down in was a hard clean winter blue from one side to the other, and the sun came up out of the sea on their left hand over the reef, low and gold, and showed them where they were.

Emeric was on the top of the last dune when it did. He had been there since before light, waiting for it.

Behind him, the marsh: reeds and black water and mud, going away inland, flat, further than he could see, silver where the sun caught the channels. The Hod sat in it on her two hulls a hundred yards back, in a great scar of churned mud, with her bent fin and her snapped mast and her house's cupola catching the light, looking smaller than she had any right to. In front of him, the dunes going down to a long grey beach; and beyond the beach the lagoon, still as glass in the windless morning, a long shallow sheet of water inside the reef with the reef's back of black rock showing above it in a ragged line and the surf going over the rock on the far side white and slow.

And in the middle of the lagoon, a circle.

He could see it now. He had felt it in the dark; now he could see it. A ring of dark squared stones, broken, standing up out of the shallow water in a great round the size of the castle ward, with gaps in it where the stones had fallen; and inside the ring the water was darker, much darker, a round black pool in the lagoon's pale shallows, as if the lagoon had an eye.

The stone was in there. He felt it, in the dark water inside the ring, the great dense shape, quiet; emptier even than last night; spent.

And along the beach to the south, where he had felt the heavy slow breathing in the dark, a dozen grey seals lay heaped together on the sand by the water's edge, and one of them lifted its head and looked at him on the dune, for a long moment, without any fear at all; and put its head down again.

Behind him somebody came up the dune through the grass.

"Highness," said Arthur. He stood beside Emeric on the crest and looked at the lagoon and the ring and the reef and the sea, for a long time, without saying anything. Then he looked at the seals. "They're not frightened."

"No."

"Seals on the Isle go into the water if you so much as stand up." Arthur watched them. "These have never seen a man."

"Or not for a long time," said Emeric.



Ned had the camp turned into a working one by nine.

He did it the way he did everything, without fuss and without asking anybody's leave. The night camp's tents stayed where they were behind the last dune, but their stoves were going, and their doors were tied back, and there was a line strung between two of them with the Hod's wet canvas on it drying. There was a fire on the beach, a real one, of driftwood, for the water. There was a trench dug at the dunes' far end, a decent distance off, with one of Abloh's spare groundsheets rigged round it on driftwood poles for a screen, because Mira had said there would be, and nobody had argued with Mira since Bren's tent. And there was the Hod's jack, out of her hold, and her block and tackle, and two long baulks of driftwood, and Ned and Cade and Bren standing under her starboard fin in the mud to their knees, looking up at it, deciding how to bend it back.

"It'll take two days," said Ned, when Emeric came down off the dune. "Like I said. If the jack holds. If the root's not cracked, which I don't think it is, it's bent. Steel bends back." He looked at Emeric with the expression he wore when he was about to say something he had been thinking all night. "You're going up."

"Yes."

"Looking for what."

"Anything that could hurt us," said Emeric. "While you're under that fin with your back to the dunes." He looked at the marsh, and the land going away beyond it, flat and then not flat, to the south, where the sun was lighting something high and white along the edge of the sky that might have been cloud and might not. "People. Animals. Anything big. I don't think there's anything. I couldn't feel anything last night for a mile. But a mile's a mile."

"And the stone," said Ned.

"Not the stone. Not today." Emeric looked at the lagoon. "The stone tomorrow. When I know what else is here."

Ned nodded, as if that were sensible, and went back under his fin.

So it was four of them who went up: Emeric, and Arthur, and Declan, and Osric. The three who had suits, and Emeric, who did not need one.

That was the thing the six knew and the town did not, and the Hod's crew knew because they had seen it: that since the spring, since the dome and the week of the pod and the night after it, Emeric could fly bare. No suit, no stones on his back, no harness; only himself, and gravity cast on his own body to hold him, and air cast round him to move him, a hole made in the air ahead and the air behind rushing in to fill it and carrying him with it, as Old Edrin had taught the lift hulls' casters to fly. Mages had always been able to. It had only ever been a question of having enough in you to keep it up, and until the pod, he had not had enough. Now he had. It was finer than the suit. A suit lifted a body; a body lifted itself, and knew exactly what it was doing, the way a hand in a glove does not.

The others watched him go up from the beach, Sully with his mouth open. Sully had heard about it and had not believed it.

Then Arthur and Declan and Osric sealed their collars, and their suits' stones came up violet at their backs, and they went up after him.



From a thousand feet, in the clean morning, he saw how big it was.

He had not understood it in the dark. He had felt the reef and the lagoon and the dunes and the marsh, and the marsh going on inland further than he could reach, and he had thought, an island; a long low sandbank of an island, a few miles of marsh and dune behind a reef, the kind of place that a fishing boat might find in the old stories and never find again.

It was not a sandbank.

The marsh went on, south and east, for miles: a great flat green-brown country of reed and water and mud, cut with silver channels, low, so low it hardly stood above the sea; the face of the place toward the north-west, toward Kaldo, toward everything, flat and wet and shrouded every night, he did not doubt, in fog. The drowned land. A boat a day out from Fletcher's village would see nothing. A man on a mast would see nothing. There was nothing to see, from the north-west, but a low dark line on the water that might be land and might be weather, and was gone by the next tide.

And behind the marsh, the land rose.

Slowly, then quickly. Out of the marsh, a long way off, the ground went up into grass, green and gold in the low sun, rolling, with the dark of woods in its folds; and beyond the grass, hills; and beyond the hills, rising out of them in the middle of everything, white with snow along their tops, with a long bank of cloud lying against their southern sides, mountains. Real ones. A ridge of them, running away to the south and east further than he could see even from here, with the sun on their snow.

Rivers came down off them. He could see three from where he hung in the air, silver threads winding down out of the hills across the grass toward the marsh, and in one of them, far off, in a fold of the hills, a flash of white that did not move, that was a waterfall.

Arthur came up beside him in his suit, and hung there, and looked.

He did not say anything for a long time.

"That's not an island," he said at last.

"It's an island," said Emeric. "It's just a very big one."

"How big?"

"I don't know." He looked south, where the mountains went away into the haze. "Bigger than all four baronies." He was guessing. He thought he was guessing low. "A great deal bigger."

Declan and Osric came up, and hung, and looked, and said nothing either.

"And nobody's here," said Osric, after a while. He said it slowly, as if he were working it out as he spoke. "A place this size. With rivers and grass and woods. Two hundred miles off our coast. And nobody's here."

"That's what we're going to find out," said Emeric.



He went out with his sense in passes, the way Armand had taught him: never far, never long, never alone in his own head without something to come back to.

He had the cord on his wrist. He always had it, now, when he meant to go a long way out: a thin plaited cord of Abloh's, knotted tight round the bone of his left wrist so that it bit a little; and before every pass he closed his right hand round it and pulled, hard, until it hurt, and held the hurt, and went out from it. And when he came back, he came back to the hurt. Not to the cord. To the hurt. A tower mage, Armand had told him, years ago, had gone out to the walls of a room once without one, and had not come back for a day and a night, and had never been quite the same after.

So he went out in passes. Short ones. Half a mile, a mile, two at the most; then back to the cord, and breathe, and the ache behind his eyes, and Arthur's voice beside him in the air saying the time; and then on to the next place, a few miles further, and out again.

They went south and east across the marsh first, low, the four of them, in a line a quarter of a mile apart, with Emeric in the middle; and at every few miles he stopped, and hung, and closed his hand on the cord, and went out.

Nobody.

Every time. Nobody. He went out over the marsh and felt it full of its small lives, fish and frogs and eels and the creeping things of mud and reed, and birds, thousands of birds, wading birds standing in the channels and wildfowl on the open water in great rafts that went up in clouds as the four of them passed over, wheeling and crying. He went out over the grassland beyond it and felt rabbits, or things like rabbits, hundreds of them, in their holes in the slopes; and deer in the woods' edges, small ones, a few dozen in a herd, grazing, that did not lift their heads as the four fliers went over a thousand feet up; and foxes, perhaps, or something like them, alone, in the folds; and hawks in the air with them, riding the morning's first warmth up the slopes. He went out over the hills and felt goats, he thought, on the high rocks, and nothing larger.

No people. Not one. No note that was a man's, or a woman's, or a child's, anywhere he reached, all that long morning. No smoke. No field. No track. No wall. Nothing, from the marsh to the mountains' foot, that a human hand had ever made.

And nothing big. That was the other thing he had come for. Nothing on the land that was larger than a deer, or denser than a goat; nothing that had a note in it like the thing in the lagoon had, or like the scale in his drawer at home had once had, or the tooth.

He came back from the fifth pass, over the foothills, with his head aching behind his eyes as if a thumb were pressed into each of them, and found Arthur hanging in the air beside him with a hand out, not touching him, ready to.

"You were a long time," said Arthur.

"How long?"

"Three hundred count. You said two hundred."

Emeric closed his hand on the cord again, and felt the hurt, and was there. "There's nothing," he said. "Nothing big. Nothing that could hurt us. No people." He looked at the mountains. "There's nobody here, Arthur. There's never been anybody here, or not for a very long time."

"Then who put the stone in the lagoon?"

Emeric did not answer that.

They flew on east.



They came to the east shore at a little after one, over the hills' last shoulder, and found the bay.

It was enormous. A great deep curve of the coast, bitten into the island's eastern side between two long headlands of grey rock that went out into the sea like arms; so wide across that the far headland was blue with distance, and so deep that the water inside it, in the noon sun, was a dark clear green going down to black. Cliffs along its northern arm, white, crowded with birds, so many that the cliffs moved. A beach along its inner shore, long and pale. And the sea outside it, beyond the headlands, quiet, grey-blue, going away east to the edge of the world with nothing on it.

A harbour. The best harbour he had ever seen, from the air or anywhere else. A fleet could have lain in it. A fleet could have lain in it in any wind.

"Gods," said Declan, which was the first thing Declan had said since the marsh.

They came down the bay's length, low, toward its inner shore; and Emeric, in the middle of the line, with his sense open and nearly too tired now to hold it open, felt something.

Not a note. Not a living thing. A place where the mana was different.

It was faint. So faint he might have missed it, if he had not been so tired that everything else had gone quiet in him. Ahead, at the bay's inner shore, in the shallows along the beach, the thin sea mana of the bay went, very slightly, thicker. Not much. Not a stone's brightness; not even a spent stone's glow. A difference. A long low ridge of slightly-more, lying in the water along the shore, in a shape, with sixteen faint knots in it spaced round its edge like the beads on a necklace; as if something there were breathing out, very slowly, a long way under the water, in its sleep.

"There," he said, and pointed. "Down there. Along the shore. Something."

They could see nothing. The tide was in. The bay's water lay smooth and green over the shallows along the beach, and there was nothing in it but the sand under it, and weed, and the sun on the surface.

"I can't see anything," said Osric.

"No," said Emeric. "Wait for the tide."



They waited for it on the beach. There was nothing else to do. The tide went out slowly all afternoon, and they sat on the pale sand above the line of the weed and ate the cold oatcakes and salt pork Declan had packed in his suit's pouch that morning, and drank from a stream that came down out of the hills at the bay's head clear and cold and tasting of stone; and Emeric lay on his back on the sand with his arm over his eyes and let his head stop aching.

The trees were the first thing.

Arthur saw them. They stood along the top of the beach at the bay's head, behind the sand, in a long grove: tall and slender, grey-trunked, with no branches at all until the very top, where a great crown of long green leaves burst out of each of them like a fountain, each leaf the length of a man, rattling softly in the wind off the sea. Arthur went and stood under them for a long time with his head back.

"What are they," he said, when he came back.

"Palms," said Emeric, from under his arm.

"What's a palm?"

"A tree from somewhere hot." He took his arm off his eyes and looked at them. They were palms; there was no mistaking them. He had seen them in a hundred pictures and once, on the other side of everything, on a beach, in a warm wind. "Somewhere much hotter than this."

"There's nothing else like them on the island," said Arthur. "I looked from the air. Oak in the woods. Ash. Pine on the hills. Nothing like those. Only here." He looked back at them. "In a row."

Emeric sat up and looked at the grove again.

It was in a row. Not a straight row; it curved along the top of the beach with the shore's curve. But the trees in it were spaced, more or less, as a man would space them who meant them to be a grove and not a wood. And there were young ones among the old, grown from the old ones' fruit, and the young ones were in the gaps.

"Somebody planted them," said Arthur.

"Somebody planted them," said Emeric. "A long time ago. A tree doesn't walk here from somewhere hot on its own."

And the tide went out.



It came up out of the water at four o'clock, at the bottom of the ebb, along the inner shore of the bay, in the last of the afternoon light.

First a line. A long straight line of dark stone in the shallows, breaking the surface, running along the shore a hundred yards out from the beach, dead straight, edged, like the top of a wall. Then, as the water went down off it, another line at its far end, square to the first, going out. Then a third. Then the whole of it, showing under the last few inches of the bay's clear water, and then out of the water altogether, streaming, weed-hung, wet, black in the low gold light.

A slab.

A single slab of grey stone, lying flat in the bay's shallows along the shore. Long. Longer than the K Amara's keel. Six hundred feet of it if it was an inch, and two hundred across, as flat on its top as a table and as square at its edges as a block of Bram's; one piece, or so it seemed from the beach, with no joint or seam he could see in it anywhere. Weed hung off its edges in long green curtains. Barnacles crusted its top in grey patches like lichen. And round its rim, spaced along its edges at regular intervals, sixteen squat dark shapes stood up out of the slab's top, each the size of a man crouching, each crusted thick with weed and shell, as regular as the bolts on a ship's plate.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

They stood at the edge of the water, the four of them, on the pale sand, in the low gold light, with the bay's water running off the slab in a hundred small streams and the gulls coming down onto it at once, screaming, to pick at what the tide had left; and looked at it.

Then Declan said what all of them were thinking.

"No man built that," he said.

He said it quietly, the way he said everything. He did not say it as a question. He stood with his helmet under his arm and the sea wind in his hair and looked at the slab and said it, flatly, as he would have said that the wind was in the west; and nobody argued with him.

Emeric did not. He was thinking about the cave under the Isle. About the swept city and its tall doors and the grey figures at them, a thousand years old. About a hall carved with lists of people, every list smaller than the last.

About the sixteen faint knots of slightly-more he had felt in the water from the air, spaced round the edge of something, breathing out slowly in its sleep.

He walked out onto it.



He walked out through the last of the shallows to the slab's edge, in his boots, to the knee in cold water, and put his hand flat on its stone where it rose out of the bay, and felt it.

Stone. Grey, hard, close-grained, cold with the sea. Not cut from anything he knew; not Kaldo stone or Grayrock stone or the grey stone of the Isle's cliffs. Under the weed and the barnacles its face was smooth, very smooth, as smooth as the golem's shell had been; and it went down into the bay's bottom further than he could feel.

He climbed up onto it. It was a long step up, his own height and more out of the water, and he did it on a lightened stride and stood on its top among the screaming gulls with the water running off it round his boots; and walked along it, across the barnacles and the slippery green weed, to the first of the sixteen squat shapes at its rim.

It was a stone. Under the shell and the weed, set into a socket in the slab's top, a great rough lump the size of a crouching man. And it was a stone in the other sense, too; he felt it before he touched it, faint, and then less faint as he came close: a held thing, a quiet thing, nearly empty, with a slow small trickle of the bay's mana going into it from the water round its foot and a slower trickle going out. Breathing, in its sleep. That was the difference he had felt from the air.

He put his hand on it.

Nearly empty. As near empty as a stone could be and not go to dust. Asleep, as the golem's stone had been asleep in its chest before he woke it; and he took his hand off it, quickly, because of that.

He went round all sixteen. Arthur came out onto the slab behind him, and Declan, and Osric last, and they stood back and watched him go from one to the next along the rim, a hand on each, a breath, and on. They were all the same. Sixteen stones, sixteen sockets, sixteen sleepers, near empty, breathing a trickle in and out. None of them was a gravity stone. He did not know what they were. He thought he could have found out, if he went into one; and he did not go into one.

"Is it these?" said Arthur, when he came back along the rim. "That pulled us?"

"No."

He was sure. He stood in the middle of the slab and turned and looked back the way they had come, west, over the hills and the grass and the marsh, a week's walk away on the far side of the island, toward the north-west reef where the Hod lay in the mud. "These couldn't pull a leaf. They're nearly empty. They've been nearly empty for a hundred years." He turned his head. "And it didn't come from here. The pull came along a line. From the reef, on the other side. From the thing in the lagoon." He looked down at the slab under his boots. "This is something else."

"What?"

"I don't know."

He did not. He stood on six hundred feet of flat stone that no man had built, with sixteen sleeping stones round its rim that were not gravity stones, in a harbour that a fleet could have lain in, under a grove of trees from somewhere hot that somebody had planted; and he did not know what any of it was.

Osric, who had walked to the slab's shoreward edge while Emeric went round the stones, called out.



The rock face was behind the palms.

They had not seen it from the air, or from the beach: the grove hid it. At the bay's head, behind the trees, the hill came down to the shore in a low cliff of the same close grey stone as the slab, fifty feet high; and the cliff's face, from the palms' roots to as high as a man could reach standing on another man's shoulders, was cut.

Marks. Rows of them. Small and deep and close, cut into the rock in long straight lines from one side of the face to the other, worn by the weather but not worn away; and Emeric, standing at the cliff's foot among the palms' grey trunks with his head back, knew them. Not their meaning. Their hand. He had seen them in a cave under the Isle, carved on the walls of a hall in long lists. He had seen them on the other side of everything, in a way, in his own head, all autumn, in the marks of a sleeping stone he had been learning to read; the same small dense strokes, the same way of turning a corner.

The same hand.

He could not read a word of it. He could read the stone's code, a little, now; he could not read their writing at all.

But he could read a map.

Because part of the face, at its left end, by the last palm, was not writing. It was a picture. A long low curve cut in the rock with a single deep line, wandering, with bays in it and headlands, from the cliff's top down to near its foot; and he looked at it for a long time before he understood that it was a coast. A whole coast. A long one, going from the top of the carving to the bottom, with a great bay cut into it near the bottom, and a long finger of land going up out of it near the top. And out to the right of it, in the sea, two shapes. An island, small, near the top. And an island, large, at the bottom right, with a great curve bitten out of its far side, and a deep notch in its near side where a ring might be.

And in each island, cut deep, the same mark. One sign, the same, twice. And between them, a line. Straight, cut into the rock from one sign to the other, all the way up the face of the cliff.

He knew the coast.

He did not know it the way a fisherman knows his own shore. He knew it the way a man knows a map he has studied: the shape of it, the long run of it, south to north. It was Ostrellin. It was the coast of the whole of the known world, from somewhere far in the south to somewhere far in the north, the way it would look if you were very high and could see all of it at once. Wrong in places; the bays were not where the crown's charts had them, and there was land at the top that no chart had at all. But it was the coast.

And the small island at the top, with the mark cut in it, was where the Isle of Shards was. And the large island at the bottom, with its bay and its ring, was this.

Two places. The same sign. A line between them.

"It's the cave," he said aloud. "The cave under the Isle. And here." He put his hand on the rock beside the line, not touching it. "They knew each other. The ones who cut the cave and the ones who made this. They were the same people, or they knew each other, and they drew a line between them, the way you'd draw a road." He looked at the line going up the face of the rock. "Twelve hundred miles. Near enough."

"Twelve hundred miles," said Declan.

"More, maybe." Emeric looked at the carving. "All the way up the coast. And they knew where both places were. They knew the shape of the whole of it."

He looked further right, past the large island, to where the cliff's face went on, and the carved sea went on, past the island, to the edge of the cut stone.

There was more coast there.

Out at the far right, past the edge of the known sea, cut fainter, worn, a second line: a second coast, going up the rock beside the first, a long way off to the east of it. And between the two coasts, in the empty sea, drawn very small, something that was not an island. Something round, with lines going down from it like roots, and nothing under the lines but sea.

Floating, he thought. Something floating.

He did not say it aloud. He stood at the cliff's foot with the palms rattling over his head and the slab streaming behind him in the last of the light, and looked at the thing drawn floating over an empty sea between the edge of the known world and a coast nobody had ever seen, for a long time; and then turned away, and walked back down to the beach.



There was one more thing, and Osric found it, at the very end of the light.

At the bay's head, where the stream came down, the slab did not end at the shore. Along the beach toward the stream there were other stones in the sand, squared, set, running out into the water in a line, half buried, weed-hung; a quay, or what had been one, a long time ago, before the sand came over it. And in the silt at its end, face down, where the stream's mouth had silted up round them, three shapes.

Squat. Thick. Each the size of a big man on his hands and knees, grey, half sunk in the silt with the weed over them. Heads, and shoulders, and arms; broad backs; short thick legs. Figures. Stone figures, lying face down in the mud at the end of an old quay, as if they had been working there, hauling something, and had all three lain down together at the same moment and not got up again.

Osric stood over them and did not touch them.

Nobody did. They had all four been on the hill in November, or had heard about it from the ones who had, or had carried Bram down the hill track on a stretcher at midnight; and they stood round the three grey shapes in the silt at the end of the old quay in the dusk, and nobody moved, and nobody spoke.

Emeric went down on one knee in the silt beside the nearest. He put his hand out, slowly, with his sense open, toward its back, where on the golem under the Isle the chest's hatch had been, on the front; and felt.

Nothing.

He went round all three, kneeling in the cold mud, with his sense on them, before he put his hand on any of them. Nothing in any of them. No charge in their frames. No tank. And when he got the first of them turned on its side, with Arthur and Declan heaving, and the silt sucking at it, and found its chest, and its hatch, and the seat behind the hatch, there was nothing in the seat. Empty. A hollow the shape of a long small egg, with silt in it.

The second the same. The third the same.

"Empty," he said. He sat back on his heels in the mud. "All three. Somebody took their stones."

Osric let out a breath he had been holding, it seemed to Emeric, since the quay.

"When?" said Arthur.

"When they left." Emeric looked at the three grey shapes lying in the mud at the end of the quay. "Whoever was here. When they went, they took the stones out of everything that walked, and left the bodies lying where they stopped." He put his hand on the nearest one's broad stone back. It was cold, and rough, and not quite like the golem's shell had been; coarser. Banded, almost, under his palm, as if it had been made in layers. "These can't wake. Not ever. There's nothing in them to wake."

"Dockhands," said Declan, looking at the quay, and at the three heavy figures at the end of it, face down, as if they had been hauling.

"Dockhands," said Emeric.

He knelt there a moment longer in the silt with his hand on its back. Then he got up.

"Tess will want them," he said. "When we come back with a ship that can carry them." He looked at the sky, going grey over the bay; and west, over the hills, where the Hod was, a week's walk off and a long hour's flight. "We go back now. Before dark. Ned'll think we've drowned."

"Ned'll be under his fin," said Osric. "He won't have noticed we've gone."

They flew back west across the island in the dusk, over the hills and the grass and the woods and the darkening marsh, four small lights at their backs; and came down on the beach by the Hod's fire at a little after seven, and Ned came out from under his fin, black to the elbows, and looked at them, and said, "Well?"

Emeric sat down by the fire on a driftwood log, very tired, with his head aching and his hands cold and the cord's mark red on his wrist; and told him.

When he had finished, Ned said nothing for a while. He sat on his heels by the fire and looked into it.

"A harbour," he said at last.

"The best I've ever seen."

"Nobody in it."

"Nobody. Nobody on the whole of it. Not for a long time."

Ned looked into the fire a while longer. He was not a man who said what he thought. He said it now.

"Fletcher would give his left arm for a harbour nobody's in," he said.

And Emeric, by the fire, with his head aching, found that he had been thinking exactly that, and a great many other things after it, since about two o'clock in the afternoon; and that Ned knew it; and he did not say anything, and nor did Ned, and the fire burned down.
