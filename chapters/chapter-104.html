Chapter 104 - Over the Water

The oil came down through the seams before the smoke did.

It came down burning. Thin lines of it, running along the underside of the deck planks where it had soaked through, and then gathering at the low places and falling in drops, each drop a small bright bead that fell through the dark of the hold and hit the bilge and went out with a hiss, or hit a sack and did not. One hit the sacks Finn had been lying on. A small flame stood up on them, blue at the root, and leaned.

Rylan was at Emeric's back with the dead man's knife. He cut the cord on his wrists, and then the sacking round his hands, and then the cords on each of his fingers one at a time, quickly, without cutting him more than twice. Emeric's hands came round in front of him like two dead things. He could not feel them. He could not close them. The blood came back into them all at once and it was like putting them in a fire.

"Up," Rylan said.

He had Finn already. The boy was against his chest with his arms round his father's neck and his face in his father's shoulder, and he was not making any sound at all now, and Rylan went up the ladder one-handed with the boy held against him with the other, and put his shoulder to the hatch.

It did not move.

He set his feet on the rungs and put his shoulder to it again, and Emeric felt him do it, felt the whole of what the rite had put in him go into his back and his legs at once, a knight's strength, the thing that made a knight, all of it on a square of planking with a bar across the top. The wood cracked. He hit it a third time. The bar on the far side broke, a long splitting crack like a tree going, and the hatch went up and over, and fire came down through the square of it like water through a sluice.



Fire was his.

Of all the things in the world, it was the one he had never had to learn. He had pulled it off candles and out of grates and off the end of his own fingers since he was a boy in the tower, and it came to him the way a dog comes to the one man in the house it has decided is its own. He did not have to make any of it. It was all here already. The boat was made of it now.

He put his hands up, his dead burning hands, and took hold of the fire in the hatchway, and turned it back.

He could not have put out a burning boat, and he did not try. He leaned on the fire in the square of the hatch the way he leaned on the still air at the end of his room, and it leaned back the other way, up and out of the square, like a curtain drawn aside, and stood there, roaring, a wall of it on three sides of the opening, with a gap in the middle the width of a man.

"Go," he said.

Rylan went up through the gap with the boy. Emeric went up after him, with the fire held back on either side of him in his two hands like two doors he was holding open against a gale, and came out onto the deck.

The deck was burning from end to end. The mast was a column of fire from the deck to the masthead, and the oil on the planks burned with a low blue flame that ran along the seams and stood up yellow wherever it found something to stand on: a coil of rope, a barrel, the quick-voiced man, who lay by the foot of the mast on his back with his arms out and his throat open. The heat was like a hand on the face. The smoke went up straight into the black sky and the light of the fire went out over the water a long way, and showed the river running fast and black and high, and far off across it on one side the town wall with its eight lamps in a curve along the top, and far off on the other side, nothing. Reeds.

He did not hold all of it. He could not have, and he did not need to. He held a lane. From the hatch to the rail on the side that faced the town, a lane the width of two men across the burning deck, the fire leaned back from it on either side as far as he could make it lean and no further, because he was going to need what he had left, and he knew it, and he was already short.

They went down the lane to the rail.



Garrin was off the boat.

Emeric did not look for him. He did not have to. He had his note now, and his body went to it of itself, the way a hand goes to an itch, before he had decided anything: low, on the water, close to it, a little way off the stern on the dark side of the boat, moving away. Moving fast. A skiff. One man in it, rowing hard, long even strokes, a man who had rowed in the dark before and knew how to do it without splashing. Going away from the town. Toward the far bank, and the reeds, and the marsh behind them, where the channels went off north and east among the reed beds for miles and nothing heavy could follow.

Emeric stood at the rail with the fire held back behind him and felt him go.

He could reach him. That was the thing. He could feel exactly where he was, as clearly as if the man had been standing on the deck in front of him with a lantern, and if he could feel him he could see him, and if he could see him he could put fire on him, the way he had put a bead on the cord on Rylan's wrist. A long way. Further than he had ever thrown anything. But there was one push left in him, one good one, and he could feel the size of it, and he could put the whole of it across that water into the skiff and the man in it, and end the thing that had started at a fire on the road south three years ago.

Or he could keep it.

Finn coughed. Against his father's shoulder, in the smoke, a small dry choking cough, the first sound he had made since the hold.

The town bank was a long way off. Too far to swim with a boy in December in a river this high and this cold; a strong man would be dead of the cold before he was halfway, and Emeric knew it the way he knew the boiling point of water. The boat was going to go. Her deck was already soft under his boots. The fire was eating its way back down into the hold behind them.

He kept it.

He felt Garrin's note go on away from him over the water, smaller, quieter, into the edge of the reeds. Into them. Thin, and then thinner, among the channels, the way a scent goes thin on the wind, until he was not sure whether he was feeling it or remembering it. Then he was only remembering it.

He would know it again. Anywhere.



"Crouch," he said.

Rylan looked at him.

"Crouch. I'm getting on your back." Emeric was already moving. "Keep the boy in your arms. Hold him tight. When I say, jump. As far as you can. Further than you've ever jumped in your life. At the town."

Rylan looked at the water between the boat and the town bank, black and fast and a very long way. He did not say that it was too far. He did not say anything. He went down on one knee on the burning deck with Finn held against his chest, and Emeric got onto his back with his arms round Rylan's neck and his knees in Rylan's sides, as a boy gets onto his father's back, and Rylan stood up under the two of them as though they weighed nothing.

They did not weigh nothing yet.

He took his own weight off first. That was easy; that was nothing; he had done it so many times behind the shutters that it came to him as easily as the fire did. He felt himself go light on Rylan's back, light as a coat.

Then the boy.

He reached past Rylan's shoulder to the small body in Rylan's arms, and took hold of it, and lifted. Only the weight of it, the way he had lifted the wood basket and the dog. Finn pushed back. Not much. A little, the way the needle on the board had swung a little for him in October, less than a man and more than a dog: a small unwillingness, from the inside, that gave. Finn went light in his father's arms. Rylan felt it. Emeric felt him feel it, felt the whole of Rylan's body go still under him for one breath, astonished, as the boy he was holding stopped weighing anything.

Rylan he did not try. He did not need to try. He had felt Rylan's note in the hold, tight and dense and enormous, a rite-made knight's, and lifting him would have been like lifting the wall. Rylan would carry his own weight. Rylan's legs were made for it.

Behind them something in the hold gave way, and the deck at the stern dropped a foot with a groan, and the fire roared up out of the hatch through the place where Emeric was no longer holding it.

He reached back. Past Rylan, past the fire, past the far rail, into the still black air on the dark side of the boat over the water, the air that had been lying there all night and had nothing to do with any of them. He took hold of it. He leaned on it, the way Linnet had told him to in bay three, gently, and let it lean on everything between it and him.

"Now," he said.

Rylan jumped.

He jumped off the rail of a burning boat with a child in his arms and a man on his back, with everything the rite had put into him, every year of it, all at once, and Emeric threw the air behind them as hard as he had ever thrown anything in his life.

They flew.

There was no other word for it, then or after. The rail went out from under them and the fire went away behind them and they were over the water, high, higher than the rail, Rylan's legs still driving at nothing and the air coming after them like a door slammed open in a gale, pushing, carrying, the town's eight lamps coming up at them out of the dark, and the river going by underneath black and fast and very far down. Finn made a sound. It was not a frightened sound. It was the sound a child makes on a swing, at the top.

The air went out of him.

It went the way it had gone in bay three, at the end of the forty feet, all at once, the way the light goes out of a lamp stone that has given its last. It was behind them, carrying them, and then there was nothing behind them at all, and nothing holding him light, and he came back into his own weight on Rylan's back with a jolt like missing a stair in the dark, and they went down.

Short. He knew it as they fell. Short of the bank.

They hit the water.

The cold hit him all over at once, like a board swung flat. It drove the breath out of him and filled his clothes and his boots and his mouth, and he went under, and came up, and his feet found the bottom. Mud. Stones. They had come down in the shallows below the bank, short of it, but not by much: the water was at his chest, and then, as Rylan waded, with Finn held up high out of it in both arms, at his waist, and then at his knees, and they came up out of the river onto the stones at the foot of the bank under the town wall, and stood there, the three of them, streaming, in the light of the lamps.



Ward was on the bank.

He was standing beside a boat. It was the gate crew's skiff, the one they kept at the foot of the river stair for the lines, and he had it half off its rollers and half into the water, alone, with his sword belt off and thrown in the bottom of it. He had his hands on its gunwale. He had stopped pushing.

He had been looking at the river. At the burning boat. He had been looking straight out across the water at the burning boat, from the bank, when the three of them came over the water at him out of the dark, high, from the boat's rail, and came down in the shallows twenty yards in front of him.

He looked at them now. At Rylan, waist-deep, with the boy held up. At Emeric.

He did not say anything at all.

He let go of the skiff and came down the stones into the water to his knees and took Emeric by the arm and walked him out of the river, and took off his own cloak, and put it round him, and fastened it at the throat with his own hands, as he had not done since Emeric was seven years old.

"Highness," he said.

That was all. He did not ask how. He did not ask how far it was from the boat to the bank, or how a man jumps it with another man on his back and a child in his arms, or why the air had come after them like that, out of a still night. He had seen it. He had his face set toward it, the way he set it toward any wall he meant to understand later, and he said nothing about it, then or after.

The barn had been straw, Emeric learned later. Straw and a broken lantern and nobody there. Ward had seen it for what it was inside a quarter of an hour, and left Osric to put it out, and come back through the South Gate at a run and up to the line room, and found nobody on the stool, and the card on the wall, and the river stair's shutter up and still. And then the water gate unbarred, and the lantern in the river at the foot of the stair, and out on the water, beginning, the fire.



They came down the river stair behind him with crossbows.

Osric first, black to the elbows from the straw fire, and a dozen of the garrison behind him, the barn men, back through the South Gate and down through the lower ward at a run, with lanterns and wound bows. A woman in the lower ward had seen it. Her house stood under the narrow walk with an upper window that looked over the wall at the river, and she had been up with a sick child, and she had seen Rylan walk the prince out through the water gate at one in the morning with a lantern, and then the lantern go into the river, and a skiff with no light go out into the dark. She had run to the guardroom in her nightgown as the barn men were coming in, and told Osric.

They came down the stair and spread along the stones at its foot and put up their bows. All of them. At Rylan.

Rylan stood still.

He stood in the shallows with the water at his knees and the lamps on him. He set Finn down on the stones behind him, gently, and put him behind his legs. Then he stood up straight in front of the boy with his hands open at his sides, empty, the dead man's knife gone somewhere in the river, and did not lift them, and did not say anything, and looked at Osric over the bows.

"He took you," Osric said. He was looking at Emeric, but his bow was on Rylan, and it did not waver. "She saw him. Out the water gate. He took you out to them."

"Stop," said Emeric.

Nobody lowered anything.

"Stop." He pushed himself off Ward's arm and stood on his own, in Ward's cloak, streaming. His voice was not loud. He did not have anything left to make it loud with. "Put them down. All of them. Now."

Ward said it after him, in the voice that he used on a wall, and the bows came down, one after another, slowly, Osric's last.

"They had his son," Emeric said. "Look behind him. That's his son." He looked at Rylan in the water. "Let the man take his son home. Seven days. Then we'll talk."

Osric opened his mouth.

"Seven days," Emeric said. "Nobody speaks of tonight. Nobody touches him. Nobody goes to his house." He looked along the line of them on the stones. "That's an order. From me. Not the captain. Me."

Nobody said anything.

Rylan looked at him across the water for a long breath. Then he turned and picked Finn up off the stones and held him, and walked up out of the river past the line of the garrison without looking at any of them, and they moved aside to let him by.



Ferra was on the river stair.

She had come down with the garrison. The woman in her nightgown had woken half the lower ward running to the guardroom, and the noise of it had come up the lanes as far as Bram's door, and Ferra had come down after the crossbows in her old coat with her hair down and a shawl over it, and had stood on the stair above them through all of it, and nobody had sent her away because nobody had seen her.

Emeric saw her as they came up out of the water. He saw her lips move.

"Three," she said.

She said it under her breath, the way she had counted the lamps on the wall the night they first came on, and the buckets on the night of the war. He did not hear it. He saw it. One, two, three, as the three of them came up out of the river onto the stones, and then her lips stopped moving, and she let her breath out.

Rylan came up the stair with Finn in his arms.

Ferra came down two steps to meet them. She took the shawl off her own head and shoulders, and put it round the boy, round and round, over his wet head and his wet back and his father's arm, and tucked the end of it in, and stepped back to let them by.

Then she looked down the stair at Emeric, standing at the bottom of it in Ward's cloak with the river running out of his boots.

She looked at him for as long as it took Rylan to go up the rest of the stair and through the water gate and out of the light. She did not come down the last steps. She did not say anything. Then she turned and went up the stair after Rylan, with her hair down and nothing over it, into the town.

Out on the river the boat burned down to the water. Emeric stood on the stones and watched it go. The mast went first, falling sideways into the river with a long hiss and a cloud of steam that went up white against the black. Then the deck. Then the rest, until there was only a low red glow lying on the water off the east wall, going slowly downstream, getting smaller, and the eight lamps above the town shining on, and on the far bank, where the reeds began, nothing at all.
