Chapter 104 - Stations

They went down to the lower water in the first week of the autumn, in the steamboat, with the tide.

It was more cargo than she had carried since the ore run. Nine crates of cells lashed in her waist under a tarpaulin, twenty to a crate, every cell the forge had made since the summer, packed in straw with their caps screwed down and their splinters dull, because Orrin would fill them at the other end and not before. A long box of stone rods from the forge, each in its own groove of felt. Bram's tools. Two reading stones and a deciding stone in a small iron case that Emeric kept on his knees. And a second case, smaller, with a lock on it that took Armand's key, which held the stones for the stations.

They had come off the new face, all of them, cut by Nevin in the pattern store over three weeks, read by Armand on three days each and entered in the strongroom ledger under a single heading in the crabbed hand. The station set. Lent. And under it a second heading, and a second set, smaller, cut from the same face in the same weeks and read on the same days. The reserve. Not yet anything.

Armand came with them. He sat in the stern with his hands in his lap and watched the banks go by, and said very little, and when they passed the place where the section had fallen in the thaw he looked at it for as long as it was in sight and then did not look back.

Ward came because the levers were his. Bram came because the housings were.

Fletcher was on the beach when they grounded. He was standing at the foot of his stocks with his arms folded, and he had evidently been standing there some time.

The hull stood on the stocks behind him.

Emeric had seen her in drawings for two years and in Fletcher's letters for one, and he had seen the section, and none of it had prepared him for her size. She was iron from keel to sheer, riveted in plates the width of a door, black with the first coat and shining where the tide had caught her, and she stood on her stocks above the high-water mark with her bow toward the bar like something that had come up out of the sea to rest. She was longer than the long room was wide. Her frames stood at their stations under the plating, every one of them where the drawing said, heavier than any join needed. The engine Bram had sent down in the summer was bedded in her and turned over sweet. Her screw hung at her stern on an iron arm that pivoted at the transom, so that it could be swung down into the water behind her or up and clear of it, a thing Fletcher had built exactly as drawn in the spring and asked about only once, by letter, to be told it was for beaching her. Along her sides, folded flat against the plating like the wings of a bat against its ribs, lay two sails on long battens of ash, which Abloh had cut in the loft over the soap house in Kaldo and sent down in a bale with a note that said Fletcher would know what to do with them. At each of her quarters, and at her bow, there was a housing let into the plating the size of a man's head, with nothing in it yet, which the drawing had said was for pumps.

And there were the holes. Low in her frames, at the turn of the bilge, at every station, round and clean and each the size of a man's fist, as they had been in the section.

Fletcher did not say anything. He waited, as he had waited through two letters and a bill and a summer.

Emeric walked past him to the hull and put his hand on the plating, where it was cold, over the hole in the nearest frame.

"You asked what I'd done to her," he said. "I told you I'd come down and tell you when there was something true to tell. There is."

He told him.

He did it standing at her side on the beach, with his hand on her, in plain words, the way he had once told Fletcher that iron did not sink if you gave it enough of the sea to push on. He told him about the black stone in the hill at Grayrock that the Crown bought at two Platinum the pound. He told him what it did when a man put his work into it, and what it did when the man let go and drew nothing back, and what it did when a charged stone stood at the other end of the thread instead of a man. He told him about the section, and the two faces, and the rivets going across the water in the thaw like shots, and why the frames at the stations had always been heavier than any join needed. He told him about a man in the Crown's own harness flying circles under a roof beam with nobody pouring anything into him, and about the cells in the crates on the steamboat behind them, and what the holes in her frames were for.

Fletcher listened to all of it without interrupting, with his arms folded, looking at his hull. When Emeric had finished he went on looking at her for some time.

Then he asked his first question, and it was a shipwright's.

"Where does it sit," he said, "against her weight?"

Emeric waited.

"Your stones. At the turn of the bilge." Fletcher unfolded one arm and pointed along her side at the holes. "That's low. That's below her middle, well below, with the engine in her and the boiler. You push a thing up from under its middle and it wants to go over. Same as a boat with her ballast on deck. Same as a man standing on a barrel." He looked at Emeric. "What stops her rolling over on your stones?"

It was the question. It was the only one that mattered, and Emeric had been waiting two years for somebody to ask it who knew why it mattered.

"The stones at the stations aren't set," he said. "They're fed, all the time, and watched. There's a reading stone on a dish of oil in her hold, and it watches the dish the way you'd watch a plumb, and the moment she leans a hand, a stone that's never tired and never asleep tells the stations on the low side to push and the high side to ease, or to pull. It's a man at the tiller who can feel the whole of her at once." He had seen Ward go over onto his face in midsummer with the push below his middle, and he had seen the deciding stone catch him. "Low's where they have to be, for the frames. The watching is what keeps her up."

"And when the watching stops?"

Emeric took the drawing out of his coat and unrolled it on the stocks and held the corners down with two of Bram's wrenches.

It was the section of her at her middle station, the whole of her from keel to deck, and it had the fed stones drawn low where the holes were. And it had another set drawn in, in a different ink, high up, on the deck beams under her deck, as far above her middle as the fed stones were below it.

"That's the reserve," he said. "If the feed ever stops, for any reason, I set those. All of them, at once, in one working, a hair under her weight. Nothing watches them. Nothing has to. They're above her middle, so she hangs from them the way a lamp hangs from a hook, and a lamp on a hook doesn't roll. And they're a hair under her weight, so she doesn't hang, quite. She comes down. Slowly. Onto whatever's under her."

Fletcher bent over the drawing. He read it the way Bram read drawings, twice, the second time slower, with his finger moving along the deck beams from station to station. He looked at the fed stones low down, and at the reserve high up, and at the line Emeric had drawn through her middle where her weight sat, and he did not say anything for so long that Ward, standing at the water's edge, looked round.

"That was the only thing I'd have argued with," Fletcher said at last. He straightened up. "Lift under her middle and nothing to catch her if it stopped. I'd have argued with that till you sent me home." He put one finger on the deck beams. "And it isn't there."

"No."

"You drew it high before I asked."

"I learned it on a man," Emeric said. "In the summer. He went over onto his face."

Fletcher looked at him for a moment. Then he rolled up the drawing, carefully, square at the ends, and handed it back.

"Well," he said. "Let's see her do it, then."

It took them nine days to fit her.

Bram seated the station stones himself, one to each hole, in housings off the any-size drawing, bolted through the frames where Fletcher had made them heaviest. He ran the rods from them up the inside of the frames, under the ceiling planking, to the deciding stone in its case on the bulkhead aft of the engine, and from the deciding stone down to a rack in her hold where the cells would sit, nine crates of them, necks in their rails. He set the dish in her hold on the centreline, gimballed so that it swung, and the reading stone on its bracket over it, looking down. He set the two brass levers on a post on her deck aft, where the helm was, heavier than Ward's and the same shape, so that a hand that knew the one would know the other. Orrin had come down on the second day with the carter, grumbling, and filled cells from dawn to dark for a week, and said that he had never in his life been paid to sit on a beach, and that it was the best posting he had ever had.

The reserve went in last. Bram seated it high, in housings bolted under the deck beams, one at each station, above the fed stones, where the drawing said. The stones sat in their cups with their collars screwed down, and Emeric did not touch them.

"They're not set," Fletcher said, looking at them.

"No. They're fitted. They're set if they're ever needed, and not before, because a set stone spends from the moment it's set and there's no way back." Emeric looked along the row of them under the beams. "It's a matched set. One face of the body, read together, fitted together. If they're set, they'll spend together, and if they're spent, they'll be replaced together, every one, never a fresh stone beside a tired one."

"Because of the section."

"Because of the section."

He did not say the other thing, which was that they were set by one man, and the man was him, and it was the one place on her where a floor still needed a man to put it there. He had not found a way round that. He had written it in the notebook in the column for things he could not answer, and it sat there, under Who tells it twice, with a line through that one and not through this.

They did it on the tenth morning, on the stocks, at low water, with the beach dry for fifty yards on every side.

Fletcher's men had been sent home. They had been sent home every evening for nine days, at the end of the day's work, before the rods went in and the stones went in and the crates were opened, and they had gone, because Fletcher sent them. On the tenth morning Emeric asked Fletcher to send them home again.

Fletcher said no.

"She needs weight walked on her," he said. "That's what your stone in the hold's for, you told me. Men walking her deck, from side to side, fore and aft. Your man at the levers can't walk her and work the levers both, and your smith is watching his housings, and the old one isn't to be let near her." He had heard that, too, and understood it without being told why. "My men built her. Six of them. Every rivet in her. They'll walk her."

"Then they'll see it."

"They'll see it." Fletcher did not look away. "They're going to see it anyway. You don't fly a thing that size off my beach in the dark and have nobody at the lower water notice in the morning that she's somewhere else. They'll see it today from her deck, with me standing there, or they'll see it one night from the shore, without me, and tell their wives it was a devil." He paused. "They built her. It's theirs to see."

It was the cost, and Emeric had known it would come, and he had not known it would come in Fletcher's voice. He looked at Ward. Ward looked back at him and did not say anything, which from Ward was an opinion.

"Six of them," Emeric said.

"Six."

"On her deck. And afterwards, the same as you."

"The same as me," said Fletcher. "Which is that nobody at the lower water will ever hear it from any of us that didn't hear it from you first." He turned toward the cottages. "They'll talk. Every man talks. But they'll talk slow, and they'll talk to each other, and they won't talk to a boat."

The six came down to the stocks in their work clothes and stood looking up at her side, and Fletcher told them, in about as many words as it took, what they were going to do. They did not say anything. One of them, the oldest, a man with a burn across the back of one hand that had come from a rivet forge twenty years before, looked at Emeric for some time with no expression at all, and then climbed the ladder to her deck with the rest.

Armand stood on the beach below her quarter, well back, with his hands at his sides, where anybody could see them.

Ward put his left hand on the lift lever and moved it forward.

For a long time nothing happened at all. The stations took the feed, and the deciding stone shared it among them, and the cells in their rails in her hold began to thin, all hundred and eighty of them together, the way a lake goes down in a dry month, a little and evenly everywhere. In the medium she was a long shape of iron with sixteen dense strange hot places in two rows low down in her frames, and above them another row, cold and quiet, waiting.

Then her keel came off the stocks.

It was a hand. The width of a hand, no more, all along her length at once, the way the section had come up off its stands in the thaw. There was grey morning light under her from the bow to the transom, and the blocks of the stocks stood under her empty, and she hung.

Nobody on her deck said anything. One of Fletcher's men put his hand out to the rail and held it, as if the deck were the thing that might move.

"Walk her," said Emeric.

Fletcher said it again, to his men, in his own voice, and they walked her. They went to the port rail together, all six, heavily, the way men walk a deck to try her, and in her hold the oil in the dish ran toward them and the reading stone saw it go. Emeric felt the deciding stone answer. The port stations took more. The starboard eased. The deck came back under the men's feet as level as a floor, and they stood at the rail and looked down at the beach a hand further off than it had been, and the oldest of them, the one with the burn, said something under his breath that was not a word.

They walked her to starboard. She came back. They walked her forward, all six in the bow, and her bow went down a hair and came back up, and they walked her aft and she did the same, and they walked her apart, three and three, and then all six at once to one side at a run, because Fletcher told them to and did not tell them why, and she leaned under it and caught herself and came back, and the whole time her keel hung a hand above the stocks with daylight under it.

Fletcher stood at her rail and did not walk. He put both hands flat on the rail and kept them there, and Emeric, watching him from the beach, understood that the old man was not holding on. He was feeling her. He was doing what he had done to every hull he had ever built, the first time she went into the water, standing on her and feeling how she lay. She did not lie on anything. He was feeling that.

"Down," Emeric said.

Ward brought the lever back, a notch at a time, and she came down a hand onto the stocks, softly, all along her length, the way a sheet comes down onto a bed. Then Ward took the lever past the notch. The stations went heavy. She settled onto her blocks with a long iron sound, not loud, the sound of a ship being told to stay where she was, and the stocks took her, and she stayed.

Nobody moved for some time.

Then the oldest of Fletcher's men came down the ladder, and walked round her bow on the beach, slowly, and looked at the stocks under her, and at the blocks, and put his burned hand flat on her plating where she sat, as Emeric had put his on the first morning. He stood like that for a while.

"Your Highness," he said, without turning round. "What else is going to do that?"

"Nothing yet."

The man nodded, as though that were a reasonable answer, and went up the beach to the cottages, and did not look back at her.

That night Emeric sat in Fletcher's cottage, in the wind off the bar, with the lamp between them, the way he had sat there two years ago with a drawing of a ship that did not sink.

"They'll talk," Fletcher said.

"You said."

"I'm saying it again so you know I haven't changed my mind." Fletcher turned his cup on the table. "Six men. They'll talk to each other tonight, and to their wives tomorrow, and by the end of the month there'll be a story at the lower water about the ship on Fletcher's beach that lifted off her stocks. It'll be wrong in every particular. It'll be closer than anything you've had up the river."

"I know."

"Is that a cost you can carry?"

Emeric thought about Saban's four versions, and the fifth, and a letter under the chancery's seal that had used the word works.

"It's one I've already paid," he said. "I just hadn't been sent the bill."

Fletcher looked at him for a moment, and then, unexpectedly, laughed, a short dry sound like a board creaking.

"I'll send you the bill," he said. "I always do."
