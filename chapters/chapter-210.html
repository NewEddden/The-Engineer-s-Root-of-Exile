Chapter 210 - License

The oil burned clean.

Bram did it at the long-room table, which was not where anybody burned anything, because Emeric had asked for it there. He had the small cask from the island on the boards in front of him, and a lamp from the finishing room with its wick trimmed and its reservoir empty, and he filled the reservoir from the cask with a funnel, and lit it, and turned the wick up, and set it down in the middle of the table beside the ordinary lamp that was already there, which burned the lamp oil off the Emberfell boats that Saban bought on the lane.

They sat and looked at the two flames.

The Emberfell oil burned yellow, with a little lick of smoke off the top of it that went up and laid a grey smear on the chimney glass by the end of an evening. It always had. Every lamp in Kaldo did it. The island's oil burned whiter, and steadier, and when Bram held a clean glass over the top of it for a count of thirty and took it away and turned it to the table, the inside of the glass was clear.

"It's lighter," Bram said. "Thinner. It came up the wick faster than ours." He put the glass down. "I ran it through the bench engine this morning for an hour. She liked it better than the lane's. Started quicker. Less soot in the bulb."

"It's been through the ground," Emeric said. "Something's taken the heavy part out of it on the way up." He did not say what. He did not know, here, what the word for it would be.

The rest had gone to the people who would know what to do with it, as he had meant them to.

The crust off the white flat had gone to the glassworks, in its jar, with no word about what it was, only that it was from somewhere and they should try it in a melt. Orrin had come up from the glassworks the week after with a small green bottle in his hand that he put on the table without a word, and it was the clearest glass anybody at the table had ever seen come out of Kaldo's furnace, and Orrin said the melt had run like water at a heat the old batch would not have moved at, and that his glassmen had stood round the pot when it poured and sworn at it, in the way glassmen swore when they were pleased and did not want anybody to see. He wanted to know where it came from. Emeric had said, from a long way off. Orrin had said, then he would like a great deal more of it from a long way off, and had gone back down the stair.

The grey shrub had gone to Imani, because it was a plant and she knew plants, and to Bram, because Emeric had told him what to do with it: to cut it up and boil it in a pot and see what came up. What came up, after an hour, floating on the grey water, was a scum of fine pale threads that clung together when Bram fished them out with a stick, and when he pressed them into a ball in his fingers and dropped it on the table, it bounced.

Bram had dropped it again. He had dropped it several times. Emeric had watched him do it with his face perfectly still and had been reminded, for no good reason, of a man he had once watched hold a flask in the forge's chamber and see it bleed away through the brick.

The stone the colour of dried blood was in Ferra's box, wrapped twice in oilcloth, and nobody had tried anything with it, because Emeric had said nobody would. The sack from under the east cliffs was in the dry store by the nitre beds, because Emeric had asked for it to go there, and the powder master had opened the neck of it and sniffed and closed it again and said he knew exactly what that was and what it was good for, and could he have a hundred more.

He laid it out for them that evening, at the table, all six, with Ward in his chair by the door and the two lamps burning side by side in front of him, one yellow and one white.

He told them what the three of them had seen on the island, in order, the way Ward told a road. The landing with its new timber. The steps. The hut and the barrels with the tower branded on their heads. The board on the inside of the door, with its rows of ten and its little boat at the end of every row. The ridge cut back in benches, and the hollow under the lowest bench, and the dead man's margin about a way down. The white flat, and the black pools, and the shrub. And the boat going out in the grey on the tenth day, as the board had said it would.

He did not tell them about the river under the sand. It was a thing the sense had given him, and nobody at the table could check it, and he had learned a long time ago, at a yard with forty men in it, what a thing was worth that only he could see.

"It's not foragers," he said. "It's an operation. It's been running for years, on a roster, from the Kesrath shore, with timber and barrels and boats paid for by somebody who keeps books. They're taking the glass stone off that ridge and selling it a splinter at a time in every port on the northern coasts, cheap, so that nobody asks." He put his hand flat on the table. "And the crystal price in four kingdoms has been fixed at the same figure for twenty years. By Duvrenne. At a banquet. I stood up at that banquet and said a price that never moves means somebody's sitting on a deposit they haven't declared, and I was sent here for saying it." He looked at the white flame. "I was right. That's what's on the island. That's where the price comes from."

Nobody said anything. Saban turned his cup round once on the table and did not look up.

"Two things want the island," Emeric said. "The engine wants oil that isn't Alina's. Every cask we buy off the lane goes in a Duvrenne ledger with our name beside it, and hull four will drink a boat's worth a voyage, and the class will drink a fleet's worth. That's one. And the other is everything else on it." He touched the bottle of green glass. "Orrin's flux. Bram's ball. The powder master's sack. And a hollow under a ridge that the man who wrote our book went down into and didn't come back up."

Ferra had her book open. She did not pick up her pen until he had finished. Then she picked it up, and did not write, and looked at him across the table.

"Say what you mean to do," she said.

"Take it. In kind. The way they're taking it. At night, off the island, in secret, and bring it home and use it."

"Then I'll say what that is," said Ferra. "So it's said, and so it's in the book."

He waited.

"The island is Verandel's Claim by the charter. It's the Crown's. The Crown's never garrisoned more than a fort at one end of it and never enforced a thing on it, but it's the Crown's on paper, and Duvrenne holds the other end of it and calls it theirs on theirs. Neither of them has ever taken anything off it openly, because the day one of them does, the other says it was an act of war, and is right." She put her pen to the page. "Duvrenne's been taking off it for years in secret. That's an act of war nobody's called one yet, because nobody's seen it. The day you start, you're doing the same. On a Crown claim, as the Crown's son, without the Crown's word. And then you're both over the line, and both hiding it, and the first one of you to be found out has started a war with the other." She wrote. "That's what it is."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"You accept it."

"I accept it."

She wrote that too. E. accepts. And the date. Nobody at the table said anything while she did it. Ward, by the door, had not moved.

The method took the rest of the evening, and it was mostly Fletcher's, because Emeric had asked Fletcher up from the yard for it and Fletcher had strong views.

Only the flying hulls went to the island. That was the first rule and Fletcher laid it down before anybody else could. A ship on the water, any ship, anchored off that island at night to load, would be seen from one fort or the other by first light, and a Randillian hull off the Crown's own claim loading cargo would be in a Duvrenne report by the end of the week, and in the Crown's by the end of the month. A hull that came down out of the dark, and loaded, and went back up into the dark, was seen by nobody who was not looking straight up, and nobody on that island had any reason to look up.

"Nearly nobody," Ferra said, and Emeric knew she was thinking of the man at the stern rail, and did not say so.

So: the hull waited in the band, over the middle of the island, dark. The teams went down in their suits, gliding, or on the chute, as the three of them had gone, and worked the night on the ground, and went up a line before dawn. Night work only. No lamps that showed. No marks on anything: no device on the suits, no barony's brand on the casks, nothing with Kaldo's name on it that could be left behind on the sand or fall off a line into the channel. Whatever came off the island came to Kaldo in the hulls and nowhere else. And from Kaldo, if any of it had to go on anywhere, it went on in Kaldo's own sea ships, out of Kaldo's own harbour, as Kaldo's own goods, and nobody on any quay would ever be able to say where Kaldo had got them.

"The shrub," said Imani. "You can't carry a boiling works in a hull."

"No." Emeric had thought about this on the long flight home, in the dark, with the cold coming off his plate. "We don't mill it there. We cut it and bring it whole. Low, at the ground, so the root stays. It grows again from the stump; the one Imani put in a pot with its root has new leaves on it already. We cut a row, and leave it, and cut the next row the next time, and by the time we come round to the first row again it's grown back." He looked at Bram. "And we mill it here, at the works, on lake water, as much of it as we like, in the open. A shrub nobody in Randillia's ever seen, boiled in a shed. Nobody can tell what island a bale of sticks came from."

"They'll ask what the sticks are," said Saban.

"Then we'll tell them. It's a shrub. We boil it. Something useful comes off it." Emeric turned the bottle of green glass a little on the table, so that the white flame shone through it. "Everything we say will be true. It's where it comes from that we won't say."

There was one more thing, and he had left it to the last because he did not want to do it.

"My father has to know," he said.

The table went quiet in a different way. Ward, by the door, turned his head.

"Not the Crown," Emeric said. "Not the chancery. Not a letter on the post that a clerk opens and copies and files. My father. That a foreign power has been mining his claim for years, and that I mean to do the same, and why." He looked down the table at Ward, and Ward looked back at him. "It's his claim. It's his war, if it comes to one. He doesn't get to find out about it from Duvrenne."

He had known about the road for two years and more. Ward had told him on the night the comb case came, with his mother's stones in it, across the hearth: one carrier, one bag, a cipher only Ward and a man called Kassim knew, going round the whole barony like a river round a stone. And Emeric had sat that night and told Ward he would never have used it. Not for the post, not for stones, not for any of it. You kept a thing like that for the day there was no other road at all.

He had not used it since. Not after Wayford, when his father's one line had come on the ordinary post and he had turned it face down on the table and left it there. Not when the summons came under the great seal. Not once.

"Write to him," he said. "Please."

Ward did not answer for a moment. Then he nodded, once, as if it were a price agreed, and that was all. But Emeric saw Ferra, at her end of the table, look at Ward and then at him and then write one word in the margin of her page, very small, and he knew without reading it that it was the date.

"Nothing on paper in the capital," said Armand, quietly, from the far end. It was the first thing he had said all evening. "Whatever the answer is. The Visitor's report is on a desk in the capital, and whoever is building a case against this barony is building it round your father. Nothing that touches that island goes anywhere in the capital that a clerk could find it."

"It won't," said Ward. "It never has."

Ward wrote it that night, at the table in the gatehouse, with the door shut, in the cipher only he and Kassim knew, on a sheet of Kaldo's own paper. Emeric did not see it. He did not ask to. He knew only what it said, because he had told Ward what it should say, and Ward had repeated it back to him in four sentences, flat, the way he reported a road: that a foreign power had been mining Verandel's Claim in secret for years, from the Kesrath shore, on a roster; that the prince had seen it with his own eyes and had proof in his hand; that the prince meant to take from it the same way, in secret, for the barony's need and the Crown's; and that he had wanted his father to know it from him.

The carrier came in the second week and went out the same night. Emeric did not see him either. Nobody ever saw him.

It was three weeks and four days before he came back.

Ward came up to the long room with nothing in his hands.

Emeric was at the table with the day book. He looked up and saw Ward in the door and saw his hands and knew before Ward said it.

"He came back empty," Ward said. "No letter. The carrier says Kassim read it to him in the usual room, the whole of it, twice, because he asked for it twice. And then he sat for a while, the carrier says, and then he said to Kassim that there was no answer, and went out." Ward stood in the door. "Nothing else. No order. No word against it. Nothing."

Emeric put the pen down.

He thought of the exile. Of his father's voice in the hall on the last night, loud, dry, carrying to every corner, saying the things a king says about a son he is sending away; and under it, once, so low nobody could have heard it but the boy it was meant for, the other voice. His father had always talked that way. The loud voice was for the room. The low voice was the truth. And there was a third thing that was neither, which was nothing at all, and Emeric had grown up knowing what it meant.

If his father had meant no, he would have said no. He would have said it in one word, in a line, on a sheet, in his own hand or Kassim's, and sent it back on the same road the same night, and it would have been the end of it. He had never in his life not said no to a thing he did not want done.

He had said nothing. He had heard it twice, and sat, and said there was no answer.

"It's leave," Emeric said.

Ward looked at him for a long moment. He had known Harrold longer than Emeric had been alive. He had been found by him on a road as a boy, and trained by his hands, and had written to him in the dark about his son for longer than the son had been alive.

"It's leave," Ward agreed.

Emeric wrote it in the day book, the way Ferra would have wanted it written: the date, and No answer by the road. Read as leave. E., W. Two initials, because two men had read it the same way, and he wanted it written that it had been two.

He sat for a while after Ward had gone, with the book open.

It was leave. He was sure of it. It was the most his father could give him on a thing like this without a word of it ever existing anywhere a clerk could find it: a king who had been told his son meant to take from a foreign power's secret on the Crown's own claim, and had not forbidden it. Nobody could ever say the king had allowed it. There was no line to point at. There was nothing.

And that was exactly what it was worth, too, if it came to it. Nobody could ever say the king had allowed it. There was no line to point at. If the day came that somebody stood in a hall in the capital and asked who had given the prince of Kaldo leave to take from the Crown's claim, there would be no answer to that either. Only a carrier who had come back with an empty bag, and a king who had said, to a man who read him his letters, that there was no answer, and gone out.

Emeric closed the day book. Silence was permission. He had understood that since he was a boy.
