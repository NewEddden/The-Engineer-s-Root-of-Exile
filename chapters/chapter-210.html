Chapter 210 - The Square

The first thing the horn ever said was a scream.

It said it at dusk on the twenty-fifth, from the counting house steps, to a square that had been half full all day and filled the rest of the way in the hour after the carpenters finished, because everybody in Wayford had watched the cut bell go up on its cradle that afternoon and wanted to know what a prince did with a bell that did not ring. The bell sat on a frame of oak at the front of the new platform with its crown sawn off and its mouth to the square, green at the lip, with the crack running up into the bronze. Behind it, bolted to its throat, the founder's cone: three pieces of brass, each a little narrower than the last, going back in a long taper to a box the size of a bread loaf on a trestle in the counting house door. Behind the box, in the dark of the doorway, the bellows, and the two hides of the bag lying flat on their frame with a slab of iron on the lid of it, and Osric and Tobe on the boards. And at the end of a leather hose, on the trestle beside the box, a cup.

Tess had the box open on her knees. She closed it, and screwed the last screw, and nodded to the doorway; and Osric and Tobe began to tread, and the bag rose under its iron, and Tess turned the brass cock on the side of the box, and the air went through it with a sound like a held breath let out.

She put her mouth to the cup and said, "One."

The bell screamed.

What came out of it was nothing like her voice: a sound like a pig being killed inside a church, rising, and rising, and not stopping when she took her mouth from the cup; and every pigeon on the church roof at the far end of the square went up at once, and every head in the square went down between its shoulders, and a horse at the posting house broke its tether and went off up the vat road with its bucket.

Tess shut the cock. The scream stopped.

In the silence that followed she sat in her chair with her hand on the brass and listened to the square, with her head on one side. She did not look embarrassed. She looked like a woman hearing exactly what she had expected to hear.

"It's hearing itself," she said. "The voice comes out of the bell and hits the counting house wall and comes back into the cup, and goes round again louder." She looked at the doorway. "It wants its ears covered."

So they covered its ears. It took Tess and Emeric and the founder an hour by lamplight: a box of planks round the cup, lined with folded felt from the hatter's on the square, with a hole in the front for a face and a curtain of felt over the hole, so that whoever spoke into the cup spoke into a small dark room of their own, and nothing that came out of the bell could find its way back in. They moved the trestle back inside the door. Tess opened the cock again, and put her face into the felt.

"One," said the bell. "Two."

It was her voice. It was her voice made enormous and flattened and strange, a woman speaking from inside a bronze bell the size of a house; but it was hers, and every word of it was clear, and it went out across the square in the dusk and struck the church at the far end and came back off it a breath later, smaller, like a voice answering itself across a valley. In the square, the people who had been crouching with their hands over their ears took their hands away. Nobody said anything. They looked at the bell.

"Osric," said the bell.

Osric was at the church door, a hundred and ninety yards off, where Tess had sent him an hour before to stand and listen, because he was the tallest thing in Wayford and could be seen from the steps over any crowd. Emeric saw him straighten up, by the church door, as if somebody had prodded him.

"If you can hear me," said the bell, "put your hand up."

Osric's hand went up.

So did a great many others. All over the square, in the dusk, hands went up: a man by the well, three women outside the posting house, a whole row of boys along the churchyard wall, and an old man in the middle of the cobbles who put his up very slowly, looking round him to see whether he was meant to. Somebody laughed. Then several people did, not loudly: people at the end of a long bad week laughing at the first thing in it that was not bad.

Tess took her face out of the felt. Her face was red to the hair.

"Osric," said the bell, rather quickly. "Hand down."

The hands came down, all over the square. Somebody shouted, from the churchyard wall, a boy's voice, small and thin after the bell's: "Again!" And the laughter went round the square once more, and Tess shut the cock with a snap and sat with her hand on it and would not look at anybody.

Osric came back across the square at a jog with the crowd parting for him, grinning, and stopped at the foot of the platform steps below her chair.

"You said my name to the whole of Wayford," he said.

"I said it to the horn," said Tess. She picked up her screwdriver. "The horn said it to Wayford. Go and tread."

He went and trod.

Then it was dark, and the laughter was over, and the square went quiet again, as it had been quiet all week; and the carpenters came back with a lantern and a ladder and a cart, and in the lantern light at the other end of the platform, the end by the counting house corner, they raised the beam.



The cellar had stood open for two days before that.

Ward had not wanted it, and Imani had not wanted it, and Ferra had said it was the only true thing he could do that week that nobody could call a story; and he had stood in the yard of the Staple house on the morning of the twenty-fourth with the gate open behind him and told Declan to let them come.

They came. They came up the hill road in a line that went back down to the bridge, and Declan let them down the long stair ten at a time, with a man of the column at the top and another at the bottom with a lantern, and the rope across the arch to the low room, and the low door at the back with the bar on it left shut, because some things were not for walking past. They walked past the chairs. They looked at the straps and the cradles and the painted ring, and the carved chair at the head of the room with its red cushion and its little table, which nobody had moved, with the dark ring of wine still dried in the bottom of the cup. They looked at the wall above the boy's pallet. Most of them stopped there, longest. Emeric saw that from the stair foot on the first afternoon: that they walked past the chairs and the ring quickly, as if they did not want to understand them, and stopped at the wall, and stood in front of the strokes in fives, and counted. They could all count.

Some of them came up the stair white and walked straight out of the yard gate and down the hill without speaking to anybody. A vat man was sick in the rag bay, against the bales, and wiped his mouth, and went and stood at the back of the line to go down again. A woman came up with her hands wrapped in her apron and stood in the yard, by the mounting block, and would not go home, and was still standing there at dusk. On the second morning somebody, in the night, had taken a stub of chalk and written a number on the wall beside the boy's strokes, in a big uneven hand, the hand of somebody who could write figures and very little else; and it was the right number.



By the middle of the morning on the twenty-sixth the square was full, and the streets into it were full back to the bridge.

He had guessed two thousand. He thought it was more. They had come in from every road since first light: the vat road, the north road, the Tollet road, the ferry at Low Bank, which had run all night; whole villages on carts, with their dinners in cloths, and children on shoulders, and old men on sticks who had been brought because they would not be left. They stood on the cobbles and on the steps of every house and in every window round the square, and on the churchyard wall and the church roof's lower slope, and on the posting house's mounting blocks and its water trough. They did not talk much. A crowd that size has a sound even when it is silent, Ottoline had said, and she was right: a low continuous sound, like the river at the weir, made of nothing but two thousand people breathing and shifting their feet.

The platform ran the whole width of the counting house front, built out over its steps on oak trestles, a yard above the heads of the crowd. At its left end, the bell on its frame, with its mouth to the square. At its right end, by the corner, the beam on its two posts, and the five ropes, and under them a long trap of planks on a bolt, with the old hangman of the parish standing beside it in his Sunday coat, with his hands behind his back. He had not hanged anybody since before the plague. He had told Declan so that morning, twice.

And between the bell and the beam, along the back of the platform, under an awning of sailcloth Imani had had put up against the sun, two benches.

The fifteen were on them. Imani had argued with herself about it for two days and then with him, and she had won both arguments, and lost the third, which was with Perrin; because on the evening of the twenty-fourth, on the bed in the long hall, Perrin had got up on his elbow and said that he was going, and that she could carry him or he would crawl, and Imani had looked at him for some time and then said she would carry him. They sat in a row in the shade of the sailcloth with blankets round their shoulders in the August noon, in the clothes the town had brought to Sethe's door all week in baskets without being asked: Perrin at the end nearest the bell, with his white hair combed; then Colley, and Jem, and Hew; Senna with her plait; Hild, with Tansy against her side under one blanket; Robin with his doll on his knees; and the rest, and at the far end, by the posts of the beam, Wat. Wat had got up out of his bed at six that morning and gone and stood by the door of the long hall in his blanket, and would not go back, and had not said why. Imani had stood in front of him for a while. Then she had sent for his boots.

Imani stood at the end of the benches. She had her bag. There was not a stone on the platform: not in her bag, not on her wrist, not in the lamps, because there were no lamps; not in the horn. He had gone over it himself at ten with his sense, plank by plank, and felt nothing anywhere on it but the fifteen thin notes under the sailcloth and the dense slow wall behind him that was Ward.

Ferra was at a table at the bell end, with the book open in front of her and her own book beside it, and a sealed bag of grey cloth, and a pen. Tess was at the brass cock in the counting house doorway, in a plain chair out of Aster's office. Her own chair stood empty in the counting house's back room, with every stone and safeguard in it that Emeric had ever been able to think of; she had got out of it at ten without a word and let Osric carry her to the plain one, and had not said why, and had not needed to.

At a second table, at the side, sat the circuit's magistrate, in his black, with his hands folded, because the notice had said the five would be brought before the people of the barony and he was the crown's law in the north and had decided that the crown's law ought to be seen to be present, though he had told Ferra privately that he did not know in what capacity. Wayford's own magistrate was not there. He had left on the night of the raid, an hour after the house shook, with two horses and his plate in a sack, by Lisle's woods road, going north; and Saban's man had followed him as far as the edge of the woods and lost him in the dark.

And at noon, when the church bell at the far end of the square had finished striking, and the square had gone so quiet that Emeric could hear the rooks in the churchyard elms, Declan's men brought the five down the hill.



The crowd let them through.

It opened in front of them on the bridge and closed behind them, all the way down the hill road and across the square, so that they came through the whole town in a lane of cobbles exactly as wide as the column round them, and not an inch wider. Nobody touched them. Nobody threw anything. Emeric had thought somebody would; he had told Declan to expect it, and Declan's men walked with their shields up on the outside. Nothing came. The crowd watched them go by, the four old men walking and the fifth carried on a door, and did not make a sound, and the silence was worse than anything they could have thrown.

Halfway across the square a woman stepped out of the front of the crowd, quite close, and spat on Placit's coat, on the breast, carefully, the way you would mark a beast at a fair; and stepped back into her place. Placit did not look at her. He walked on with the spittle on his good dark coat and his close white beard lifted, looking at the platform.

They brought them up the steps at the right end and stood them under the beam, in a row, facing the square, with a man of the column behind each, and laid Dray's door on the planks. Dray had been talking since the bridge. He went on talking, to nobody, quickly, about the night the house shook, about black things that came down out of the sky on wings, that walked on air, that came in through windows. Nobody listened. A few people in the front of the crowd looked at him with the faces of people looking at a drunk in a church.

Hollin's soft jaw was trembling. Vennor looked at his feet. Strade stood quite upright, with his bound hands folded in front of him like a clerk's at a counter, and looked out over the heads of the crowd at nothing, as he had looked at nothing on the floor of the strongroom. And Placit looked along the platform at Emeric, past the benches and the fifteen on them, past the bell, and inclined his head; very slightly, the bow of a man of one house to a man of another, across a room, at a dinner.

Emeric did not return it.

He was standing behind the bell, by the counting house door, where the felt box was. He had been standing there since the church struck the half before noon. His hands were at his sides. He had been aware of them all morning. He was aware of them now, and of the twenty yards of planks between the bell and the beam, and of how long twenty yards took; and of the five old men under the ropes, and what was still in his arms and his back from the cellar, quiet, banked, patient, waiting for him to let it go. He did not let it go. He stood there and did not let it go, and that was all he did, for a while, because it took all of him.

Then he stepped into the felt.



It was dark in the box, and close, and smelled of the hatter's shop. He put his mouth to the cup. Through the slit at the bottom of the curtain he could see a strip of planks and Tess's hand on the brass cock, waiting.

She turned it.

"My name is Emeric," he said, and heard it go out of the bell over two thousand heads, enormous, flattened, his own voice and not his, and come back off the church a breath later. "I hold Wayford. I have held it four years."

He stopped. He had written it out the night before, on a sheet, and he had the sheet in his coat, and he did not take it out.

"You have read the notice," he said, "or had it read to you. You know what the charge is. Most of you have been down the stair by now. You've stood in the room." He was quiet; the bell was quiet. "I could tell you what was done in it. I know most of it. I've read their book." He looked through the slit at the planks. "I'm not going to. If I tell you, then it's a prince telling you, and tomorrow somebody in the posting house will say the prince was always going to say that, wasn't he, and who knows what's true. You'd be right to say it. You've had lords telling you things all your lives."

He took a breath in the dark.

"Hear it from them."

He stepped out of the box.



Perrin went first, because he had asked to.

Imani had the cup brought to him on its hose, because he could not walk the length of the platform, and Tess opened the cock, and Perrin sat on the end of the bench with his blanket round him and the cup in both his hands and looked at it for a while, as a man looks at a ladder he has to climb. His white hair was combed flat. Somebody in the town had brought a coat for him that was too big, and the cuffs came down over his knuckles.

"Perrin," he said, into the cup.

The bell said it to the square, and the square leaned toward it.

"From Tollet. Nineteen." He stopped. He had said it in that order every night for two years, Imani had told Emeric, so as not to lose it, and he said it in that order now. "I'm nineteen."

Two thousand people looked at the white hair.

"They came on a Thursday," said Perrin. "After dark. Two summers back, at midsummer. My da owed Placit's steward for a cow that died. Two Silver and six. He couldn't pay it, so they came with a cart, with a cover on it, and a paper with the magistrate's seal, and they said I'd go for a season, to the Staple, to work the rag, and the debt'd be struck, and I'd be home for harvest." He turned the cup a little in his hands. "Mam cried. Da didn't say anything. I got in the cart. I thought it was the rag."

He was quiet. The bell carried his quiet out over the square as a faint rushing, the bellows' breath with nothing in it.

"They took us round by the back lanes," he said. "So nobody'd see. And in at the back of the house, and down the stair. And there was the room." He looked at the cup. "You've seen the room. You've been down. I watched you go up the hill from Sethe's window, all the last two days." He was quiet. "In the morning they put you in the chair. They strap you, and they put the stone at the back of your neck, and it pulls. You don't feel it go. That's the worst. You'd think you'd feel it, and you don't. You just get cold. From the middle out. Like sitting down in the river in March, only it's inside. And you forget things." His voice did not change. "Your mam's face. You forget it by dinner time. You know you had a mam, and you can't see her. It comes back at night, on the straw, a bit. Then in the morning they put you in the chair and it goes again."

Somewhere in the square a woman made a sound, and stopped it.

"We asked, every time the door went," said Perrin. "Is it the night. Is it the hour. Because the morning was only the chairs. But at night, twice a year, it was the ring." He told them about the ring. Not much; he did not need much. The painted circle round the drain, and the eight stones on their stands, and the five standing round it in their good coats with their hands folded, and one of the young ones in the middle, stripped, on the stone, and the stones lit, and the sound they made all night, singing, and the sound the one in the middle made. "The last one was Lady Day," said Perrin. "This year. A boy from up our road. Sixteen. I knew his mam." He was quiet. "In the morning they took him through the low door at the back. With the lime." He looked at the cup. "That's where you go. When you're done. Through the low door. Everybody knows that, down there. Nobody says it. You just watch the door."

He held the cup a while longer.

"I told the little ones to count," he said. "Robin. And the girl. Count the days till somebody comes who isn't the cart. So they'd have something." He was quiet. "I didn't think anybody would. I just wanted them to have something."

He gave the cup back to Imani. Then he took it back from her, quickly, before Tess could shut the cock, and said into it, in a different voice, a younger one:

"My mam's Bess, at the bottom house by the ford at Tollet. If she's here. I'm all right."

Out in the square, not far from the front, a woman in a brown shawl sat down on the cobbles, all at once, as if her legs had been cut, and the people round her bent down to her and closed over her and she was gone.

Perrin did not see it. He had given Imani the cup, and shut his eyes.



Colley started on the lie.

He had been told it so many times, Imani said afterward, that it came out of him before anything else could, the way a song comes out of a man who has been singing it all his life: that he had gone to work for the Staple for a season, at the rag, and that he was well, and that the work was fair and the food was fair, and that he sent his love to his mother and his sisters, and that he would be home by...

He stopped. He sat on the bench with the cup in his hands and his mouth open, and looked at the cup as if it had said it and not him.

"No," he said.

The bell said no to two thousand people.

"No. That's what they told us to say. If we went home. They made us say it every night on the straw, the ones who were going to be let go, over and over, till you said it in your sleep. I went for a season. I worked the rag. I'm well." His hands were shaking on the cup. "I'm not well. I didn't work the rag. They came at night with a cart." He looked out at the square, for the first time, at the faces. "They came at night with a cart, and a paper, and they took me off my mother's floor."

Out in the square, here and there, all through the crowd, Emeric saw people turn their heads. Not toward the platform. Toward the people beside them. A man with grey hair, by the well, who was not old. A man on the churchyard wall. Another at the corner of the posting house, standing very still, with his hand over his mouth, and the people round him turning one by one to look at him; and him not looking back at any of them, only at the boy on the bench with the cup, who had just said in front of the whole of Wayford the thing he had been told never to say.



Jem did not use the cup at all.

He took it from Imani, and stood up, which he could not do, and stood anyway with one hand on the sailcloth pole, and looked out over the square; and Emeric saw his face change, and knew before he knew how he knew it that Jem had found somebody in the crowd.

"You stood in the door," Jem said.

It went out of the bell like a stone off a roof. In the middle of the square, by the well, a man in a farmer's smock with his hat in his hands went white.

"You stood in the door," said Jem, louder, and the bell made it louder than any voice in the world. "You held it. When they came. You held the door open for them and you didn't look at me. You said it was a season. You said, it's a season, Jem, it's only a season and the rent's paid." His voice cracked across the top. "You held the door."

The people round the man by the well stepped back from him. Not far; a step. It was enough. He stood alone in a little ring of empty cobbles in the middle of the square, with his hat in his hands, an old man who was not as old as he looked, with his son's voice coming down on him out of a bell.

He did not go. Emeric watched him not go. He stood in his little ring of cobbles with his face white and let his son say all of it, everything, the door and the cart and the season and the rent; and when Jem had finished, and Imani had taken the cup out of his hand and sat him down, and Jem had put his face in his hands on the bench, the man by the well was still standing there with his hat, looking up at the platform. He did not try to speak. He would not have been heard.

After a while, a woman beside him, a stranger, reached out and took his elbow. He let her.



Hew could not remember how many winters.

He tried. He held the cup in both hands, the way Perrin had, and said he was Hew, from Coldwell, and that he had a holding of Vennor's on the hill above the village, four acres and a cow, and that they had come for him in the autumn of a bad year when the barley had failed, and that he had been under the house for... and stopped.

"Three winters," he said. "Four." He looked at the cup. "I counted at first. Then I didn't." He was quiet. "There was frost on the grating once, at the top of the stair. I saw it when they brought the straw down. That was one." He was quiet a long while. The bell rushed softly. "I don't know."

Imani put her hand on his shoulder.

"I had a wife," said Hew, to the cup.

That was all. He gave the cup back.



Hild had not spoken since the cellar.

Not to Imani, not to the nurses, not to Tansy except with her hands. She sat on the bench with her daughter against her under the blanket and watched the cup go along the row toward her, Perrin's hands and Colley's and Hew's, and Emeric saw her watch it, and saw her look at the brass box in the doorway and at the bell and at Tess's hand on the cock, and look for the stone in it; and not find one. She looked a long time. Then she held out her hand for the cup.

"They took her because she filled quick," said Hild.

Her voice was hoarse and small and very steady, and the bell made it the voice of a woman standing at the top of the church tower.

"She's small, and she's young, and the young ones fill quick. They said. The keeper said. He said it to Lord Hollin on the stair one night, I heard him, the little ones fill quick, and Lord Hollin said, then use her." She did not look at the beam. "So they used her. Every morning. In the end chair, by the arch, so I could see." Her hand moved on the blanket, over the girl's back. "She'd hold her breath. In the chair. To make it go quicker. I saw her do it. I said, don't, Tansy, breathe, and she said, if I'm quick, Mam, they'll let you off. If I fill quick they'll let you off the chair." Hild was quiet. "And they did. Some mornings. They let me off, because she'd filled quick, and they'd enough." She looked down at the cup. "She bought me mornings. She's ten."

The square made no sound at all.

"I'd like to say his name," said Hild. "Lord Hollin." She looked along the platform, then, for the first time, at the beam, at the soft-jawed old man standing under the second rope. "That's all. I'd like to have said it out loud, where people heard."

She gave the cup to Imani.

Under the beam, Hollin's jaw had stopped trembling. He was looking at the planks.



Tansy wanted to.

Imani had said she was not to, and Hild had said she was not to, and Tansy had listened to them both with great seriousness, and when the cup came back along the bench she put out her hand for it. Imani looked at Hild. Hild looked at her daughter, and at the cup, and at her daughter again. Then she nodded.

Imani lifted the girl onto her own lap, blanket and all, and held the cup for her, because her hands were not strong enough to hold it to her mouth. Tess, at the cock, opened it all the way.

"My name's Tansy," the girl whispered.

The bell said it. A whisper, the size of the square. It went out over every head in it and struck the church and came back, a child's whisper, small and clear and enormous, and Emeric saw men in the crowd put their hands over their faces at the sound of it, just the sound, before she had said anything at all.

"I'm ten," said Tansy.

She looked up at Imani. Imani nodded.

"I was good," Tansy whispered. "I was quick."

And then she did not want to any more, and turned her face into Imani's dress, and Imani gave the cup back to Tess over the girl's head without looking, and held her.



Robin gave his name and his age and his place, as he had given them to Imani in the long hall, in that order, in a clear flat voice. Robin. Twelve. Dray's holding on the north road. Then he said his mother had been in the chair by the arch, before Tansy, and that she had gone through the low door at Candlemas, the winter before last; and he said it in the same voice as his name.

"Perrin said to count," he said. "Till somebody came who wasn't the cart. So I counted. Every day. On the wall." He held the cup carefully, like something he had been told was valuable. "Six hundred and forty."

The bell said it. Six hundred and forty. In the square, Emeric saw the people who had been down the stair look at each other; because they had stood in front of that wall, and counted, and somebody had chalked the number on it in the night.

Robin looked along the platform. Not at the beam. Past it, past the bell, to the counting house door, where Emeric was standing.

"Can I go to your school?" he said.

The bell said that too. Out over the whole of Wayford, in a boy's clear flat voice, enormous: Can I go to your school.

Somebody in the crowd laughed. It was not a cruel sound. It was the sound a woman makes when something breaks in her that has been holding for years, and it turned into the other thing halfway through, and she could not stop it; and the people round her held her up.

Emeric could not answer through the horn. He was at the door; the felt was behind him. He went along the platform instead, past the benches, to where the boy sat with the cup in his hands, and crouched down in front of him on the planks, so that their faces were level.

"Yes," he said.

Robin looked at him for a while, considering it, the way he had considered it in the cellar when Emeric said he was not the cart.

"Can Tansy?"

"Yes."

Robin nodded. He gave the cup to Imani, and picked up his doll off his knees, and sat back on the bench; and that was all. He did not seem to think there was anything else to say.



Wat would not take the cup.

He looked at it when Imani held it out to him, at the end of the bench by the posts of the beam, and Emeric saw him hear what she said, and saw him try. His mouth moved. Nothing came. He looked at the cup and then at Imani and then at the cup, with the whole square waiting, and his hands stayed in his lap under the blanket, and nothing came.

Imani did not ask him twice. She took the cup back, and stood beside him with one hand on his shoulder, and put the cup to her own mouth.

"His name is Wat," she said.

The bell said it. It was the first time the square had heard her voice; it was low and plain and it did not shake, and the bell did not make it shake.

"He's thirty-one. He's from Hollin's holding at the Ridings, past Coldwell. He's been under that house four winters. Longer than anybody else down there who's alive." She was quiet. "He doesn't talk. He talked the first winter, the others say. He stopped." She looked down at the top of Wat's grey head. "He got up at six this morning and stood by the door till I let him come. He wanted to be here. So he's here."

She gave the cup to Tess.

Out in the crowd, near the posting house, an old man's voice called out, small and cracked without any bell to help it: "I knew his da." And then, louder, to the people round him, who had turned: "Old Wat, at the Ridings. I knew his da. Best hedger on the hill." And then he could not say anything else either.

Wat had heard. Emeric saw him hear it. He sat on the end of the bench with Imani's hand on his shoulder and looked out over the square toward the posting house, toward the old man's voice, and his face did not change, and his hands did not move. But he looked toward it until the old man was hidden again in the crowd.



Nobody had asked Maud to speak.

She came out of the front of the crowd at the bell end of the platform after Wat, when the square had gone quiet again and Emeric was about to step back into the felt; a small square woman of sixty in a grey shawl, with a basket on her arm, as if she had come to the square to buy candles and had stayed. She came to the platform's edge, which was a yard above her head, and put her basket down on the cobbles, and took something out of it, and held it up to Ferra, at her table, without a word.

A letter. Folded small and soft as cloth with handling, the folds gone brown.

Ferra took it. She unfolded it on her table, carefully, because it was coming apart along the creases, and read it; and Emeric, from the door, saw her read it twice, and then turn to the book, and turn the pages back, a long way, years, and run her finger down a column.

Then she stood up, with the letter in one hand, and went into the felt.

"This is a letter," said Ferra's voice, out of the bell. It was the first time Ferra had spoken to more than forty people at once in her life, and her voice through the bell was exactly as it was across a table: small and exact and unhurried, reading a figure. "Written six years ago, in the spring, in a clerk's hand, to Maud, on the Tollet road. It says: your son Lorn has gone to work for the Staple for a season, at the rag, and is well, and sends his love to his mother. It's signed for the Staple. There's no name."

The bell rushed softly. Ferra turned a page; they heard it, enormous, a sound like a sail filling.

"This is the Staple's book," said Ferra. "The one the five ordered burned. Under Intake, on the third of June six years ago: Lorn, of the Tollet road. Lord Hollin's holding. One Gold four Silver. Taken. Family told, Staple. One season." Another page. "Under Served, at the end of the season: nothing. The line's empty. He wasn't let go." Another page, further on; the sail filling again. "Under Losses. The spring after. The fourteenth of April. L., Tollet road."

Out at the edge of the platform, Maud stood with her hands at her sides and did not move.

Ferra came out of the felt. She went back to her table, and took up the bag of grey cloth with the seal on it, the seal that had been on it since June, and broke the seal with her thumbnail, and turned the bag over on the table beside Maud's letter. Gold rang on the boards. Twelve coins. Ferra laid them out in a row with one finger, beside the soft brown letter, so that the whole front of the crowd could see.

Then she went back into the felt.

"Lord Hollin gave the Holder of Wayford this purse," said Ferra, "at his own door, after dinner, in June this year. He said it was the usual. Twelve Gold." She was quiet. "Lorn was held against one Gold and four Silver."

She came out.

Maud looked at the gold on the table for a while, and at her letter beside it. Then she turned round, slowly, away from the table and the bell, and looked along the platform to the beam; to the five old men standing under the ropes, and to the second of them, the one with the soft jaw. She looked at him until the whole front of the crowd had followed her eyes. He did not look up from the planks.

She raised her arm and pointed at him.

She did not have the horn. She did not need it. The square was so quiet that her voice went to the church door on its own.

"I paid him the rent," said Maud. "Every quarter day. For twenty years. I paid him the rent on the quarter day he had my boy." She did not lower her arm. "I paid it the quarter day after."

She looked at Emeric, then. Straight at him, up the platform, across the gold.

"Kill them," said Maud.



The sound the square made then was the sound he had been afraid of since the cellar.

It began at the front, round Maud, and went back through the crowd like fire through stubble: not a word, not at first, but a sound with no words in it, low, rising, two thousand people letting out the breath they had been holding since noon. Then words in it. Hang them. Kill them. Hang them now. The crowd moved; he felt it move, the whole weight of it, toward the platform, the way water moves against a dam; Declan's men along the platform's front set their feet and put their shields up and the front of the crowd came against the shields and pressed and did not stop. Somebody threw something. A cobble, prised up out of the square. It came out of the crowd over the shields at the beam end, at the five, end over end, and Ward, who had been standing behind Emeric at the door and had not seemed to move, was at the far end of the platform with the cobble in his hand.

He looked at it. He looked at the crowd. He put it down on the planks at his feet, carefully.

Emeric went into the felt.

"Not yet," he said.

The bell said it over all of them, flattened, enormous, not loud enough. He said it again.

"Not yet. We haven't heard all of it."

The crowd did not stop. But it slowed. He felt it slow, through the planks: the weight against the shields easing a little, the sound dropping, not much. He stood in the dark of the felt with his mouth to the cup and his hands shaking against the box, and understood that the thing in his own arms and back, the thing he had been holding down all morning, was the same thing that was out there in the crowd; that it had got out of him in the cellar and now it was out there, in all of them, and that he had put it there, with the bell, on purpose, and it was exactly what he had wanted, and it was the most frightening thing he had ever heard.

"There are three men who kept that cellar," he said. "For wages. They were down there the night we came. They're going to tell you what the five did. And then you'll have heard all of it."



The keepers told it.

The oldest of the three told most of it, the man of fifty in the leather apron who had knelt in the guardroom door and babbled; he stood at the front of the platform between two of Declan's men, with the cup on its hose in his hands, and his voice through the bell was the voice of a man who had been sentenced to say this and had made up his mind to say all of it at once, quickly, before he could stop.

Twelve years, he had kept the cellar. Hired at the posting house by a clerk of the Staple's, for twice a labourer's wage and his cottage rent, to sweep and carry straw and feed them and put them in the chairs and take them out. He did not ask what the chairs were for. Not the first year. By the second year he knew. By the third he did not ask himself any more, because a man cannot ask himself the same question every morning for nine years and still get up. He had a wife and three children in the cottage the Staple paid for. He said that, and then said it did not matter, and he knew it did not.

The five came down at night. Not every night. Placit most, two and three nights a week; Hollin next; the others less. They came down the stair with a lamp and sat in the carved chair, one at a time, the others waiting in the guardroom with the keepers, drinking; and the one in the chair would take a stone from the racks in each hand and sit with his eyes shut and draw, a long while, like a man with a pipe. Placit liked the blue stones best. Hollin liked the small ones. Hollin said once, on the stair, that the young ones' were sweeter, and laughed; and the keeper had laughed too, because Hollin was a lord, and he had been laughing at it in his sleep for six years.

Under the beam, Hollin had shut his eyes.

And twice a year, the ring. The five came down together, in their good coats, and Marrow with them, and with Marrow another man, in a grey travelling cloak with the hood up, who had come every time, at Lady Day and at Michaelmas, for seven years, and whose face the keepers had never once seen. He brought a book. He stood at the head of the ring and read from it, aloud, in a voice like a priest's, words the keeper did not know; and went round the ring and touched each stone on its stand with his bare hand, one after another, and each one lit when he touched it. Then the five stood round the ring with their hands folded until morning. The man in the hood left before it was light. He never ate, and never sat, and never spoke to the keepers at all.

"Who was he?" said Emeric, from the door, without the horn.

The keeper looked at him, and shook his head, and said into the cup that he did not know, Highness, that nobody knew but Marrow, and that once, the second year, he had asked Marrow, and Marrow had looked at him in a way that made him not ask again.

Emeric looked along the platform. Marrow was there; Rylan had brought him down from the counting house that morning and stood him at the back of the platform by the bell's frame, with his clean hands bound in front of him, between two of the column. He had been listening to all of it with an expression of mild, polite, intelligent interest, exactly as he had looked up out of the ash in the strongroom; and at the keeper's words, at the hood and the book, his face did not change at all.

But his note did. Emeric felt it. The same small flinch he had felt in the strongroom when the man said nobody. The look up, for a breath, at somebody who was not on the platform.



Then Ferra read them the book.

She went into the felt with the Staple's ledger in her arms and her own sheet on top of it, and read them the totals in the voice she used for the quarter's figures at the long table at home: the same voice for every line, with no line louder than another. Twenty years. Two hundred and ninety-seven people taken down that stair against a debt, with a magistrate's seal. Fifteen of them on the benches behind her. Sixty-one in the column headed Losses, which was the lime, behind the low door; and one more, the sixteenth name on the last page the five had let be written, whose line stopped on the sixth of August and went nowhere, and which she could not account for, and said so. And two hundred and twenty in a column headed Served: let go home at the end of a season, with the debt struck, and told what to say.

"Of the two hundred and twenty," said Ferra, out of the bell, "a hundred and four are in the burial book of this parish, inside five years of the day they came home. Of a wasting. Of a decline." She turned a page; the sail filled. "Of the sorters' cough." She was quiet. "I haven't had the other parishes' books yet. I've written for them."

The square did not make the sound it had made for Maud. It made a different one. Emeric heard it begin, all through the crowd, not at the front but everywhere at once, low: the sound of a great many people remembering a burying. A brother who had gone for a season in a bad year and come home grey and quiet, and coughed through one winter, and the next, and not a third. A husband. A son. The sorters' cough. Everybody in Wayford had stood at the side of a grave for the sorters' cough, and shaken their heads, and said it was the rag, the rag was cruel; and they stood in the square now and understood, all at once, together, what it had been.

And into that sound, from under the beam, a clear dry voice that needed no bell said: "Sixty-two."

Every head on the platform turned.

Strade was standing where he had stood all afternoon, upright, between his guards, with his hands bound in front of him. He had not moved. He did not look at the crowd. He looked at Ferra's table.

"Sixty-two," he said again. "Not sixty-one." He turned his bound hands, and with two fingers drew a folded sheet out of the breast of his coat, and held it out, toward the bell end of the platform, as a clerk holds out a voucher across a counter. "The eighth of August. One. Your sixteenth, Chancellor. Not entered. The book was shut by then, on Lord Placit's order; I kept the sheet to enter when it was opened again." He held it out. "A book that is not right is not a book."

Ferra came out of the felt.

She walked down the length of the platform, past the bell and the benches and the fifteen, to the beam, and stopped in front of him, and took the sheet out of his fingers, and unfolded it, and read it. Emeric watched her read it. Small upright figures, neat as a fence. The eighth of August, ten days before the night the house shook, when the carts had already stopped and the five were already shutting their doors and their books. One. And a name, in the margin, that Emeric did not see; the name Ferra had drawn a ring round in Sethe's counting room at two in the morning, a week before.

Ferra stood in front of Strade with the sheet in her hand for the space of a breath. The two of them, Kaldo's chancellor and the Staple's book-keeper, a yard apart, with a loose sheet of figures between them. Strade looked at her the way one craftsman looks at another's work. Ferra did not look at him at all. She looked at the sheet.

Then she went back to her table, and sat down, and took up her pencil, and corrected the figure in her own book, and initialled the correction; and went back into the felt, and said, in the same voice as every other line, "Sixty-two."



When Ferra had finished, Emeric turned to the magistrate's table.

He did not use the horn for that. He did not need the square to hear the question. It needed to hear the answer.

"The crown's law," he said. "You're here. Will it try them?"

The magistrate stood up. He was a careful grey man who had ridden the northern circuit for twenty years and had never in those twenty years been asked anything on a platform in front of a whole town, and he looked at the crowd before he looked at Emeric; and then he went, deliberately, to the felt box, and put his head in, and Tess opened the cock.

"These are lords," said the magistrate, out of the bell, "holding under a charter of the crown. A circuit magistrate cannot try them. A baron's court cannot try them. Only the crown's own court can try a holder of the crown's charter, in the capital, before the crown's own judges." He was quiet. "If they are put into my hands, I will send it there today, under my seal, with everything I have heard."

"How long?" said Emeric, outside the felt. The magistrate heard him through it.

"If the court will hear it," said the magistrate, "a year. Two." He hesitated. "Their families will have friends at that court, Highness. Lord Placit's wife was born at a table where the court dines."

The crowd had heard. The sound began again at the front.

The magistrate came out of the felt and stood beside it, a careful grey man in black, with his hands folded, and looked at Emeric. He did not say anything else. He had said what the law was. He had said it well, in front of everybody, and it was true, and Emeric understood that he had said it so that it would be on the record, in every memory in that square, that the crown's law had been offered and had said a year or two; and that the magistrate did not expect to be listened to, and had not come to stop anything.

Emeric went into the felt.

"Then not by writ," he said.

The bell said it over the square, and the square went quiet to hear what came after.

"By my order. The Holder of Wayford's. Mine." He was quiet. "It's not the crown's judgment. It's mine. If it's wrong, it's my wrong. I'll carry it. You won't." He stopped. "They hang today."



He spared four.

He did it at once, in the same breath, through the bell, because if he waited for the square to finish hearing the first thing he would never get the second said. The three keepers would live. He had given them his word on the stair in the cellar, on the night, that if they told all of it, in the square, in front of everybody, they would live; and they had told it, and he would keep his word, because a lord who broke his word to men he had in his power was the thing they had come to the square to see hanged. And Marrow would live, because Marrow knew who the man in the hood was, and who had bought the stones that went south in the iron box every week for twenty years, and who stood above the five; and Emeric meant to know it. Every name.

The square did not like it. He had known it would not. The sound came up at the front again, ugly, with words in it now, against the keepers, against Marrow, against him; and the crowd came against the shields again, hard, and a second cobble came over and Ward did not catch this one, because it went wide and broke on the bell's frame with a crack like a shot. Declan's men pushed back. A shield went down. For a breath it was very close, closer than anything on the night of the raid had been, a town against forty shields.

He did not argue with it. He stood in front of the felt box, outside it, where they could all see him, beside the three keepers, close enough to touch them, and stood still. That was all. He let them see him stand there. He let them hate him for it, if that was what they needed; better him than three men in leather aprons with a wife and children in a cottage. He did not know how long it was. Long enough that his hands, at his sides, stopped shaking.

Then the pressure against the shields eased. Not because he had said anything. Because, at the front, by the bell end of the platform, Maud had picked up her basket off the cobbles and turned round to face the crowd, with her back to the platform, and stood there; and the people pushing at the front had come face to face with a small square woman of sixty in a grey shawl, standing between them and the shields, who looked at them until they stopped.



They hanged Dray first, because he could not stand, and it was quickest.

Two of the column held him up under the rope, one at each arm, while the old hangman put the noose round his neck and the hood over his head, and Dray talked all the way through it, into the hood, about the black things on wings, about men who walked on air and came in at windows, faster and faster. Nobody in the crowd listened. A few people near the front laughed, uneasily, as people laugh at a drunk at a burying. Emeric watched him. The column men let go and stepped back, and the hangman drew the bolt, and the trap went, and Dray stopped talking.

Emeric watched all of it. He had told himself in the night that he would, and he did. He did not look away when the rope went taut. He did not look away after. It took a short time and a long one at once, and he watched it, standing by the bell, with Ward behind him; and at the end of it, when it was done and Dray turned slowly on the rope above the open trap, something came up in him that was not his.

A kitchen. A window with a red geranium in a pot on the sill. A woman's voice, from another room, calling a name; not his name; a name he did not know, a child's name, the way a mother calls it at dusk, twice, rising at the end. Marrow's. Out of nowhere, standing on a platform in the August sun with a man dead on a rope in front of him, Marrow's kitchen came up behind his eyes, whole, warm, smelling of bread, and the woman called the name again, and was gone.

He stood very still until it had gone. Then he went on watching.

Hollin went second, silent. He did not say anything at all, from the time they brought him to the trap to the time the hood went over his face. He looked once, before the hood, along the platform at the benches; at Hild. She was looking at him. She did not look away. Then the hood went on, and he went, and she watched it, and so did Emeric.

Vennor was light.

He was the smallest of the five, a little dry man who had said forty words at his own dinner, and he went through the trap and did not die. The rope was set for a heavier man; the old hangman had set them all alike, because he had not done this since before the plague and had forgotten that men were not all alike. Vennor did not die. He hung and kicked, and the rope turned, and the crowd, which had been silent for Dray and silent for Hollin, made a sound at last, and it was not a good sound.

The old hangman stood on the edge of the trap with his hands at his sides, white, looking down.

Emeric was halfway along the platform before he knew he had moved. Ward's hand closed on his arm from behind. He stopped. He stood there with Ward's hand on his arm, and looked down through the trap, and made himself watch while the old man got down off the platform's edge onto the cobbles under it, slowly, as an old man gets down off a cart, and went under the beam, and took Vennor round the knees with both arms, and hung his own weight on him, with his face against the man's legs, until it was done.

It was not quick. Emeric watched all of it. When it was over the old hangman let go and stood under the beam with his arms hanging, and did not come out, and Declan went down and brought him out by the elbow, gently, and sat him on the counting house step.

Strade went fourth. He walked to the trap on his own. On the edge of it, while the hangman's hands were still shaking on the noose, he turned his head and looked at Emeric, along the platform, past the bell; one long steady look, without anything in it that Emeric could name, except perhaps a kind of attention. The look of a man who had sat across a dinner table in June from a drunk prince in a gold-laced coat talking about his father, and had not believed a word of it, and had been waiting all summer to see how long the prince would keep it up.

The hood went on. Strade went, and Emeric watched, and so did Ferra, from her table, with her hand flat on the open book.

Placit went last.

He walked to the trap the way he had walked across the square: upright, with the spittle dried on the breast of his good coat. On the edge of the trap, as the hangman lifted the noose, he turned, as Strade had, and looked along the platform at Emeric. But he did not look at him the way Strade had. He looked at him as one gentleman looks at another, across a room, at the end of a dinner that had not gone well; with a kind of tired courtesy.

"You'll find it's the same everywhere, Highness," said Placit.

He did not raise his voice. He did not need the bell. He said it pleasantly, and a little sadly, as an old man passes on a piece of information to a young one who will need it; and the front of the crowd heard it, and the platform heard it, and Emeric heard it, and it went into him and stayed.

Then the hood, and the rope, and the trap.

Emeric watched it.



Nobody cheered.

One man did, near the back, by the churchyard wall, a single shout, high, on its own; and the people round him turned and looked at him, and he stopped, and did not do it again. After that the square was quiet. It stood and looked at the five shapes turning slowly on the beam above the open trap in the August sun, and did not cheer, and did not move, while the shadow of the beam crept across the open trap. Then, from the back, from the streets that came into the square, it began to go home. Not quickly. A family at a time, a village at a time, with their dinners still in their cloths, uneaten. Nobody looked at the platform as they went. Nobody looked at Emeric. Here and there a woman looked at the benches under the sailcloth as she passed, at the fifteen, and put her hand to her mouth.

Maud went last of all. She picked her letter up off Ferra's table, folded it along its old brown folds, and put it back in her basket; and left the gold where it was.

Ferra wrote in the book while the square emptied. He was standing beside her table and saw her write it. She wrote it in her ordinary hand, small, under the day's date, in a line by itself, without hurrying, and did not look up when she had finished.

Wayford. Five, by his order, not by writ. Entered because it happened.

She blotted it. Then she shut both books, and put the twelve Gold back in the grey bag, and stood up.

Imani was getting the fifteen off the benches and onto their litters for the walk back to Sethe's hall, one at a time, talking to each of them in her ward voice. She did not look at the beam. She had not looked at it once, all afternoon; he had watched her not look at it. Wat was the last. He stood up off the end of the bench by himself, in his boots, and let Imani take his arm, and looked once at the five shapes on the beam, at Hollin's, for a while, without anything in his face at all. Then he let her lead him down the steps.

At dusk Declan's men cut them down and gave them to whoever came for them. Placit's son came for Placit's, with a cart and two grooms, and did not look at anybody. Nobody came for Vennor's until full dark, and then it was a woman in a veil, alone, with a hired barrow.



He was all right until he was in the room.

He was all right through the walk back across the square, and the litters going in at Sethe's door, and Imani's voice going down the long hall, and supper, which he ate, because Maren was not there and Ferra put the bowl in front of him and watched him until he did. He was all right through Ward's report, which was four lines, and the column's, and the magistrate's courteous note, delivered by a clerk at nine, saying that the magistrate would send his account of the day to the crown under his own seal in the morning and wished His Highness a good night. He was all right on the stair.

He was in Sethe's old room, with the door shut, sitting on the edge of the bed to take his boots off, when his hands began to shake.

He watched them. He had his left boot half off, and his hands were on it, and they were shaking, a fine fast tremor from the wrist, the way they had shaken on Imani's desk the morning after the boat; and he could not stop them. He took them off the boot and held them together. They shook together. He put them flat on his knees and pressed down, and his knees shook with them.

He heard the door. He did not look up. He knew who it was without his sense; he would have known it without anything.

Ferra shut the door behind her. She did not say anything. She came across the room and knelt on the floor in front of him and took his left boot off, the rest of the way, and then his right, and put them side by side under the bed, squared, the way she put down everything. Then she got up and sat on the bed beside him, close, and put her arms round him.

That was when it went.

He had held it since the mounting block. He had held it on the mounting block with Imani's hands on his face and the whole column watching from the walls; he had held it on the stair outside Placit's grate and on the counting house step with Garrin's words in his ears and in the felt box with the bell saying his words over two thousand people; he had held it through Tansy's whisper and Maud's arm and Vennor's legs and the old man's face against them. He had held it because somebody had to be the wall, and it had always been him. And Ferra put her arms round him in Sethe's old room with the door shut, and did not say anything, and did not ask anything, and he could not hold it any more.

It came out of him the way the weight had come out of him in the cellar: all at once, without his leave, from somewhere very low. He made a sound he had not made since he was a boy in another world, in another body, at the back of a church at a funeral; and then he could not stop. He put his face against her shoulder and shook, and wept, and she held on. She held his head against her with one hand, her fingers in his hair, and the other arm round his back, hard, as hard as her small arms would go; and did not say there, or hush, or it's all right, because it was not, and Ferra did not say things that were not so. She held on. That was all. She held on as if he were a column that would not come right and she meant to sit with it all night until it did.

He did not know how long. A long while. The candle on the chest burned down a finger. Down in the square a dog barked at something, and a cart went by on the cobbles, slowly, toward the bridge.

The door opened again.

He felt her this time. Even through it, even with his face in Ferra's shoulder: Imani, on the stair, and on the landing, and in the doorway; and he had not sent for her, and nobody had. She had come up from the long hall in her ward dress with her sleeves still rolled, and her hair coming down, and she stood in the doorway a moment and looked at the two of them on the edge of the bed. Then she shut the door behind her, softly.

She came and knelt on the floor in front of him, where Ferra had knelt. She took his right wrist out of his lap, gently, and turned it over, and laid two fingers on the inside of it, and looked at nothing for the time it took to count. He felt her count it. Then she put his wrist back in his lap and left her hand on it.

"Fast," she said. "Not dangerous." Her voice was very low. "Shaking's the body putting down what it's been carrying. It'll stop when it's done. Not before." She was quiet. "Don't try to make it."

He could not have answered.

Ferra lay back on the bed, slowly, with her arms still round him, and took him with her; and Imani got up off the floor and came round the bed and lay down on his other side, on top of the blanket, in her ward dress, against his back, with her arm over him and her hand flat on his chest where she could feel his heart. Nobody said anything. Ferra had his head against her shoulder still, and her fingers in his hair. Imani's forehead was against the back of his neck. They lay like that, the three of them, in their clothes, on top of the blanket, on Sethe's old bed, with the candle going down, and he shook between them, and wept, and they held on.

Nothing else happened. Nothing else was asked for, or offered, or wanted.

Some time after midnight the shaking slowed. It did not stop; it came and went, like a fever, in long waves, and between them he lay still and breathed, and felt the two of them breathing against him, front and back. Imani fell asleep at about one. He felt her go: her hand on his chest went heavy and stayed there, and her breath on the back of his neck went long and slow, the way it did, all at once, as if she had found a door in the dark and gone through it. A healer sleeps when she can, she had told him once. She had been on her feet since five. She did not wake when the next wave came, and the next; only her hand on his chest pressed down a little each time, in her sleep, and let go again when it passed.

Ferra did not sleep.

He knew, because every time the shaking came back her arms tightened, at once, before it had properly begun; and every time it went, her fingers moved in his hair, once, as if she were checking a figure. She lay awake all night with her eyes open in the dark, looking at the ceiling over his head, holding on; and he lay between them and shook, less and less, and somewhere toward the grey of the morning, with Ferra's fingers still in his hair and Imani's hand still on his heart, he slept.



When he woke it was full light and Ferra was gone.

Imani was still asleep against his back, with her hand where it had been. He lay still so as not to wake her, and turned his head on the pillow, and looked across the room.

Ferra was at the table by the window, in yesterday's dress, with her hair put up again, and the Staple's book open in front of her, and her own beside it, and her pencil going; in the light from the square, going down the Intake column, name by name, writing the villages on a sheet for the letters to the priests. She looked as though she had slept eight hours. She looked the way she looked every morning.

She did not look up. But after a while, without stopping, she said, quietly, so as not to wake Imani:

"There's tea. It's cold. I'll not get you more till you've drunk that."
