Chapter 32 - Feedstock

The pile in the lower ward took eleven weeks to arrive at and nine days to sort.

Bram had priced it in an afternoon in September and had been right about the price and wrong about the sorting, which he said out loud on the first morning with his boot on a Wayford harness.

"I said six weeks of charges for one furnace," he said. "That's true if you throw the lot in. Throw the lot in and every tap comes out different and you've no way of knowing why, and in March you'll have a bar that bends and a bar that snaps and both of them stamped the same."

"Then we sort it."

"Aye. Into what."

Emeric had been waiting most of a week for somebody to ask him that.

He got four men and a scale and a chalk and spent the whole of the first day on the ward cobbles, and by evening the pile was four piles and every man on the sorting crew could say the difference without him.

Mail went in one heap, because mail is soft iron drawn into wire and it had been mended by men who mended carts and half of it had been mended with whatever was on the bench. Spearheads and axe heads went in the second, which was the honest metal, and there were about nine hundredweight of it. Helms and caps went in the third, and the third was the awkward one, because a cap can be anything at all and a man cannot tell by looking. The fourth pile was small and had four Wayford harnesses in it and the ruined enamel off a dead baron's breastplate, which had gone into the middle furnace on the fifteenth day in August, and which Emeric had watched go in and had not, on the day, been able to say what he was thinking about.

"You're going to want a book," Bram said, on the third morning, in the flat voice of a man who has seen where a thing is going and would like to arrive before it does.

"I'm going to want a book."

"Say it, then, so it's yours and not mine."

"Every charge gets a number. What went in it, off which pile, by weight. What came out of it, what it was made into, and where that went." Emeric was already writing it. "So that in March when a bar snaps we can walk it back to a charge and find out whether it was the mail or the caps or somebody's afternoon, and we cut up the pile that did it instead of guessing."

"And who writes it when you're not on the floor."

"The shift man."

"You've give that man a great deal to do this month," Bram said, and went back to the tap, and did not smile in any way Emeric could have pointed to.

The problem arrived in the second week of November and it was a new kind of problem and Emeric was ashamed of how much he enjoyed it.

Three furnaces stood on the eastern arm. One was lit. The wheel turned the blast and the hoist off one shaft and the men charged between blasts and not across them, which had cost nothing and worked from the first hour and had gone onto the wall in letters a foot high. The building could eat, at a run, with two furnaces lit and the wheel behaving, more iron in a season than the whole coalition had brought up the hill in August.

What it had to eat was four piles in a lower ward and about seven weeks in them.

"So it's not a foundry problem," he said, at the table, to nobody in particular. "It's a supply problem. The building's fine. The building's too good."

"You are pleased," Vantic said.

"I've had four months of problems where the answer was that I'd built the wrong thing. This one's the answer is I built the right thing early and now I have to go and find it something to chew on." He caught himself. "I'm aware of how that sounds to a creditor."

"You may be pleased. I have written down that you are pleased and the reason, which is a reason my house understands." She turned a page. "It is the fourth entry this month in which the difficulty is that the barony has more capacity than it has material, and my house prefers that difficulty to its opposite by a wide margin. What I would like is the number of weeks."

"Seven, at one furnace."

"Then say seven weeks in the room where Saban is standing."

Saban came down on the ninth with mud to the thigh and a face on him, and he had been up the river road, and he told it in order because Emeric had asked him a year ago to always tell it in order.

"There's a hill up there with about fourteen hundred people on it and nobody who can sell me a stone," he said. "That's the whole of it and I'll spend ten minutes on why."

"Spend it."

"Renner's steward is alive and answering letters and has no authority to sign anything, and he knows it, and he's the most miserable man I've met in nine years. There's a widow in the hall who has not been out of it. There's a boy of eleven who is the baron in the sense that there's nobody else, and there's a crown writ that will come up that road eventually and settle who holds what, and until it comes there is not a man on that hill who will put his name on a contract for ore out of workings whose ownership is a question." Saban sat. "I stood in a yard with three men who all wanted to sell me ore, and every one of them wanted somebody else to sign it."

"And the workings."

"Standing. Head frames idle, water rising in two of them, and a hundred and forty-nine men in the ground down here that came off that hill." He said it flatly. "They've not got a labour problem yet. They will have. That's not this winter's business and I'd not have you build anything on it."

"Then Duvrenne."

"Then Duvrenne, and here's the shape of it, and you'll not like the middle." Saban put a folded sheet on the table. "Eleven days by road. Six by water onto the western arm, which I have walked twice and sounded once, and a barge will come up it as far as the old quay below the mill and no further. Twice the price at the far end and four times the carriage, and paid before it moves, because a Duvrenne factor has never heard of you and has heard of Grayrock."

"And the middle."

"The middle is that the water route shuts. Not with ice. With weather. From about the first week of December there is a fortnight in three where a loaded barge does not leave that coast, and after the new year there's a stretch where nobody sensible tries at all." He tapped the sheet. "So it's two loads before the year turns, or it's one load and a long spring."

"Two loads is what."

"Two loads is your furnace lit from the day the scrap runs out until about the end of March, at one furnace, at the rate you've been running." Saban shrugged. "Then you're waiting on April like every other man on this coast."

Vantic asked her question at the point where Emeric had already decided and had not yet said so, which he came to recognise as her habit.

"Who bears the barge," she said.

Saban looked at her the way a man looks at a dog that has spoken.

"Say that again."

"You have described a contract in which Kaldo pays at the far end before the ore moves. So Kaldo has paid for a quantity of ore and owns a quantity of ore, and the ore is then put on a boat in a season you have just told this room is bad, and if the boat is lost, Kaldo has paid for a hole in the water." She had her case open. "Who bears that. I am not asking rhetorically. I am asking whether it is written."

"It'd be the ordinary way of it that the buyer bears it once it's loaded."

"Then it is the ordinary way of it, and the ordinary way of it will put this barony out in one night in December." Vantic wrote something. "Two things, and neither is a demand, because I have no veto and I am tired of saying so. The first is that the payment goes as two payments against two loads and not one payment against two, because a factor who has all of it will ship both in the same week to be rid of the obligation and he will ship the second one in the fortnight you have just been warned about. The second is that you insure the second load or you do not send it, and I can tell you who writes that at the coast and what they will want for it, and it will be a great deal less than the ore."

"And you'd write to them for me."

"I will write to them for my house, which currently has a security interest in a foundry that runs on that ore." She closed the case. "The letter is the same letter. I would rather you understood which one I am writing."

Saban did not say anything for a moment, and then said, to Emeric, in a voice pitched to carry, "Where'd you get her."

"I owe a great deal of money."

"Aye, well." He took the sheet back. "It's the best morning's advice anybody's given me in this room and it come off the far side of the table. I'll ride Thursday."

The last question was the one that took him four days, and it was not about ore at all.

It was about what the foundry made while it was eating a lower ward.

Ferra put it up on the eleventh, without heat, in the way she put up things she had already worked through twice.

"There is nobody to sell to," she said. "The Wayford ironmonger will trade with Kaldo in a year and not before. The two houses in the capital have declined. Grayrock cannot sign its own name. Milford has money and Paxton is not going to buy iron from you this winter, because he is owed four parts of a harvest that does not exist and every coin he sends you is a coin he is choosing not to mention." She turned the page. "So every hour that building runs converts coin that has been borrowed at interest into iron that nobody will pay for. I am not saying stop. I am saying that if you run it and make weapons you will have a shed full of weapons and a debt, and if you run it and make nothing you will have forty trained men who go home and do not come back."

"Then it makes what Kaldo buys."

"Kaldo has no money."

"Kaldo has no money and Kaldo has a spring." Emeric had the list in front of him and had had it in front of him since the eighth. "Two thousand acres going in again and four thousand six hundred wanted. Ploughshares. Coulters. Harrow tines, and the tines are the thing, because every harrow in this barony is wood and every wooden tine is a man's morning twice a season. Hinges. Barrel hoops. Nails, and I want to say the word nails out loud in front of everybody because a nail is not a thing a prince notices and there is not a building going up on this plain that is not waiting on somebody's nails."

"And who buys them."

"The venture buys them, out of next year's harvest, at a price you set now and write down now, and Dunnel reads it before I see it." He looked up. "That's not a sale. I know it isn't. It's the barony owing itself something, and in a book that's a circle, and I'd rather have the circle than a shed of swords."

"It is a circle," Vantic said. "It is also the only thing on this table that puts a tool in a field in April. My house will not object to it and I would not report it as a sale, and I would ask you to have Mistress Ferra keep it in a column of its own with that word at the top, so that in three years nobody reads it as revenue, including you."

"Write it as internal," Ferra said, and had already written it.

So the foundry ran through November on soft mail and mended caps and the honest metal off two hundred and fifty men's spears, and the number one furnace ate a war in about seven weeks, and what came off the benches was hinges.

Emeric walked the floor on the twenty-second at about nine in the evening because he could not sleep, and found the shift man and four others still at it, and a charge book on the desk with eleven numbered entries in a hand that was not Bram's and not his.

Declan's housing had been back on the rack with the coupling reseated since the middle of September, which nobody in the building found interesting any more.

Bram was at the far end with the tap-bar across his knees, not working, watching the note the furnace made.

"Seven weeks," Emeric said.

"Seven weeks, and then either there's a barge or there isn't." Bram did not look round. "You know what I've been sat here doing."

"Tell me."

"Counting what's coming off this floor and putting it against what my father's shop did in a year, and it's not close, and my father's shop was the best shop in this barony for thirty years." He turned the bar over. "I keep waiting to be frightened of it. It's not come. What's come is I'd like a second wheel and an axle to cast it with, and I'd like Saban's barge, and I'd like about four more men that can read a tap, and every one of them's a want and not a fright, and I don't know what to make of a year where all my troubles are wants."

"Enjoy it for about three weeks."

"Aye," Bram said. "I thought as much."
