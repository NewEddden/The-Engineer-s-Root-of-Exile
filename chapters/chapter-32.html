Chapter 32 - Crestless

Saban came back in the first week of summer with a full cart, a clean set of accounts, and three questions that were not his.

He gave them in the order he had received them, which was the order he gave everything in, and he did not editorialise, which was the single most useful thing about him.

A factor out of somewhere had asked, in Wayford, over a drink, who the soap belonged to. Not where it came from. Who it belonged to.

A clerk at the Grayrock scale house had asked what the volume of Kaldo's ore order had been the previous quarter, which was a question a clerk at a scale house has no business needing an answer to, since the scale house had weighed it.

And a man in a good coat, pleasant as anything, had asked whether the southern barony had a new lord, and what sort of man he was.

"That's the one," Saban said.

"Which one."

"The third. The first two are commerce. Men ask about commerce all day, it is what a market is." He turned his cup round on the table. "What sort of man he is. That is not commerce. You do not need to know a man's character to buy soap off him."

Ferra, at the end of the table, did not look up from her sheet.

"You need to know it to decide whether to take his barony," she said.

"I would not go that far," Saban said, "and I would not go far from it either."

None of it was threatening. That was the part Emeric kept turning over for the next several days. Nothing in the three questions would have survived being written down. A factor, a clerk, a man in a coat, in three different rooms over five weeks. Any one of them was nothing. What made it something was that somebody was collecting.

He had spent a career in another life watching people build a picture of a thing before deciding what to do about it. He had built pictures himself. He knew what the early part looked like, and it looked exactly like this, which was to say it looked like nothing at all.

He went to Ferra with a number and she fought him for two hours.

"Better a knife you never need," he said, "than an empty hand in a war."

"That is true." She wrote something. "It is also what a man says immediately before he spends a year's profit on things that stab people."

"Both of those can be true."

"They are both true. I am not disputing the sentence, my lord, I am disputing the total." She turned the sheet round so he could see it, which she did when she wanted to win. "Here is the school. Here is the second kiln. Here is roofing the Works before winter, which you have deferred twice. And here is your knife."

"The knife is bigger."

"The knife is considerably bigger."

They went at it until the candle wanted trimming, and neither of them gave ground, and both of them were right, and at some point around the ninth pass it stopped being an argument and became the thing they did instead of saying something else.

She signed it in the end because she had costed the alternative, which was what she always did, and which was why he never bothered trying to win an argument with her by being clever.

"For the record," she said, capping the ink.

"Noted."

"I have not said it yet."

"You are going to say that if nobody comes, this was a waste."

"I am going to say that if nobody comes, this was a waste, and that I will have been wrong in the most fortunate possible way, and that I would like that outcome very much." She put the ink away. "Try to give it to me."

He made the first suit himself and it took him eleven days and it was bad.

The second was worse, which he had not expected and which taught him more than the first. The third was decent. He was not a smith and had never pretended to be one, and what he was doing was not smithing. He was making a thing that could be copied, and the difference between those two activities is roughly the difference between writing a letter and designing an alphabet.

Then he put the pattern down in front of Bram's two apprentices and told them to make thirty-nine more.

They stood there.

He waited, because he had learned to wait on this floor, and after a while the elder one, Tobe, asked the question.

"When do we start hammering?"

It was a completely reasonable question, and it was the whole of the old world in eight words. You take a man. You measure him. You raise the plate over weeks, over months, with a hammer, and at the end of it the thing fits him and nobody else in the world, and the smith's name is on it and rides on it. That was what making armour was. That was what it had been for as long as anyone in that building had a memory of anyone.

"For about a fifth of it," Emeric said. "For the rest, you pour."

"Pour."

"Pour."

He made the argument properly, because he needed them to believe it rather than obey it, and because he had learned that the difference showed up in the work.

You put metal in a mold and you get the same piece. Then you get the same piece. Then you get the same piece. Not a worse one. Not the apprentice's version of the master's. The same one, the tenth exactly as good as the first, because the pattern is never tired and never in a hurry and has no opinions about the man it is making the piece for.

Three sizes instead of forty men. A pauldron off the middle pattern fits any man in the middle range, and if it does not quite, you pad it, and the padding costs an afternoon and the fitting costs three weeks.

And then he said the thing that actually landed, which was not about speed at all.

"A man takes a hit on the left breast. The plate is stoved. Under the old way, he stands down for six weeks while somebody raises him a new one, and for six weeks the barony has thirty-nine men. Under this way, he walks to a shelf, he takes the plate off the shelf, and he is back on the wall before the bruise has come up."

The younger apprentice, Wick, looked up.

"There's a shelf?"

"There is going to be a shelf."

That was what did it. Neither of them cared about the production rate. Both of them had spent their whole working lives watching fighting men stand idle for a month over a piece of metal, and had accepted it the way you accept weather, and it had never once occurred to either of them that it was a choice somebody had made.

Bram let the whole speech run and then corrected it, which was the pattern.

"Cast armour is not forged armour," he said. "It will not be, ever, and if you tell those two otherwise they will believe you and somebody will die of it."

"Go on."

"Cast metal has grain in it that goes nowhere. Forged metal has grain you put where you want it. A point comes in at the breast, at the helm, at the front of the thigh." He tapped each place on himself with two fingers, unhurried. "Those are forged, by me, out of good stock, and they take twice as long as anything else in the suit and they are what the man is actually wearing. The rest of it, the skirt, the fauld, the arm harness, the greaves, the whole back of him, pour it. I do not care. Pour it in your sleep."

"So a hybrid."

"So a proper suit," Bram said, "with the cheap parts made cheaply, which is what a proper suit always was, and nobody ever wrote it down because nobody ever had to."

Emeric took the correction, in front of the apprentices, the way he had taken the one about the furnace, and he noticed both the taking and his own mild reluctance to take it, and he did it anyway.

The suits were better for it. Every one of them was better for it. Ten years later a man in another country would take one apart to find out how it had been done and would assume, from the forging on the breast and the casting on the skirt, that it had been made by two workshops who did not like each other.

They asked what to put on them.

It came up in the eighth week, when the first four were finished and standing on frames in the yard, and it came from Wick, who wanted to know what the device was going to be so he could work out how to leave room for it.

"Nothing," Emeric said.

They waited for the rest of it.

"There is no rest of it. Nothing. No crest, no device, no colour."

Ward was leaning on the rail and did not move, which from Ward was a comment.

The reasoning took Emeric about a minute to give and he had spent three weeks building it.

Every barony in Randillia marked its men. It was not decoration, it was administration: the crest said whose these men were, who answered for them, and who would be answering for them afterward. A company with no crest on it was a company nobody was answering for, which in this kingdom meant one thing.

And everybody knew three things about mercenaries. They are expensive. They are temporary. And they go when the money goes.

So: a man rides past Kaldo in the autumn, or stands in the Wayford market buying information the way you buy fish, and what he hears is that the broke southern barony has forty men in good plate outside its wall and not a device among them. He does not conclude that Kaldo is strong. He concludes that a desperate lord has spent every coin he has on hired steel, that it is costing him a fortune he does not have, and that the whole arrangement comes apart the moment the money does. He puts a season on it. Maybe two.

Which was the precise opposite of the truth in every particular.

These men were from here. Their mothers were three streets from where they stood. Wick's father had a boat. Half of them had buried somebody in the ravine in the last two years. They were not going anywhere whether they were paid or not, and the day the money stopped they would still be on that wall, because it was their wall.

He stood in the yard and looked at four empty suits on four frames and understood, with some precision, that he had just done a new kind of thing. He had lied about the barony. Not to anyone in particular, and not in words, and not in any way a man could be called on. He had arranged a set of facts so that the true reading of them was the wrong one, and then put the facts where people would find them.

He did not feel clever about it. He noted the absence of that feeling and did not examine it either.

The four in capital harness were the exception, and the exception was deliberate too. Ward, Rylan, Declan and Osric kept the Crown's markings, because four Crown-marked men standing in the middle of forty blank ones did not spoil the picture. It completed it. A broke lord with hired steel and four royal minders his father had sent along to watch him is a considerably sadder figure than a broke lord with hired steel, and sad figures do not get looked at twice.

The cost of the whole business got raised once, quietly, by a man named Ossen who was forty-four and had been on the wall under three barons.

"A man who wears no crest," he said, "is not protected by one either."

The yard went still in the particular way a yard goes still.

"Say the rest of it," Emeric said.

"A baron's sworn man taken in the field is ransomed. Fed. Walked home eventually, if somebody pays. That is not a kindness, it is that his lord will want him back and everyone knows it." Ossen was not being dramatic about it. He had the tone of a man reading out a price. "A mercenary taken in the field is a man nobody is going to pay for. What is done with him is whatever there is time for."

"Yes," Emeric said. "That is correct and I should have said it first."

He could have issued it. He had the authority to issue it and every man in that yard knew he had the authority to issue it, and issuing it would have taken four seconds.

He put it to them instead, because it was not a thing he was able to decide on another man's behalf, and because a decision about what happens to you after you are captured is not the lord's to make.

They took it, and they took it lightly, which bothered him more than a fight would have. Some of that was Ossen's own point cutting the other way: the company was not leaving the barony. Every one of them would be standing on ground he lived on. The day any of them crossed a border, the decision would come back up with a real price attached to it, and Emeric said so out loud so that nobody could later claim he had not.

And some of it, he thought afterward, was Corliss.

Most of the men in that yard had watched him take Corliss apart in front of everybody on a Tuesday and put him back together by the end of the week, and had drawn a conclusion from it, and the conclusion was not that the prince was fair. It was that the prince asked. It was a low bar. It was higher than any bar they had been given before.

Corliss was in the yard for the vote and did not say anything at all, and that was its own kind of comment, and Emeric did not go looking for what kind.

The four capital suits were the only part of the year that he did alone, at night, with the door shut.

What Ward, Rylan, Declan and Osric had arrived in three years ago was mediocre steel over very good crystals. That was the capital's whole armour doctrine in one sentence and it was not stupid: steel is cheap and crystal is not, so you put the money where the magic is and accept that the plate is ordinary.

The crystals in those four suits were the finest objects in the barony by a wide margin. Emeric had known that from the first month and had never once taken them for anything else. It was a written rule, in his own book, in his own hand, from before he had anything worth spending them on: the four sets are the four men's. Not a resource. He had reread it more than once on evenings when he wanted to reread it.

So he did not take them. He moved them.

One suit at a time, out of the capital's steel and into Kaldo's, forged breast, cast fauld, Bram's grain where a point would come in.

And while each stone was out of its housing, he went into it.

The capital cuts a command into a stone from the outside, by hand, with an iron. That is the entire craft and it has been the entire craft for four hundred years. And it works, and the men who do it are not fools, and it has one hard limit that nobody has ever been able to argue with, which is that a stone has a surface and a volume and every instruction takes up room. A capital armourer fits perhaps a handful of workings into a good crystal and then stops, not because he has run out of ideas but because he has run out of somewhere to put them.

Emeric was not working on the surface. He was not limited by how much room there was, because from where he was standing the room was not the constraint.

And the thing that struck him, the first night, with Ward's stone, was not how much more he could fit. It was how wasteful what was already in there was.

The capital's workings were correct. Every one of them did exactly what it said. They were also written by men who had never once been able to see what they were writing, who had no way to check whether an instruction was doing its work twice, who had never in four centuries had any instrument for asking whether the thing could be done in fewer steps. They were correct and they were bloated, in exactly the way that any code is bloated when nobody has ever profiled it, because nobody had ever had a profiler.

He did not add much. He mostly took away. It took him four nights a suit and he slept badly through all of it and would not have traded the work for anything he could think of.

The test was three beats and none of them went the way he expected.

Comfort came first and came instantly. They had the suits on for ten seconds before all four of them knew, and what they knew was not that it was lighter, though it was. It was that the weight sat where a man carries weight instead of where a plate happens to hang.

"It is the first armour I have worn," Osric said, turning on the spot with his arms out like a man being fitted for a wedding, "that does not make me walk like my father."

It was also, and nobody had planned this and Emeric would have denied planning it, extremely handsome. Bram's forging took a polish that the capital's flat grey never had, and the cast pieces came off the pattern with a hard clean edge on them, and the whole thing read, at twenty paces, as something that had cost a great deal of money.

Declan was insufferable about it for a week.

"He has looked at himself in the trough," Osric reported, on the fourth day. "Twice. Once was checking the fit. The second time was not."

"It was the fit," Declan said.

"It was not the fit."

Defence came second, and it was the only part of the year that Emeric would think about afterward with something close to discomfort.

They asked, reasonably, whether it stopped anything. It was a fair question and there was no answer to it that did not involve hitting one of them.

"Ward. Do not move."

Emeric picked up a sword off the rack, took two steps, and swung at the man's chest at full speed.

Ward did not move.

He did not brace. He did not shift his weight. He did not turn his shoulder, or set his feet, or close his eyes, and his hands stayed exactly where they had been, and the blade came in at the breast with everything Emeric had in it and did precisely nothing.

Emeric lowered the sword and found that what he was thinking about was not the armour.

On Earth this man would have been one in a generation, and nobody would ever have been able to explain to a recruiter why. There was no test for it. It did not appear in any measurement. It was not courage in the way that word is usually used, because courage implies a thing overcome and there had been nothing in Ward's face to overcome. He had been told not to move by the man he guarded. The sword was real. Those two facts had been weighed against each other somewhere and the weighing had taken no time at all.

He wrote it down that night in the flattest language he could manage, because he could not write it any other way and because the other way would have been unbearable.

Mana came third and that was where the four of them stopped being polite.

He told them to run their workings, and Osric ran the first enhancement, and then stopped, and then ran it again, and then said a word that Doran would not have wanted in the yard.

What had taken five took one.

That is a sentence with two entirely different sentences inside it. It means a man can hold what he used to hold, for five times as long, on the same reserve. It also means he can spend the whole reserve at once and get five times the force out of it. Either one changes what a man is. Both of them together change what a formation is.

Osric wanted to try things. Declan wanted to know the failure mode, which was Declan entire. Ward asked how long it took to do and whether it could be done again if a stone were damaged, which were the two questions Emeric would have asked, and neither of which he answered fully.

Rylan worked it out faster than any of them and went quiet.

He stood at the edge of the yard in a suit of black steel with his hands at his sides and did not say anything for a long time, and Emeric understood, eventually, roughly what he was doing. He was counting backward. He was in a different yard, ten years ago, with different men in it, running the numbers on what a hard minute instead of a hard fifteen seconds would have meant for people who were not available to be asked.

He had been cold with Emeric since Corliss. Months of it.

What he said, when he finally said something, was, "You could have given us these two years ago."

"I could not have. I did not know how."

"No," Rylan said. "I suppose you did not."

And that was all of it. He did not apologise for anything and neither did Emeric and nothing was resolved, and it was a crack about the width of a hair in something that had been solid since the spring, and it did not close.

The bows took the rest of the summer and were a smaller decision than they looked.

He considered the longbow for about an hour. It is a better weapon in almost every way that a weapon is measured, and it is unavailable, because a longbowman is ten years old when you start and twenty-two when he is useful and has a skeleton that has grown wrong from the draw. Emeric had neither ten years nor the boys. He was not going to pretend otherwise for the pleasure of owning a better weapon on paper.

A crossbow is a fortnight to competence. The draw lives in the mechanism rather than in the man, which means a farmhand and a veteran put a bolt in roughly the same place, and that sentence was the entire argument, and it was the same argument as the seed sieve and the master pattern and the iron foot cut in Bram's shop and every other decision he had made since the parish hall. Take the skill out of the hands and put it in the object. Then the object can be copied and the hands do not have to be.

The foundry cast the locks and the nuts off one pattern. The prods were a problem for a month and a half and were eventually solved by laminating them, badly at first and then acceptably, and Emeric recorded the prods as the one part of the year he had underestimated.

The engines were two, and he was honest with himself about them in the book, which was the only place he was honest about anything.

They were the least examined thing he had built all year. He did not expect a siege. Everything in his reading said that nobody sieges an island with one bridge if there is any alternative, and there was always an alternative. He built them anyway, for two reasons, and one of them was sound and one of them was superstition dressed as logic.

The sound reason was that a wall with nothing behind it is an invitation to find out what is behind it.

The other reason was that the timber and the labour existed now, and he had learned something in another life about the difference between a thing you can build and a thing you could have built.

Ferra priced the whole year at the end of it and read the total out to him.

It was a large number. She let it sit in the room for a moment.

"A knife you never need," she said.

"Better than an empty hand."

"You have rather a lot of knives now. You have, by my count, more knives than hands."

"That is how you end up with a lot of hands."

It was a bad joke. It was a genuinely bad joke, and she laughed at it, briefly and without dignity, and then put her face back on and went back to the sheet.

The last line of the year was not funny at all, and he wrote it in the book after she had gone.

Forty men in plate with nothing on it. Four more in harness that no armourer in the kingdom could reproduce and most of them could not explain. A hundred bolts and the machines to make more. Two engines nobody had thought hard enough about. A barony that eighteen months ago could not have put nine men in the field in matching boots.

And he still did not know who had asked what sort of man he was.
