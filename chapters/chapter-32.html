Chapter 32 - Hundredweight

In January every hearth in Kaldo was burning, and every hearth in Kaldo made ash.

It came into the soap yard by the bucket, by the sack and by the barrow, from the lower ward and the forge district and the high street and the farms, from houses that had not sold the yard a bucket all summer because in the summer they had nothing to burn. Abloh's boys weighed it at the gate and paid for it at the posted price, and the posted price was the one Emeric had stood in the lower market in June and promised at the top of his voice, twice: that he would buy ash at the same price whoever made the soap, and would go on buying it.

Among them, every week, was the woman whose hands the third batch had burned in June. They had healed shiny across the backs, and she held her sack out to Abloh's boys with them, and took her coppers, and said to Emeric once, in passing, that it was the first winter of her life she had been paid for her own fire.

The shop could not use half of what came in. It had nowhere to put the rest. And it was paying for every bucket.

Ferra did not tell him she had warned him. She had, in June, at the ash scales, before he knew her name: the price worked in summer and would not survive a winter. She did not say a word about it now. She put two pages side by side on her table instead, the June page and the January page, and let him look.

The January page had three lines for every one on the June page.

"The shop can sell a third more soap than it did in the autumn," she said. "If it works the yard harder and the women wrap faster. Not three times more. Nobody in Kaldo washes three times more in January." She put her finger at the foot of the January page. "At the posted price, it can go on paying for ash for about nine more days. Then it stops paying for anything else."

"Then the price stays and I pay the difference."

She had known he would say it. He could see her having known it. She did not argue, which was worse than if she had.

He had thought about the other way, and he made himself think about it again at her table, properly, because she was owed that. He could drop the price. He could post a winter price and a summer price, and be right to, and every bucket of ash in Kaldo would be worth less the next morning than it had been the night before, and the women who had been paid for their waste for the first time in their lives would learn what a prince's word was worth when it got expensive. The lower ward had only begun to believe him in the summer. He was not going to teach it to stop.

So he counted it out at her table, from the pouch, while Bram pretended not to watch from the stove: the difference for January, coin by coin, in a stack by her elbow. When he was done the pouch was flat enough to fold.

Ferra counted it again. Then she drew a line across the page and wrote under it, in her plain small hand: Lent by E. to the shop, against the ash. To be repaid.

"It's not a loan," he said. "I'm not going to ask the shop for it back."

"Then don't ask." She wrote one more word, in the margin beside it: spring. "It's still a loan. The shop owes it. When the shop has it, the shop pays it. I'm not having a gift sitting in the middle of the books where nobody can see what it cost."

He could have argued. He did the arithmetic on arguing instead, and found it came out the same as the arithmetic on not arguing, and let it stand.

The ash the shop could not use did not go to waste, in the end, though it had to wait a month to stop being one. Wood ash was potash, and potash was what a field wanted and seldom got. In February Farris came in with his cart and took a load of it out to the west side, to go on his field when the ground opened. He said his grandmother had thrown the hearth ash on her cabbages every spring, and that nobody he had ever heard of had had enough of it to throw on a field. Hollis came the week after and took exactly as much as would cover one strip.



She came to the other thing in February, at the same table, at the end of the month's pages, as though she had been waiting for the right column to put it in.

"There's a chest of crystals in your room."

He looked at Bram. Bram was mending a hinge by the stove, and did not look up from it, and said to the hinge that in July, at this table, the prince had told him the crystals for the furnace would come out of a chest his father had given him, and that he had not been told to keep it to himself.

"You said it at this table in July," Ferra said. "I was sitting here." She was not looking at him; she was looking at the column. "You've been paying for the guards out of that pouch since April, and the school's households since September, and now the ash. I know what's in the pouch. I don't know what's in the chest."

"Thirty-eight," he said. "And no."

"No to what?"

"To selling them." He gave her both reasons, because she would want both and would know if he gave her one. A chest of uncut crystals with no crest and no record sounded untraceable, and was the opposite, in a town this size: the first manasmith in Wayford who saw thirty-eight good stones come in from Kaldo would know there was only one man in Kaldo who could have had them, and would wonder where he got them, and would wonder aloud, to whoever paid him to. And he needed them. Every one. For a furnace that was not built, and a foundry that was not lit, and other things he had not drawn yet and could not have bought again at any price once they were gone.

Ferra listened to both, and did not say whether she agreed with either.

Then she wrote, at the foot of the February page, below the totals, in the place where she kept the things that were true and were not money: One chest. Not on the books. Not to be.



The plain bar could not be what Kaldo sold to the world. He had given the plain bar away himself, in the lower market, the whole method, twice, at carrying volume. Anybody with lye and fat and a kettle could make it, and the woman in Wayford who heard how it was done from a carter would be making it inside a month.

What went out of Kaldo had to be a bar nobody else could make the same.

Abloh made it in the first fortnight of February, at a bench at the back of the soap yard he would let nobody else near. Ferra's count had turned up four households in the barony that kept bees, all of them on the west side, all of them with honey that went nowhere but their own tables and a little to the inn. Abloh took the honey and worked it into a finer bar than anything the yard had made, with the lye eased and the fat clean, and the bar came out of the mold a pale gold, smooth as a pebble from the river, and smelling faintly of summer in a yard that smelled of January.

The first honey batch came out of the mold brown, and smelled of burnt sugar across half the yard. Abloh had put the honey in with the lye still hot, and the lye had cooked it. Emeric knew what hot lye did to sugar the way he knew his own name, and opened his mouth, and Abloh said, "I know," without looking round. The second batch he made with the honey stirred in at the very end, when the soap had thickened and cooled enough to hold a spoon upright, and it came out gold.

It wanted a mark. Something pressed into it while it was soft, so that a buyer in a town that had never heard of Kaldo would know the next one was the same, and would ask for it by the mark.

Tess cut it, out of the last of Bram's pear plank, with the same knives she had used for the letter blocks: one arch, with the line of the channel running over the top of it, the way the aqueduct crossed the west farms. She cut it backwards the first time without being told. Abloh pressed it into a bar, and lifted the stamp, and the arch stood up off the gold in clean relief.

By the end of the month people in the lower ward had stopped calling it the honey bar and were calling it the arch.

He took the first box of them down to the shop himself.

There was a queue. There was usually a queue now, in the mornings. Sela was at the counter with a line of six in front of her, and she said, "Put it on the end," without looking up, and took two coppers from a woman with a basket and gave her change, and went on to the next.

He put it on the end. That was all. It was civil, and it was busy, and she had not looked up because there were six people waiting, not because it was him, and he stood in the door of the shop until a woman with a basket needed to get past him.

He took a few of them up to the ward as well, and stood at the side door with them, since he had no yes for that day. Imani came to the door with her sleeves rolled and a cloth over her shoulder. Behind her the CATCHING room's door was shut, and there was a chalk mark beside it he had not seen before, which meant somebody was in it.

She looked at the gold bars in his hands.

"No," she said. "Honey brings flies. I've flies enough. I'll have the plain."

She took a crate of the plain ones from the boy behind him and shut the door, and he went back down the hill with the arch bars still in his hands.



The carter had been on Emeric's mind since June.

A Wayford man, with a cart and two horses and a regular round on the south road, who had stood in the soap yard in the summer and asked what a hundredweight would cost. Emeric had not been able to fill a hundredweight then. Salt came monthly and the kiln was small. He had taken the man's name, and had kept it, and in January, with the yard working hard and the ash coming in by the barrow, he had found that at last he could.

He did not write to Wayford by the road. His letters by the road went north and were not answered, and he had stopped expecting them to be. He gave this one by hand to a Kaldo man off Ferra's count who carted hides to the Wayford tannery every few weeks, and paid him to put it into the carter's own hand and no other.

The carter came in the last week of February, down the south road in the thaw, with the mud to his axles and the letter folded in his coat. He took it out at the South Gate and showed it to the guard, as though it might be needed to get him in.

He got his hundredweight of plain bars at the price he had asked about in June. Ferra had set it in January, and had not let Emeric raise it for the delay.

They brought the bars up to the gate yard in crates from the shop, and the carter backed his cart in under the arch, and Emeric took off his coat and loaded it himself.

He did not explain why. There were four crates, and by the second there was a crowd at the edge of the gate yard, and the petition line had stopped being a line, and nobody said anything at all. They stood and watched a prince carry soap. Abloh, who had come up with the crates, looked at him once and then handed him the third without comment, and the carter, who had been standing back with his hat in his hands because a prince was loading his cart, put the hat on the seat and climbed up to stack the last. It took a few minutes. Emeric would not have given them to anybody else in Kaldo for any money.

When it was done the carter tied the load down, and paid Ferra, who had come up with her book and counted it twice, and shook Emeric's hand with a grip like a horse collar, and climbed onto the box.

Emeric went up onto the wall above the South Gate to watch it go.

It went over the moat bridge, and out along the road between the fields, and round under the west wall, and up the long slope northward toward the Whispering Woods, slowly, the horses leaning into the mud. Nothing about it was remarkable. A cart, two horses, a man on the box, a load of soap under a tarpaulin.

It was the first thing Kaldo had made with its own hands and sold outside the barony since the war.

He watched it until it was a dark speck on the road and then until it was not anything, and stayed a little after that, with his coat over his arm and the wind off the farms getting into his shirt.

In Ferra's book that night it had a line to itself.
