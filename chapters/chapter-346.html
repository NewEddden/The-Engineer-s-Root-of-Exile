Chapter 346 - The Line

He had slept, by Ferra's count, about two hours.

She didn't say how she knew. She sat up at a quarter to seven in his bed in the castle at Kaldo, in one of his shirts, rested and pink and entirely awake, and looked down at him and said, "You were up at four. You went to the window twice. You said a number out loud."

"What number?"

"Eleven." She considered it. "It might have been elevens."

On his other side Imani lay face down with one arm across his chest, heavy as a sandbag. Both of them had slept like stones dropped down a well. He had lain between them in the June heat, wanting a number of things very clearly for several hours and doing none of them, because they had said one wedding and he had heard them. He was a king now. A king could manage that much. It had not been restful.

Imani lifted her head off the pillow.

"He looks terrible," she said to Ferra, across him.

"He was up at four."

"I slept beautifully." She took her arm back and stretched until something in her shoulder clicked, and on the way past she put two fingers under his jaw without asking. "Seventy. You're alive. Is there breakfast? I'm not eating yours today. You're having it."

Somebody knocked, twice, the way only one person in the castle knocked.

"There's a ship in from Duvrenne," said Saban, through two inches of oak. "Flying the king's own pennant. She came round the South Sentinel at six. I'd like a word before ten." A pause. "I wasn't listening. I'd like that on the record too."

Ferra put her face in her hands.



Saban had the word over eggs, in the small room off the kitchens where the household ate, standing, because he didn't sit when he was explaining something.

"His envoy was on the bench yesterday. He wasn't. A king on a bench at another king's crowning is a king who bowed. So he kept off the coast till you were crowned, and now he's here first, one king to another, before Velmoor or Solreth have finished packing."

"That's clever," said Emeric.

"It's very clever. He's asked for the long room at ten. He's brought his daughter."

Ferra's spoon stopped.

"Alina," said Emeric.

"As his diplomat. She does all his treating." Saban looked at him. "She was at your father's table. You'll remember."

He remembered eleven cups and a price schedule and a woman with her hair up off her neck who had gone quiet when she should have been loud. He remembered that his father had sent him south for it. He did not remember her face very well, which bothered him more than the rest.

Ferra had brought a fat sheaf of paper down to breakfast, her draft of the Isle terms, written since March a clause at a time, and was reading it with her eggs going cold.

"He'll open with the insult," she said, without looking up. "It's the one thing he holds that you can't buy back with oil."

"I know."

"Then don't let him spend it."



The king of Duvrenne was a short, broad man with a grey beard cut square and the build of somebody who had carried things once and still could. He spoke Randillian slowly, as a man does who had it put into him by tutors and never liked it. His envoy sat on his left with a clerk behind. Alina sat on his right.

She wore her hair up, as she had at his father's table. She was older, and so was he, and she looked at him across the oak with the plain attention of someone checking a coin she'd been given once before and found light.

On Emeric's side sat Ferra, as Chancellor, with her draft; Saban; Armand, in the bow tie; and Imani, because nobody had told her not to, and in any case it was too late to try. Ward stood at the door, as he stood at every door Emeric was behind.

The king of Duvrenne laid his hands flat on the table.

"The last time a son of Harrold spoke to my house," he said, "he called me a thief at his father's table. In front of seventy people and a band of musicians. I came to see if the new king is the same man."

"He isn't," said Emeric. "Before we talk about the Isle, I want to say one thing. So it's not part of the trade."

The king's eyebrows went up a fraction. Emeric turned in his chair, away from him, to the woman on his right.

"Princess. I was drunk at my father's table, and I said what I said about your father's mines to your face, in a room that had come to watch you bargain. I should have said it to him. Sober, on paper, with the yield reports asked for in writing. I'm sorry for how I said it."

Alina didn't move.

"For how," she said.

"Yes."

"Not for what."

"The price was fixed," said Emeric. "Twenty years at one rate, on a crystal that had moved in living memory. You knew it when I said it. You went quiet."

The envoy began to rise. The king put two fingers on his sleeve, and the envoy sat down again.

Alina looked at Emeric for a long time. Then she let her breath out through her nose, which was the first unplanned thing he had seen her do.

"I've had years to decide what I'd say if you ever said you were sorry," she said. "I had a good deal of it ready. None of it was for this."

"What was it for?"

"For a drunk who'd say he hadn't meant it." She folded her hands on the table. "I'm relieved. I'm also insulted. I find I can say both of those to you, which I could never have said to the other one."

"It was fixed," said the king of Duvrenne.

His envoy shut his eyes.

"The yield was falling. I'd had it from the mine reeves the winter before. That price would have carried my treasury eleven years past where it should have." He said it flatly, like an old man giving tomorrow's weather. "You were right. You were also a drunk boy shouting at my daughter across a table. I've had a long time to decide which of those I minded more."

"Which was it?"

"My daughter," said the king.

Armand, at the end of the table, very slightly moved his pen.

"So," said the king. "The Isle."

He was good at it. He went through the war on the Claim as a farmer goes through a bad harvest, item by item, without bitterness: his landing, Kaldo's wells, the burning lake, his own dead by number. He did not ask for Kaldo's. Then he said that his family had signed treaties over the Isle for three generations and fought over the pitch lake for sixty years regardless, because paper was paper.

"I want something that holds when you and I are both dead," he said. "You're a king without a queen. My daughter is the best diplomat I have and she isn't married, because I've kept her unmarried for her use. That was selfish of me. I'm told so at home." He did not look at Alina. "I offer her to you. Bind the crowns past any treaty, and the Isle settles itself."

Alina sat with her hands folded and her face composed and did not look at anybody. She had known it was coming. Emeric could see that she had known, and that she had spent the voyage deciding not to mind.

Ferra turned a page.

He waited for her to look up. She didn't. She read down the page with her pen in her hand, made a small mark against something in the third clause, and went on reading.

"That's..." said Emeric. "I'm very honoured."

Imani made a small noise into her hand that might have been a cough.

"I can't. I'm promised."

"Promised," said the king of Duvrenne. "You were crowned yesterday."

"I asked last night."

The king's eyes went along the table and stopped on Ferra, because everybody on the continent with a treasury knew the Chancellor of Kaldo was a smith's daughter and how the king's charter had been written.

"The Chancellor."

"And the Secretary of Health," said Emeric.

Imani lifted one hand an inch off the table, like a girl in a schoolroom.

The king looked from one of them to the other. "Both."

"Both."

The envoy's clerk had begun to write. The king held up a hand without turning round, and the clerk stopped.

"You have refused me," said Alina, quite pleasantly, "in front of my father, the morning after you asked two other women to marry you."

"It sounds worse when you say it."

"It sounds exactly as bad as it is."

Ferra turned to her second page and, at last, looked up.

"He wouldn't dare in any case, majesty," she said to the king of Duvrenne. "One of us could make his death look like an accident." She tipped her pen toward Imani without looking at her. "And the other could pay to have it written down as one."

"Neatly," said Imani.

The king stared at Ferra. Then he laughed, one short bark, like a dog surprised by its own voice, and put his hand over his beard.

Ferra went back to her page.

It was then that the K Discovery went past the long-room windows on her way down to the yard, low and slow, a long grey hull with no sail on her anywhere. Nobody in Kaldo looked up at her any more. Alina did. She watched the whole length of her go by, and when it had gone she went on looking at the empty sky.

"Four years ago," she said, "the wardens of our camp on the Isle swore to me that there'd been something over the camp in the night. A long shape with no sails. I had two of them flogged for drinking on watch." She turned her head. "In the morning the camp was empty."

Nobody said anything. Saban looked at the ceiling.

"You were the ones stealing our oil," said Alina. "You took the camp."

"There were men of Randillia in that camp," said Emeric. "Some of them for eighteen years. Chained. Digging ground your crown hadn't any right to dig."

"And your wells on the Claim had a right to the oil."

"No more than yours." He held her eye. "So we were both wrong. Or we were both within our rights. I don't mind which you'd rather. Either way we sign the same paper."

Alina thought about it with her whole face, which he suspected she rarely let anyone see.

"Then we were both wrong," she said, "and I don't have to like you."

"You don't."

"Good." She turned to her father. "He's right about the paper."

The king of Duvrenne did not say whether she was. He stood, and said he would send his terms up from his ship before the evening bell, and bowed to Emeric, king to king, the depth exactly right. Then he bowed to Ferra, a little lower, and to Imani lower still, with a face so straight that Emeric understood he was being made fun of and didn't mind in the least.



His father found Maeve at one, on the castle stair, with her bag already down in the hall and her hat in her hand, and asked her for an hour.

Emeric was two steps below them with Ferra's Isle draft under his arm. He heard Maeve say the express went at three, and his father say he knew when the express went, he'd been on it, and Maeve say that he had been on the switchback, which was not the same thing at all; and then the two of them stood on the stair and looked at each other, each plainly about to win.

"An hour," said Harrold. "I should have done it on the water. I didn't. That's mine." He looked down the stair. "I'll need your hand for it."

Maeve looked from one of them to the other.

"What's under the glass?" she said.

Nobody answered her. She put her hat back on.

They went over the north bridge in a line, Harrold first, at the pace he had now, which was a young man's and which he still hadn't learned to spend; he kept arriving at the far end of things and stopping to wait for people who were already beside him. The willows were full of midges. Two knights stood at the far bridge doing nothing, and the younger saw who it was and found something to do with his collar. At the door Maeve stopped, as everybody stopped the first time, because the heat came out at her like the breath of a bread oven, green and thick, and pressed behind the eyes.

"You'll feel the mana," said Emeric. "It won't hurt you."

"I didn't ask if it would." She went in.

She looked at the tree first. Everybody did; it was built to be looked at, or it had grown that way, the long pale trunk going up into the light and the crystal hanging in it like fruit nobody would ever be allowed to pick. She looked at it for about as long as it deserved. Then her eyes came down the trunk to the roots, and to the mattress at its foot, and to the woman on it under a light blanket with her hair plaited and her hands folded, and they stopped there.

Maeve didn't say anything. She took her gloves off. She did it without being asked and without looking at what her hands were doing, a finger at a time, and put them in her pocket, and Emeric understood that some part of her had worked out what the hour was for before the rest of her had agreed to it.

"That's Amara," she said.

"Yes," said Harrold.

"She's breathing."

"Since February."

"February." Maeve turned round and looked at her father, at the young face and the old eyes in it, and Emeric watched her put February next to the moat and the rowing boat and her father walking off round the dome with his hands in his pockets, and get the sum. "You let me row round it."

"I did."

"Twice."

"Twice," said Harrold. He sat down on the edge of the mattress, by the folded hands, as he sat every day. "I told myself it was for your safety. You go back to a palace where forty people read your letters. It was partly that." He looked at the blanket. "Mostly I didn't know how to begin, and the boat was moving."

Maeve held her hand out to Emeric without looking at him, palm up, the way a girl holds her hand out for the reins.

"Skin," he said. "It's going to feel like going down a stair in the dark. I'm in front."

"You were always in front," said Maeve. "You were always wrong about where the bottom step was."

He took her down.

She came carefully, which surprised him, and then all at once, which didn't. He felt her arrive in his room the way you feel someone come into a kitchen behind you, and felt her look at the walls and decide not to, and then he held the door open behind them and let the dome in, and let his mother in.

Maeve stood very still.

"Maeve," said Amara.

"You're dead," said Maeve. It came out quite reasonable, as if she were correcting a date.

"I was. It didn't take." His mother looked her over, unhurried, from the hat to the travelling boots, the way she'd looked over Corbin, as if checking the corners of a room she'd once known, though she had never once been in a room with this one. "You've kept your mother's shoulders. Good. He remembers them trying to hide them on you, when you were small."

"They still try." Maeve's free hand went to her collar and came away. "Father knew at the moat. Waylon knew. He knew." A tilt of the head at Emeric. "The Chancellor, I suppose, and the Secretary of Health, and the knights on the bridge, and the swans..."

"Not the swans."

"...and nobody thought I'd want to be told." She stopped. She hadn't meant to stop, Emeric thought; the sentence had simply run out of floor. "I'd have wanted to be told."

"I know," said Amara. "I told Harry so this morning, and he's been looking at his boots since breakfast. That's why you're here and not on your train." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "You're the one who pushed him in the river."

Maeve's chin came up. "He learned to swim."

"He came home with weed in his hair and wouldn't tell Harry who'd done it. Not under any threat." She tipped her head at Emeric. "I came home through him. I've seen a fair amount of what he keeps, and you're in a lot of it. A girl of six on the bank with her hands on her hips, telling a boy of fourteen to kick. You were the only one in that house who'd have bothered." His mother's voice did something then that Emeric felt rather than heard. "Thank you for bothering. After."

Maeve didn't answer that. She looked away, at the walls she'd decided not to look at, and then back, and her face had gone red across the cheekbones the way Waylon's went red to the ears, and for a second they looked so alike that Emeric had to remember they had different mothers.

"Does mine know?" she said.

"Not yet."

"Then don't you dare put it in a letter." It came out fast and certain, a woman back on ground she knew. "She'll be insulted for a year and she'll be right. She was your friend. She talked about you when nobody in the palace would say your name; she used to stop in the gallery in front of the place your picture had been. You tell her to her face, or you let her come and see for herself, and you don't write it down and put it on a boat with net twine."

"I wasn't going to," said Amara mildly.

"Good."

"And you'll keep it, in the capital. In the capital I'm a cocoon in a crypt that Sabina moved one night, and I'd like the capital to go on thinking so until Ostrel stops asking this family which rooms we sleep in."

Maeve looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed, short and not very happy.

"You've just told a woman who lies to her father by reflex to keep a secret from four hundred courtiers," she said. "I'll manage."

When Maeve's hand moved in his he brought her up.

The dome was bright and green and humming, and she was on her knees on the boards beside the mattress, and didn't seem to know it. She stayed there a little while. Then she got up and put her gloves back on, both of them, and settled them at the wrist, and looked at her father on the edge of the mattress.

"Coward," she said.

"Yes," said Harrold.

She bent and kissed him on the top of the head, quickly, as if she were stealing it, and went out.

Waylon was on the gravel with her bag, which nobody had asked him to fetch. He had straw on one sleeve. He looked at her face and then, warily, at Emeric's.

"'You'd not have believed me,'" said Maeve.

"Would you have?"

"No." She took her bag off him, and gave it back, because it was heavy and he was there. "Walk me to the station. Slowly. I want to shout at you all the way and there isn't time."

They went off over the north bridge, the two of them, Maeve talking and Waylon carrying, and the knights on the bridge found the willows very interesting. Emeric stood in the door of the dome and watched them go. Behind him, by the tree, his father had not moved, and wasn't going to for some time.



The terms came up at six.

They were hard. The whole pitch lake; a tenth on Kaldo's wells to Kesrath's harbour reeve; the high mage returned first; free seats for Duvrenne's sons at the knights' base; and no Kaldo cloth in any Duvrenne port.

Saban read them standing up at the long table, where the six and Emeric had been sitting since the bell.

"Those are the terms of a man with money," he said. "He lost his landing on the Claim. The Empire doesn't pay for losing. His chest ought to be empty, and he's writing like a man with his chest full. Somebody's paid him since."

The door opened and Waylon came in, dusty from the station, where he had put Maeve on the afternoon express. He went straight to the sideboard, as he went to every sideboard, and sat down at the end of the table with bread and cheese and his mouth full.

"What've I missed?"

"Waylon," said Ferra.

She didn't look up from her page. She said it the way she'd say the answer to a sum somebody else had stopped trying to do.

It took a minute.

Armand got there first; Emeric saw it land in his eyebrows, and then a slow, wicked delight. Saban next, with no change in his face, which was how Saban looked when something was very good. Imani laughed out loud. Bram said, "Hm," with a great deal in it. Ward, at the door, closed his eyes.

Emeric got there after Imani. He looked down the table at his brother.

Waylon had stopped chewing.

"What?" he said. Then, slowly, as the faces round the table arrived at him one after another: "No."

"I'm not asking you to," said Ferra. "I'm saying a name. She's a person, she'll have views, so will you, and nobody at this table is going to make anybody do anything." She turned her page. "But the line has another son. He's Amara's, he's got the eyes, and he's the one Randillian in this castle who has never insulted anybody."

"I've insulted loads of people."

"Horses don't count."

"There's a problem," said Saban.

"I know," said Ferra. "A princess of Duvrenne doesn't marry a king's brother with a mare and a saddle."

Emeric already had it. He'd had it since about the time Imani laughed, and he didn't like it, and he said it anyway.

"Aldane's duchy."

The room went still in a different way.

"It escheated in February," said Emeric. "It's the crown's to give. The crown's me."

"That," said Saban, "is exactly what your father would have done."

"I know."

"A title for a match. The oldest land in the north to the king's brother before the crowning ink's dry. Every old house in the kingdom will read it the way they've read it for three hundred years: the new king's the old one in a different coat."

"Saban's right," said Armand. "And there's the other thing. You sat under an apple tree once and said no more nobles."

"I know what I said."

"Then you'll know you're about to make one."

Emeric looked at the table. They were both right. He had stood on the cathedral steps yesterday thinking all of it, and had not meant this.

"It has to go to somebody," said Ferra.

Everybody looked at her.

"If it doesn't, it sits under a crown steward for ten years. Stewards collect the rent and wait to be moved somewhere better, and the tenants pay for it in the roof over their heads." She laid her pen down. "Give it to a duke who holds it under the charter. Hearing Rule, thirty-two hours, a school in every parish by a date, all written into the grant. The first duchy in the kingdom held on those terms. The old houses will read it how they like. In ten years they can read the school figures."

Saban said nothing for a while. Then he said, "Write the terms into the grant. All of them. And I'll want to watch who complains."

"I've never been to Highcairn," said Waylon, from the end of the table, in a voice like somebody who had been hit with a plank.

"Forty horses, if the water's good and there's meadow," said Ferra. "Sixty if somebody knew what they were doing."

Waylon stared at her.

"You asked me that," he said. "In May. On the tram."

"Under the arch. Yes."

"Before any of this."

"I open the crown's post," said Ferra. "He's been asking about the king's marriage in his letters since March. Very politely. Three times." She picked up her pen. "I wanted to know the answer in case anyone asked."



The mare was in the long stable behind the Gate Avenue, where Waylon had put her because nobody in Kaldo had a better one, and she was in a temper because a cart had gone past on the cobbles with something squealing in its axle.

He went in and stood with her. That was what you did. You didn't talk to them, whatever people said; you stood, and you breathed slow, and after a while they forgot to be angry because you weren't, and you could lean on them.

He leaned on her.

A duke. As of about seven o'clock he had a duchy he had seen on his father's map in brown ink and never once set foot in, with a great house at the heart of it, whose hall the old duke would keep for as long as he lived, and stables round it big enough that somebody had asked him, a month ago, how many horses it could keep. He had answered without thinking, the way he would have answered how many hands a mare stood. Forty, if the water's good.

Now he was thinking about water. Was the house on a river or a hill. You'd want the yard below the house and the wells above the yard, or you'd be drinking your own horses by August. Mares and foals on the far meadow, away from the road. A school in every parish by a date, Ferra had said. He didn't know how many parishes. He wasn't sure what a parish was for, apart from christenings and the tithe.

The mare blew at his collar.

"I know," said Waylon.

All his life he had stood a step behind Emeric: first because Emeric was older, then because Emeric was drunk and somebody had to be there when he fell over, and lately because nobody could stand beside him at all and somebody ought to be close. He had liked it there. People looked past you. Now Emeric had put a duchy in his hands like a man handing back a hat, and looked unhappy doing it, and Saban had said your father, and Emeric had said I know.

So it cost him. Waylon thought that was probably the right way round.

"She's got a good shoulder," said somebody at the stable door.

It was Alina. She'd come down from the ship on her own, in a plain grey riding coat that was Duvrenne cut and very good, and she stood in the doorway with the evening behind her and looked at the mare and not at him.

"Long in the back," said Waylon. "She'll go all day."

"And she hates the train."

"Who told you that?"

"Your brother's Chancellor, an hour ago, in a note. With a list." Alina came in and stood on the mare's other side. The mare looked round at her and let her stay. "You're three years younger. You build things badly and mend them well. You hate dinners. You've never been to the house you've been given." She put her hand on the mare's neck, under the mane, where they liked it. "She said she was sure I'd want to check every line myself."

"Did she."

"She was very clear that nobody was making anybody do anything." Alina looked at him over the mare's neck. "My father wasn't clear about anything. Her note came down to the ship at seven. He read it, and told me he would now like me to consider the king's brother, and went to have his supper."

"Are you considering him?"

"I'm looking at him." She did, frankly. "I don't remember you at all from that dinner. Were you there?"

"I don't remember. I was young for dinners. If I was there, I was the one not saying anything."

"Then you were the only one." She almost smiled. "My father thinks a marriage is a stone in a wall. He isn't wrong. I'd still like to know whether the stone's a person first."

"I'm mostly a horseman."

"I've met worse things." She scratched the mare where she liked it, and the mare leaned on her, which the mare did not do for people. "I've watched a good many marriages made across a table. Some of them came out happy. I'd still like to meet a man more than twice."

"There's time," said Waylon. "My brother asked two. The family's well ahead. We can afford to go slow."

Alina laughed, properly, the second unplanned thing he had seen her do. It was a short laugh and a bit rough, and the mare's ears went back at it and then forward again.

"Show me where the water comes into this yard," she said. "I want to see how you'd do it better."

He showed her. It took an hour. He didn't notice the hour.



The terms took weeks.

They took them at the long table with the windows open on the June heat and the town going on below: the brickyard whistle at noon, the tram bell, a fishwife in the square at five with a voice like a cannon. Alina sat across from Ferra with her own stack of paper, and they went through it a clause at a time, as two people go through a rope looking for the weak strand.

Imani sat beside Ferra with the ward's weekly book and the regrowth sheets for Danner's arm, because the treaty table was where Ferra was. Some afternoons she fell asleep against Ferra's shoulder with the pen still in her hand, and Ferra went on through the cloth duties without moving the shoulder, turning pages one-handed. Alina watched it happen the first time and said nothing. The second time she lowered her voice to argue the harbour reeve's tenth without being asked. Ferra thanked her in the passage afterwards. Alina said she had sat through enough councils to know who at a table was worth keeping awake.

The cloth was a real fight.

"You'd drown Kesrath in it," said Alina. "One of your looms does the work of nine of our women. You'd land a season's bolts on our quays at a price our weavers can't touch, and by spring you'd have a sister kingdom full of women with nothing to do but hate you."

"You'd have cheaper coats," said Ferra.

"I'd have riots in cheaper coats."

Emeric thought she was right, and said so. Ferra looked at him over the paper as if he had cut a column in half in front of her. Then she looked back at Alina.

"One in ten for five years," said Ferra, writing as she said it. "Falling to one in twenty. No cap. You buy the loom plans at cost, and Bram sends two men to Kesrath for a year to show your mills how to build them. Your weavers end up with looms. Not in the street."

Alina read it upside down. "One in eight, falling to one in twenty, and three men." Ferra said, "Done," so fast that Alina spent the evening going over the figures for the robbery, and could not find where.

The seats were a worse fight, and it was Ward's.

"We fought his levy on the Claim in December," he said, at the long table, after Duvrenne had gone back to the ship. "I'll not teach his sons the holding in the spring."

"The Church killed his sons too," said Ferra. "On their floor. Every winter. Same as ours."

"I know what the Church did." Ward looked at Emeric, not at her. "Teach Duvrenne's sons, and next year Velmoor wants seats, then Solreth, then the Empire. One place in the world makes a knight without killing him, and it's this town. Every crown that can't have it will want it burned."

"Then they train here," said Emeric. "Only here. They go home knights. They don't go home knowing how."

"Teach them," said Ward, after a while. "A Duvrenne lad's mother cries the same. I'm telling you it's a risk so you've heard it out loud, from me." He put his hand flat on the table. "Capped. Paid. Kaldo only. And I pick them."

"You pick them," said Emeric.

It went in as eleven lines in Ferra's hand: capped places at the knights' base for the sons of Duvrenne, paid by Duvrenne's crown, trained in Kaldo only, chosen by the master of the base, from the day the knights' order was published. Ward initialled it with a W that went through the paper.



Vantic found the silver on the twentieth.

She had been in Kaldo since the crowning with the two ledgers, the Empire's and her own House's, reading them in a counting room over the bank's half-built hall with the shutters shut against the heat and a glass of something cold at her elbow. Her note said, Come and look at something that will spoil your afternoon. V.

He came, and Ferra with him, which Vantic had certainly expected.

"His chest," she said. "Duvrenne's. It went in empty after the Claim. I've the Empire's payments to him in this one." She tapped the grey ledger. "Those stopped at the burning lake. Then in March, here." She tapped the other. "My own House's bills, on Kesrath. Drawn at Ostrel. In Ostrel silver. Every month since the letter went." She looked up at Emeric. "You'll remember the letter."

"I wrote it."

"You told the pope to keep off Kaldo, and he has." Vantic leaned back. "He's just been paying the man next door instead. Through my House. Which I resent more than I'd have guessed."

Ferra had the column by then. Her lips moved on the figures.

"It's the same coin," she said. "As the token off the river bank. Same weight, same mint." She looked at Emeric. "Saban said some of Kesrath's new silver was Ostrel-struck. The autumn before the war. He wrote it down."

"So the pope wanted a war on our border without his name on it," said Emeric.

"Or a king on your border who owed him," said Vantic. "Duvrenne isn't stupid. He knew whose silver it was and took it anyway. And then he came here the morning after your crowning with his daughter."

"He wasn't buying us," said Emeric slowly. "He was getting out."

"Go on."

"A king who's married into Kaldo can't be told what to do by Ostrel, or by the Empire. You can't buy family." He thought of the square beard and the short bark of a laugh. "He took their money and meant to marry his way out from under it."

"That's what I'd have done," said Vantic, "in his place, if I'd had a daughter and no conscience." She watched him a moment over the glass. "Both of them, I hear."

"Saban?"

"Maeve. In a letter from the capital, four pages, a good deal of it underlined." Vantic raised her glass an inch to Ferra, and then, a little later, to him. "It's very efficient of you."

He laughed. Ferra didn't. Ferra was looking at Vantic, and then she looked back at the column, and wrote something down.



Ward had wanted the rematch since the knights' rooms held in March. He waited until the crown was on and the terms were moving, because Ward did things in their order, and asked at the base on the twenty-fourth, after the dawn drill, in front of the whole class, so it was settled.

"Earth and gravity," he said. "And hands. Nothing that kills. No fire, and nothing off the yard. Rylan calls it."

"Wooden blades?"

"To start." Ward looked at him. "They'll not last."

By ten the fence round the big yard was three deep: the class, the knights off duty, Arthur and Mira and Bren at the corner post, the cooks in their aprons, and a crowd of boys from the school campus who had walked out on the rumour and would be in trouble for it. Imani sat on the rail by the gate with her bag at her feet.

"If either of you breaks anything," she said, "I want to be told which bone. Not shown. Told."

Ward bowed to her. So did Emeric.

Then they went to the middle of the yard, and Rylan said, "Go," in his voice that never changed, and Ward came in.

The ghosts came up off him at once. Last time, in the old yard, Ward had been a line drawn on glass: one ghost, one cut. Now there were six. Nine. A fan of grey Wards going every way, high, low, a step left, the blade turning and not turning, all equally pale. None sharpened. Ward was coming in and had not yet decided what to do when he got there, and the ghosts would tell Emeric nothing until he did.

He had been training it since the old yard. It showed.

The first cut came from the fourth ghost and Emeric took it on the blade. The second came from none of them; Ward changed his mind mid-step, like a man changing feet on a ladder, and the wood rapped Emeric's shoulder hard through the shirt.

"Touch," said Rylan.

Somebody at the fence made a noise like a kettle.

Emeric stopped looking at the ghosts, which was the hardest thing he did all morning. He watched Ward's hips instead, the old way, the way his hands had learned it in another life in a gym that smelled of tape and rubber floor, where a man had told him the shoulders lie and the hips can't. He went in low, under the blade, close, and hit Ward in the body twice with the hilt and his left fist, and felt the second land.

"Touch," said Rylan.

Ward went back two steps and put his left foot down, and the yard came up.

It came up under Emeric's heels in a ridge, a foot high, then three, and he went over backwards with it and let it take him. In the air he turned and pulled himself down, and landed beyond it with his weight doubled under him and his heels a hand's depth in the ground.

Ward tried the planted step.

He had watched Emeric do it for years. Now his stone had given him the weight for it, and he tried it in the middle of a fight, because Ward did not practise things where they couldn't hurt him. He set his right foot down hard to stop dead, pulled the earth up round it to hold him, and the yard took him to the knee.

He stood there in a hole to his knee.

Along the fence nobody breathed.

"Don't," said Ward to the whole yard, without looking up. He pushed, and the earth gave him back with a sound like a cow coming out of a bog. "Again."

They went again.

The wooden blades lasted a quarter of an hour. Ward put a pillar of earth up under Emeric's mid-cut and it split to the hilt, and Ward threw his own away rather than keep the advantage, and they went to hands. Ward grappled as knights had been taught to for three hundred years, with the knees and the weight. Twice he had Emeric down with an arm across his throat and the ground stiffening round his shoulders, and twice Emeric took his own weight off and came up like a cork. Each time it cost him, a long cold drain in the chest.

The third time Ward had him down he didn't get up. Ward had learned. He'd pinned the arm and the shoulder and was making the ground do the rest, and the ground was very heavy.

So Emeric made Ward heavier.

Not much. He couldn't afford much by then. A third again, pressed on Ward's back for three breaths. Ward grunted and his arm slipped, and Emeric rolled out and was up and three yards off before Ward had his hands under him.

They stood and breathed. Both of them were bleeding somewhere. Ward's lip was split. Emeric had grit in his teeth.

"Good," said Ward.

Then he raised the wall.

He did it at full. Emeric had never seen him do anything at full. Ward put both palms on the yard and the ground in front of him rose in one slab, earth and old drainage stone and the clay under it, pressed as it came until it rang: eight feet high, three thick, the width of the yard. The fence went back a step. Somewhere behind it was Ward, deciding.

Emeric went through it.

The way round was round, and Ward would be waiting round it. So he fell at the wall sideways, the way he fell off a roof, three paces of fall with all the pull he had left, and put his right fist out in front of him because there was nothing else to put.

Something in his hand went off.

It went before the knuckles touched, a thing in the bones he had not done: a ring laid ready under the skin, lit by the contact, and the mana packed itself round his knuckles, hard as a mallet head and soft as a glove, and took the stone. The wall broke open round his fist and he went through three feet of rammed earth in a spray of clay and came out the other side on his feet. It cost him like a big cast. His sight dimmed at the edges.

Ward was there. Of course he was there. He'd decided, and his ghost was a single sharp line, the only one, and it was already moving, and so was Emeric. Ward's left hand came down on the side of Emeric's neck, flat and hard, where the blade would have gone. Emeric's left fist went into Ward's ribs under the arm, where the plate would have been jointed.

They stood like that, both of them, with Ward's hand on his neck and his fist on Ward's ribs.

"Both," said Rylan. "Same time."

Nobody at the fence said anything. Then the boys from the campus began to cheer and could not be stopped, and the cooks joined in, and Bren beat on the corner post with the flat of his hand.

Emeric didn't look at his right hand. He knew roughly what it would look like and didn't want to know exactly.

Ward looked at it. He took Emeric's wrist and turned the hand over, palm down, in the daylight. The knuckles were split across, all four, and bleeding, and the back of the hand was already swelling. Ward pressed each knuckle with his thumb in turn. Nothing moved that shouldn't. He pressed the bones behind them, the long ones, one at a time.

"One in one," said Ward.

"I didn't do that."

"I know you didn't."

"Something in my hand did that."

"Armand told me in May." Ward let go of the wrist. "He read it in you in the sleep. Laid in with the bone." He looked at Emeric's face, and something at the corner of his split mouth moved. "I said I knew."

"You didn't know."

"I said I knew," said Ward, "which is the same thing."

Emeric thought about being furious. He hadn't the energy. He sat down on the broken wall instead. Imani came across the yard with her bag, unhurried, and took his right hand in both of hers and looked at it with great interest and no sympathy.

"Skin," she said. "And the swelling. Nothing broken." She took Ward's ribs in her hand through his shirt, without asking him either, and Ward stood for it like a horse being shod. "Two cracked. You'll strap them." She looked from one of them to the other. "A draw?"

"A draw," said Ward.

"A draw," said Emeric, and meant it this time, and Ward heard that he meant it and nodded once, and went off across the yard to have his ribs strapped, walking slightly sideways.



He could not have lit a candle by evening. He sat on the terrace at Tamsy Brook's under the pear with his right hand in a bowl of cold water, the glass doors folded back behind him, and Ferra across the table doing the cloth figures again because Alina had sent up a note.

September was in the pear.

It was on the near branch, looking down at him with one black eye, as it had every evening since he caught it in May and gave it back. It knew him and didn't trust him, which was fair. It came as far as the near branch, waited to see whether he would do anything interesting, and when he didn't, went back up.

"Don't," said Ferra, without looking up.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're drained to the bottom. Imani said not a spark."

"This is less than a spark." He was already doing it, the smallest thing he knew how to do: the door a hair wide, and the squirrel's note, which he'd had since May, small and quick and greedy, like a pin dropped in a dish. Over its eyes he laid one hazelnut, brown and fat and perfect, in the crook of the far branch where the pear leaned out over the garden wall.

September's head turned.

It went along the branch in a series of small falls, round the trunk and out along the far branch to the crook, and put its face in where the nut was.

There was no nut.

It looked under the crook, and on the other side of the branch, and down at the garden, and back at the crook, with its whole small body. Then it sat up on its haunches and looked straight down the length of the pear at him.

Emeric had been looked at by the provost of the capital, two hundred and twelve lords, and a king of Duvrenne. He had not been looked at like that.

"Oh, no," said Ferra, who had looked up after all.

September turned its back on him and sat facing the garden wall with its tail flat.

It did not come back for a week. It came down for Ferra. It came down, pointedly, for Imani, who had never given it anything and now gave it a pear core every evening specifically to watch his face. Every time Emeric came out onto the terrace it went to the far branch and sat with its back to him.

"It's in a sulk," said Tamsy, wiping the next table.

"It's a squirrel."

"It's a squirrel in a sulk," said Tamsy. "I've run a kitchen. I know a sulk."

Ferra wrote it on the envelope she kept for September, under the date: Showed it a nut that wasn't. It knows.



They signed on the ninth of July, on the Isle.

The K Amara came down the coast in two days and anchored off the strait in a flat calm, the island low and yellow on the water to the west. Emeric's hand was still yellow-green across the back. He could close it.

He went ashore first, by air, at dawn, because Rylan had asked. Rylan flew at his shoulder in his suit, low over the Claim's wells, which were pumping, and the dune drum with its lid still scorched, and the desert rubber trees in their grey ranks along the wadi where Emeric had first seen the sap run white out of a cut. Then the lake.

They came down on the north shore by the white stone, and walked it.

Rylan's line ran a quarter of a mile across the pitch lake to the south stone, an iron picket every fifty yards, the rags gone grey in six months of sun and standing out in the breeze like the tails of small animals. The pitch was cold and hard as road. Here and there a bubble came up through it and burst with a soft wet sound, and nothing else happened.

Rylan walked a pace behind and to his left, as he had walked behind Ward for most of his life. At the eighth picket he stopped, and Emeric stopped with him. There was nothing different about the eighth picket.

"Here's where the fire stopped," said Rylan. "On the eighteenth. We put it out from that side and they put it out from that, and we met here."

"Do you want it moved?" said Emeric. "In the treaty. We could. Ferra says the line's ours to draw."

Rylan looked along the pickets to the south stone. He thought about it, honestly, which Emeric had not expected.

"No," he said. "It's where we stood. It's where they stood. If you move it you've to tell somebody why." He looked down at the black. "I'd rather nobody had to be told anything about this lake again."

At the south stone an old sergeant of Duvrenne's was sitting with a pipe, waiting. He stood up when he saw who it was and looked along the line, and Rylan looked along it, and they both nodded, as they had in December. Then Emeric and Rylan went up into the air and back out over the strait.



The high mage came out to the K Amara in Kaldo's own boat, at ten, before the king of Duvrenne did, because Emeric had said he would be handed back first and not traded.

He had been five months in the ward under the castle with two knights on his door and Imani's healers on his back. He came up the side in a chair on a whip, a lean grey man with a hooked nose and long fingers, and they gave him two sticks and he stood up on them. He would walk with one by the winter, Imani said. He would never hang sixty feet over a field again.

Armand had argued against Emeric about it the night before, in the chart room, for an hour.

"He'll spend the rest of his life asking," Armand had said. "I have sat with men like him. You don't take a man's sky and not tell him what took it. It isn't done. Not in the tower, not anywhere."

"If he knows, Duvrenne knows. If Duvrenne knows, the Church knows by the spring."

"He'll guess."

"Then he'll guess," Emeric said. "He won't know. There's a difference, and Kaldo lives in it."

Armand had looked at him with his young face and his old eyes and said, "That's the kind of thing a king says," and had not meant it kindly.

Now the high mage stood on his two sticks on the market deck, and looked at Emeric, and asked it. He asked in his own tongue first and then in careful Randillian.

"What was it? On the drum. I was in the air, and then I was not. No skin. No field under me. Nothing." His long fingers moved on the sticks. "I have been a mage forty years. There is no such nothing. What was it?"

Rylan was standing at the rail. He had been at the drum that day. He had written two men's names in the detachment's book afterwards, under this man's sky.

"I'm sorry," said Emeric. "I'm not going to tell you."

The high mage looked at him, and then at Rylan, as if Rylan might. Rylan looked back with no expression at all.

"Then I will guess," said the high mage.

"Yes," said Emeric. "I expect you will."

Duvrenne's boat took him off at a quarter to eleven, sitting upright in the stern with his sticks across his knees, looking back at the hull the whole way.



The market deck was the K Amara's widest place, a long flat run of planking under an awning where in Kaldo the fruit sellers set up on feast days. The treaty table was two trestles with Ferra's green cloth over them.

The king of Duvrenne came up the side at eleven with Alina and his envoy, and sat down, and Emeric put Vantic's two ledgers in front of him before anything else.

The grey one first, open at Vantic's mark: the Empire's money to Duvrenne for the landing on the Claim, and a page on, the Empire's money to Corbin's war in the same season, through another house.

The king read it. He read it slowly, all the way down, as a man reads a will.

"They paid us both," he said.

"Both of us. For the same season."

The king closed the grey ledger with one finger. Then he looked at the second ledger, the brown one, Vantic's House's own, and did not open it.

"I know what's in that," he said.

"I thought you might."

"I took it," he said, without shame. "A king with an empty chest and an enemy who flies takes the silver that's offered." He put his hand flat on the brown cover. "I didn't know about the other. The Empire paying your brother."

"He knows," said Emeric. "He knows he was walked."

The king looked out at the island, low and yellow under the noon sun.

"So we were both bought to keep fighting," he said. "Whoever won would owe the Empire, and whoever was left would owe Ostrel." He breathed out through his beard. "Give me the pen."

Ferra had made the treaty short, because long ones were for lawyers to live in. The Isle of Shards held by both crowns: each its own end and its own wells. The pitch lake shared, on Rylan's line, the pickets to stand. The shard fields with whoever held them on the day of signing. No third crown to land without both kings' leave in writing. The reeve's tenth struck out. Then two clauses not about the Isle at all: Ward's eleven lines on the seats, and the cloth at one in eight falling to one in twenty, with three of Bram's men to Kesrath for a year.

"A condominium," said Emeric, when he'd read the first article aloud. His head gave him the word and he let it out, because it was harmless and it was the right word. "A country held by two crowns at once."

"Is that a thing where you come from?" said the king of Duvrenne.

"It's a thing now," said Ferra.

Alina was reading the second copy against Ferra's, line by line. Imani, beside Ferra, had taken Ferra's other hand under the table and was holding it on her own knee, out of sight, the whole time, without seeming to know she was doing it. Emeric knew. Alina, he was fairly sure, knew.

"Clause six," said Alina. "You've written whoever holds them on the day of signing."

"Yes."

"Which is today."

"Yes."

Alina looked at Ferra over the paper. "You've had men on the west cliffs."

"Since the winter," said Ferra. "Fennick's charges. You'll have heard the sea."

"I heard it."

"Then you know who holds them."

Alina held the look another breath, made a small mark in her margin and said, "I'll send a man to look, all the same." Emeric understood that she would, and that if Ferra's paper was a yard out anywhere, Alina's man would find the yard.

They signed at twenty past twelve. Emeric signed for Randillia slowly, because the knuckles still argued, and dated it at the top right without being looked at. Ferra and Alina witnessed for their crowns, one name under the other, in two hands that might have had the same tutor.

Then the wax, and the seals. The king of Duvrenne sat back in his chair under the awning and looked at the paper for a long time.

"My grandfather signed two of these," he said. "I'll go home and tell him."

"Is he alive?"

"No," said the king. "I'll tell him anyway."



Waylon and Alina told the family on the twenty-second, at supper, at the long table, without any ceremony at all.

They had been doing their own terms for five weeks, it turned out: in the long stable, on the lanes outside town, and for one whole day at the horse fair at Milford, where Alina beat a dealer down so far on a two-year-old that he cried. They had a list, two sheets in Alina's hand, with three lines at the bottom in Waylon's, slow and bad. The first was about the yard. The second was about the water. The third said No dinners in the first year, and Alina had initialled it.

"We'd like it at the wedding," said Alina. "Announced. Not before. My father knows; he's very pleased with himself and I'm not letting him have a feast about it." She looked down the table at Ferra. "Your seating."

"I'll do the seating," said Ferra.

"She's going to do the seating," said Imani, "for the rest of her life."

Waylon said nothing. He ate. Once, while Alina talked to Armand about the tower, he gave her a short sideways look, the one he gave a horse that had cleared a gate he hadn't been sure of; and Emeric, who had known that look since the nursery floor, put his cup down and looked at the window for a while.

Ferra laughed at ten o'clock.

She was in his room by then with Vantic's brown ledger on her knees, checking it against her own for the bank's opening at the end of the month. Imani was on the bed with the ward's book. Emeric was at the window with his hand in a bowl of cold water, because it still swelled in the evenings.

Ferra made a small sound. Then another.

He turned round. Her finger was on the column of Ostrel silver into Duvrenne's chest, and she was red to the fringe and laughing, silently, then not at all silently.

"What?"

"The pope," said Ferra. She put the pen down because she was going to drop it, and pressed her wrist to her mouth, and tried again. "The pope has spent a year's silver..." Imani was sitting up. "...buying Kaldo a sister-in-law."

Imani fell back on the bed.

It took her a long while to pick the pen up again. When she did, she wrote at the foot of the column, very neatly, Returns: one duchess, and ruled a line under it, and had to put the pen down a second time.

Out on the far branch of the pear, below the window, in the dark, September sat with its back to the castle.
