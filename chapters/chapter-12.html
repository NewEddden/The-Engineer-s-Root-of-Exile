Chapter 12 - What the Execution Cost

Imani came up to the castle on her own, which she had not done once in the whole time he'd known her.

She'd let him come down to her. She'd stood in Bram's back room and taken his map apart and made it worse and truer. She'd walked the houses herself and brought him the results in the order she'd walked them. All of that had happened on her ground, and Emeric hadn't registered that it was her ground until she was standing in his and it was obviously the wrong room for her.

"Weir Row's stopped boiling," she said.

He put the pen down. "How many houses."

"I'm not going to give you a number. You'll do something with a number." She stayed standing. "Enough that I noticed without counting. Enough that I'm here."

"Is anyone sick."

"Not yet. That's not the useful part of the question and you know it, or you will in about four days."

He did know it. The water had never worked because people believed him. It had worked because enough of them did the thing long enough for the thing to become what people did, and the ones who'd started first were the ones who'd started because Imani stood at the north well and said to. That was the whole mechanism. It had never had a second leg.

"They think it doesn't work?"

"They know it works." Imani said it the way she said everything she'd stand behind. "The Hallards buried nobody. The Corwin girl came right. Old Pell's still upright and drinking straight from the north well, and he's the exception they talk about, not the rule, and they know the difference. Nobody in that row thinks the boiling is a lie."

"Then why."

"Because it's yours."

Emeric sat with that a moment. "The boiling is water and fire. It doesn't belong to anybody."

"It belongs to whoever told them to do it, and that was me, and I did it because of you, and everybody on that row knows the order those went in." She finally sat, which he took for the concession it wasn't. "Kettle on the hob is a thing a neighbor can see through a window. You've made it a statement. A woman who boils is a woman who's decided the prince was right about the water, and this month that reads as a woman who's decided the prince was right about the baron. So she doesn't boil, or she boils after dark, which is worse, because then she's doing it and hiding it and one night she's tired and she doesn't."

"Bram told me hope's costly here. That nobody would pay for it twice."

"He told you the price." Something moved in her face that wasn't quite anger. "He didn't tell you whose purse it comes out of."

He waited.

"I told this town for five years that it was the ground. Five years of being wrong out loud in a place where being wrong out loud is most of what a healer has. Then you turned up with a map and I looked at it and I decided, on my own, that you were right, and I went and stood at that well and spent what I had left on you." She wasn't raising her voice either. He was beginning to think it ran in whatever this town was made of. "That was mine to spend. I spent it. I'd spend it again on the water, because the water's true."

"And the rest of it."

"You didn't ask me before you took a baron off that chair." She held his eyes. "I'm not saying you were wrong to. I'm saying you spent me doing it, and you never once had to decide to, because it never occurred to you that I was a thing that could be spent."

Emeric started three answers and got rid of all of them. The first was that Wexler had financed his murder, which was true and was the reason and not the proof, and Bram had already taught him what that sounded like. The second was a plan for getting the row boiling again, which she had preemptively refused by declining to give him a number. The third was an apology, and he could feel the shape of how she'd take an apology, which was as one more thing arriving from the castle for her to hold.

"What do you want me to do," he said.

"Nothing. There isn't a thing." She stood up. "That's what I came to say. You've spent four months looking for the lever on everything you've touched, and you've found it every time, and I wanted you to hear from somebody who likes you that there isn't one on this."

"There's always a lever."

"Then it's a long one and you're standing on the wrong end of it, and I'll be dead before it moves." At the door she stopped. "I'll keep telling them to boil. I've not stopped. But when they ask me why they should listen to me now, I've got nothing but the same map, and they've already seen the map."

She went back down the hill to her own ground. Emeric sat in the wrong room for a while afterward and did not write anything in the list, because there was no column for it and inventing one would have been a way of pretending he'd solved something.

Ferra had been running the barony's paper for a month by then and nobody had ever said so out loud.

It had happened the way these things happen, which is to say by accident and then by habit. She'd taken the lower ward's books because he'd asked her to and there was no one else. She'd taken the provisioning cycle after that because the burial line needed a column to sit in and somebody had to build the column. Then the quartermaster started walking his questions to Bram's house instead of the castle, because that was where the answers were, and once the quartermaster did it the mill did it, and by the third week two of the tenant farms had done it too.

Wenn had been steward of Kaldo castle for eleven years, doing the work of three men, and had come to Emeric twice in that month with the same request phrased two different ways, and Emeric had missed it both times because Wenn was too proud to say the word help.

He caught it the third time by accident, standing in the entry hall with the torch brackets still half empty, watching a thin tired man carry a question up two flights of stairs that a woman across town could have answered in four seconds.

So he fixed it, the way he fixed things.

He had the offer worked out before he got to Bram's. He'd thought about the objection too, and he'd thought about it carefully, because he had spent the last month reading his own handwriting in a list with an empty space under it and had no interest in being handed a second one. The objection was going to be that she was a smith's daughter, and it was going to be that she was a woman, and it was going to come from people who had never once been asked to justify a steward before because every steward they'd ever seen had been a Wenn.

He had answers for both. He'd built them on the ride down. They were good answers.

Ferra listened to the whole offer without touching the ledger in front of her, which he'd learned meant she was already three steps past it.

"You've thought about how it looks," she said.

"I have."

"No, you haven't." She turned the ledger a quarter turn, the way he turned a cup. "You've thought about how it looks that I'm a woman and my father swings a hammer. Which is the objection you can win, so it's the one you built for."

"Then what's the one I can't."

"My father holds the barony's only smithing contract. Exclusive, long-term, in writing, signed by you." She said it in exactly the register she'd used to read the fish-monger's bad hand off a page. "Every coin that leaves this barony for iron goes to this house. You want to put me over the column that pays it."

The room did a thing Emeric had felt precisely twice before, once at a campfire with a knife coming out behind Ward's back, and once reading a date in a discharged debt.

He'd have caught it in eleven seconds in his old life. Less. It was the first item on a list he'd written a hundred times in a different language: who signs, who's paid, and are they the same house. It was so far below his own floor that he hadn't looked at it, and the reason he hadn't looked at it was that he had stopped seeing Bram as a party to a contract somewhere in the last four months and started seeing him as the man who gave him a back room and asked no questions about it.

"You knew this a month ago."

"I knew it the day the quartermaster came to this table instead of that castle." She still hadn't touched the ledger. "I've been keeping the smithing lines in a separate book and not entering them, and I was going to tell you when you noticed, and I've been waiting a month, and I'd like it on the record that I was starting to get annoyed."

"Consider it recorded." He made himself sit with it another moment before he reached for a fix, because reaching for the fix was the reflex and the reflex was the problem. "You could refuse the smithing lines. Recuse. Someone else signs them."

"Who."

"Wenn."

"Wenn can't read a column against a delivery. That's not an insult to Wenn, it's what eleven years alone does to a man." She finally sat back. "And it's worse than nothing, because then you've got a signature on the page from a man who couldn't have checked it, and now the page lies with a name on it. I'd rather it was blank."

"Then say what works."

"Two books and two hands. I hold the barony. Somebody who isn't me and isn't my father holds the iron, and they get to see my book any hour they want without asking me first, and I don't get to see theirs." She let it sit. "And it has to be somebody the town knows. Not one of yours. If you put your own man on it, you've made an inspector out of a friend of yours and the town will read it as a curtain."

"That's not a small list of people."

"It's a list of one, and you're not going to like it, and neither is he."

Emeric worked it out about a second before she said it.

"The mill," Ferra said. "Petram's cousin has run the accounts on that mill for nine years and it's the only set of books in this barony that were clean when you got here, and I know because I checked them first, before I checked anything else, because I wanted to know what clean looked like in this town so I'd recognize it."

"He's related to a man in my jail."

"He is. And every soul in Kaldo knows he is, which means nobody can say you picked him because he'd be soft." She almost smiled, tired around the edges of it. "You wanted the objection you couldn't win. That's it. You're going to hand your iron accounts to a traitor's cousin and the town's going to hate that too, and it'll hate it for a reason that isn't about my father, and I'll take that trade every day."

"You're saying yes."

"I said exclusive the first time and I meant it. I don't do half-measures on paperwork I actually respect." She pulled the ledger back toward her at last. "Write it down. All of it, including the part where I told you and you hadn't seen it. If you leave that bit out and it comes out later, it comes out as a thing we hid."

He wrote it down that night and then sat looking at what he had written, because there was a sentence underneath it that he had not put in the book.

Every part of what they had just built rested on the page. Not on her honesty, which he did not doubt, and not on the mill clerk's, which he had no view on yet. On the page. A thing a stranger could pick up in ten years and read without needing either of them alive to explain it. Two books and two hands only worked because both books could be looked at by somebody who was neither hand, and every check he had invented since the spring was a check on a document. All of it stopped working the moment the real record lived anywhere a person had to be admitted to.

He wrote that down too, four lines, in the list where he kept things that were not problems yet. Then he went to bed and did not think about it again for a season.

Wenn took the news standing in the corridor that had once held art on both sides, and Emeric had readied himself for a proud man's fight and got something worse.

"Thank God," Wenn said.

Then he apologized for saying it, which took longer than the saying, and then he asked, carefully, whether he was to leave the castle. Emeric told him no. Household, provisions, the roof, the well in the courtyard, the fifty things that had to happen daily for the building to keep standing, and none of the ledgers. Wenn worked out what he'd been handed at roughly the speed of a man who had been drowning and had not known there was a word for it.

It was, Emeric thought afterward, the cleanest thing he'd done in a month, and it did not help at all, because Wenn's opinion of the arrangement was of no interest to anybody in Kaldo and had never been going to be.

What the town heard was that the prince had put a girl over the barony's money.

It moved the way everything moved here, which was through the wells and the market and the two streets that had anything on them. Emeric heard the polite version from Rylan and the actual one from nobody, because nobody says the actual one to the crown's man, and by the time it had been in the air a week it had acquired the shape it was going to keep. That the prince had killed the baron and installed his own. That the smith had sold him a daughter and been paid in the barony's iron for it. That there was a way to be paid by this castle now, and the way was to be liked.

None of it was true. All of it was the correct thing to think, given what could be seen from a street.

Ferra started walking to the castle every third morning, because the work needed the castle's papers and the castle couldn't come to her. Emeric noticed the change in her about six days in, and it wasn't in anything she said.

People were polite to her.

She'd lived in that town her entire life. She'd poured tea for men who came to argue with her father and had heard every one of their arguments through the kitchen wall. She'd corrected the fish-monger's threes for years. And now the fish-monger stood up when she came past his stall and called her by a title she'd had for eleven days, and did it in front of two other men so they'd see him do it.

"That's what kindly sounds like now," Emeric said, before he could think better of it.

Ferra looked at him. "Who said that."

"Corliss. About his wife, at the well."

"Then Corliss is a better bookkeeper than half this castle." She set her papers down. "It's not the worst of it. The worst of it is that they're not wrong. I do sign the column now. When Maren from the north row can't get her roof timber, the person actually standing between her and the timber is me. She used to be able to shout at nobody about it. Now she can shout at me, and she has, and she was right to, and I couldn't help her, and she'll tell the well I wouldn't."

"Wouldn't or couldn't?"

"There's no difference from the outside. That's the entire job. I've had it eleven days and I've worked out that much."

Bram said nothing about any of it, which Emeric took at first for approval and understood by the end of the second week was not that at all. He'd given his consent in that front room months ago, plainly, on the understanding that his answer wasn't the one that mattered. He wasn't going to take it back now. He was also not going to pretend he hadn't watched the price arrive.

He said one thing, once, hauling a length of pipe stock off the bench.

"You'll want to be sure it's worth it to her and not just to you." Then he went back to the pipe, and that was the whole of it, and it was worse than an argument because there was nothing in it Emeric could take apart.

He saw Sela on the eleventh day of it, from the top of the mill road.

He knew it was her before Rylan said anything, from the fact that Rylan stopped walking. She was down at the bottom by the second row of houses with a basket on her hip, talking to another woman about something that made both of them laugh, briefly, the small ugly startled laugh of people who had not planned on it.

Emeric did not go down.

In the winter he'd told himself the reason was that his face was one more thing to carry and she'd carried enough. That had been true. It was still true, and it was no longer the reason, and he made himself be exact about the difference because being inexact about it would have let him keep the cheaper version.

The reason now was that he knew who had bought her son's box.

If he went down that road he would either lie to her about it or he wouldn't. If he lied, then every kind thing he'd ever put in front of her, the coin, the three drafts, the plain register he'd been so pleased with, sat on top of a lie he was choosing while looking at her. If he told her, then a fifty-year-old man who had spent sixteen years quietly burying people this town wouldn't bury became, that afternoon, a man the whole town knew had taken from the treasury. Corliss had said it himself. Two thousand people would hear the first half. Eleven would ever hear the second.

Corliss hadn't asked him to keep it. Corliss had asked him nothing at all. That was somehow the part that settled it.

So he stood at the top of the mill road holding a true thing that belonged to a woman he could see from where he was standing, and did not carry it down to her, and understood that he had now done this twice and that the second time was not the same as the first. The first had been cowardice with a decent reason folded inside it. This one had a price on it, and the price was hers, and she would never be told what she'd paid or that she'd paid anything.

"She's well enough," Rylan said, eventually. "Since you're not going to ask."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"I know." Rylan started walking again. "That's why I said it."

He got the list out that night and looked at the empty space under the two facts from the ward, and found that it had company.

The water was true and people were letting it go because of who it had come from. Ferra was the correct appointment and it was costing her the town she'd grown up in. Imani had spent five years of her name on him and had come up the hill to tell him there was no lever, and she had been right, and he had checked.

Every one of them had the same shape. He had a method that worked on things and it had produced, in four months, a clean well, a solved garrison, a dead baron, a functioning treasury, and a town that trusted him less than it had in the autumn.

He wrote that down, because it was true and because he'd stopped letting himself off the ones that were true.

Then he pulled the field surveys across the desk, because the fields didn't care, and the fields wouldn't hold it against him, and there were two thousand acres out there that would go in whether or not anyone in Kaldo was speaking to him by spring.

Bram had told him it moved when they saw what he did next.

The farm was next. It had been next since the capital. And somewhere in the ride back from the mill road he had stopped thinking of it as the thing he owed the barony and started thinking of it as the argument he was going to make, which was, he suspected, the first genuinely political thought he'd had since he got here.

He didn't much like having had it. He wrote that down too.
