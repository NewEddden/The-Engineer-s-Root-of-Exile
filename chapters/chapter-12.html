Chapter 12 - Turned Earth

Ferra brought him the garrison before the fields were half dug, because the garrison was faster to read than dirt.

"Wexler's muster roll," she said, and laid it flat, and beside it a second sheet in her own hand that was shorter and told the truth the first one didn't. "Same disease as the debt pages. He wasn't hiding soldiers. He was hiding wages. There are men on this roll drawing a knight's pay who have never stood a knight's watch, and men drawing next to nothing who do the actual work, and the gap between the two is where his money went."

Emeric leaned over it and saw what she had done at once, because it was the thing he would have done. She had set skill against pay, duty logged against coin drawn, and let the mismatch sort the room. A soldier's wage tracks a soldier's worth, roughly, the way an honest measure tracks the thing it measures. Break that link and you get the shape of a lie, one name at a time.

"This many," Emeric said. Fewer than the roll claimed. More than he'd feared.

"That many I'd stake the books on." She tapped a smaller cluster, set apart. "These I'm less sure of. High pay, but the skill isn't low, it's only unrecorded. Could be Wexler paid them well to keep quiet about the rest. Could be the record's just thin. The number can't tell you which."

He should have stopped there. He knew that afterward, the way he knew most things afterward.

But the sort was clean, and clean was the thing his mind reached for the way a hand reaches for a rail, so he took Ferra's flagged names and started building the list of who left. Overpaid and unskilled, out. Overpaid and unclear, out until proven otherwise, because a barony this thin couldn't afford to keep paying a maybe.

It was efficient. It was defensible. Every name on it earned its place by a number he could point to.

He brought it to Ward the way he brought most finished things to Ward, expecting it to be received. Ward read it the way he read everything, once, slowly, top to bottom. Then he set a finger on a name near the middle of the cut list and didn't lift it.

"Not him."

"He's overpaid for his record. Ferra flagged him herself, the unclear pile. I'm not cutting on a guess, I'm cutting because I can't afford a guess."

"You're cutting on a number." Ward still hadn't moved his finger. "This is Declan's cousin. Osric drilled beside him for two years before any of us came here. He took a wage he didn't argue over because his mother's in the low town and the coin kept her roof on, and he never once asked what Wexler wanted for it, which is the whole reason his record's thin. He kept his head down and did the work and stayed out of the parts that got other men paid."

He finally looked up.

"The record's thin because he was clean. Your number reads clean as bought."

Emeric opened his mouth to argue the method and found the argument had nothing under it.

The logic was sound. He had built a filter to catch men who took money for nothing, and the same filter caught a man who took money and quietly gave everything and left no paper trail doing it, because a paper trail was the one thing that would have got him noticed by the wrong people. Low profile read identically to no profile. His system could not tell loyalty from absence, because loyalty, done right, looks like absence on a page.

"How many others," Emeric said, and heard how quiet it came out.

"On this list? That one I'm sure of. The rest I don't know well enough to swear to, which is its own answer." Ward lifted his finger at last. "I can't hand you a clean sort either. I'm telling you the clean sort is the problem. You want to know which of these men to keep, you're going to have to let me, Osric, Declan and Rylan stand in a yard with them for a week and watch, and you're going to have to trust four men's eyes over one good page. I know exactly what that costs you."

It cost more than he wanted to admit. He wanted to build the whole barony toward the opposite of that, toward numbers a man could check instead of judgments a man had to trust.

He had also just watched his best tool nearly throw away a loyal man for the crime of being quiet, and he had done it himself, with a clear head, in an afternoon, and he would have signed it if Ward hadn't happened to know a name.

He struck the man off the cut list. Then he struck the entire unclear pile off it and handed the whole thing to Ward, to be settled in the yard by men who knew faces, and he made himself say the part he'd rather have kept.

"I'd have cut him. On the number. And I'd have been sure I was right the whole time I was wrong."

"I know," Ward said, not unkindly. "That's what I'm for."

The men Ferra's page had caught cleanly, the overpaid and plainly idle, whose loyalty had always run to whoever signed the wage and would run off the moment the wage did, those Emeric let go without ceremony and without delay.

Some of them he suspected had been Wexler's eyes as much as his soldiers, and every day they sat in the lower ward was a day they watched. So he moved fast, and moved before the barony changed much further, so that what they carried out the gate was already going stale in the carrying.

They rode off knowing Kaldo as a plague-scarred farm town with a strange third-born prince and a half-mad plan to grow legumes on dead ground. They did not know what the fields would become, or the forge, or the men left standing in the yard behind them, because none of that had happened yet on the day they left.

Emeric noted, without yet knowing what to do with the thought, that a man carrying an outdated map is more dangerous than a man carrying none, because he thinks he knows the ground.

What was left, when the padding was gone and the quiet loyal man was back on the rolls where he belonged, was twenty. Twenty men who could actually soldier, most of them underpaid for years, all of them watching to see whether the new lord was one more mouth making promises or something they hadn't met before.

"Twenty," Ward said, looking at the yard the first morning they stood in it. "It's not many."

"It's enough to start with." Emeric was already seeing it the way he saw everything, as a system underbuilt rather than a number too small. "They've been kept like ornaments. Nobody's asked them to be good at this in years. That's not a shortage of men. That's a shortage of anyone bothering."

He set the routine himself, over Ward's shoulder and with Ward's corrections, because Emeric knew how to build a training program the way he knew how to build anything, from the outcome backward, and Ward knew the thousand things about actual soldiering that no outcome-backward plan would ever contain.

Between the two of them the twenty started drilling like men who had been reminded they were men and not furniture. It would be a while before it showed. Emeric was learning to be patient with things that only showed later.

By that same morning the laborers were moving in the fields and the barony had a noise to it again, over ground that had been dead or dying the week before. The miners went up toward the hills. The farmers started a fresh survey. The blacksmiths didn't need telling at all.

Emeric walked the farms with Farris and listened to what the crews made of what he had laid out. They wanted to know why he had split the ground into three sections of three instead of the single wide field they were used to working.

He gave it plain. A section resting while the other two carried the load wasn't a section wasted, it was next season's yield being kept back instead of spent. Farris chewed on that a moment, weighing whether a prince's idea was worth the trouble of taking seriously, then nodded like it had settled something in him.

"My father ran this land before the war took the hands to work it right," he said. "Always said the ground gets tired same as a man does. Never had numbers to prove it. Just the feel of it, after thirty years."

"He wasn't wrong. There's a reason under the feeling. Legumes put back into the soil what grain pulls out. Rotate them through and the land mends itself instead of needing a whole season lying fallow."

That carried further than the layout ever had. Farris turned it back to the rest of the crew in his own words, plainer ones, the kind that stick with men who would sooner trust a neighbor's phrasing than a prince's. By midmorning the rotation had stopped being Emeric's idea and started being a thing the farmers understood well enough to argue over among themselves, which was the whole point. A plan only he understood was a plan that died the first day he wasn't standing there to explain it again.

There was one thing about the ground that had made the whole plan possible, and Emeric had been quietly grateful for it since the first survey.

The town sat on an island, or near enough to call it one. The river came down out of the hills and split above Kaldo, one arm running wide to the west and one to the east, the two of them wrapping the town between them and closing again below it into a single current that ran on toward the coast.

It meant one road in over the upstream ford, one way out down the water, a town you could wall and hold as a single thing. It also meant water on two sides to draw from, and a fall in the land from the split down to the joining that would carry water through a trench on nothing but its own weight if a man graded it right.

He put the pump-house and the grain works on the western arm where the flow was steadier, and left the eastern arm and its faster water for the works that would want the current later. He had walked both banks twice before he let a single shovel bite.

They asked, too, about the sections he wanted left alone entirely, the strips that wouldn't grow anything at all. He told them again that he had plans for those and that it would make sense later.

He wanted to say more. To tell them about the thing he had in his head, the water network that wouldn't need a body walking the line by hand. Pipe instead of open ditch. Brass heads set along the rows to throw water over them like weather instead of letting it seep, and a single clever stone at the head of the line doing the walking so no man ever had to.

He had the drawing of it folded into a coat he wasn't wearing, worked and reworked until the shape of the thing was more certain than the season that would let him build it. He kept it behind his teeth.

He was still early with the Control Rune and still finding the edges of what it would do, and the edge he had found most recently was that every stone in that network would have to pass through his own hands first, one at a time, which turned a clever idea into an arithmetic problem he hadn't solved. Promising it now would have cost him more than saying nothing.

He went to Bram's after. The forge was busier than usual, new commissions coming in from every corner of the barony and all of them landing on the one man who had to actually make them. The spare ore was already glowing, going to tools for the mining crews first and plows for the farm second.

Emeric caught Bram in the gap between one heat and the next and put in his first real order. Plows. As many as the farm could use before planting slid behind. Bram said he'd have a pair by day's end.

He came back an hour past that, wiping soot off hands that had given up on clean. "Got your first two," he said, tipping his head at the pair cooling by the door. "And some numbers you'll want before you plan much past today."

The ore, told straight and unpolished in either direction. Enough for what the season asked. Plows, basic tools, nothing past that. Jabari's hill workings were steady in the shallow drifts, and the deep sections were their own trouble, and Bram flagged them honestly rather than pretend at an answer he didn't have.

The air went bad the further the crews pushed down. Close and foul enough that shifts were shortening on their own without a single order to that effect. Nobody was hauling much ore out of the deep because nobody could stay down there long enough to try.

Emeric put it where he had been putting most things lately. A real problem, not an emergency, worth solving properly rather than solving badly today just to feel like he had done something. "We'll get to that," he said, and meant it as a plan and not as a way of setting it down to forget.

Next morning the farmers had their plows and were turning the sectioned fields, the rotation lines going in right where he had walked them. The dirt broke easier than he had expected of land that had sat half dead for years, which told him more about the soil underneath than anyone had thought to check before now.

Turned ground wanted seed, and seed was the next place the old way ate hands the barony didn't have.

Broadcasting by fist was how it had always gone in, a sower walking the rows with a bag at his hip flinging grain in an arc. A good sower laid it even and a tired one laid it in clumps, and across nine sections there were not enough good sowers with enough unbroken days to do it before the window closed.

He had the answer half drawn already from the irrigation, because it was the same shape of problem. A thing that had to be told to spread itself evenly down a run. A cart hopper over a spinning brass disc, the seed fed down onto the disc and thrown outward in a fan as the cart rolled, and a route working cut into a plain stone to time the feed so the disc got the same measure whether the ox pulled fast or slow.

One cart and one hand could seed in a morning what a line of sowers took three days to walk.

He built it and rolled it down the first section himself, and it laid a fan of seed as even as anything a fist had ever thrown, and he was pleased with it for exactly as long as it took the near ground to come up.

When it did, it came up wrong. Not thin, not clumped, nothing a bad sower would have made. It came up in bands. A dense green stripe near the cart's line, thinning as the fan reached out, then thickening again where the next pass overlapped, a regular gradient laid down the whole run as if someone had ruled the field.

It took him a day of standing over it to see what he had done, and when he saw it he felt the particular flat annoyance of a man outwitted by his own faithfulness.

The spreader had done exactly what he built it to do. It had spread the seed evenly, perfectly, mechanically. The trouble was the seed. He had loaded it straight from the bin, and a bin holds what a bin holds, grain of every size the harvest made, plump heavy kernels and light shrivelled ones and everything between, all poured onto a spinning disc together.

A spinning disc does not throw a heavy kernel and a light one the same distance. The heavy ones flew far, out to the edge of the fan. The light ones dropped near. The disc had faithfully sorted his mixed seed by weight and laid each grade in its own band across the field, and the bands had come up as bands, because he had fed a gradeable thing into a machine that graded and it had done the one thing he asked twice over.

The machine was not wrong. The input was. He had built a thing that distributed whatever he gave it with perfect fidelity and then handed it something uneven and been surprised to get evenness of the wrong kind back.

A faithful distributor is only ever as even as the thing you pour into it, and it will spread an unevenness as diligently as anything else. Better, in fact. It will sort it cleaner and turn a hidden variation in the seed into a visible one in the field.

The fix wasn't in the disc. The disc was fine. The fix was a step before the disc that the fist-sowers had never needed, because a fist does not care what size a grain is.

He set the farmhands to running the seed through a graded sieve before it ever reached the hopper, screening it to a single grade so that what fed onto the disc was uniform, every kernel near enough the same weight to fly the same distance. Mundane work. A frame and a mesh and an afternoon, no stone in it at all, the least clever part of the whole machine and the part that made the clever part worth building.

Graded seed onto the disc, and the disc threw it even, and the next section came up in one flat unbroken green with no band anywhere in it.

He kept the by-catch too, because the sieve turned out to be a sort as well as a fix. The light shrivelled grain the mesh held back was poor seed and perfectly good feed, and the plump grain that fell through was the best of the harvest going into the best ground, which meant the same afternoon's work that saved the field also improved the seed stock.

He filed that under the small pleasures, the times a thing built to solve one problem quietly paid out on a second.

He did not tell the farmhands it had failed first. They had seen the bands. Farris had seen him stand over them for a day. There was nothing to say about it that the field hadn't already said plainly, and he had learned, slowly, that admitting a miss out loud to men who watched it happen was worth more than explaining it.

The irrigation went in as parallel runs, not one main channel. In another life he had watched a single point of failure drag down everything downstream of it more than once, and the habit had followed him here. One length clogged or collapsed would cost him a section of field, not the farm.

The trenches ran off the western arm and fell with the land toward the joining below the town, gravity doing the carrying, exactly as he had graded them. Jabari's crew, on loan for the day while the hill had a slow stretch, packed the clay lining and grumbled good-naturedly about a depth he had called for that ran deeper than plain irrigation asked. None of them pushed on it outright. They had learned that his overbuilding usually meant something, even before he said what.

The pump-house went up on the western arm that same week, over the steadiest stretch of the current, and it was the one part of the water works Emeric let himself overbuild without pretending he wasn't.

A wheel in the flow, wide and slat-bladed, turning a lift pump that raised water off the river and into a timber tank set high on the bank. Gravity carried the trenches. The tank carried the reach, the higher ground and the far corners the land's fall would never have watered on its own.

It was more than the fields strictly needed this season. He knew it while he was drawing it and built it anyway, because a water works sized to exactly today is a water works that fails the first day tomorrow asks for more.

He built the wheel to fail gently, which was a habit older than Kaldo. The river ran full now. It would not always. In a dry month the arm would drop below the blades and the wheel would sit there turning nothing, and a system that died the first dry week wasn't a system, it was a wet year's luck with a roof over it.

So the pump took a crank as well as a wheel, a plain wooden arm a man could throw by hand when the current quit. And beside the crank, off a cartwright's frame that Farris swore had held up a hay wagon in his father's time, a seat and a chain and a set of pedals, so a man with legs could do the river's work for as long as his legs held out once his arms gave.

It looked absurd, a farm cart married to a pump, and Emeric did not much care how it looked. It meant the tank filled whether the river gave or not.

Jabari's crew built it grumbling and rode it laughing. By the end of the day the absurd thing had a line of men waiting a turn on the pedals, racing to see who could keep the water climbing longest, which was more interest than the ditches had got out of anyone.

Not one of them asked what would happen the day the stone he kept fussing over went quiet and the wheel and the crank and the pedals were all the farm had. Emeric didn't raise it either. He had built the mundane bones of the thing to stand alone precisely so the question wouldn't need asking, and he took the quiet as proof he had built them right, and filed nothing at all, because there was nothing in the moment that told him to.

He set the water crystal himself, off to one side of the main trench, catching runoff into a basin he had them dig a touch larger than the job wanted. One stone, one plain release working, nothing networked, nothing that needed the Control Rune at all. A first small test of the idea before he trusted a whole line to it.

It was small enough that nobody asked after it the way they had asked about the empty lanes. Just a prince fussing over water the way he fussed over everything. He liked that it went by unremarked. Not everything needed an audience.

The array lanes went in last, staked out and left bare. Long straight gaps running the length of each field, wide enough for a full plow team to come about, walled on one side by a strip nobody was to break ground on at all. To anyone walking it, it read as waste. A farm with holes cut into it for no reason a person could name.

One of the younger farmhands finally asked outright what the empty strips were for, half joking that the prince meant to grow air out there.

Emeric let the joke stand. He gave them something nearer the truth without handing over all of it. That the space would end up catching something out of the open sky the same way the fields caught rain, and that it would make more sense once there was something standing in it to look at.

Not a lie. Not an explanation either, and he knew the difference even if nobody else in the field did yet.

The name stuck before the day was out. By the time the crews broke for evening they were calling the bare lanes the sun-collecting array, half in jest and half because nobody had offered anything better. Emeric heard it secondhand from Bram, who passed it along like he found it funnier than he was letting on.

He let the name sit. It wasn't accurate, not really, but it wasn't wrong enough to bother correcting, and correcting it would have cost him an explanation he wasn't ready to give.

By dusk the fields carried the shape of an actual plan instead of a list on a desk. Turned earth in clean rotation lines. Trenches cut and lined, waiting on water that wasn't running yet. A basin catching runoff nobody gave a second thought. Bare lanes holding open for something the barony didn't have the ore or the skill to build, not this season.

It didn't look finished. It looked, for the first time since he came to Kaldo, like something that was going to be.

Jabari found him at the edge of the last field, watching the light go orange over ground that had been dead a week ago. He had come up from the hill still wearing the day, dust worked into the creases of his hands, worn down the way a man gets after a full shift underground and a walk past the farm on top of it, when he could have gone straight home.

"Haven't seen it look like this in years," Jabari said, more to the field than to Emeric. "Ran cattle through here when I was young. Before the war took the cattle and the plague took the reason to keep trying."

Emeric had nothing useful for that, so he didn't reach for something that wasn't. He stood next to a man who had earned the right to look at a field of dirt and feel something about it.

"Ore's holding," Jabari said, once the quiet had run its length, going back to the practical the way men who had worked their whole lives tended to. "Deep sections still worry me. But what we've got covers what you need for now."

"That's all I asked for," Emeric said. "Anything more than that this early would have worried me worse than the shortage does."

Jabari came near a smile at that, the kind of near that meant something coming from him. He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to.

He turned back toward the hill to make his report, and Emeric stayed a while watching the last of the light go, over turned fields on one side of him and, back up the slope behind him, a yard where twenty men were learning to be soldiers again.
