Chapter 12 - Door to Door

He was bad at it.

Four houses in the first morning. Nobody converted. The fourth shut the door while he was still explaining the part about the wells, which he had rehearsed, and which he had cut down twice because it had been too long.

Imani did eleven in the same stretch of morning.

Bram did not knock at all. Bram stood in doorways and told men what they were going to do, and they did it, and the whole transaction took about ninety seconds and contained no information of any kind.

It took Emeric two more days to work out what he was doing wrong, which he later considered slow.

He kept explaining.

Explaining is what you do when you need to be believed. Bram did not need to be believed. Bram needed to be obeyed, and had thirty years of credit in that district, and spent it three coppers at a time without appearing to notice.

So he stopped leading.

He carried the water. He held the map when the map was wanted and kept it shut when it was not. He answered questions when he was asked one directly, which was rarely, and he let Imani talk.

It was a demotion and he gave it to himself, and it worked inside half a day.

He hated it enormously.

Rylan found this unbearably funny. Rylan found it funny on the first morning and was still finding it funny eight days later, standing at the end of a lane holding two empty buckets while a prince of Randillia was told by a woman of twenty-five to wait outside.

"Stop enjoying it."

"Can't, Your Highness."

The failures were not stupid. That was the part he had not been ready for.

He had come into it braced for superstition and got economics, over and over, from people who had done the arithmetic before he opened his mouth.

Fuel. Telling a poor household to boil its water is telling it to spend money. Three times a day, every day, in a town on a rock with no wood on it. This was the largest single obstacle in Kaldo and it had nothing to do with belief at all. Several people agreed with him entirely, said so, and did not do it.

Distance. The Mere was uphill. Hauling clean water from above the town was an hour a day, every day, on legs that already worked twelve.

The church. Doran had said nothing, either way, and in a parish silence is not neutral. Households asked what the priest thought, got nothing, and that ended the conversation politely and completely.

The crown. A few doors shut on Emeric's face before Imani could open her mouth, and there was nothing under those to argue with.

The fix was Bram's and it arrived in one sentence at the end of a bad week.

One fire per street. Not one per household.

A shared kettle, a rota, and the fuel spread across nine families instead of sitting on one. Somebody's boy fed it, somebody else's mother watched it, and the cost per house fell by a factor that made the whole thing possible where it had been impossible the previous afternoon.

Emeric had not thought of it.

He said so, out loud, in the forge district, standing in front of four men and a blacksmith, and the reason he gave was the accurate one: he had never once in his life worried about the price of firewood.

That admission did more for him in that quarter than anything he had said since he arrived, and he had not made it for that reason, and he was irritated for about a day afterward to notice how well it had worked.

The fix was also teachable, repeatable, and ran perfectly well without him, which he noted and did not mention to anybody.

The trials ran a fortnight and they ran in Imani's hand.

He had offered to keep the register himself and she had looked at him until he stopped offering. Her terms. Her book. Households that had agreed to be tested, boiled water and clean cloth on one side of the page, ordinary practice on the other, and she tracked first symptoms herself because she did not trust anybody else to know what first symptoms looked like.

The odds moved.

Not cleanly. The numbers were small and the households were not alike and a man with any training in this would have torn it apart inside five minutes, and Emeric could have done it himself and did, twice, on paper, at night.

It was not enough to convince a skeptic. It was a very great deal more than enough to convince Imani, who had been waiting five years for a number that pointed anywhere at all.

The two houses were on the same low street and he did them together on purpose.

Both households in the same doorway at the same time, so that nobody could say afterward that it had been done behind anyone's back. Draw from above the town. Boil what you drink. Share the Cooper's Row fire. Nothing else changes.

One took it.

One did not, and the reasons were entirely theirs and entirely reasonable from where they were standing. It was a long walk. Fuel costs money. And that well had been good enough for their mother, who had drunk out of it for fifty-one years and died of something else.

He offered twice and stopped.

Holding that line cost him more than anything else he did that month. He had the whole of the crown available to him in the sentence. He could have finished it in four words.

Imani had told him beforehand, flatly, on the corner, that if he pushed once then every yes she got for the next year would be a yes given to a prince rather than a yes given to the truth, and that she would have to live in this town afterward and he would not.

He had agreed with her. Agreeing with her and standing in the doorway were different activities.

Three weeks later the refusing household lost the grandfather.

The daughter went down four days after him and pulled through. Across the lane, the house that had complied had a child go down with something in the same fortnight and recover fast, and it was probably not the same illness at all.

None of it was clean. Imani would not claim it as proof and said so, in front of people, twice.

It did not matter in the slightest.

The street drew its own conclusion, which was the conclusion Emeric had wanted and had not been allowed to argue for, and by the end of that week the Cooper's Row fire was going before dawn.

He handled it badly in private.

He went over the offer sentence by sentence, in a cold room, looking for the version that would have worked. There was one. There is always one. He got as far as constructing it and then found he could not put a value on what the rest of the street would have cost.

Ward, who had watched him do this before on a road with a dead man on it, gave him nine words.

"You let it happen. You didn't do it. Different ledger."

And then refused, entirely and without discussion, to say anything further about it that night or ever.

The wins came in over the following fortnight and none of them were his.

The north well started boiling as a street rather than as households, which halved the fuel and doubled the compliance in one stroke.

Sela's remaining son went down in the fourth week. Imani sat with him three nights and he lived.

Two weeks after that, Sela was the one telling the north well what to do. She was better at it than any of them. She had a way of asking a question that did not leave the other person anywhere to stand, and nobody in Kaldo had ever argued with Sela about anything and won.

She still would not look at him.

He noted that the fix had worked and the wound had not, and that these were separate accounts, and that the second one was not open to him. He did not try to collect on it.

What came out of the parish house, eventually, was careful and small.

The prince's work is not against the faith. Boiling water harms nobody.

It was the weakest endorsement available to a man in that office and it doubled their conversion rate overnight, and Emeric understood exactly what it had cost Doran to say even that much, because Doran had not said one word to him directly since the night of the map.

He had still not asked him to move the rows.

He knew he was going to have to. He kept not doing it.

At six weeks, Wexler was waiting in the entry hall.

"Hello, Emeric."

The title was absent and the absence had been chosen, and the choosing was the entire content of the sentence. There was nothing else in it.

Emeric registered, with some precision, that he could not call it out. Bad blood with the baron in a town that would cheerfully cast a prince as the villain of any story it was offered was not a purchase he could afford this month or next.

So he gestured the old man into the side room off the hall rather than answer in it.

It was another man's house and he had not asked, and the two of them walked through the door knowing exactly what had just been done and by whom.

"Six weeks," Wexler said, sitting. "Spent counting corpses."

Emeric gave it to him flat, without performing any of it.

Water sources. Burial sites. Dates of first symptoms out of the parish book and out of the healer's own record. All of it cross-referenced against household distance from the branches and against the fall of the ground. A pattern, not a proof. A fortnight of trials on agreed households. The odds moved.

"It doesn't take without somebody they already trust. I can hand a stranger a theory and get nothing. Imani hands them the same theory and it's done by dinner."

"Bram doesn't take to strangers."

"He didn't take to me either. I gave him something worth taking to."

The disdain went.

What replaced it was the stillness of a man recalculating a threat he had priced too cheaply. Six weeks of watching a royal embarrassment wander around a dying town, expecting it to burn itself out the way everything in Kaldo burned itself out, and what had finally brought him downstairs was a woman running a rota at the north well and a blacksmith walking into the parish house with the prince in front of the whole street.

Emeric put two things to him and separated them deliberately, because he had finally learned to ask for the cheaper one first.

The latrines move downstream of the wells, or the wells get cut above the latrines, whichever is cheaper to dig. Baronial ground. Baronial money. Wexler's to grant and nobody else's.

The rows were a separate matter. He said so, and did not press them, and watched the old man notice that he had not.

The fight over the first one ran eleven days.

It was not shouted. It was conducted across a table in a side room in fragments, mornings mostly, twenty minutes at a time, between a man who had somewhere else to be and a man who was pretending he did.

And Wexler was never once wrong on a fact.

He was correct that the barony had nothing in the treasury. He was correct that new wells through that rock meant a year of work. He was correct that the men to do it were not in Kaldo, having been taken by a war and then by a sickness, in that order, and that the ones left were feeding themselves twelve hours a day.

Every objection was true. Every one of them was aimed. And Emeric spent eleven days learning a distinction he had not previously known was available, which is the difference between a man lying to you and a man handing you accurate information in the order most likely to stop you.

He did not win it with rank and he did not win it with argument.

He won it by finding the one thing Wexler could not refuse without saying something out loud that he did not want on a page.

A written order costs nothing.

Wexler did not have to find the money. He did not have to find the men, or the year. He had to sign that when the money existed, the ground was available and the permission already given.

The old man read it twice and understood precisely what he was being handed.

He signed it anyway, because refusing a hypothetical in writing is the kind of thing a man has to explain later.

"You'll never dig it."

"Probably not."

"Then what did you just spend eleven days on?"

"Permission. It keeps."

It was the first thing he had won in this place and he was happy about it the whole way up the hill.

Then he sat down with it and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was savage.

He had an order. He had a barony with nothing in it. He had a population that worked twelve hours a day for food and could not be paid in anything else. He had won, at some cost and with considerable cleverness, the right to do a thing he could not pay for.

He wrote it into Armand's notebook underneath the well entry, and the entry now read as a joke against himself, and he left it there on purpose.

The rumor started somewhere in the days after that and it did not come from Wexler.

It came from a steward's remark, a guard's cousin, a girl who cleaned in the keep and had a mother on Foulard Street. It had no origin and it arrived everywhere at once, the way the only kind of news that travels in a town like that one travels.

The version that reached the low streets was that the crown's boy had been reading the death book and meant to turn out the graves.

Emeric registered the town going colder and misread it completely.

He assumed it was the well speech catching up with him. Somebody had finally repeated the part about the dead in the ground to somebody who had family in it, and it had gone round badly, and he had said it badly in the first place. His own error was the explanation two lifetimes had trained him to reach for first and he reached for it without breaking stride.

The branches dropped through the end of that month.

The low streets began to smell worse than the thing that had met him two miles out on the road, which he had not previously believed was available.

Imani started working longer days and stopped pretending she had ever done otherwise. She also, on correct professional grounds, refused the parish-book argument to his face when he tried to lean on it in front of a household: a record of who died is not a record of what killed them, and he knew that perfectly well, and she was not going to stand there while he used it on somebody who did not.

She was right. He had nothing to show her that would move it.

Not yet.

He went back up the hill with a signed order in his coat that was worth exactly the paper, a river dropping toward the worst month of its year, and a town that had decided he was coming for the graves.
