Chapter 11 - Head Count

No writ came. Emeric had half expected one, some line of crown parchment making the arrangement official, and what arrived instead was nothing at all, which the castle steward explained with the weary patience of a man who had answered the question before.

A barony without a baron reverted to whatever crown authority was resident in it until the crown troubled itself to appoint. That was Emeric. It made him responsible for everything and lord of nothing, and it would hold exactly as long as nobody in the capital thought about Kaldo, which historically had been a very long time.

He decided he could work with that and went at the farm.

A harvest does not wait for a man to finish reorganizing the world around it. The standing crop, whatever the old fields had managed on their own while he was away at the capital, was ready now, this week, before he laid a single line of the larger plan he was carrying folded in his head.

So the first real work he did in Kaldo was not the clever work. It was getting a thin ordinary crop off the ground before the weather turned it to nothing, and it was there, doing the oldest work there was, that he learned the first of the season's lessons the expensive way.

They threshed on a beaten dirt floor behind the mill, the way the barony had threshed since before the barony had a name. Men and women swinging flails in a rhythm older than the crown, beating the grain loose from the straw one slow armful at a time.

It worked. It had always worked. It was also brutally slow and it wanted more hands than the barony could spare in the one week it all had to happen. Emeric watched it for the length of a morning and did what he always did, which was see a machine standing where a crowd of tired people stood.

The idea wasn't complicated and he didn't need magic for the bones of it. A drum set with wooden beater bars, turned by a belt off the mill's own wheel, the sheaves fed in at one end so the beaters knocked the grain loose against a screen without a human arm having to do the knocking.

Bram made him the drum in a day and a half of grumbling, iron pins in an oak body. Emeric set a plain water-stone at the axle with a single channel cut to hold the drum at a steady speed against the wheel's dips, so the beat came even whether the current surged or slacked.

He fed the first sheaves in himself because he wanted to see it, and it worked, and he let himself be pleased for most of an hour before the pile coming off the screen told him he had built the wrong thing well.

Half the grain was cracked. Not threshed. Cracked, the kernels split and crushed where the beaters had come down harder than the grain could take, and a cracked kernel would not store without spoiling and would not sow, which made it neither seed nor bread but only a faster kind of loss.

Running through the cracked half, worse in its own way, was a scatter of heads that had gone through whole and unbeaten, the grain still locked in the straw. Those sheaves had come in a little greener and damper, tougher in the stalk, and the same beat that shattered the dry ones had barely troubled them.

He stood there with a handful of the mess and understood he had made an error he had made in more than one shape.

He had set the drum to a speed. One speed, fixed, chosen because it seemed a reasonable middle and because a fixed number was a thing his mind reached for the way a hand reaches for a rail. But the grain was not one thing. It came in dry and it came in damp, brittle and tough, sheaf by sheaf, and a beat that was right for one was wrong for the next. He had built a machine that treated every armful as though it were the same armful, and it had faithfully, evenly, mechanically ruined the ones that weren't.

It was Farris who put the last of it into words, and he didn't do it to teach, only to say what was plainly true while he pulled a cracked ear apart with a thumbnail. He had threshed on that floor since before the war, more harvests than he'd bothered counting.

"Never hit it all the same," he said. "You listen to it. Dry sheaf sounds hollow, you go light. Green one fights you, you lean in. My arm knew it before I did."

He said it the way a man says water is wet. It was the whole problem and the whole answer, spoken by someone who had never once thought of it as either, because to him it had only ever been the work.

His arm knew it. That was the thing the machine didn't have and the flailing crowd did. A hand that felt the grain it was hitting and answered what it felt. The old way had never trusted a fixed number, because a person cannot help reading the real one, stroke by stroke. Emeric had taken the single advantage the old way had and engineered it out, and called the result progress because it turned faster.

So he rebuilt it, and the rebuild was the part he kept.

He left the drum and the beaters and the mill-wheel drive exactly as they were, because the bones were sound. What he changed was the stone. He cut a reader into it, a second channel that did nothing to the grain and only listened to it, sensing the load coming through the drum by the drag it put on the axle, the way a damp tough sheaf hauled at the beaters and a dry brittle one gave almost no resistance at all.

Then he tied the reader to the driver, so the stone no longer held one speed but rode the load. Easing the beat where the grain came dry, pressing it where the grain came green, moment to moment, faster than Farris's arm and without tiring the way Farris's arm did by the end of a long day. It did with a stone what Farris did with a lifetime of instinct, which was refuse to trust a number when it could read the thing itself.

He kept the manual baseline under it, because he was learning to keep the manual baseline under everything. Pull the stone out and the drum still turned off the wheel at a plain fixed speed, and a person had to stand there feeding it careful and pulling the wet sheaves aside by hand. Slower, with more cracked grain than the reader allowed, but the crop still came in.

The stone made it good. It did not make it possible. That distinction was becoming the whole of how he built.

The corrected drum ran through the rest of the threshing on a fraction of the hands the floor had wanted, clean grain off one end and straw off the other. Farris walked past it once, watched it ease off a hollow-sounding dry sheaf, and gave it a small grudging nod that Emeric valued more than most things men had said to his face.

He filed the lesson under the ones he meant to keep. Never trust a fixed number when you can read the real one.

The phrase wasn't entirely his own. It was in Armand's notebook, the slim leather thing handed across a banquet table with both hands as if it might bruise, in a cramped hand Emeric could now picture as clearly as if the page were open on the desk in front of him. A number on a page is only a guess a man made about a thing he wasn't touching at the time. The real one is whatever the thing is telling you now, if you have the wit to feel for it.

A stone did not care what the tables rated it to hold. It held what it held, that day, in that hand, and a smith who trusted the figure over the crystal in front of him was a smith who would one day pour more into it than it could pay back out. Every man who ever blew the skin off his palms did it trusting the page over the stone.

The threshing floor had only taught him the same thing in grain.

Threshing gave him clean grain and a second problem in the same motion, because a good part of what came off the drum had come in damp, and damp grain put into store was grain that heated and molded and rotted the sound grain packed around it.

The barony's answer had always been the sun and the weather and luck. Spread it on cloths and pray for a dry week, and in a bad year the luck ran out and a share of the harvest turned in the bin before winter was half through.

Emeric wasn't willing to store a season's food against the weather's mood, so he built a way to dry it that didn't ask the sky's permission. A low shed with a slatted floor, the grain spread thin across the slats, a fire stone bedded in a brick channel beneath, throwing a steady gentle heat up through the grain to carry the damp off.

He set the fire stone, lit the first batch, went to see to the drum, and did not think about the shed again until Farris came at a run to tell him it was smoking wrong.

He had made the drying stone the way he had first made the threshing one. He had set it to a heat and left it there. It held that heat faithfully, exactly as told, and kept holding it long after the grain was dry, because he had told it how hot to burn and had not told it when to stop.

The batch nearest the channel had gone past dry to scorched, brittle and split and as useless as the threshing rejects, and the shed had begun to smell of the sharp edge of grain a breath away from catching. A shed full of bone-dry grain over an unattended fire that did not know its work was finished was not a dryer. It was a granary fire that hadn't happened yet.

The threshing lesson had been about reading the load. This one was about knowing when to quit, and it was a different lesson wearing a similar coat. A fixed heat with nothing to stop it could only run one direction, toward too much, until a person happened to be standing there to end it. He had built a machine that could start and could not finish.

The fix was a target and a cutoff. He cut the drying stone a reader as he had the drum, except this one read the grain instead of the load, the moisture coming off it as it dried, and he set the stone a mark to aim for. The dryness that stored well and no dryer than that. When the grain reached the mark the reader cut the heat and held the stone cold, and the drying stopped itself, and a batch could sit finished on the slats overnight with nobody watching and no fire waiting to find the dry patch.

Below the stone, the mundane bones again. The channel drew a plain draft whether the stone lived or died, so a man could dry a batch on a low wood fire and his own judgment if it came to that. Slower, more spoiled to the weather, but not lost.

He stood in the doorway of the fixed shed with the warm smell of drying grain coming off the slats and thought that he had now been taught the same thing twice in one week by two different machines. A thing which does not read and does not stop will faithfully carry an error all the way to ruin, and the whole trick of building was to give the machine the one human thing the human hands had always brought without noticing.

Clean grain, dried to a mark. The last of the chain was keeping it, a full year, against damp and vermin and time.

Here he had a rune he had not used before, one he had been turning over since he first read it in the pouch of his mother's stones. A preservation working. A stasis that dragged decay within its field down almost to nothing, spoilage slowed to a crawl and held there, the grain inside kept not untouched by time but very nearly so, for as long as the stone fed the field.

His first thought, because it was always his first thought, was to do it big. One great sealed vault under one strong stasis field, the whole harvest inside, the barony's food held all but perfect for as long as the stone held charge. It was elegant. He drew it that evening and liked it, and then made himself do the thing he was slowly training himself to do, which was ask how it broke.

It broke two ways.

The first was the stone. One vault, one field, one stone holding it, and if that stone failed, all at once and without warning, the whole year's food began to spoil in the same hour. He had spent a week teaching a threshing drum and a drying shed to fall back to something a man could still work by hand, and here he had nearly hung the entire winter on one rock in one wall because the drawing was clean.

The second was subtler, and he only saw it because the drum and the shed had put him in the habit of looking.

Stasis held things in the state it found them. It did not clean them. Seal a bin with a damp pocket in the heart of it, or a few weevils riding in on the straw, and the field would drag the damp and the weevils down to the same near stillness as the good grain, slowing them almost to nothing and never quite to a stop. When the field came down months later the rot would take up exactly where it left off and the weevils resume exactly as hungry, turned loose into the food at the moment it was most needed.

Stasis did not defeat a problem sealed inside it. It held the problem at the door, slowed and waiting, and handed it back the instant the door opened. A machine that preserved whatever state you gave it and cleaned none of it demanded, more than any machine he had built, that you give it a clean one.

So he built the store the way Kaldo was teaching him to build. The mundane baseline did the real work and most of it. Grain genuinely dried to the mark, held in bins sealed against damp and lined and limed against vermin, kept the better part of a year on its own strength, the way good granaries had kept grain for a thousand years before anyone in Kaldo had a stasis stone to spare. That was the floor, and the floor alone would carry the barony through a winter. The stasis was a supplement laid over it, stretching the keeping of the bins meant to hold longest, buying margin, not standing as the thing the food depended on.

And he did not build one vault. He built many small bins, each one a man could walk around, sealed and stored on its own, so a failure took a bin and not the crop. Each got its own modest stone where a stone could be spared at all, and the ones without simply relied on the honest dry baseline.

He made it a standing rule, written down and given to Farris to enforce whether or not the lord was standing over it. No bin was sealed until it had been walked, checked dry to the core, and cleaned of any damp or vermin a hand could find. You never put a problem into stasis. You inspected first, because the field would keep faith with whatever you handed it, and it could not tell good from bad, only now from later.

Three machines in a week and the same lesson wearing three coats. Emeric came out the far side of the thin harvest with clean grain, dried to a mark, stored in a way that leaned on no single stone.

It was a small crop. It fed the town and left a little over, no more. But he had brought it in with a handful of the hands the old way wanted, and along the way he had learned, on grain that cost little to spoil, how to build the things a whole barony's survival would soon hang on. He preferred to learn expensive lessons on cheap grain. He was not always going to get the choice.

With the old crop in, he turned to the larger thing, which had never been the standing harvest at all but the redesign of the entire farm around the season to come.

Planning didn't start at a desk with him guessing what a crew needed. It started with the right people in a room. So he went to Bram first and asked him to gather everyone who would have a hand in the work, as soon as it could be done.

Then the castle, where the office was his now in the provisional way that everything was his now. Not on loan. Not held for anybody else's benefit, and not granted either, and the weight of that settled somewhere behind his sternum and stayed.

He sat and made a list. Not the old one, the cold-room list of things wrong with Kaldo, which had gone from seven items to ten and had not gotten smaller since. A working list. The kind he used to build before a review, back when a review meant something different than it did now.

Labor. Head count against whatever the ledger claimed the barony had. Farmers, miners, smiths, split by trade and checked against who could actually stand in a field tomorrow rather than who happened to be written down.

Land. Fields cut for rotation, nine divisions at the least, the fallow cycle built in from the start instead of bolted on after the soil quit. Layout fixed now, before a shovel touched dirt. Moving a ditch after you've dug it is a different job than digging it right the first time.

Water. Irrigation lines, clay-lined and hand-dug, no metal spent on anything that didn't need it yet. Grading mattered more than material this early, but grading only ever carried water downhill, and some of the ground he meant to work sat above the river's easy reach. That wanted a lift. A wheel in the current, a pump, a tank set high enough to hold a head of water against the dry weeks, all of it built out of wood and the river's own weight so none of it leaned on a crystal to run.

Materials. Ore, honest numbers only, enough for what the farm needed this season and nothing dressed up to sound like more. Clay, wood, stone for everything that didn't strictly demand iron.

Storage and processing. This one he could finally write with something under it instead of a promise. The drum ran and read its own load, the shed knew when to stop, the grain went into small sealed bins no single failure could empty at once. It was built for a thin crop and a small town, and the season coming would ask it to handle many times that, which meant more drums, more sheds, more bins. The same design multiplied rather than a new one invented. He preferred a problem that only got bigger. The ones that got stranger were the ones that cost him.

Reserved. Space in the layout he wasn't ready to account for. Grading a little deeper than it needed to be. Rows set a little wider than rotation called for. Nobody would ask why today. Somebody would ask eventually, and he meant to have a true answer ready instead of an excuse.

Six items. He looked at the page longer than six items warranted, the way he always had, trusting a plan a little more once he had seen it laid down in ink. Then he added a seventh in a different hand, because it belonged to a different part of the barony and he wanted it to look different on the page.

Garrison. Wexler's men, now his. A count against the payroll, same as the townsfolk, and the same question underneath it. Who was real and who had only ever been written down.

He knew almost nothing about most of them beyond the fact that a dead traitor had paid their wages, and that was exactly the problem. He wrote a note to run the whole roster through Ferra once the farm figures firmed, because whatever Wexler had done to the barony's books he had almost certainly done to its muster rolls too, and payroll was the one place a lie like that couldn't entirely hide.

He added a second note to put the farm wages through her as well. She had earned that with a ledger already, and he wasn't about to keep a payroll in his own head when somebody better at it sat one building over.

The meeting filled a room built for fewer people than it held.

Bram's forge crew took one wall, arms crossed, soot still under their nails from a job they had left half done to be there. Jabari stood off from his own men in the manner of someone used to being blamed first and asked last. He had kept a crew on the old hill workings through the plague years on nothing but stubbornness, hauling thin ore out of half-collapsed drifts because the alternative was no ore at all, and he watched Emeric with the flat patience of a man who had sat through more noble promises than he cared to count.

The farmers hung loosest of the three. More of them than Emeric had expected and somehow fewer at the same time. He filed the wrongness of that without yet knowing why it snagged.

Bram kept the introductions short. Nobody in Kaldo had patience for a speech and Bram knew it better than anyone in the room. "This is the prince." He said it as though it closed a subject rather than opening a hundred. "He's got work. Paying work. Figured you'd rather hear it from him than from me."

Emeric had the numbers set before he opened his mouth. Nine fields for rotation, a rough count of hands per section, the ratio of farmers to labor hours he needed to clear before the season turned. He gave it plain. No court language. Fair wage, paid on time, work that would look strange before it looked useful.

He watched the room while he talked, the way he did now, cataloguing faces because he had long since stopped trusting himself to read them. The doubt sat plain on most of them. Arms crossed, weight back on the heels. A prince talking crop rotation like a man who had turned soil himself didn't reconcile easily for people who had spent a decade watching the old baron's promises dry up the moment the ink did.

It helped more than he had expected that half of them had spent the week watching a strange drum thresh their own thin crop on a fraction of the hands it should have wanted, and had seen the lord pull it apart and rebuild it in the open when it came up wrong rather than pretend it hadn't.

Bram caught the doubt and stepped in without being asked. "I've worked with him," he said. "Pays what he says he'll pay. Won't waste your time telling you something's fine when it isn't."

Short. No polish on it. The kind of word that lands harder for being brief.

It was enough to tip the room. Not to eagerness, not yet, but to a wary willingness to be shown, which was the only opening Emeric had ever actually needed.

He worked through the farmers' questions about field division and Jabari's flatter ones about ore. How much, for what, when he would see it come back as tools instead of talk. Emeric answered straight. Enough for what the farm needed now, not enough to pretend Kaldo was suddenly rich, and no intention of pretending.

Jabari's face didn't soften exactly, but something in his shoulders let go a fraction. There is a particular relief in being handed a true small number instead of a false large one, and Jabari had clearly been handed the second kind before.

It was somewhere in the middle of walking the farmers through the rotation that the head count nagged hard enough to make him stop and do it. He ran the room against the figure he had pulled from the barony's official ledger that morning, the same figure Bram would have used to guess how many chairs to drag in.

It didn't match. Not by a little.

He said nothing about it there. He set it beside the other thing that hadn't added up since he arrived and made a note to put it to Ferra. Old rot in the records, or something newer and worth worrying over.

By the time the room emptied he had commitments from all three groups, wages agreed, a rough date to break ground, and a promise to himself to walk the fields on his own two feet before he settled where anything got dug, built, or left conspicuously and deliberately empty for later.

He went back to the office to sort the next day, and had barely sat before the other thought caught him up.

He had nearly forgotten the rest of what his father pressed on him at the banquet, a pouch of uncut stones he had dropped into a trunk and not looked at since. He fetched it and tipped it out across the desk.

Mostly flat stones, plain, no affinity, the sort a tutor used to teach a child the basics before letting them near anything that did real work. A few water-tuned pieces among them. One or two that read as fire the second he lifted them, a warmth under the skin with nothing to do with the room, the same pieces he had bedded under the drying shed that week.

He couldn't have said why he expected the Control Rune to matter here. A feeling, only, the kind he had learned not to wave off even when he couldn't defend it yet. He set one of each stone beside it and reached for the delving space.

It came easier than it had any right to. The shallow way, the waking way he had been drilling since the back room at Bram's. Not instant, but the gap between deciding to go under and being there had shrunk to almost nothing.

The space still wore the shape of his office. Same desk, same walls. The only tell that he wasn't fully under was the stones on the desk registering the way nearby devices used to surface on a screen back home. Present. Listed. Waiting to be asked.

He had the rest of the day. He decided to test the thing rather than assume it.

The water stone answered first and fastest. He didn't carve anything onto it. He didn't touch it at all. The Control Rune simply addressed it, the way a name gets an answer without a hand needing to move the thing it names. He set a release timing from the Control Rune's side and the water stone obeyed at once, with no separate instruction cut into its own surface.

That settled what he had half guessed in the office that afternoon. He would not need ten tuned crystals strung along a ditch. He would need one Control Rune doing the addressing and a row of dumb, uncarved water stones doing exactly what they were told.

And the dumb ones were the part that made the whole thing affordable. He didn't need tuned pieces or good ones. He needed plain water-stones by the basketful, and there was a place a man could get plain water-stones by the basketful.

The river below the town ran its bed over gravel that was half common stone and half the low, dull, barely-charged kind the trade didn't bother to cut. Too weak to be worth a carver's time and exactly strong enough to hold a single instruction told to it from somewhere smarter. A boy with a sieve could pull a season's worth out of the shallows in an afternoon.

The clever stone was his mother's, and there was only the one he had tested so far. The obedient ones cost nothing and lay in the riverbed by the thousand.

He filed that harder than he had filed anything all week, and then he found the price, which he did not much like.

A stone he had never handled did not answer. He tried it twice on a water-tuned piece still sitting in the pouch, unopened, three feet from his hand, and got nothing back at all. It wasn't distance. It was that the Control Rune addressed things it had been shown, and being shown meant the stone passing through his own fingers while he was under, one at a time, however briefly.

Which meant a hundred stones in a ditch was a hundred stones through his hands. Not a great labor for a hundred. A considerable one for a thousand, and an impossible one for the barony he had drawn on paper, and every one of them requiring the one resource he had sworn not to build anything on, which was himself standing in the middle of it.

He wrote that down under the discovery rather than beside it, because it wasn't a footnote. The cheapest solution he had found in this world had quietly handed him back the leash he had spent a month arguing his way out of.

Whether the registering could be done by somebody else, or taught, or done once to a stone that then vouched for others, were three questions he had no way to test alone, and he set them aside for a man who had spent forty years in a tower being difficult to surprise.

The flat stones answered too, though there was less to ask of them. No affinity meant nothing to direct past a simple release of charge. Useful anyway, and proof the principle held for the cheapest substrate he owned and not only the specialty pieces.

The fire stone answered slower and the line to it felt thinner, less sure. He couldn't yet say whether that was the stone, the distance between two signatures that didn't match, or only him asking a fire question in a vocabulary that was mostly water. He filed it. Not urgent tonight.

He left the black flecked stones where they were, still bedded in the axle housings of the carriage that had carried him out of the capital. Four of them, and no more where those had come from, and whatever the Control Rune could or couldn't do to a gravity stone was not a thing to learn by accident under a vehicle he still needed.

By the time he surfaced the morning's plan had changed shape, and not in any way that stung to admit. One stone addressing a network instead of ten each carrying its own copy of the same order. The ditches wouldn't need a man walking the line to check each length. They would need the Control Rune at the head of it and everything downstream simply doing as it was told.

He set that beside the seventh item on his list, the one in the different hand, and thought that a barony was really only ever two problems wearing different clothes. A great many pieces that had to be told what to do, and the hard narrow question of which of them could be trusted to do it.

The water stones were easy. They had no loyalty to be wrong about. The men in his lower ward were the other kind, and Ferra was going to have to read them the way she read a scraped ledger page, by what the numbers did when nobody was arranging them to lie.
