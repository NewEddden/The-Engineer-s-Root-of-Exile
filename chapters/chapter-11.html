Chapter 11 - The Map

He went to the forge first, because the order mattered.

If he collected the healer first and brought her to the blacksmith, he was a man assembling people. If he collected the blacksmith first, he was a man taking advice. The difference was invisible and it was the whole thing.

Bram was already suspicious before he got the door open.

"I said I'd walk you to the priest. I didn't say I'd join a committee."

"There's no committee."

"There's a healer standing outside my door."

"There's no committee yet."

Imani had come because he had told her, in one sentence at her door, that he had a way to find the ones she could not count. She had not asked what the way was. She had put her coat on.

"You're getting an afternoon," she said. "I'm billing you for it."

She was not entirely joking. He had the strong impression that she kept the account in her head, and that nothing entered in it was ever forgotten.

Rylan was in uniform and carrying the writing box, which made him look official, and which made the other two treat him like a piece of furniture that had been brought along in case anyone needed to put something down.

He let them. He had grown up here. He knew what the uniform bought and what it cost.

Halfway up the street Bram spoke without looking up from the cobbles.

"Your big one's been on the tannery roofline a quarter of an hour. He was at my shop all six hours the other day, up on the rope walk. Are we going to keep pretending, or shall I stop noticing."

Emeric took about four seconds.

The tannery roofline ran along the top of the south road. It overlooked, among a great many other things, the alley narrow enough that he had turned sideways past a stacked cart, and stopped walking, and told Rylan it was nothing.

So the thing that had been sitting behind his eyes since the second day was six foot six and on his own payroll.

The relief was immediate and physical and he allowed himself the whole of it. It was also, though he did not examine this, the problem. Two points do not make a line. But a man who has just explained one of them away has no appetite left for the other two, and the other two, the hall in the capital and the treeline on the fifth night, went quietly into a drawer. The gallery above the kitchens went in after them, and the long wood on the last day of the road, though neither had felt like the others. He shut the drawer and did not look at why he was so glad to.

"He's meant to be resting the arm," Emeric said.

"He's meant to be resting the arm on a roof, apparently."

Imani did not turn around. "He'll come when it goes red."



The parish house sat high on the south-east shoulder of the inner ward, beside the church, and smelled of damp stone and old candle.

Doran was in his fifties and had the specific exhaustion of a man who performs ceremony for a living, which is the exhaustion of doing a demanding thing on a schedule set by other people's grief. He produced wine. It was terrible. He apologized for it twice, once when he poured it and once, some minutes later, when nobody had drunk any.

Bram did his four sentences and none of them were flattering.

"He stood at my back six hours with work in my hands and didn't open his mouth once. Most men of his sort would have sent a boy. I'm not telling you he's good and I'm not telling you what he wants, because I don't know either." He found a chair and put himself in it. "Make your own mind up."

Then he stopped talking, because that was the whole of what he had promised.

It was enough.

Doran gave up the book at once, and not reluctantly, and the speed of it knocked Emeric off a footing he had spent a week building. He had prepared three arguments. He had prepared a fourth in case the first three insulted the man. He had prepared for a refusal he intended to accept gracefully so that he could come back in a month.

The priest went and fetched it himself, and came back pleased.

Nobody had ever asked to read it.

That was the thing Emeric had not accounted for. Doran had kept a record for twenty-six years, by hand, in a town where nothing else was written down at all, and in all that time not one living person had come to the door and asked what was in it.

He talked about it the way a man talks about people rather than data. The name. The day. What he had understood the cause to be, in his own words and not a physician's. And for the plague years, where they lay, because the families had needed to be able to find them afterward and there had been no stones for a long time.

"You wrote the ground down," Emeric said.

"They asked. I wrote what they asked."

Emeric asked for the second thing while the book was still open, because he had learned that much.

A map of the barony.

Doran said there was a tithe map. Decades old, drawn by somebody's clerk to record which household owed what, which is why it had never been of the slightest use to anyone since the tithe stopped being collected in anything but grain.

Emeric went very still.

A record of every dwelling in Kaldo, with its position marked, made for reasons that had nothing to do with anything and which no one had thought about in thirty years. The only property survey in the barony. Sitting in a cupboard in a church because it had been filed as an accounts problem.

Imani noticed the stillness. She did not say anything about it. She did not forget it either.

He cleared the table and drew the town fresh.

The tithe map was for ownership. He needed slope, and ownership does not care about slope. So he drew it himself, from memory, and found out while he was doing it how much of the last week he had spent walking.

The circle of the wall, with the moat ring outside it and the one gate in the south. The inner ward rising in the middle, and the canal ring round the foot of the keep. The river down the whole east side, and the stone bridge over it. The aqueduct coming in on its arches from the Mere, and the place where it stopped short because a span was down. The churchyard high on the south-east shoulder of the ward, with the rows below it. Every well he had passed, and he had passed all of them, because he had made a point of it on the second day without admitting to himself why. The latrine ground. Then, roughly, the fall of the land from the keep down to the low south-east quarter, where the ground bottomed out against the wall.

Doran watched a prince draw his parish from memory and said nothing at all.



The plotting took four hours and it was not grim the whole way, which surprised everybody in the room.

Doran read. Name, street, year. Emeric marked. And Imani, who had not been asked to do anything and could not possibly have stopped herself, said who they were.

She had known almost all of them. That was the part that took the longest and the part nobody wanted to shorten.

Once, early in the plague pages, Doran read a name and Imani did not say who she was. Bram had been working sums under his breath at the end of the table, the way he did at the forge, and he stopped. Doran read the next name. Imani told them who he was, and after a while Bram started again.

Somewhere in the second hour she and Bram got into a dispute about a man on Cooper's Row, on the question of whether he had died of the sickness or of being ninety. Bram held that the man had been dying steadily since before the war and had simply arrived. Imani held that a person can be ninety and still be killed by something, and that Bram had not been in the room. Bram pointed out that neither had she, she had been three streets away delivering a Tarrow, and Imani said that she had been three streets away delivering a Tarrow and then she had come, which was the difference between them.

Doran ruled on it like a magistrate, for the sickness, on the entry in his own hand.

Bram accepted the ruling and made it extremely clear that he was accepting it under protest.

Everyone was briefly and guiltily cheerful for about ten minutes, and then Rylan, marking a sheet, stopped.

He had found a boy he had played with. Two streets from his mother's house. Dead in the plague's first year, while one of them was in the capital drawing a guard's wage and the other one was not, and nobody had written to tell him.

Bram went past him to fetch more candles, put a hand on his shoulder on the way, and did not say anything about it at all.

The shape was visible well before they finished, and the first person to see it was not Emeric.

Imani stopped in the middle of a sentence and leaned across the table, a twist of dark hair coming loose from the knot at the back of her head, and put one finger down on the two lowest wells.

"There."

Her other hand had gone down on the map beside his to take her weight, and the edge of it was against his knuckles. She was looking at the wells. He lifted the pen clear of the paper, so as not to mark her, and looked where she was pointing.

The high streets were nearly clean. Two or three dots each since the rows began, in a town that had buried four hundred people it could account for. The streets under the rows were black.

And it was not a smear. That was the thing. It had edges. The densest ground was a wedge running from the rows down to the two wells beneath them, and it stopped where the rise of the inner ward turned the drainage, and it stopped hard.

The older pages said the same thing more quietly. Before the rows, the low streets had always buried a few more than the high ones, which is what low streets do. After the rows, it was a wedge.

Doran said the obvious thing, and he was entitled to.

"The poor live low. And the poor die."

Emeric said, out loud, that this was a fair objection and that it was the first thing he would have said himself, which cost him nothing.

Then he put his finger on Foulard Street.

Poor as anything in Kaldo. Low. Wet. Four dots in four years. It sat near the west wall, on the far side of the inner ward from the rows, and its well drew from ground that had nothing buried above it, for no better reason than that the dead had been carried the other way.

"Poverty doesn't do that," he said. "Water does."

Imani had gone quiet some minutes before. Now she reached for the pen, and stopped, and pushed a clean sheet across the table to him instead.

"Write these down. Nobody else ever has."

The book held the ones the church had buried, which meant the ones somebody had paid for. Nobody had entered the others, and nobody had said a word over them either, because the rite cost money too. She had washed most of them herself, for the box somebody bought, before somebody dug them in. She did not say who. He did not ask. Across the table Doran did not look up from his own hand.

She had been carrying them for four years in the only place she had to put them, and it took another hour.

The room went quiet listening to a woman recite the dead of a town out of the inside of her own head. Street, house, month, and when she could not be certain of the month she said so, and he put the dot in anyway with a small ring against it, because she was not going to make the pattern prettier than the truth was.

Ninety-odd, that she could place. More that she could not.

He wrote down every one of them, with a name where she had one. When she was done he folded the sheet into the back of Armand's notebook, by itself. It was the first list of them that had ever existed.

Her dots did not scatter the shape. They deepened it.

Almost all of them fell inside the same wedge, and the edges got harder rather than softer.

Emeric put the pen down and gave her the credit in a room where it would travel.

"I had a guess and a third of the writing. She had all of the writing and the wrong name for the thing. Anybody who tells this back to somebody else tells it that way."

It cost him nothing. It was also true. He was aware of both of those at the same moment and did not enjoy being aware of it.

Bram looked at him for slightly longer than the sentence needed. Imani did not look up at all. Something in her jaw changed, slightly, and stayed changed.

Then he explained the mechanism, and he did not lead with creatures too small to see.

She had handed him the better opening on her own doorstep four days earlier. It comes up off the ground where the water sits. She was right about the ground. She was right about the water. She had the direction backwards.

It did not come up. It went down. Through the soil, from the rows and from the latrines, into the wells sunk beneath them, and then into people, and out of people, and back into the ground, and round again. Four years of a town drinking its own dead.

Then the small version of the rest of it. It was alive. It was too small to see. It travelled in water and not in air.

Bram asked how he knew.

Emeric said he did not. Not the way he wanted to. Everything on that table was a pattern and a pattern is not a proof, and if any of them wanted to hold him to it they should hold him to it. What he could do was make it into a proof, if they gave him a month and some households willing to try it.



Doran raised the other problem, and there was nothing hostile in it.

"If it is a creature and not a judgment," he said, "then what were we praying about."

Emeric had an answer ready and heard how it sounded before it got out of him. What he said instead was that he did not know, and that it was not his subject, and that he was not going to pretend it was in order to win an afternoon.

Doran accepted that, and it was the first thing all night that he had looked at Emeric properly over.

The last piece landed about twenty minutes later, and nobody said it.

Doran was reading his own hand, working back into the plague years, and he came to the entries where the rows begin.

He stopped.

He had put them there. The road had been shut, and the villages outside the wall had been burying their own and would not have opened their ground for anybody. Bodies had been stacking in a town with one gate and he had made a decision, and the decision had been the churchyard, and then the ground beside the churchyard, and then wherever there was ground.

He had been right. There had been no other thing available to any man standing where he was standing.

He did not say one word of it.

He went grey in the face, and put the book down carefully, as though it had become heavier, and asked very politely whether they might continue tomorrow.

Emeric watched it happen and understood the whole of it and did not reach for it, because the man had just been handed the worst thing a priest can be handed about his own parish, and the worst possible person in the world to hear about it from was a prince.

Bram saw it too.

Bram stayed behind.

What he said to Doran in that room is not written down anywhere.



Outside, in the dark, Imani was already moving.

She was not waiting a month for trials. She had been telling people to keep off wet ground for four years and getting nowhere with it, and now she had a sheet of paper with a shape on it that she could put a finger on.

Emeric warned her it would not take from him. He was the crown. It would not take.

"I'm aware," she said. "Nobody said anything about you coming."

Bram caught them up at the corner with the candles still in his hand.

"Forge district's mine. Every man in it owes me work."

"Then we start tomorrow. Door to door."

"All of them?"

"You wanted to fix a town."

Somewhere behind them Ward did not say anything at all.

They were going to go out in the morning and tell three thousand people that the ground they had buried their families in was killing them.
