Chapter 11 - The Roster

The gate guard knew who he was and opened the gate. That was the whole of it. No one came down from the wall, no one ran ahead to the castle, and the two men repairing a cart at the corner of the first street stopped talking when the horses passed and started again after.

Emeric had expected something. He'd spent most of the ride refusing to name what, which was its own kind of admission.

Word had beaten them home by four days. A crown dispatch reaches a castle first, but a castle in a town this size is not a sealed room, and by the time Emeric rode through the gate everyone in Kaldo had already been handed the same short version. The prince came. The prince read the baron's books. The baron went to the capital and was condemned there.

Rylan peeled off toward his own street without being told, and Emeric noticed that he didn't look up at the windows the way he had the first time they'd walked it.

He went to the forge before he went to the castle, which was probably a mistake and was certainly a habit.

Bram let him wait through the finish of a hinge, then set the piece down and wiped his hands and said, "You want the polite version or the one people are actually saying."

"The second one."

"That the crown came to finish what it started." Bram said it flat, no weight on it, the way a man reads out a bill. "War took the men. Plague took the rest. Healer wrote to the capital three times and got nothing back for it. Whatever Wexler was, he was here, and he'd been here a long time, and nothing worse ever came down that road while he was sitting in that chair. Then you show up, and inside a season there's a sentence on him."

"He financed an attempt on my life."

"I know that." Bram picked the hinge back up, turned it, set it down again. "You know how that sounds coming out of a prince's mouth in a town that lost four hundred men to a levy? It sounds like the reason. Not the proof."

That was the part Emeric had already worked out on the road and hadn't wanted to be right about. He had a coin trail, a route, a seal, and a judiciary ruling with Keller's name at the bottom of it. He could nail all of it to the church door and it would change nothing, because the argument in the town wasn't about whether Wexler had done it. It was about whether the crown got to be the one who decided.

"Not everyone," Bram added, because he was fair before he was kind. "Weir Row's boiling their water and half of them know why. That's not nothing. But it's not most, and it doesn't hold up against a baron waiting on a rope."

"How long does that take to move?"

"Longer than you'll like." Bram went back to the forge. "It moves when they see what you do next. Not when you explain what you did last."

What he did next was the farm. That had been decided before the capital and the ruling hadn't changed it. The fields wanted turning, and the fields would not wait on anyone's opinion of him.

The dispatch came on the third day. One line, sentence carried out, and nothing under it. He read it twice and put it in the drawer with the other things that had finished without closing.

Ferra brought him the lower ward's books the same afternoon, stacked in the order he'd asked for and not the order they'd been kept in. Muster roll. Pay list. The quartermaster's column. Provisioning draws going back six years, which was as far back as anyone had bothered to keep them.

"The roll says seventy-one," she said. "Ward counted fifty-eight men in the ward."

"Wexler drawing pay on the difference?"

"No." She'd clearly checked that first, which he appreciated. "The roll was never corrected after the plague. He drew for fifty-eight and let the paper stay wrong. It's the cleanest thing in his accounts."

Emeric filed that somewhere uncomfortable and didn't look at it again.

He worked the pay list for two days. The crystal stayed in his hand for most of it, the shallow read, awake, the one that let him hold six years of small entries at once and turn them without setting any of them down. He sorted by date instead of by category, the way he had with the discharged debts, because dates were the only thing in the whole barony that couldn't be argued with.

The pay itself was boring, which was the correct outcome. Fifty-eight men, quarterly, no anomalies worth the name. Wexler had bought loyalty the way a man buys grain, in bulk and on schedule.

Then the provisioning column.

Small draws. Irregular. Never the same amount twice, never large enough to require a second signature, spread thin across six years the way water spreads across a flat floor. One hand signed for all of them.

Corliss.

He didn't jump at it. He took two days over a page he could have decided in an hour, and he wanted the record to show as much. He checked the quantities against the provisioning cycle and they didn't match it. He checked whether another man could have signed under the name, and Ferra said no, one hand, no scraping, no second ink, which was the tell that had made Wexler's pages sing and made these ones quiet. He checked whether the draws stopped when the garrison was under strength and they didn't. He checked whether they clustered around anything.

They clustered around three things.

One draw fell inside the week of the road ambush. One fell within four days of a date he'd taken off Cobb's route schedule and had not been able to forget since. And one was dated the morning after Tam was found on the mill road.

He read that line six times. It did not come apart on the sixth pass any more than it had on the first.

"It's a pattern," Ferra said. "It isn't a reason."

"It's three coincidences with a shape."

"That's what I said. In your words." She didn't move to take the page back. "You've got a man drawing coin he doesn't account for, on days that matter. That's worth asking him about. It isn't worth being sure about."

"If he's bought, and he knows I've got the books, he has one night to move." He was already standing. "I'm not giving him the night."

He had Corliss taken off the duty roster at midday, in the lower ward, while the garrison was drilling. It was fast because fast was the point, and it was public because there was no version of pulling a man off a roster of fifty-eight that wasn't. Two of his own men walked him to a room off the guardhouse and stood outside it. Nobody drew anything. Nobody had to.

Fifty-seven men went back to drilling and every one of them had watched the crown's man take one of theirs out of the line.

Ward came to him that evening with Rylan.

He knew from the shape of the two of them in the doorway that it wasn't a report. Rylan stood the way a man stands when he's been asked to be present rather than to speak.

"Corliss," Ward said.

"Six years of undocumented draws out of provisioning. Three of them dated inside a week of the ambush, the route, and Tam." Emeric turned the sheet so it faced them. "That's not a hunch. That's the same method that got Wexler."

"He's my cousin," Rylan said. "Nessa's side. Been in Kaldo three generations same as her."

Emeric took a moment with that, then set it aside, because setting things aside was what he was good at. "Then you can't be objective about him."

"No," Ward said. "Neither can you."

"I've got paper."

"You've got paper on one man out of fifty-eight." Ward didn't raise his voice. He never did, and it was always worse for it. "I'm telling you no."

The room was quiet for a second longer than it should have been.

"Say the rest of it."

"Wexler's coin is in every man down there. All of them took it. That's what a garrison is." Ward put two fingers on the edge of the desk, not on the page. "The ones who took it and would still die on that gate, and the ones who took it and would open it. Your column doesn't separate them. The money looks the same going into both hands."

"Then give me a better instrument."

"Four of us move into the barracks. Me, Rylan, Declan, Osric. A week. We eat what they eat, we drill when they drill, we sleep in the same room and we don't wear your colors." Ward straightened. "At the end of it I tell you which ones I'd stand next to."

Emeric argued.

He argued that it was slow, and that the farm ate first, and that a week of his only four trusted men doing nothing was a week the planting window didn't have. Ward said the ward would still be there after the planting and so would the risk, and that the risk got worse the longer it sat with a man pulled off the roster in front of it.

He argued that it was dangerous, that putting all four of them inside a barracks of unknown loyalty was the single most efficient way to lose all four at once, and that Ward of all people should be able to see the arithmetic of it. Ward said that if the ward was that far gone then Emeric was already dead and just hadn't been told yet, and that finding out was the job.

Then he made the argument he actually believed, the one he'd built everything else on since the day he'd walked the wells with Rylan.

"It doesn't scale. It isn't teachable. There's no version of what you're describing that I can write down and hand to a steward, and if I can't hand it to someone else, then it's not a system, it's you. Which means the day you're not here, the barony has no way to know its own men. Everything I've built here I've built so it survives me. This doesn't survive anybody."

Ward thought about that. He actually thought about it, long enough that Emeric had time to believe he'd landed it.

"Then it doesn't scale," Ward said. "You still have to do it once."

And there was nothing on the other side of that. Emeric looked for it. He turned the thing over the way he turned every problem over, waiting for the seam to show, and what he found instead was that his objection was entirely correct and entirely beside the point. He had proved that Ward's method could not be reproduced. He had not touched the question of whether it worked, and he had a man sitting in a room off the guardhouse who was going to be one or the other by the end of the week regardless of which of them was right about procedure.

He had been right four times running. The water. The smith. The ledgers. Wexler. Every one of those had come off paper, and paper had never once failed him here, and somewhere in the last two months he had stopped treating that as a run of results and started treating it as a property of the world.

"A week," he said. "You report to me at the end of it. Not during."

"That's the way it works."

"One thing." Emeric held his eyes. "When you come back, you tell me how you knew. Not just the names. I want the shape of it."

"I'll try. Some of it won't come out as words."

"Try anyway."

They moved into the lower ward the next morning. Emeric went to the fields and was, by every account he later got secondhand, poor company for eight days.

Ward came up on the ninth, and Rylan came with him, and this time nobody had to ask him to.

Ward gave the names first. Four to move out of the ward.

"Say why. Each one."

"First talks. Anyone who buys him a drink gets whatever he's got, and he doesn't know he's doing it. Second's afraid. Badly, all the time. That's fine in a soldier right up until you ask him to hold something alone. Third's done nothing wrong at all and hates the crown enough that I'd rather he never had to choose."

Emeric was already writing. "Those three I can hand to a steward. A man who talks when drink's bought. A man who won't stand alone. A man who'd have to choose. Any careful person living in that barracks for a week finds those."

"Aye," Ward said. "So write them down."

Emeric stopped writing, because he had expected an argument and been handed the point instead.

"Most of it isn't mine," Ward said. "Most of it's just being in the room. That's a week and four men you already have. Buy it every year if you like."

"And the fourth."

Ward was quiet for a moment. "I wouldn't put him on a gate at night."

"Why."

"I don't know."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," Ward agreed, and offered nothing after it, and did not look away either.

Emeric held that for a long moment and then set his pen down, because there was no column it went in and pretending otherwise would have been worse than leaving it blank. Eleven, Ward said, he'd keep close. The rest were ordinary men who had been drilling for weeks because nobody had told them to stop.

"None of them signs for anything," Emeric said.

"None of them signs for anything," Ward agreed.

"And Corliss."

Ward sat down, which he almost never did in that room. Rylan didn't. He'd gone very still by the door, the way he had on the street the evening they'd found Tam.

"Third night, one of their sergeants got far enough into a jug to stop being careful with me. He wasn't defending the man. He was angry that I was in his barracks at all, and it came out sideways." Ward looked at the wall for a moment. "That part was luck. I want that said plain, because you'll want to make it something else later. A jug and a man who resents you. Anyone could have bought it."

"Then buy me the rest of it. What did he say."

"The draws are real. Corliss took them. Six years of them, and he wouldn't deny one of them to your face."

"Then what am I missing."

"The rows past the wall."

Emeric didn't understand for about two seconds, and then he did, all at once, the way the ledger map had come together on Bram's table.

"The ones with nobody left to pay the priest," Ward said. "They don't go in the parish book, because the book only counts what's paid for, and they don't go in consecrated ground for the same reason. Somebody still has to buy the box and the ground and the digging. Corliss took provisioning coin and bought them. Seven the sergeant knew of since the plague. He'd been at it a long while before that, for the poor ones nobody buries either, and the sergeant didn't have a number for those."

"Imani gave me eleven names off the top of her head." He heard how thin his own voice had gone. "Eleven deaths that weren't in the book. I put them on the map. I made the map worse, because worse was truer, and I was pleased with myself about it."

"You never asked who dug the holes."

"No."

"He didn't write it down because writing it down makes it theft in ink," Ward said. "Which it is. And because Wexler would have stopped it inside a week, which he would have."

Emeric already knew what the third date was. He asked anyway, because he wanted to hear it said out loud and because he thought he probably ought to have to.

"The morning after Tam."

"A box, the ground, and two men to dig it." Ward's voice didn't change at all, which was how Emeric knew what it was costing him. "Sela couldn't pay. The barony wasn't going to. Corliss drew the coin the same morning they brought him in off the mill road."

Rylan said, "She thinks it was the ward."

Both of them looked at him.

"I stood in her kitchen four days after with his purse in my coat." He wasn't quite talking to either of them. "She said the garrison had seen to the burying. That's what she'd been told and that's what she believed, and I believed it too, because that's what a garrison does for one of its own when there's coin for it. There wasn't coin for it. I never once asked where it came from." He stopped. "He's my wife's cousin. He sat at our table at midwinter and he never said a word."

"He wouldn't," Ward said. "Saying it once makes it a thing he did, and then it's a thing he can be stopped from doing."

Emeric thought about the letter. Three drafts. The careful ones burned. The coin he'd counted out himself and sent with Rylan four days later, and the small clean satisfaction he'd taken in getting the wording right.

"Your book had the draws," Ward said. "It didn't have the holes."

He went down to the guardhouse himself. He did not send for the man.

Corliss was somewhere past fifty, thick through the shoulders, with a face built for standing in weather and not much else. He stood up when the prince came in because thirty years of drill made him, and Emeric could see him hating it.

Emeric laid it out plainly. What the column showed. What the three dates were. Why he had acted the same day rather than the next. That he had been wrong, that the record would say so in writing, and that he would say it standing in the lower ward in front of the men who had watched him walked out of it.

Corliss let him finish.

"You can say it in the ward," he said. "You can't say it in the market."

"No."

"Two thousand people in this town, and every one of them will hear that the crown's man pulled me off the line, and about eleven of them will ever hear the other half." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "My wife's already had it twice at the well. What did he do. Asked kindly, both times. That's what kindly sounds like now."

"I know what it costs. I'm not going to pretend the correction pays it back."

"And you'll want me grateful you looked."

Emeric didn't answer that, because the honest answer was somewhere close to yes and he'd only just noticed it.

"I stole from the treasury," Corliss said. "Sixteen years. You've got six of it on the sheet, you can count that much. Do what you're going to do about it and let's have it done."

And there it was, the second edge of the thing. He couldn't leave it. A man taking unrecorded coin out of a treasury with no consequence is a rule, and the rule is that the coin is there for the taking. He also could not punish it. Not that. Not for those seven, and not for however many came before them, and not for the box on the mill road morning.

So he did what he always did.

"There'll be a line for it," he said. "Burials, for anyone the parish won't take. Fixed sum each quarter, out of the barony, written down. You sign for it in the open and nobody has to ask you what it was for."

Corliss looked at him for a long moment.

"Sixteen years I've been doing that quiet," he said. "Now it's yours, with your name over the top of the column and a clerk to check it."

"That's not what it is."

"That's what it'll be, in the book." He picked his belt up off the table. "Then write it down, Your Highness."

He said it in the ward the next morning, because he'd said he would and because doing it late would have been its own answer. Fifty-four men, the four already gone to other posts before dawn. He kept it short. The column, the dates, the error, whose it was. No explanation of the burials, because that wasn't his to give away, and he'd asked first and been told no.

Corliss stood in the second rank and looked at a point somewhere over Emeric's shoulder for the whole of it, and went back to the forms when it was done.

Emeric tried to write the lesson down that evening and couldn't get the sentence to hold.

The obvious version was that judgment beat paper, and it was wrong, and he knew it was wrong before he finished thinking it. Three of Ward's four names were already in his hand in ink. The thing that saved Corliss had come off a drunk sergeant, and Ward had made a point of calling that luck, and luck bought with a week and a jug is a thing a barony can budget for.

What was actually true was narrower and worse. His method had no instrument for a fact that nobody had written down. It never would have. And the reason nobody had written this one down was that writing it down would have gotten a man punished, which meant the gap wasn't an oversight in the records. It was a feature of them. Every ledger in this barony was missing exactly the things it was dangerous to be caught doing, and he had spent two months treating those books as though they described the place.

He got that far and stopped, because the next line wanted to be a method for finding such things, and he didn't have one, and putting a hopeful sentence there would have been the same error in a different hand.

He wrote the two facts down and left the space under them empty.

Corliss worked the weeks after correctly and completely, and did not once come to the castle for anything he could handle in the ward. Outside, through the shutter, the lower ward was drilling, fewer of them than there had been and every one of them now a name Emeric could put to a face, which was more than he'd had and less than he'd wanted.

One of them would not look at him again for a long time, and had earned that.
