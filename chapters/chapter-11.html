Chapter 11 - What the Stone Was For

He was small, and his mother's hands were over his.

He'd had this dream perhaps a dozen times since the exile. It never changed. A slate on a table, a stylus in his fist, too big for him, and her fingers closing over his to turn the angle of it, a little flatter, like that. She was saying something. He could feel her saying it, the breath of it against his hair. He couldn't hear a word.

This time there was something underneath.

The slate was on a table, and the table was in a room. He knew the room. Not from Kaldo and not from the palace, not from anywhere he could name, and he knew it the way you know your own front door in the dark. Plain walls. A light that came from nowhere. Shelves along one side, mostly empty. And the sense, not a memory, only the sense, that she used to come here. That this was somewhere she went.

He woke with his hand closed around the grey stone and the stone was warm.

Not warm from his hand. Warmer than that.

He lay still. Then he got up, lit a candle, put the stone on the desk in front of him, and sat looking at it.

There was no reason for tonight. Nothing had happened; he hadn't done anything different. He'd carried it every day for three months, in his pocket, in his hand, on his desk, and somewhere in those three months a sum had been quietly adding itself up, and tonight it had come out.

He took out the notebook and started a list of what he didn't know.

What it cost, and whether the cost was his or the stone's. Whether the stone would run out. Whether the room was real or something his own mind had built around a dream. Whether you could go in and fail to come back out.

He was four hours at it. The list got long. Somewhere near the end of it he was thinking about a consent packet, page four, paragraph two, and the first night he'd read it, at a kitchen table in another world. All rights to any outcomes arising from participation. He'd signed it anyway. He'd been curious and short of money and sure he'd notice if anything went wrong.

He didn't sleep again that night.



"I need you to watch me sleep," he told Ward in the morning.

"No."

"It's an experiment."

Ward looked at him.

"Observe me," Emeric said. "Is that better?"

"Worse."

So he explained, as much as he could, which wasn't much. A stone his mother had given him. A dream that wasn't a dream. A room. He wanted to go back to it on purpose, and he wanted somebody awake beside him while he did, somebody who'd know what normal looked like and could tell when it stopped.

Ward listened to all of it without a change of expression. When it was over he said, "Fine," and then, after a moment, seriously, "Do you want me to sing?"

That night Emeric lay down with the stone in his hand and Ward sat in a chair by the bed.

Afterward Ward told him what he'd seen, in the flat order of a man giving a report. Breathing slow and even. Skin cool, not cold. Pulse steady, a little slow, the pulse of a sleeper. Nothing wrong with him at all, by any measure Ward knew. Only the stone in his hand, which had got warm enough that Ward had noticed it from the chair.

Emeric checked the rest himself, when he woke, the way he'd learned to check things.

He reached into the stone with the sense he was slowly learning to use, and found nothing at all. A drained mana stone feels quiet, like a sleeper. This didn't feel like anything, as if there were no stone there to reach into. It had no signature of its own.

But he did. Lying there, he could feel the level of his own mana drawn down, slowly and steadily, like a cistern with a tap left open. The draw was his. The stone was only the shape the doing took.

He sat up, slowly, and looked at the grey thing in his palm.

For four hundred years people had tested these stones and found them empty, and given up. They'd done it with the best instruments they had. And every one of those instruments had been pointed at the stone.

Nobody had thought to look at the man holding it.



The second night he went in on purpose.

He lay back with the stone in his hand and Ward in the chair, and he didn't try to sleep. He thought about the room. Plain walls, the light from nowhere, the shelves. He held it in his mind the way you hold a word on the tip of your tongue, and then he let go of everything else, and he was there.

It was exactly as he'd dreamed it. A room without corners, quite; the walls went soft where they met, as if the place hadn't bothered with the parts that didn't matter. The light was even and came from nowhere. The table was there. The shelves were there, and empty.

"Show me what you are," he said aloud. His voice sounded perfectly ordinary.

Two shapes rose out of the air above the table, all at once.

The first was a fork. A line that ran along and then split in two, one path going one way and one the other, with something at the split that he could only describe as a question. If this, then that. If not, the other.

The second was a ring. A line that went out, and round, and came back to where it had started, and went round again. Do this. Then do it again. Until.

He knew them both, from the trial, from twenty years of somebody else's career that had landed in his head like luggage: a branch and a loop. The two oldest shapes there were, in that work. Everything else was built out of them.

He didn't touch either one. He walked around them, slowly, and looked at them from every side. He didn't know what touching them would do, and he'd been warned, in another life, about men who were sure they'd notice if anything went wrong.

He was there, by his own count, for most of an hour.

When he came out Ward was sitting exactly where he'd left him, and the candle had barely moved.

"How long?" Emeric said.

"Five minutes. A bit more."

He took the notebook, turned to a clean page, and crossed out the word he'd been using in his head since the banquet. Hollow. That had been the wrong instrument's conclusion. He wrote control stone in its place, and beside it working name, because he didn't know yet whether it was the right one.



He tried it awake the next afternoon, sitting at his desk with the stone in his hand, and it worked the same.

Ward watched from the doorway. Afterward he said it had been the worst five minutes of the last fifteen years. Emeric had sat there with his eyes open and nothing behind them. His face had gone slack on one side. If Ward hadn't known what he was doing, he'd have thought it was a stroke, and he'd have been off down the stairs for the healer.

Emeric wrote it down, in order, before anything else:

Time runs wrong. An hour inside is a few minutes out.

Defenseless. Anyone could walk up and put a knife in me.

Looks like a stroke. Never in front of anyone who doesn't know.

Then he took the practice inside with him.

It was almost too easy, once he'd thought of it. In the room nothing glowed through a shutter. Nobody could see a glyph that formed in a place that didn't exist. And an hour in there cost Ward five minutes of sitting in a chair.

He'd spent three months on the coin, the candle and the slate, a handful of slow minutes a night, behind shutters, with his hand wandering. In the room he did it over and over. He lifted the coin and held it, and set it down, and lifted it again, a hundred times, until the shape of the lift was something his body knew without asking. He bent the candle flame and held it. He drew the stylus strokes until they came out the same every time.

He was no stronger. The pool he drew from was the same size when he came out as it had been going in, only lower. But every repetition made him more exact, shaving a little waste off the motion as a file takes a burr off an edge.

For three months the work had been slower than his patience. That first long night in the room, for the first time, it was the other way round.



On the fourth night he took all ten of them out and laid them on the desk.

His mother's, and Corbin's nine.

Side by side they looked the same: grey, egg-sized, carved all over in the same fine lattice. Except that his mother's had soft edges. The lattice on it had been worn smooth in places by years of handling, a child's hands, then his own.

He picked up one of Corbin's and tried it.

Nothing. He held it in his hand and thought about the room, and reached, and nothing answered. The room didn't come. The stone sat in his palm like a pebble from a riverbed.

He tried two more. The same.

So either Corbin's nine were dead, all of them, for some reason he couldn't see. Or it took years of handling before a stone would open, years of carrying it every day, the way he'd carried this one as a boy. He had no way to tell which. He had nothing to test it with but time.

He put Corbin's back in their case and held his mother's.

She'd given it to him before he was seven. On the far side of the wall, in the years he couldn't reach. And holding it was the only thing that had ever let him see over.

He stayed there a long time, holding it.

Then he turned to the back of Armand's notebook, the very back, past the theory and the diagrams, where the old man's handwriting got small and cramped. He'd skimmed it on the road. He read it properly now.

Eleven tests. Over nine years. Armand had tried to make a Hollow Crystal do something eleven times, with eleven methods, and recorded every one. Heat. Cold. Charge from a full mana stone pressed against it. Water. Light. A tower mage's best sense, held on it for an hour. Every test was careful and every result was the same. Nothing. No response. Inert.

And every test had been pointed at the stone.

For four hundred years the kingdom's best minds had looked in the wrong place, and at some point, long ago, they had stopped looking.

He sat with the notebook open on his knee and something in his stomach went quiet and heavy.

A thing this useful didn't stay unknown for four hundred years by accident. Somebody, somewhere, must have stumbled into the room before him, in four centuries of people carrying these stones in their pockets. And it had never got out. It wasn't in Armand's thirty years of notes. It wasn't in the tower, or the Circle, or anywhere in the capital that he'd ever heard of.

He had no evidence of anything. He had a feeling, the kind his old life had taught him to distrust and his father's whisper code had taught him to keep. Somebody kept knowledge like this buried, and had kept it buried a long time.

He was a disgraced third son in a dying barony with four guards and a prisoner. Whoever they were, he wasn't ready to meet them.

So almost nobody would know. Ward knew. That would have to be enough, for now.



He went to the forge the next morning, because there was one person in Kaldo he'd seen look at a piece of iron the way he'd just looked at the branch and the loop.

He didn't get to ask.

He'd barely opened his mouth when Bram, without looking up from the anvil, said, "Come to the house."

Emeric stopped.

"Thursday. After the fire's down." Bram turned the iron over and struck it twice. "My daughter's been asking what it is we talk about."
