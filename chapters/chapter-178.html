Chapter 178 - Her Own Line

The sheet came into Kaldo on the seventh of June in a pedlar's pack, between a card of horn buttons and a bundle of bootlaces, and by the evening of the eighth there was not a tavern in the lower ward without a copy of it on the wall.

Rylan brought one up to the keep. He had taken it off the door of the Anchor himself, and the landlord had watched him do it and said nothing, and there had been three more on the bench inside. He put it on the long table in front of Emeric without a word and stood back from it, as a man stands back from something he has carried up a hill in his bare hands that turned out to be hot.

It was a single sheet, printed on one side, on grey rag paper that was not Kaldo's, in a heavy black letter that was not Kaldo's either. Not from the mill. Not from the plain press. Somebody else's press, somewhere else, by somebody who had set it carefully and well. At the top, across the whole width, in the largest type, it said: THE BOOKKEEPER'S BOX.

He read it twice.

It did not say anything that was not true. That was the thing he noticed first, and went on noticing, the second time through, with the sick slow attention of a man reading his own name in somebody else's letter. It said that the prince of Kaldo had a partner, a smith's daughter, who had been seen by half the south dancing with him at last autumn's harvest. It said that she kept the books of four baronies, which everybody knew. And it said that she kept another book besides. That in a locked box in her room in the keep, in a cipher nobody else could read, she held a file on every family in the four baronies: who they owed, who they loved, who they were afraid of, where their children went to school, which of their brothers lived in which town. It asked its readers whether they had been asked. It asked whether they knew what was written about them, and by whom, and for whom. It asked, at the bottom, in a smaller letter, what kind of prince kept such a box, and what kind of woman held it for him.

It did not name her. It did not need to.

Ferra, at her place, read it upside down from across the table, without reaching for it, and turned back to her book.



"I'll answer it," said Emeric.

They were all at the table by then. Ward had come in off the wall, and Saban from the market with a second copy in his pocket, from a different pedlar, and Bram from the works, and Imani, and Armand at the end with his eyes shut. Hale with the minutes. Ferra at her book, writing, as if the morning's business were the morning's business.

"I'll answer it in print," said Emeric. "From the keep, under my own name, on the mill's paper, on every tavern door in four baronies and up the road as far as Wayford. That I asked for the file. That it was my order. That it exists because a man named Garrin knew Rylan's children's names and nobody in this barony did, and I'd not have another man made into a door. And that if anybody wants to know what's in it about them, they can come to me and I'll..."

"No," said Ferra.

She had not looked up.

"Ferra."

"No." She finished the line she was writing, and blotted it, and put the pen down, and looked at him across the table. "Read it again. The last line. What kind of prince keeps such a box, and what kind of woman holds it for him." She did not raise her voice. "It's not about the box. It's about me. It says I'm yours. A smith's daughter the prince dances with, who keeps his secrets. And the day you put your name on a sheet on every tavern door in the south saying she's only doing what I told her, you've said it for them, in your own hand, on our own paper." She picked up the pen again. "A prince defending his woman. That's what it wants. You'd be the second sheet."

"Then what do you want me to do?"

"Nothing," said Ferra. "It's my line. I'll answer it."

"How?"

"In my own money," said Ferra, and went back to writing.

Saban, beside her, took the second copy out of his pocket and laid it on the table beside the first, and smoothed it flat with one hand, and looked at the two of them side by side with his head on one side.

"Not ours," he said. "Not the mill's paper, not our type. Rag from the north, I'd say, and a broken letter in the fourth line, see, the g, with half its tail gone. Same break on both." He folded his own copy. "Somebody's press, with a g that wants replacing. That's a thing a man can follow." He put the copy back in his pocket. "If the chancellor has no objection."

"Follow it," said Ferra, without looking up. "Don't answer it."

Imani had not said anything. She sat at her end of the table with her arms folded and the sheet in front of her and her face like the ward door. Emeric had been waiting for her, since he read the last line. She had stood up in a room above Kaldo years ago and said she did not agree with the file, and that she would say so at the table, in front of all of them, and that Ferra could write it down. Ferra had written it down.

"Imani," said Saban, carefully, because somebody had to.

"I said at this table I didn't agree with that box," said Imani. "I still don't." She pushed the sheet away from her with one finger. "I said it here. To her face. Where it belonged." She stood up. "I'll not say it to a pedlar. And anybody in my ward who asks me, I'll tell them I've a sick list to see to, and they can ask the chancellor themselves." She went to the door. "Which they won't. Because they're frightened of her. Which is the only sensible thing anybody in this sheet's said."



He went to the counting room that night to argue, and did not get to.

She was at her desk with the lamp, as she was every night at that hour, with the day's book and the quarter's columns and the sheet, which she was using to keep her place in the Milford ledger, folded in four. She did not look up when he came in. He shut the door and stood with his back to it.

"It's going up the road," he said. "Saban says there were copies at the ford chapel on the fifth. And at the crossing inn. It's going north faster than we can take it down."

"I know. I've had it from Saban twice since supper." She turned a page.

"They'll read it in Wayford. In the capital."

"They'll read it in the capital whatever you do. They'll read your answer in the capital too." She turned another page. "Do you know who'll read your answer most carefully? Whoever paid for this one. Because then he'll know he can make you do a thing. He'll know where the string is." She looked up at last. "I don't want him to know where the string is."

"I don't like standing here doing nothing while they..."

"I know you don't." She looked at him over the lamp. "That's the cost. You don't get to make it easy on yourself. That was one of the terms." She held his eye. "Not just for her and me. For this, too."

He had nothing to say to that. It was true, and he had agreed to it in her room with Imani two years ago, and it had not occurred to him then that it would one day mean standing in a counting room with his hands empty while a printed sheet on grey rag went north up the road with her in it.

"Sit down," said Ferra.

He sat down, on the clerks' stool, across the desk from her.

"You can do one thing," she said. "Sit there while I work. And don't talk." She dipped her pen. "I want somebody in the room, and I don't want to be told anything."

So he sat. She worked. The lamp burned, and the pen scratched, and the river went by under the window in the dark, and somewhere below in the keep the exchange's racks clicked once, faintly, through the floor, somebody calling somebody at the end of the evening. After an hour she reached across the desk without looking up from her column and took his hand, and held it, on the desk, among the papers, and went on adding with the other; and did not let go of it until she had come to the foot of the page and the figure was right.



The records hall opened on the twentieth of June.

It had been building since March, on the north side of the square, where the old corn exchange had fallen down in the plague years: the first building in Kaldo put up in the new cement from the kilns, plain and grey and square, with a slate roof and tall windows, and inside, one long room with a counter down the middle and shelves behind it to the ceiling. It had been in Ferra's book for a year under the heading of the census, because the four baronies' census was four baronies' worth of paper and the counting room had run out of shelf. It was to have opened in September. Ferra moved it to June, and paid the masons the difference out of the quarter's contingency, and put the difference in the book on its own line, and did not explain the line to anybody.

On the morning it opened, a notice went up on its door, on the mill's paper, in the plain press's type, and the same notice went up the same morning on the doors of the posting houses at Grayrock and Milford and Wayford, and on the church door, and on the board at the gate. It did not mention the sheet. It did not mention any box.

It said that the four baronies' census was now kept in the records hall in Kaldo square, and at the stewards' offices in Grayrock, Milford and Wayford; and that any householder in the four baronies might come to any of them, on any weekday between the second bell and the fifth, and ask to see his own household's line, and be shown it, and have it read aloud to him if he could not read; and might have any error in it corrected, on his word, at no charge, and the correction initialled in his sight. It said that he would be shown his own line and no other, and that no other householder would ever be shown his. And it was signed by the chancellor of the four baronies, with her own name, which was the first time most people in the south had seen it written down.

Ferra stood behind the counter herself, the first day, with two clerks, from the second bell to the fifth.

They came. Not many, the first morning: a dozen, and most of them came to look at the building, which had a floor of red tiles that nobody in Kaldo had seen the like of. But by noon there was a line out of the door and across the square, because word had gone round the market that you could see what the keep had written about you, and the line was the sheet's readers, every one of them, with the sheet in their pockets.

And they were shown their line.

Emeric watched it from the door for an hour in the afternoon. He watched a fishwife from the lower ward put her hand flat on the counter in front of Ferra and say she wanted to see what was written about her, all of it, and be quick, she had a stall; and Ferra take down a book from the shelf, and open it at a page, and turn it round, and put her finger on a line. The fishwife could not read. Ferra read it to her. Name. Age. The house on Cooper's Row. A husband, deceased, the plague year. Two children living, their names and ages. Trade, fish. Rent paid to the barony, so much, by the quarter, paid up. Church, the parish. Well, the north well.

That was all. That was the line.

The fishwife listened to it with her chin up. When Ferra had finished, she looked at the book, and at Ferra, and at the book again, as if she were waiting for the rest of it.

"My boy's not nine," she said at last. "He's eight. He'll not be nine till the winter quarter."

Ferra took up her pen, and crossed out the figure, and wrote the right one above it, and initialled it, and turned the book round so the woman could see her do it.

"Eight," said Ferra.

"Well," said the fishwife. She looked at the line a moment longer. Then she said that she had come all this way, and stood in a line in the sun for an hour, for that, and that she did not know what anybody had been making such a fuss about; and went back to her stall.

That was the day. A man from the cooperage whose name had been spelled wrong since the census, and was not any more. A farmer from the west side who was certain he had paid his rent on the spring quarter and was shown that he had, and was disappointed. An old woman who wanted her dead husband taken off and was told, gently, that he had been taken off, three years ago, and wept. A great many people who looked at a line that said how old they were and where they lived and what they did and who they lived with and nothing else, and went away bored.

The file stayed where it was, in the left-hand box in her room, in her cipher. She did not deny it. She did not mention it. Nobody asked her about it at the counter, all that week, to her face, not once. Emeric would not have believed it if he had not been there.

A second sheet came into Kaldo at the end of the month in the same grey rag with the same broken g, saying the same things louder. The Anchor's landlord took it down off his own door before Rylan got there, because, he said, nobody had read it and it was making the door look untidy. A third came in July, and the pedlar who carried it could not sell a single copy in the lower ward and gave the bundle to Maren for lighting the range.



Saban came back from the north on the second of July with mud to his knees and nothing in his hands.

He had followed the broken g for nine days. Up the capital road to Wayford, where the printers on the square had never seen the face and said so, and he believed them. North again, to two more towns, asking at every printer and every bookseller with the sheet in his hand, and at a third, a market town four days up the road, a printer's journeyman who had once worked for a man who had a g like that in a fount he bought second-hand from the capital. The man had sold his press two years ago. The journeyman did not know to whom. Somebody with money, he thought, who wanted a press of his own and did not want to be seen buying one. A gentleman's house.

"Which house?" said Emeric.

"He didn't know." Saban sat down at the long table with his boots still on. "I asked which road the cart went out on, the day the press was sold, and he told me, because he'd helped load it. The east road out of the market town." He took off his hat. "There are nine houses worth the name on that road in the first two days. Three of them would hire a printer. One of them," he paused, "is the Sallow country. That's all I've got. A road and a cart and a journeyman's memory of a morning two years ago." He put the hat on the table. "I lost it there. It could be any of the nine. It could be none. A man who buys a press so as not to be seen buying one doesn't keep it at home."

Ferra, at the end of the table, did not look up from her book.

"Then we don't know," she said.

"Then we don't know," said Saban.

"Good," said Ferra, and turned the page. "Write it down that we don't. I'll have it in the book." And then, a little later, still writing: "Isolde wrote. Did you hear? Her father's found her a husband. A lord in the north, at midwinter." She did not say what else the letter said. "Whoever it is, it isn't her."
