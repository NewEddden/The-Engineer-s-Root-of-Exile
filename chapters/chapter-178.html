Chapter 178 - Hammer Down

Bram had not struck iron at the anvil since the spring, and he had not noticed until the boy asked him.

It was the Hesketh boy. Edwin, old Hesketh's grandson, who had come down from Wayford the winter before last with a letter from his grandfather and a bundle and a pair of hands too big for him, and been put to the mill yard, and then to the forge, because Jorrin said he had the arms for it and nothing in his head yet to get in the way. He was sixteen. He had the anvil in the old forge now, Bram's anvil, the one Bram had brought up from the village when there was no foundry and no mill and no converter and nothing in Kaldo that made iron but Bram and a bellows. The boy stood at it every day from first light and drew out bar for the rail works' spikes and the yard's bolts and anything else that did not need the steam hammer, and he was slow, and he was careful, and he was not bad.

On a morning at the end of the first month of autumn he was drawing a spike and the point kept splitting, and he stopped with the bar in the tongs and looked round the forge until he found Bram in the door with his tally book, and said, "Master, how do you get the point to close?"

Bram looked at him.

He had come to the old forge to check the coal against the book. He had not come to show anybody anything. He had not stood at an anvil with a hammer in his hand since before the fight, since before the levy, since the spring; he worked it out, standing in the door, and it came to half a year. He had been in the mill, and the converter shed, and the rail works, and the yard at the lower water, and the long room, and the pattern store at night with Emeric and the cells, and walking the flat. He had a room over the mill office with three clerks in it now who kept his tallies, and a boy who ran between the works with slips, and he had had to learn their names. He had not noticed that he had not been at the anvil. He had not had time to notice.

"Give it here," he said.

He put the tally book on the bench and took the tongs from the boy and put the spike back in the fire. He waited for the heat. He knew the heat without looking; he looked anyway, the way he always had, at the colour, and took it out at the straw and laid it on the horn and picked up the boy's hammer.

It was a good hammer. It was not his. His was on its hook on the wall.

He showed him the stroke. Not the blow; the turn. A quarter turn of the bar between every blow, not a half, and the hammer coming down a little off the square, so that it pushed the metal in toward the point instead of out to the sides, and a lighter blow at the last, and lighter again. He did it slowly so the boy could see. He did it four times, turn and strike, turn and strike, and the point drew down and closed, clean, and he put the spike back in the fire and held out the tongs.

"Like that," he said. "Off the square. Push it in."

The boy took the tongs and looked at the point, and at him, and nodded.

Bram put the hammer down on the anvil's heel, where the boy kept it, and picked up his tally book, and went out to check the coal.

He did not look back at the anvil. He did not think about it all morning. It was not until the evening, in the long room, when Ferra looked up from her book across the table at him and looked at him for longer than she usually did, and said, "What's the matter with your hand?", that he found he had been rubbing the palm of his right hand with the thumb of his left, slowly, the way he had when he was a young man and his palm ached at night after a long day at the fire, and that it did not ache, and that he had been rubbing it all evening.

"Nothing," he said.

She did not believe him. She went back to her book.

He did the year's count from the works in the last week of autumn, as he always did, for Ferra's book. He did it at the desk in the room over the mill office, with the three clerks gone home and a lamp, from the tallies, one works at a time.

The yard at the lower water first, because it was Fletcher's and Fletcher was the one who would read it.

The second coaster was launched, in the middle of summer, the week before the levy marched, off the second slip: the same pattern as the Emberfell, plate hull, Bram's boiler, a copy of the steamboat's engine, Abloh's gaff sail. Saban had named her the Marsh Hen, which Fletcher had said was no name for a ship and Saban had said was the name of every bird on the marsh, which was where she would be going, and it had stayed. She had made two runs to Emberfell since, under Rowena's escort. With the Emberfell that made two Kaldo hulls on the lane, and the hired sulphur coasters, and Rowena's escort with them every run.

Hull three's frames stood on the first slip, where hull two had been built, beside the empty berth where hull two lay between flights with a canvas over her. Long and narrow. Fletcher had drawn her in a fortnight after Emeric said reach, and had her ribs up by the end of summer, and did not talk about her.

The Hod was rebuilt. Her new bottom was in, and her new keel rack, and she had two rail paths now, one each side, and the stone at the helm that went cold under the pilot's hand if the lift bank ever ran on what it held. Emeric called it the watchdog. Ned Tallis called it the cold stone, and would not take her up without putting his hand on it first. She had flown four times since the summer. Ned said she was better than she had been. He said it every time.

The rail works: eleven hundred lengths rolled this year, more than twice last year's, with the new rolls Jorrin had cut in the spring. The Grayrock line past two thirds. Laid from the fourth mile to the ninth, and the cuttings through the hill's shoulder begun, and the harvest men back from the harvest as the bounty had bound them to be, all but eleven. Ferra's figure, which Bram had checked, was still the autumn after next. Bram thought it might be the summer. He did not say so. He had learned.

The watcher had stood at the gauge on the rail works engine every working day of the year, under the new rule. Nobody had overridden him. Nobody had asked to.

And the powder house and the mill's lean-to. Eight hundred and forty pieces bored and locked and stocked this year, off the mill, Jorrin's hands and the boy Edwin's and nine others. Forty-one thousand cartridges rolled by the island women. Two hundred and six pieces had come back from the stream ground in the carts broken or burst or lost. The rest were racked in the old armoury under Enno's lock, drawn every morning and fired off on the lower field and racked again, and marked on the slate.

He wrote them all in a column, and totted them, and looked at the total for a while.

He had spent his life making things one at a time. A horseshoe. A hinge. A plough-share. A sword, when he was young and somebody would pay for one. He had made them with his own hands, at his own anvil, and every one of them had been his, and he had known every one of them the way a man knows his own children's faces.

He did not know any of these. He had not made any of them. He had made the mill that bored them, and the rolls that rolled the rail, and the hammer that the steam drove, and the gauges that told the men at the mill when a bore was true. He had made the rule that said nobody overrode the watcher. He had made the thing that made the thing that made them, and he had made it so that other men could run it without him, and they did.

He wrote the total in Ferra's book in his own hand, and blotted it, and went to bed.

The pitch went down on the first avenue in the second week of the last month of autumn, on a dry cold day with a north wind, before the frost.

It was the last of it. There had been a great deal once: Rowena's gift, off her own boats, and before that the haul from the Duvrenne shore, which Bram had never seen and only heard about. It had gone into everything. Into the knots' casings, and the flasks' lids, and the rack seals, and the ship's seams, and the Hod's. Into the jars for the levy's food. Every seal in Kaldo that had to keep out water or keep in charge had Rowena's pitch in it, or Duvrenne's.

There were four casks left. Emeric had them brought to the flat.

The first avenue ran from the palace's ring canal north-east through the first quarter to the gate in the wall that would be the north-east gate: a straight broad road, sixty feet between the house fronts, that had been laid in the summer on a bed of broken stone and gravel rolled flat, and that had turned to mud in the first rain and been rolled again and turned to mud again. Emeric had stood on it in the mud with Bram in the second week of autumn and said, "The thaw will take it. Every spring. We'll be laying it again every year for ever." And then, "Unless it's sealed."

So they sealed it. Not all of it. The first three hundred yards of it, from the ring canal to the first cross-street, which was as far as four casks would go. They melted the pitch in a great iron pan on a fire on a cart at the road's edge, the four casks one after another, and mixed it hot with sand and fine gravel from the quarry's screens, and carried it in buckets and spread it on the rolled stone with wooden rakes, a strip at a time, and Jorrin's roller came after, a great iron drum filled with water, pulled by six men, and rolled it flat while it was hot. It smoked. It stank, a thick sweet black stink that got into the throat and the clothes and hung over the first quarter for two days. The children came out of the houses to watch and had to be shouted back.

By dusk there were three hundred yards of black road, flat and hard and smooth as a table, going off from the ring canal between the stone house fronts toward the wall, and the rain that came in the night ran off it into the gutters and did not stand, and in the morning the women of the first quarter came out and walked on it in their pattens as if it were a floor in a church.

Emeric stood at the end of it with Bram in the morning and looked at it.

"That's all of it," Bram said.

"Yes."

"There's no more."

"No," said Emeric. "Not here. There's no more of that anywhere but one place."

"Duvrenne."

"Duvrenne."

Bram looked at the road. Three hundred yards. There were miles of streets drawn on Weekes's sheets for the whole city, and the first avenue alone ran near a mile to the wall.

"That's Alina's," he said.

"Yes," Emeric said.

He did not say anything else about it. He stood at the end of the black road with his hands in his coat and looked down it toward the wall, and Bram looked at him, and then looked at the road, and did not ask.

He walked the wall with Armand on the last day of the season, because Armand asked him to.

The season had closed that week. The frost was coming, and Tessaly Rook and the other earth mages had set the last course of the season on the east side by the marsh the day before, and the masons had struck the last joint, and Weekes had walked the chain along the top of it and written the height. Half height. The whole circuit, two miles and three furlongs round the flat, from the ridge to the marsh and back to the ridge, standing on its footing at half the height of the wall that would be, a grey stone band going round the whole city with the ring canal inside it and the palace inside that.

They walked along the top of it from the west gate to the north. It was broad enough for two men abreast, and more; a cart could have driven on it. Armand walked slowly, with his stick, and stopped every hundred yards, and did not say why.

He was thinner. Bram had seen it all summer and not said anything, because it was not his business and because Armand would not have liked it. He had gone thin in the face first, and then in the hands, and now his coat hung on him. He had been at the handcart at noon on the flat most days that summer, with Hobb and the book, and he had stopped taking the flasks behind the cart where nobody would see. He took them in front of everybody now. Hobb wrote his name and his mark, the same as the mages'. He had stopped pretending.

They stopped at the north corner, where the wall turned. From there you could see the whole of it: the palace's great square hole with its forty vaults finished and roofed and the first course of the palace's ground floor going in on top of them; the ring canal, full of grey water; the first quarter with its black avenue and its two streets of houses and smoke going up from every chimney; the water tower at its corner. The rest of the flat staked out and empty inside the wall, waiting. The camp on the foundry bank, smaller than it had been. The lake. The island.

Armand leaned on his stick and looked at it for a long time.

"Two years," he said.

"Two seasons."

"Two years. I was on this ground with a chain when there was nothing on it but grazing and the Tarrants' pegs." He looked down at the wall under his feet. "I shan't see the top of it."

Bram did not say anything. There was nothing to say that Armand would have wanted to hear.

"I've done your reading," Armand said.

"I know. You said, in the summer."

"I've done it again. I did it last week, while you were at the pitch. You were asleep in your chair in the long room, and I didn't wake you." He did not look at Bram. "It's moved again."

"How much?"

"More than last winter. Twice as much as last winter." Armand shifted his weight on the stick. "More than a year on the band. More than a year of your hand on the cells. It's still small. You're still a smith of no capacity to speak of, by any measure any tower ever took. But it moves, and it's going one way, and it's going faster."

Bram stood on the wall and looked at the city.

He did not tell Armand the rest. He had told nobody. That since the summer he had not had to try. That he did not build the boiler any more, stay by stay, to get into his room. That he sat in the pattern store at night with a stone under his hand to write a cell, and the room was simply there, the way a door is there in a house you have lived in all your life; he did not have to look for it, he did not have to think about it, he reached and it was open. He had opened it once, in the autumn, at the mill desk with the tally book in front of him, without meaning to, because he had been thinking about the gauges and his hand had been on the band, and he had been inside it for the space of three breaths before he knew where he was, and had come out cold.

It was easier every month. He did not like it. He did not like it at all. He had spent his life on things that were hard, and that stayed hard, and that you got good at by doing them ten thousand times; and this was not getting easier because he was getting good at it. It was getting easier because something in him was changing so that it would be.

"I don't like it," he said.

Armand looked at him for the first time.

"No," Armand said. "Nor do I." He turned back toward the west gate. "Walk me down. My legs are gone."

He went into the old forge on the way home, at dusk, because the coal count was wrong again and he wanted to see the bins.

The boy was still there. The fire was banked. Edwin was at the anvil in the last of the light with a spike in the tongs, drawing the point, turn and strike, off the square, and the point was closing clean.

He had Bram's hammer in his hand.

No. Bram looked again. It was the boy's hammer, the one that lived on the anvil's heel. Bram's was on its hook on the wall by the door, where it had hung since the spring, and he stood in the door and looked at it. A four-pound cross-pein with an ash haft that he had made himself when he was twenty-two and re-hafted four times. The face was bright. It was clean. Somebody had oiled it; it had not been him.

The boy struck, and turned, and struck, lighter, and lighter, and held the spike up to the last of the window's light and looked at the point, and put it in the quench, and did not look round.

Bram stood in the door with his tally book, and looked at his hammer on its hook, clean, and at the boy at his anvil not using it.
