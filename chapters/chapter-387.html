Chapter 387 - Burned

Saban read the sermon out on the Monday morning, in the long room, with the king two days out over the sea and the regent on the Tuesday train to the capital, and Corbin sat at the head of the table in the chair and listened to it the way he listened to everything now, with his hands flat on the wood.

It had been preached on the Sunday in the cathedral at Randillia City, by the bishop of the capital, from the great pulpit under the Eye, and by noon the same day it had been preached in every church that took the capital's letter, which was most of them. Saban's man in the cathedral close had taken it down in shorthand from the fourth pew. Keller's clerk had taken it down again from the door. The two copies agreed to the word, which Saban said was the most frightening thing about it.

"'A shepherd does not leave his flock to go fishing,'" Saban read. He read it without any voice at all, the way he read a bill of lading. "'And yet we are told the king of this realm will take ship, with his household and his soldiers and his witches, for the cursed sea, on the business of a creature out of it. For a year. And who will keep the flock? An old man made young, we are told, by means we are not told. An old man seen in the streets of the duke's town in company with a woman who wears the face of his dead queen.'" He turned the page. "'I do not say what these things are. I am a simple priest. I say that the Light weeps for a realm that is left so, and I say that the Light does not leave its children without a shepherd for long.'"

"Simple priest," said Bram.

"He has three houses," said Saban, "and a carriage with a crest on it that he bought from a bankrupt baron in year nine. Simple." He went on. There was a good deal more. There was a child brought up into the pulpit at the end, a little girl from the cathedral school in a white dress, and the bishop had put his hand on her head while he said the last part, about the shepherd, and the whole nave had wept, the man in the fourth pew said, and he'd wept a bit himself, and he wasn't ashamed to say so, because it had been beautifully done.

Saban put the copy down.

"They'll say it again on Sunday," he said. "And the Sunday after. Never the same words twice and never a word a magistrate could hang a man for. By Lantern Night they'll have said it in every town between here and Ostrel, and the ones that didn't hear it in church will hear it in the market." He looked down the table, at Ward, at Armand, at Bram, and last of all at the head of it. "And somebody will come here. To this room. To that chair. I told you on Friday."

"You did," said Corbin.

"I'm telling you again."

"I heard you the first time," said Corbin, and moved on to the next paper, which was Cade asking for more rivet steel.



The bishop came on the Wednesday.

He came on the morning train from the capital with a valise and a clerk, and showed his letter at the visitors' desk in the station like anybody, and stood very patiently while the girl at the desk read it, and put his hand on the reading plate when she asked him, and smiled at her when the plate gave her his card. The card said Isidore, bishop, of the chapter of the cathedral at Randillia City; to pray for the regent's house; four days. The girl at the desk had been at Sela's school and didn't go to church. She gave it to him anyway. Visitors with a letter and a clean card got a card; that was the law, and the law had been made so that nobody at a desk had to like anybody.

He walked up from the station. He didn't take the tram. He walked up Gate Avenue in the March sun, slowly, an old man in the grey of the chapter with the small gold Eye on his breast, looking at everything: the lamps, the trams, the shop windows with the glass in them, the frame going by on its two great legs with a beam on its shoulder and a man in its chest, the children who stopped to stare at him and not at the frame. He stopped twice to bless people who asked. He stopped once at a fruit stall and paid for an orange with a coin from his own pocket and ate it on the castle steps, neatly, with a handkerchief, and then asked the knight at the door, very politely, whether His Highness the Prince Corbin might see an old man for a quarter of an hour, if His Highness had a quarter of an hour, and if he hadn't, the old man would wait.

Corbin had a quarter of an hour.

He'd known since the bishop's card went into the bank at the station that he would. Saban's people at the desk had a way of making sure the chair knew what came in, and the chair had known by half past nine. He'd sat with it for an hour and a half. He'd thought of refusing. Refusing would have been the easy thing and the thing Saban had told him to expect of himself. And it would have been a story by Sunday: the duke would not even see a bishop. He'd seen how stories were made. He'd made them himself, for twenty years, at his father's elbow and Augustine's.

So he had the bishop brought up to the box room.

It was the little room off the long room where the papers came to him: a desk, two chairs, a window onto the inner ward, and on the desk the square wooden box with a slot in its lid that Ferra had built in December, into which every second in Kaldo was supposed to put anything that ran from their own work into somebody else's, with a reason on it. There were eleven papers in it this morning. Corbin had read nine. He put the lid down on them and stood up when the bishop came in, because his mother had taught him to stand for an old man, and his mother didn't teach things twice.

"Your Highness," said the bishop.

"Bishop."

"Isidore, please. I'm too old for the other." He sat down when Corbin did, carefully, in the way of a man whose knees have opinions. He had a broad pink kind face with deep lines in it, and white hair that wanted cutting, and very clear pale eyes, and soft hands that he folded in his lap. He looked at the room. He looked at the box. He looked, briefly and without any appearance of looking, at the place under the desk where Corbin's left boot was, and Corbin felt the drain stone against his skin at his ankle, cool and flat and dumb under its gold leaves, the way he always felt it when somebody looked there.

"I've brought you a letter," said the bishop. "I'll give it you in a moment. I'd like to say a few things first, if you'll let me. They're not in the letter, and I'd sooner say them to your face than have you read them."

"Say them."

"You're very like your father," said Isidore. "Did you know? Not to look at. In the way you sit." He smiled. "I knew him when he was younger than you are. I've been forty-four years in that chapter. I've seen your father go out of the capital for wars and progresses and come back to it every time, and I've seen your brother crowned, which I'm told I must count." He paused. "I've never seen a king leave the realm."

Corbin said nothing.

"No," said the bishop. "I wouldn't either, in your place. You've been told not to." He unfolded his hands and folded them again the other way. "I'll be plain. I'm an old man and I've no time to be anything else. His Holiness has asked me to come and see you. Not your father. Not your brother. You."

"Why?"

"Because His Holiness remembers you."

The window was very bright. A tram went by in the outer ward with its bell going.

"He remembers you as a boy," said Isidore gently. "He remembers a boy who used to sit on the stair outside the queen's tower, at the palace, every afternoon, while she worked. Who never went in. Who sat on the bottom step with his back to the door and his knees up, and listened to her work, and never once went in, though the door was always open." He looked at Corbin with his clear pale eyes. "He remembers you telling him so. You were eleven. You'd been in a fight with a groom's boy and you came to confession with a split lip, and you didn't confess the fight at all. You confessed the stair. You said you didn't know why you went and sat there every day, and you didn't know why you never went in, and you thought it might be a sin. And he told you it wasn't." The bishop's voice was very soft. "He's never forgotten it. He wanted you to know that. That somebody remembers."

Corbin found that his hands were still flat on the desk, which was good, because he'd told them to be.

He knew who'd heard that confession. It had been Augustine. Augustine had heard every confession he'd made from the age of eight. And Augustine was gone; they'd told him that much in the keep, the first night, with Ward in the room: that the man was gone out of his own body as if he'd never been in it and something else wore him now, wore his face and his voice and the inside of his head, and spoke from the pope's chair at Ostrel with all of it. So it was true, then. All of it. The something else had the whole of him. It had the stair.

And it had sent a kind old man to give it back to him, wrapped up, as a present.

"I'd like the letter," said Corbin.

"In a moment." Isidore leaned forward a little. "The realm can't be without a king for a year. You know that. You were raised to know it. Every lord in the capital knows it, and the ones who don't will by Lantern Night, because we'll have told them. And the Church cannot, in conscience, set its seal on a regency held by a man made young by a craft it doesn't know and wouldn't bless if it did, who keeps company in the open with a woman who wears a dead woman's face." He spread his soft hands. "So the Church will say what's true. That the realm has a king still, under God, while its king is abroad. That it has the old king's eldest son. Who stood at his father's side in the capital for twenty years, and knows its every hall and every lord. Whose claim no council ever set aside, and no court. Whom His Holiness remembers." He let that sit. "The Church will say it from every pulpit in Randillia, and the lords will hear it, and the crown will come to the man it ought to have come to in the first place, and nobody will have had to raise a hand. It's already half said. You heard Sunday's."

"I heard it."

"You need do nothing," said the bishop. "That's the beauty of it. You needn't sign anything or say anything or go anywhere. Only not refuse it, when it's offered. And it will be offered. And when it is, your mother, who I'm told is kept in this town on a chain like yours, will be kept on nothing, by the Church's own word, and come home to the capital to a house of her own, as is fitting." He sat back. "That's all. That's what I came to say. The rest is in the letter, with His Holiness's seal on it, so you'll know I'm not an old fool who's made it up on the train."

He took the letter out of the breast of his grey coat and put it on the desk between them. It was heavy, folded in three, with a great seal in white wax on it that had the Eye in it, and under the Eye the crossed keys that only one man in the world put his hand to.

Corbin looked at it.

He didn't pick it up. He sat with his hands flat on either side of it and looked at it, and while he looked at it he let himself think the whole thing, all of it, once, the way his brother had taught him you thought through a thing you were afraid of, so that it couldn't creep up on you afterward.

The crown. Not Harrold's, not lent, not minded for somebody at the bottom of the sea. His. The thing he'd been born to be the one in line for and hadn't been, the thing he'd lain awake for at fourteen and at twenty and at thirty, the thing that a voice in his head that wasn't his had told him for twenty years was his by right and was being stolen from him a day at a time by a boy with a stone in his pocket. The capital. The great hall, and the lords standing when he came in. People stopping. Looking at him. His mother in a house of her own with no chain on her, giving dinners, cutting people. The Church at his back, with all its pulpits, saying every Sunday that he was the shepherd.

He thought it all. He let it come all the way up.

And then, because he'd been made to sit in this town for three years and work for it, he thought the rest.

What the crown would cost, and who'd pay. The knights at the gate, who'd stood there with their pikes the day he came in on the fleet with Kaldo's dead under canvas on the deck, and who'd looked at him, and who'd brought him his food since without once spitting in it. The box, and the eleven papers in it, two unread, one of them Cade's. The girl at the station desk who'd given an old priest a card because that was the law. Tess on her stick at the Forge door with the ring on her finger, saying thank you to him and not to his brother. A frame on Gate Avenue with a man in its chest and a beam on its shoulder. And a bishop in grey who'd walked up from the station to see all of it with his own eyes before he came in to ask a man to give it away.

He'd seen the bishop's face on the walk up. Saban's people had told him that too. He'd stopped at every lamp.

"Bishop," said Corbin.

The old man waited.

"Tell His Holiness," said Corbin, "that I remember the stair too."

The bishop's face softened, very slightly, at the edges, the way a man's does when a hard sum comes out.

"And tell him," said Corbin, "that I know who's asking."

The softening stopped.

Isidore looked at him for a long moment across the desk. He didn't look angry. He didn't look anything; he had the stillest face Corbin had seen on a churchman since the last one he'd sat across a table from, and he'd sat across a table from that one for twenty years. Then the bishop sighed, a small tired old man's sigh, and put his hands on the arms of his chair, and stood up, in the careful way he'd sat down.

"Then let me give you a blessing at least," he said, "before I go. I'd give it to anyone. I've given it to six people between here and the station."

He lifted his right hand.

And Corbin knew what was coming before the old man's mouth opened, because his body knew. His body had known those words for twenty years. It knew them the way a dog knows the sound of its own lead coming off the hook. His shoulders went loose. His breath went slow. Something in the middle of him that he'd only lately learned had a door in it went soft at the hinges, all on its own, without asking him, and swung.

"Open the door, child," said the bishop, in a voice like a hand laid on the back of the neck. "Hold nothing back from Him. Do you give yourself to the light?"

Corbin's mouth opened.

He felt the words in it. He felt them there on his tongue, the two of them, as easy and as worn as two steps of a stair. I do. He'd said them before every Seeing he'd ever had. He'd said them to Augustine with Augustine's hand on the back of his neck, in a closed room at the hill house, the first time at fourteen, and every time after, for twenty years, and every time the door had opened and something had come in and walked through him and shown him what to want. He'd said them so many times that they'd stopped being words. They were what his mouth did.

The drain stone at his ankle was very cold.

He shut his mouth.

He shut it slowly, and carefully, the way you'd close a door on a room with someone asleep in it. He put both hands flat on the desk on either side of the white seal and pressed down until he could feel the grain of the wood in his palms. The door in the middle of him stood open a moment longer, swinging, and he looked at it, the way his brother had taught him to look at a thing, all of it; and then he put his weight against it, from the inside, and it shut.

It took longer than anything he'd ever done.

"No," said Corbin.

The bishop's hand stayed where it was for a breath. Then it came down.

"No," said Corbin again. His voice was quite steady, which surprised him. "I've been given to the light, Bishop. I was given to it for twenty years. I've had all of it I can eat." He stood up. His knees, he noticed, were not entirely sure of the floor, and he stood anyway. "The train goes at four. My man will see you to it. You'll want your orange peel; you left it on the steps."

Isidore looked at him.

"You'll keep the letter?" he said, at last.

"I'll keep the letter."

"You'll want to read it."

"I'll want to show it to people," said Corbin, and opened the door.



Saban had the watcher's slip by six.

It was three lines in a hand nobody would look at twice. Bishop Isidore of the chapter, up from the station on foot, 10.40. Seen at the box room 11.02 to 11.43. Left with his clerk, to the station, to the 4 o'clock. Letter delivered; not carried out. The prince saw him to the door. The prince stood in the door a while after.

And under it, in a different hand, Lysa Orme's, from the station desk, where she'd sat since the morning with her palm flat on the card bank's reading plate as the bishop's card went in, the way Armand had shown her:

Read him at the plate. Cold. Not one of theirs. He couldn't have walked anybody. L.

Saban read it twice. Then he folded it in four and put it inside the band of his hat, and put the hat on the table in front of him, and sat in the long room with the lamps coming up and looked at the hat for a long time. Corbin's door was shut. A line of light showed under it.

At ten the Well sent him the king's message from the wall water. He read it, and wrote his answer on the back of the cipher pad, and looked at the hat again, and then he added the last line, and gave it to the girl to send.

Nothing here that wouldn't keep till Friday. That was true. He'd see on Friday whether it had.



It kept till Saturday.

The ships came in on the Friday evening in a squall, all three of them, the K Endeavor first with the Challenger in her cradles and Fletcher standing on the Challenger's deck under her keel in the rain with his arms folded, and then Zale's ship and the K Tam together out of the west a little after dark, low and dripping, with their lamps on. Emeric came down the gangway with Ferra and Imani and the smell of the far sea still on them, and went to bed, and slept like a man hit with a spade. Harrold's train came in from the capital at eleven. Nobody told anybody anything. Corbin's light was on late.

The long table sat at nine on the Saturday morning, the way it always did in the weeks the king was home, with the March light coming in grey through the tall windows and the coffee on the sideboard and Harrold at the foot, in his coat, because the regent sat in when he was in Kaldo and he'd been in Kaldo four days of the last nine. Ferra had her book. Saban had his hat. Ward had his chair pushed back from the table by a hand's breadth, the way he always sat, so that he could stand up without moving it.

Corbin came in last.

He didn't go to the chair. Emeric was home, so the chair was the king's; he went to his own place halfway down on the window side and stood behind it, with a folded letter in his hand, and didn't sit. Ferra, who was about to open the book, didn't.

"Before the sheets," said Corbin. "If I may. It's mine and it runs across works, so it goes to the table." He didn't look at Saban. "Bishop Isidore of the cathedral chapter came to see me on Wednesday, at eleven, in the box room. He was there forty minutes. He offered me the crown."

Nobody moved.

"Not in so many words," said Corbin. "He was careful. He said the Church will say from every pulpit in the kingdom that the realm has a king while the king's at sea, and that it's the old king's eldest son, and that I need do nothing but not refuse it when it's offered. That it's half said already. That when it's done my mother comes off her chain and goes home to the capital to a house of her own." He put the letter on the table in front of him, seal up. "This came with it. His Holiness's seal. I haven't opened it. I thought somebody ought to be watching when it was opened who wasn't me."

He stood there behind his chair with his hands at his sides.

"I told him no," he said. "That's the short of it. The long of it is that he knew something only Augustine knew. Something I told Augustine in confession when I was eleven. About a stair." He paused. "So whatever wears Augustine has all of him. It sent a kind old man to give me back a thing of mine, as a present, so I'd know it remembered me. I'd like that written down somewhere, by somebody, because it's the most frightening thing anybody's ever said to me and I've been in a delve with my brother." He looked at Ferra. "And then he blessed me. Or tried to. The words. The ones before a Seeing."

Imani's hand had stopped halfway to her cup.

"I didn't say them," said Corbin. "I wanted to. I'll say that too, so it's said. My mouth wanted to. It nearly did." He sat down at last, in his own chair, quite slowly. "That's all. I'm sorry it's Saturday. I wanted it in front of all of you and not in one ear. And I wanted to see whether I'd still say no by Saturday. I have." He looked down the table at Saban for the first time. "You'll have known since Wednesday."

Saban took his hat off the table. He took the slip out of the band, and unfolded it, and laid it on the wood beside the letter, and turned it round so that Corbin could read it.

"Since six on Wednesday," he said. "I'd have asked you at noon today."

"I know," said Corbin. "I was going to make you wait till eleven."

Somebody laughed. It was Bram, a short bark like Maren's, and he stopped it at once and looked at the ceiling.

Ferra had read the slip upside down. She read the second hand on it, and looked up at Corbin.

"He wasn't a delver," she said. "Lysa read him at the plate. He couldn't have walked you. If you'd said the words, nothing would have come in."

Corbin was quiet for a moment.

"I didn't know that," he said.

"No," said Saban. "You didn't."

At the foot of the table Harrold hadn't moved at all. He was sitting with his big square hands on the table in front of him, looking at his eldest son halfway down the window side, and his face was doing a thing Emeric had never seen it do, which was nothing whatever, very hard, the way a man holds a full cup in a moving cart.

"They were offering you my chair," said Harrold.

"Yes, sir."

"And your brother's crown."

"Yes, sir."

Harrold looked at him a while longer. Then he nodded, once, the way he nodded at a paper that was correctly drawn, and looked down at his hands, and didn't say anything else, and Emeric understood that he couldn't.

Ward pulled his chair in by the hand's breadth.

"That's what slow looks like," he said, to nobody, and pulled the first sheet off Ferra's pile.



Emeric didn't hear much of the sheets.

He sat in the chair at the head of the table with the papers going round him, Cade's rivet steel and Ilsa's escrow and the Isle and the stores and the K Tam's report in Nell Haddow's square fist, and said yes and no and in a week, and wrote his initial where Ferra's finger went; and all the while, underneath it, he was on the stair.

Not on it. At it. He went to it the way you go to a sore tooth, and there it was, the way it had been since February in Imani's back room: the palace, the tower, the long stone stair going up to his mother's door with the light coming down it, the bottom step. A frame. Every edge of it, the worn place in the stone, the smell of the wax on the banisters, the angle of the afternoon. And nothing in it. A small boy had sat there, his brother; his brother at eleven had told a priest about it; a man who was gone now had kept it for thirty years and more; and a thing in a pope's chair had sent it back across the kingdom wrapped in an old man's kindness to buy a crown with. And the one person it had been sent to had said no. And the other person who'd ever held it, who'd been in the room with that small boy every afternoon and had known why he sat there, had cut it out of himself with his own hand and put it in a stone on his brother's ankle, so that it could kill him.

He'd kept it there three weeks after he knew what it had cost. He'd argued it badly at this table and they'd let him. Imani had said: When you burn it, it'll be because you've decided to. Not because I found it.

He looked down the table at his brother, who was reading Cade's paper about rivet steel with every appearance of interest, and who had sat in a room on Wednesday with the oldest key there was being turned in the oldest lock in him and had held the door shut from the inside, not knowing it couldn't have opened, not knowing that if it had, the stone on his ankle might have decided he'd given the Church a way in and stopped his heart.

He decided.

It wasn't a large thing, when it came. He'd thought it would be. It was a small thing, very plain and very quiet, like setting a cup down on a table. He'd been carrying it so long he'd thought it would be heavy.

When the table rose at half past eleven he said, "Corbin," and his brother stopped at the door.

"Majesty."

"The small room. Have you a quarter of an hour?"

Corbin looked at him. The quarter's check had fallen due on the Wednesday, while the king was at sea, and his face said that he'd noticed it hadn't been done, and that he didn't think this was it. He didn't say so.

"I've a quarter of an hour," he said.

Ferra had got up with the others. She'd picked up her book. She stood by her chair with it under her arm and her other hand in the pocket of her jacket, and Emeric looked at her, and she looked back, and took her hand out of her pocket, and opened it.

There was a key in it. A plain steel key on a loop of red tape, with a paper tag on the tape that said, in her hand, sundry.

"Since the twelfth of February," she said. "Every day. It's been wearing a hole."



The small room was the same as it had been in December. Two chairs facing, a table between with nothing on it, a window onto the upper ward. A tram went by with its bell. Corbin sat down in the chair by the window, the one he always took, and put his foot up on the low stool without being asked, so that the anklet showed above his boot, the gold leaves on it catching the grey light; and held out his hands on the table, palms up, out of habit, and then looked at them, and put them back in his lap.

"It's not a check," he said.

"No."

Ferra had come in behind them and shut the door, and stood against it.

Emeric didn't sit. He went down on one knee by the stool, the way you'd kneel to look at a horse's foot, and put his hand on his brother's ankle, over the band, over the gold leaves, with his palm on the flat of the drain stone where it lay against the skin.

Corbin went very still.

The stone was cool under his hand. An old stone and a dumb one, that had done one thing for three years, which was take what a man threw and give it to the air. And in it, now, a second thing. He went into it through the mana that ran between his brother's skin and the stone, the way he'd gone in on the fourth of December, and found it at once, because it was the only thing in there that was shaped like him.

It didn't know him.

That was the thing he hadn't been ready for. He'd thought, if he'd thought about it at all, that it would be like finding a coat of his own in somebody else's cupboard: his shape, his smell, his. And it was his shape. It was a piece of him the size and grain of the rest of him, sitting in the dark of the stone with its three conditions and its one act folded up in it and nothing else, no room in it for anything else, the way he'd made it. But it didn't turn when he came in. It didn't know he was there. He'd cut the line, and it had no line home, and it looked at him, if it looked at anything, the way a stone looks at a hand. It was waiting. It had been waiting for three months on his brother's ankle for its three conditions to come, and it would have gone on waiting for the rest of Corbin's life, and when one of them came, it would have done the one thing, and stopped his heart, and burned itself to nothing, and never once known that the man it was made of had wanted it not to.

He thought, quite clearly: that's what she is. Two thousand years of these. That's what it's like to be her.

Then he stopped thinking, and burned it.

He didn't do it with a spell. There wasn't a spell for it. He did it the plainest way there was, the way a delver drains a stone by touch: he opened his hand on it and drew. He drew the mana out of the drain stone through his palm and into himself, the stone's whole store, every grain of it, all the years of what Corbin had thrown and it had held and given to the air, all the little it had kept for itself; he drew it the way you'd draw water out of a well with your own two arms until the bucket comes up dry. The stone emptied under his hand. It went lighter, and lighter, and he felt it start to go: the long fine lattice of it in the dark slackening, a grain at a time, the way the ice goes in a pond in March. The piece of him inside it didn't move. It didn't try to. It didn't know how. It sat where he'd put it with its conditions folded in it and the stone went out from under it, and then there was no stone, and then there was no piece. It went the way a match goes out in a cupped hand. No sound. No light. Nothing came back up his arm to him. He'd known nothing would. He'd known for three months. He reached for it anyway, as it went, the way you'd put your hand out for a cup you've knocked off a table, and there was nothing to reach.

He went to the stair. It was still a frame.

He took his hand away.

The drain stone was grey under the gold leaves. Then it was white. Then, as he watched, it fell in on itself, quite softly, and ran out of its setting between the leaves in a little trickle of fine pale dust, like the sand in a glass, onto the top of his brother's sock and the cloth of the stool; and the setting was empty, a little hollow in the gold the shape of a thumbnail, and that was all.

Corbin was looking at the dust.

He looked at it for a long time. He didn't move his foot. He didn't say anything. The light from the window lay across his face, and Emeric, on one knee on the boards beside him with the dust on his own fingers, looked up and saw his brother's face, and saw that Corbin knew. Not what. Not all of it. But that something had been in there, near him, in December, at the check that was longer than usual, when there had been a lot of dust in the chapel; something that wasn't Augustine's and wasn't his own; and that it was gone now, and that it had gone this way, in his brother's hand, in front of him, on purpose.

Corbin lifted his eyes from the dust to Emeric's face.

He held them there a moment. Then he looked away, at the window, at the upper ward, and said nothing at all.

Ferra came away from the door.

She knelt down on the other side of the stool, with her book on the floor beside her, and put the plain steel key into the plain steel lock in the band's inner face, where nobody but the three people who'd ever seen it fitted knew there was a lock at all; and turned it. It was stiff. It hadn't been turned in three years. She turned it with both hands, and the hinge gave, and the band opened like a mouth, and she lifted it off his brother's leg in both hands and held it there between them, gold outside and grey steel within, with the empty setting in it, warm from his skin.

Under it his ankle was white, a band of white the width of three fingers, softer than the rest of him, printed all round with the pattern of the leaves.

Corbin looked down at it.

He put his hand down, after a while, and touched the white place with two fingers, very lightly, the way you'd touch a bruise to see if it still hurt.

Ferra wrote in her book. She wrote it on her knee, kneeling on the floor, and Emeric read it upside down: 7 Mar. Sundry: one key, returned. One band, off, C., in the small room, by E.'s hand and mine. The stone first. F. Then she shut the book, and got up, and put the band in the pocket of her jacket where the key had been, and stood by the door again.

Corbin took his foot off the stool. He put it down on the floor in its sock, carefully, as if the floor might have changed.

"Is that all, Majesty?" he said.

His voice was quite level. It was the voice he'd used at that door in December. Emeric heard it, and heard what it was holding up, and stood up off his knee and brushed the dust off his fingers onto his trousers, and some of it stayed.

"No," he said. "The Board sits at two. You're entered."

Corbin turned his head.

"Entered for what?"

"Your stone. Bonded. Service on the record." Emeric looked at Ferra, and Ferra looked at the ceiling. "Saban entered you in January. With a condition on it that it wasn't to be heard till the anklet was off, because a stone won't bond through a drain. Nobody told you, because Saban said it wasn't to be a carrot." He paused. "It's two o'clock. The anklet's off."

For the first time since the bishop had lifted his hand, Corbin's face did something.

It wasn't much. It was the face of a man who has been told the name of a horse he'd forgotten he'd backed. Then it went still again.

"Saban did," he said.

"In January."

"Before Wednesday."

"Before Wednesday," said Emeric. "Before any of it."

Corbin looked at the empty band of white at his ankle. He looked at the dust on the stool.

"Majesty," he said. And then, after a moment, as if trying it: "Emeric."

Emeric heard it and couldn't have said when he'd last heard it in that voice.

"My mother's," he said. "Her anklet. Will you..."

"Not yet," said Emeric.

Corbin waited.

"Not yet. Ask me again when I'm home."

His brother looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way their father did.

"I'll ask," he said.



The Board sat at two in the records hall, in the rain, as it did every other Saturday, with Master Aubin in the chair and Prudence Fairley on his left and the holdings steward on his right, and Armand and Imani at the ends, and the benches a third full, which for a wet Saturday was a crowd. Emeric sat on the back bench, where he always sat. Imani got up and went to the front bench before the case was called, because the man entered was her husband's brother and the rule was the rule.

Saban stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands and entered him.

It took eleven minutes. Saban read the record off one sheet, in the voice he used for bills of lading: the names given to the watchers at the keep in the first week, every one checked the slow way and every one good; the confessor in the crowd at the crowning, pointed out and not kept quiet; the chair since December and the box with eleven papers in it most mornings; a bishop, on Wednesday, at eleven, and what he'd offered, and what he'd been told. He didn't say what had been on Corbin's ankle, or what had come off it at noon. He said: "The terms were reviewed in writing on the date set. He's kept them. All of them. That's the record."

Prudence Fairley had the clause open in front of her. She'd read it, Emeric thought, every sitting since November.

"And not by birth," she said.

"By birth," said Saban, "he'd have had it at fourteen. By birth he'd have had a crown on Wednesday. He hasn't had either."

Prudence looked at him for a while. Then she looked at Corbin, standing in the doorway behind Saban in his good coat with his hands at his sides and one ankle, under the cloth, a band of white.

"Is he still in an anklet?" she said.

"Not since noon," said Saban.

Prudence wrote something. She looked at it. Then she put her pen down.

"Then I've nothing," she said, and sounded, Emeric thought, a little surprised at herself.

Granted, wrote the clerk, under Aubin's hand. Service on the record. The terms kept. Not birth; the board notes he was refused by birth twice and is granted on what he did. One abstaining (kin).



He swallowed it in the dome at six, on Zale's old board on the tree's north side, with the evening coming down blue through the glass and the tree humming over him.

Imani sat with him, as she'd sat with every one of them. Harrold sat on the bench by the north door in his coat and didn't move from it. Amara's body slept on its mattress against the trunk with the blanket to its chin, and her made one sat on the gravel a little way off with her knees drawn up and looked at her husband's eldest son, whom she'd known for eight years before she died and had always left the door open for; and Corbin, lying on the board in his shirtsleeves with the stone in his palm, looked back at her, once, for a long moment, and nobody in the dome said anything.

Then he swallowed it, and lay back, and shut his eyes.

Emeric stood at the dome's door until his brother was under. It took about a quarter of an hour. At the end of it Corbin's breathing went long and slow and deep, the way Zale's had, and Waylon's, and Imani looked up from her watch and nodded at the door, and Harrold on his bench put his hand over his face and kept it there.



He asked them that night.

He'd meant to ask them better. He'd had it in his head all the way back from the dome how he'd ask, which room, which words, and then he came up the keep stair at gone ten and opened the door of their rooms and they were both there, Ferra at the table in her nightgown with the book open and her stockinged feet on the stove's fender, Imani on the bed on her stomach with a medical paper and a pencil and her hair down, and he stood in the doorway and couldn't remember any of it.

Ferra looked up.

"You've not washed your hand," she said.

He looked down at it. There was still dust in the creases of his fingers, fine and grey and pale, and a little under the nails.

"No," he said.

Imani put her pencil down. She rolled over onto her side and looked at him, propped on one elbow, and didn't say anything, which from Imani at ten at night with a medical paper was a great deal.

He came in and shut the door and sat down on the end of the bed, by her feet, with the dusty hand on his knee.

"I couldn't have asked you yesterday," he said. "I want you to know that. I've wanted to ask since October, since the bay house. Since she showed me. I lay there that night between you and thought about it until it was light. And I couldn't. Not with a one-way piece of me on my brother's leg. I couldn't sit here and ask you for the other kind with that still in a stone." He looked at the hand. "It's dust now."

Neither of them said anything.

"The sharing," he said. "Ilenne's people's. One stone. All three of us at once. Each of us carrying the other two." He found he'd started talking too fast, the way he did at a bench when he'd got a thing nearly right, and made himself stop, and start again, slower. "Nothing lost. That's the whole of it. It's a closed loop; it can't take anything from either of you. You'd know where I was. A thousand miles out, under the sea, you'd know if I was hurt or frightened or near. And I'd know you. And you'd..." He looked at Ferra, and at Imani. "You'd have a bit of each other too. Not like... It isn't like that. It's not wanting. It's carrying." He stopped. "And you'd feel the place where Corbin was. Where the stair is. You'd feel it and you couldn't fill it. I can't give you that bit back. I'd be asking you to carry a hole."

He stopped again, because he'd run out.

Ferra looked at him across the room for a long moment. Then she licked her thumb, and turned back through her book, a long way, past the winter, past the sheets and the seconds and the roster, to October, and found a page, and turned the book round on the table, and pushed it across toward the end of the bed where he could read it.

It was a line in the bottom margin of the twenty-seventh of October, under One way costs the maker, in pencil, very small, the way she wrote things she wasn't ready to have read.

Three, through one? Not while the anklet's on. Wait for him to ask.

He looked at it.

"You've been waiting since October," he said.

"I've been waiting since the twelfth of February," said Ferra. "October I was only thinking about it." She took the book back. "Yes."

"You've not heard the..."

"I've heard all of it. I wrote it down the night she showed you. I've read Armand's notes on the pope's stones. I know what a hole is." Her chin came up. "Yes. It's mine to say and I'm saying it. Yes."

He turned to Imani.

Imani had been looking at his hand on his knee the whole time. She sat up now, slowly, on the bed, with her hair coming down round her shoulders, and reached out and took the hand, the dusty one, in both of hers, and turned it over palm up, and looked at the grey in the creases with her sight open. He felt her go over it, careful and narrow, the way she did.

"There's nothing in this," she said. "It's only dust."

"I know."

"I wanted to be sure." She didn't let go. "My mother carries my father. I've never once heard her say she wished she didn't." She looked up at him. "Yes. Of course yes. You great fool. I'd have said yes in October if you'd asked me in October, anklet or no anklet, and then I'd have made you take it off." She considered. "Which, to be fair, I did try."

"You did."

"I'm only saying."

Ferra had got up from the table. She came over and sat down on the bed on his other side, so that he was between them, the way he was every night, and took his other hand, the clean one, and laced her fingers into it, hard.

"Not tonight," she said.

"No."

"You've a brother under a stone in the dome and you've been on your knees in a small room and you're grey to the wrist." She looked at his face. "And you'll want to do it properly. And so shall I. And she'll want to have read everything that's ever been written about it, which is nothing, so she'll want to have read Armand's notes twice." Imani made a noise that admitted it. "When the lanterns go up. Lantern Night. That's three weeks. That's the last one before we go."

"Lantern Night," said Imani.

"Lantern Night," said Emeric.

Ferra let go of his hand long enough to reach behind her for the book, and the pencil, and wrote on the day's page under the key and the band, without hiding it this time, in her ordinary hand: Asked. Yes. Yes. 31 Mar. Then she put the book on the floor and lay back across the bed, and pulled him down by the clean hand, and Imani, on his other side, still holding the dusty one, said, "Wash that before you touch my face," and didn't let go of it at all.
