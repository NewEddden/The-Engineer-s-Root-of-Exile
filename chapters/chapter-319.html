Chapter 319 - Rods

The first rod fell flat.

It came out of the Hod's belly hatch at nine in the morning on the eighth of November, four miles above the old north quarry, and Emeric watched it go from the hatch's edge with his sense on it all the way down: a bar of iron eighteen feet long and as thick as a man's thigh, with a gravity stone set into its after end in a socket Bram had turned for it and coded to Heavy, so that it would not only fall, but be pushed, the whole way, at twice its weight. For a breath it went down straight, nose first, the way a thing that heavy ought to go. Then it began to turn. It turned slowly at first and then not slowly, end over end, tumbling like a stick thrown for a dog; and it went into the quarry floor four miles down sideways, flat, broadside on, with a sound that came up to the Hod a long time later as a flat dull crack like a door slammed in another house, and lay there.

They went down to look at it. It had made a long shallow trench in the quarry's floor, the length of itself and a foot deep, and broken some rock, and bent.

"That would have taken the roof off," said Ward, standing over it. "Of anything. And not the floor. And not the vault." He looked at the bend in it. "It wants to go in. Not lie down."

The second rod fell flat too. It had a pointed nose, filed to a cone by Bram's fitters overnight, because a pointed thing went in where a blunt thing lay down, Bram said, which everybody knew; and it went out of the hatch and turned over and came down sideways like the first, and lay in the quarry floor a hundred yards from the mark with its pointed nose sticking out of the trench like the head of a fish in a net.

The old north quarry had been worked out before Emeric came south. It lay in a fold of the hills above the Mere, a long deep bowl of grey stone with sheer faces on three sides and a cart road in on the fourth, and nobody had taken a block out of it in twenty years; and since August there had been a white cross painted on the middle of its floor, forty feet across, by Bram's boys with a bucket of lime, and a hut on its rim with a stove in it where Ward kept the drop book.

Emeric had chosen it because it was empty. He had stood on its rim in August and looked down at the floor for an hour, with his sense out, to be sure there was nothing living in it bigger than a fox; and then he had said, aloud, to Ward, to Ferra, to Bram, and to Linus, who had come up with the survey sheets, the thing he had decided he would say every time he came up to that rim.

"Never on the city," he had said. "Never on men. Never on the Isle."

Ward had written it at the top of the drop book's first page.

He said it again on the morning of the second rod, standing on the rim, looking down at the bent iron in the white cross; and Ward, beside him, did not write it again, because it was already written, but nodded.



He did not draw anything. That was the rule he had made for the rods in August, and he kept it, and it was harder than he had expected.

He knew what was wrong. He had known on the first rod's first turn, watching it tumble from the hatch; it was the thing that was wrong with any long heavy body falling a long way through air, that the air pushed harder on whichever end was ahead, and turned it, and went on turning it, unless something at the back caught the air better than the front did and held the back behind. He could have drawn it in a minute on the back of the drop book. He did not. He went up to the Hod's hatch for the second rod and watched it turn, and said nothing; and he came down to the hut on the rim and sat on the bench by the stove with his hands round a cup and said nothing; and he waited.

Sorrel came up the cart road on the ninth with a sack over her shoulder and her limp worse than usual from the hill, and sat down on the bench beside him without asking, and took four things out of the sack and laid them on the bench in a row.

They were darts. Little ones, the length of her hand, whittled out of pine, each with a lead weight bound to its nose with thread. The first had no tail. The second had a tail of two paper fins, the third of three, and the fourth of four, set round it like the feathers on an arrow.

"I've been throwing them off the long shed's roof," she said. "Since you sent me the drawing of what the first one did." She picked up the one with no tail and held it out at arm's length over the quarry's edge and let it go. It turned over in the air and fell sideways, tumbling, down the face. "That's yours." She picked up the one with four fins and let it go. It went down nose first, straight as a plumb line, all the way to the quarry floor, and stuck there in the grit, upright. "That's mine."

"Why?"

"Because the air pushes hardest on whatever's in front, and whatever's in front wants to swap with what's behind. Everything that falls wants to fall back end first, if the back end's heavier or slipperier." She dusted her hands. "An arrow's got feathers. A sycamore seed's got a wing. A falling leaf's got nothing, so it goes everywhere. Your rod's a falling leaf made of iron." She looked at him sideways. "You knew."

"I knew."

"And you didn't say."

"I wanted to see who would."

Sorrel considered this, and him, for some time, out of the corner of her eye, with the face she had used on him on the water stair in the year of the first gliders, when he had pushed the air like a door.

"You're a very irritating man," she said. "Four fins. Like the dart. Big ones. At the back. Out in the air, past the rod's own wind, or they'll do nothing." She stood up. "I'll want to see them before they're bolted on. Bram puts things on too close to themselves."



Fennick did the nose.

He had been waiting to. He had been waiting, Emeric thought, since the year he first blew a cliff face and found that a charge put against a thing did one thing and a charge with a hollow cone in the face of it did another, a thing he had called his flat-in-one-direction and spent ten years trying to make do what he wanted; and when he heard about the pointed nose on the second rod, lying in its trench like a fish's head, he came up the cart road on the tenth with a box of drawings under his arm and his white eyebrows going, and laid the drawings out on the hut's table and stood over them with both hands flat and talked for an hour.

A rod that fell four miles, he said, was not a spear. A spear went in because a man drove it. A rod went in because it was going so fast when it got there that the stone in front of it had nowhere to go but out of the way; and the point did not matter much, at that speed, or the shape, so long as the nose did not splash. That was what the pointed nose had done. It had hit and splashed, like a dropped egg, spread its own iron out sideways into a mushroom and stopped.

"You want it hard," said Fennick. "Harder than the rock. Much harder. So it doesn't splash; so it stays a point all the way in and the rock does all the giving." He tapped a drawing. "Tungsten. Bram's had it since the year of the lamp filaments, a little. It's twice the weight of iron for its size and three times as hard as anything we make. Not the whole rod; there's not that much in the world. A nose. A plug of it, a foot long, sleeved into the iron like a tooth in a jaw." He tapped another drawing. "And the stone at the back pushing the whole way down, not just Heavy at the top and leave it, but pushing, so it goes faster than falling, and faster again. And the fins."

"And?" said Ward.

"And then," said Fennick, and Emeric saw his face change, "we hollow it."

They looked at him.

"Behind the nose," said Fennick. "A chamber. Two feet of the rod made hollow and packed with charge; and a fuse in it, a delay, like the fuse on the cove's stick but short, a breath, less. The nose goes into the vault. The rod goes in behind it, all the way, through the stone, through the vault, into the crypt under it. And then the fuse." He took his hands off the drawings. "Then it goes off. Inside. Not on the roof. Not on the vault. Under them. A rod alone puts a hole through a building. This one puts a hole through it and then takes the building off the hole from the inside."

There was a silence in the hut.

"How many to bring down a cathedral," said Ward.

"Of the plain kind?" Fennick looked at the drawings. "A few dozen. Of these?" He did not answer at once. "Fewer," he said. "A good deal fewer."

Emeric looked at the drawings on the table. He had not drawn them. He had not said one word about them. Fennick had come up a cart road with a box under his arm and made them out of ten years of blowing up cliff faces and a thing he had found out by himself about a hollow charge, that Emeric had known a name for since before he came south and had never once said. And they were good. They were what a man would draw who had never seen a war fought from the sky and had worked out from the ground up what would bring a stone vault down from above with the fewest things falling.

Fewest. That was what he held onto, standing over Fennick's drawings in the hut with the stove going. Not that they could. That it would take fewer.

"Build it," he said.



Linus came up on the eleventh with forty years of wind.

He had kept it since before Kaldo had a survey office, since he was a young man on the river with a theodolite and a notebook, every day: the wind at the ground, morning and evening, its strength and its quarter, and later, when Kaldo had kites and then balloons and then ships, the wind aloft, at a thousand feet, at five thousand, at the Band. Forty years of it in a row of black books on a shelf in the survey office, and since the summer, in a set of tables he and the school's weather class had made out of the books for the drop, by height and by month and by the hour.

"A rod falling four miles," said Linus, "goes through every wind there is between here and there. And every one of them pushes it." He laid the tables on the hut's bench. "It's not much, at the speed it's going. But it's not nothing. Four miles of a westerly at the Band and a northerly at the ground, and your rod lands two hundred yards east and a hundred south of where you dropped it over." He put his finger on a column. "Unless you drop it two hundred yards west and a hundred north."

And Ivo ground the sight.

It was the last piece, and the one Emeric had wondered most who would make, because it was a thing of glass and nobody else in Kaldo could. It went in the Hod's hatch on a frame: a tube with lenses in it, looking straight down, with a cross of fine wire at its focus and a little brass dial on its side that turned the cross off its centre by Linus's tables, for the height and the wind and the speed the ship was going; so that a man lying on the hatch's floor with his eye to the tube saw the ground four miles below him, and the cross on the ground, and when the cross lay on the thing he meant to hit, he said now, and the rod went.

Ivo called it the drop sight. Emeric had a different name for it in his head, an uglier one, bombsight, and did not say it.



The third rod went out of the Hod's hatch on the fourteenth, at nine, with Ward lying on the hatch floor with his eye to Ivo's tube and his hand up.

It had Sorrel's fins on it, four, big, out past the rod's own wind on short struts, painted white. It had Fennick's nose, a plug of grey tungsten sleeved into the iron, plain, not hollow yet. The stone in its after end was set to push the whole way. And the Hod had come in over the quarry from the north-west, not straight, by Linus's tables, because the wind at the Band was out of the west that morning and the wind at the ground out of the north, and Ward's dial was set off its centre by an amount that looked to Emeric like nothing at all.

"Now," said Ward.

The rod went.

It went straight. It went out of the hatch nose down and stayed nose down, the fins catching the air and holding the back of it behind the front, so that it went down through four miles of sky like a plumb bob on a string; and Emeric, at the hatch's edge, had his sense on it the whole way and felt the stone in its tail push, and push, and the rod go faster than falling, and faster, until it was going faster than he had ever felt anything go that was not lightning; and it was a long time going down, and no time at all.

It went into the quarry floor a stride from the white cross's middle.

The sound came up to the Hod a long while later. It was not a crack this time. It was a deep thud, like a door shut very far underground; and from the hatch, through Ivo's tube, Ward saw the quarry floor jump, all of it, and settle, and a little dust go up off it, and nothing else.

They went down. There was a hole in the white cross a stride from its middle, round, the width of the rod, very clean, with the rock round its lip cracked out in a star. That was all there was to see. No rod. Linus dropped a lead line down the hole, and it went down, and went down, and they ran out of line at sixty feet.

Ward stood at the hole's lip.

"Sixty," he said.

"More," said Linus. "That's all the line I brought."

Ward looked across the quarry floor at the two bent rods in their trenches, the first and the second, and back at the hole.

"The bell tower," he said, "is a hundred and forty feet." He looked up at the sky where the Hod hung, small, very high, a dark speck. "They'll hear that in the capital from one end to the other. The whole city. And they'll see it come down."

"That's what it's for," said Emeric.

"I know what it's for." Ward did not take his eyes off the speck. "I'm saying it'll work."

The king had come up to see the third. He had asked to, at the table, the night before; and he stood on the quarry's rim in his brown coat with his hands behind his back, beside Ferra, and did not come down onto the floor to look at the hole. He looked at it from the rim, for a long time, as he had looked at Saban's chest under the Venn's in August, and Emeric, at the hole, felt his note up there, steady, with something going on under it that it took him a moment to place, because he had never felt it in his father before.

It was the note of a man doing a sum. A soldier's sum: what a thing like that would do to a man under it, and how far off a man would have to stand. Harrold had been a knight before he was a king. He had stood in a line, once, as a boy, and watched things come at it.

Then the note settled. The king turned to Ferra and said something Emeric could not hear, and Ferra wrote it in her book, and the king went back to the cart road and down it, walking, alone, without waiting for anybody.

"What did he say?" Emeric asked her, that night.

Ferra did not look up from the book. "He said, now I understand the four hundred yards." She turned a page. "And then he said, make it six."



Escort one tried her keel battery on the fifteenth.

That was the other thing the fleet could do straight down, and Fletcher had been waiting to see it since the spring. The escorts could not fire their turrets out of their sides in the air any more than the K Amara could without a pin; and they had no pins yet, because the pin stones for the escorts were in the stone book's second line and Tess's hands and Emeric's were full. But a gun pointed straight down did not swing a ship on her handle. It pushed her straight up, the way a man on a cart pushes himself up off the seat if he throws a sack down off the tail; and a ship that hung from a gravity matrix that pushed only up and down could take a push straight up, and let it go, and come down again, and be where she had been.

So escort one hung over the quarry at a mile, with her keel battery's six short barrels pointing at the white cross from her belly, and fired them.

She went up. Emeric was on the rim, and he saw her go: six barrels firing together, and the whole black hull of her jumping up off its place in the sky by the height of a house, straight up, level, like a lid lifted off a pot; and then her stone-keeper, the woman at her matrix's levers whose only job was to keep her where she had been told to be, took the jump in the matrix and let her down, smoothly, the height of a house, onto her place again, and held her there. In the quarry, the white cross had six new holes in it. Not deep. Ugly.

Fletcher, on the rim beside Emeric, let out his breath.

"She'll do," he said. "Straight down she'll do." He looked at the six holes. "Not as clean as the rod."

"Nothing's as clean as the rod."

"No." Fletcher looked at him sideways. "That's what worries me about it."



Sorrel came to the hut on the sixteenth to watch Bram's men bolt the next set of fins onto the next set of rods, and to tell them they were too close.

They were too close. Bram's men took them off and put them on again further out, on longer struts, while Sorrel stood over them with her hands on her hips and her bad leg cocked; and when they were done, and she had tugged each fin with both hands and looked along it with one eye shut, she came and sat on the bench in the hut by the stove, beside Emeric, where she had sat with her darts, and was quiet a while.

Bren and Mira were in the hut too. They had come up from the K Amara's aprons on the Hod with the rods that morning, because Ward had given the three of them to the drop crew for the week as hands, and they were sitting on the floor by the door with their backs against the wall eating Maren's bread out of a cloth.

"You said something that pushes," said Sorrel.

Emeric looked at her.

"The first summer. On the hill. After the first one flew." She was not looking at him. She was looking at the fins on the rods through the hut's open door, white, on their struts. "I said I wanted to go up off the flat and not just off a hill, and keep going, and land where I liked. And you said then you'll want something that pushes." She folded her hands on her bad knee. "That's six years. And I've been making wings that don't push, because that's what I know. Gliders. Air's a river; you lean on it. You don't push it." She looked at the fins. "But those push. In a way. They catch the air going by and they push back on it, and that holds the rod straight." She turned her head and looked at him at last. "So what pushes a wing?"

He had known she would ask. Not today; some day. He had known since the hill. He had a whole world of answers for it in his head, a whole century of them, every engine and every airframe and every mistake anybody had ever made, and he had decided on the hill, watching her fly the first one off the slope with her bad leg tucked under her, that he would give her one answer at a time, when she asked, and only the one she asked for, because everything he gave her would be asked about later by somebody else, and because it was hers.

"A wing that goes round," he said.

Sorrel looked at him.

"Take a wing," he said. "A short one. Two of them, on a shaft, one each side, like the arms of a windmill but small and shaped like your wings are shaped, curved on top, flat underneath. And turn the shaft. Fast." He held up his hand, flat, and turned it slowly on the wrist. "A windmill's the wind turning the wing. This is the other way. The wing turning in the air, pulling itself forward through it, the way a wing pulls itself up. And whatever the shaft's fixed to comes with it."

Sorrel's eyes had gone very far off. He could see her see it, the way he had seen Tess see the cloud: the turning wing, the air coming off the back of it, the pull.

"A propeller," he said. "That's what it's called. Where I read about it."

"Turned by what?"

He said nothing. He looked at her, and said nothing, and waited.

Sorrel looked back at him. And then she laughed, short and furious, and turned round on the bench to where Bram had just come in at the hut's door with a coil of strut wire over his shoulder.

"Bram," she said. "What's the lightest engine you've got?"

Bram stopped in the door. He looked at her, and at Emeric, and at the two of them sitting on the bench together with Emeric's hand still up in the air, flat, turned on its wrist; and he did not ask what they had been talking about.

"For what?"

"To turn a shaft. Fast. On a wing."

Bram thought about it. He put the coil of wire down on the floor.

"Lightest I've got that you'd trust in the air," he said, "is a band engine off a pump, and it weighs what a man weighs." He looked at her. "Every pound of engine you put in that wing of yours is a pound the wing has to carry. And the fuel. And the shaft. And the thing you've put on the end of the shaft." He picked up the wire. "Find me a wing that'll carry a man and a man's weight of engine and you can have one. Then we'll talk about making the engine lighter. That's a long road."

"I've got time," said Sorrel.

"You'll need it," said Bram, and went out to the rods.

Sorrel sat on the bench with her hands on her knee, looking at nothing. Then she reached down and picked up a splinter of pine off the hut's floor, a long thin shaving off one of the darts, and held it between two fingers, flat, and turned it, slowly, in the air in front of her face; and then turned it on its back.

She held it flat, the long way up, between her finger and thumb, like the arms of a windmill laid on its side, and turned it round and round, horizontally, above her other hand.

"If it pulls a thing along," she said slowly, "it'd pull a thing up. Pointed up. A wing going round, flat, over your head. It'd pull you straight up off the ground. You wouldn't need a hill. You wouldn't need to run. You'd go up off a cart's tail like a sycamore seed going backwards." She turned the shaving. "And sit there. In the air. Over one place."

"The stones already do that," said Bren, from the floor by the door, with his mouth full.

Sorrel looked at him.

"The deck stones," said Bren. "On the Hod. On the black barge. Up, down, sit there over one place. Without a noise." He swallowed. "For a charge."

Sorrel looked at the shaving in her fingers for a while. Then she put it down on the bench, and picked it up again, and turned it a different way, flat, and held it under her other hand like a lid.

"A boat," she said. "On air. Blow air down under a boat, a lot of it, through a skirt round the bottom, so it sits on a pillow of air over the water and nothing touches. No drag. It'd go over mud, over sand, over the Mere's shallows where you can't take a lighter."

"So does a hull on stones," said Mira.

She said it kindly. She was sitting with her back against the wall and her plait over her shoulder and her bread in her lap, and she said it the way she had said, it is your hair growing, to Bren in the yard: as a thing that was simply so.

Sorrel looked at the shaving.

Emeric, beside her on the bench, said nothing at all. He had two words in his head for the two things she had just made out of a pine shaving in a quarry hut in a morning, and they were both Earth's, and they were both the names of machines that had taken Earth's best men forty years and a war to make work, and that nobody in this world would ever need to make, because a gravity stone in a frame already did what they did, silently, without a blade turning; and he sat on the bench with the two words in his head and did not say either of them.

Sorrel put the shaving down.

"Forward, then," she said. "It pulls forward. That's all I want from it. The stones can have up." She stood up off the bench, with her hand on the hut's door frame for her leg. "The stones can have up and they can have sitting in one place. I want a thing that goes forward through the air, on a wing, with nothing in it that a dead zone can stop." She looked at Bram's back, out at the rods. "A band engine stops in a dead zone."

"It's shut," said Emeric. "Not dead. It'll start again."

"It'll start again on the ground, after I've hit it," said Sorrel. "Find me one that doesn't stop." She looked at him. "That's the next thing I'll ask you. Not now. When I've got a wing that'll carry a man's weight of engine." She went out of the door. "You'll have the face again."



Armand saw it at supper on the seventeenth, and it was the first time anybody but the three of them had.

They were in the long room, all of them, the six and Armand and the king, because it was the night before the leaflets went to Hobb's press and Ferra wanted every word on the sheet read aloud once at the table before a single letter was set; and Armand was at the head of the table in his chair with his knees up, and Ferra was reading. Imani was beside her. Ferra read the front of the sheet, and then Doran's letter, which Doran had written out fair himself in his old upright hand and sent up the hill that afternoon with a boy; and then she stopped, and put the sheet down, and looked down the table at Armand.

"And then yours," she said. "The proof. You've not given it me yet."

"I'm giving it you now," said Armand.

He did not. He was not looking at her sheet. He was looking at her, with his chin on his hand and his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, the way he looked at a stone he had been given to read and found more in than he had been told; and then at Imani, beside her; and then back at Ferra, slowly.

Emeric felt him do it. Armand had a sense that went further than anybody's in Kaldo but his own; and when Armand put the whole of it on a thing, at a table, without moving, it went into the thing like water into sand, and you could feel it go if you knew how. It went into Ferra. It went into Imani. It was in them a long time.

Then Armand took his chin off his hand.

"You're both heavier," he said, "than I left you."

The long room went quiet.

"Last winter," said Armand. "I read you both, at this table. Your numbers, the pair of you. Ottoline put them on your cards. Commoner's numbers, both, good ones, the sort of number I'd give a strong woman who's never been near a stone." He looked from one to the other. "And they're not that now. They're more. Not a great deal more. More than either of you could be by eating well and sleeping." He put his spectacles back down onto his nose. "Commoners' numbers don't move. I've been reading them sixty years. They don't move."

Nobody said anything.

Ferra had put her sheet down. Her colour had come up from the throat, a little. Imani's face had not changed at all. She took the two cards out of her pocket, where she had carried them since the night she brought them up the stair, and laid them on the table, side by side, and pushed them up the board toward Armand with two fingers.

Armand looked at them. He picked one up and held it to the lamp, and read the first number, and then the second, the one under it, the one that had never been on a card before; and his heavy brows went up.

"What's this?"

"The room," said Imani.

Armand looked at her over the card.

"How much of it's empty," said Imani. "Today. Not what you could hold; what's left to fill." She folded her hands. "You never read that."

"No," said Armand. "No, I never did." He put the card down very carefully. "I never once could." He looked at her for a long moment, and then at Ferra, and then, last of all and for longest, down the table at Emeric, who looked back at him and said nothing.

"I see," said Armand.

He did not say what he saw. He picked up his wine and drank some of it, and put it down, and took a folded sheet out of the inside pocket of his long dark coat and held it out down the table to Ferra.

"The proof," he said. "As promised. Signed." He settled back in his chair. "Read it to us, if you'd be so kind. I'd like to hear how it sounds in somebody else's voice before forty thousand people read it in their own."

Ferra took it. She did not read it at once. She sat with it in her hand and looked at Armand, and then at Imani's two cards lying on the table between them, and Emeric could see her doing a sum, and could see that she had not got to the end of it, and that she was not going to try to get to the end of it at a table with the king on the bench and Bram at the corner.

The king had not said anything. He was on the bench by the wall, where he always sat, and he was looking at Imani with a faint frown, the way a man looks at a thing he has seen before somewhere and cannot place. Then the frown went, and he looked away, at the window, and said nothing.

Ferra unfolded Armand's sheet, and began to read.
