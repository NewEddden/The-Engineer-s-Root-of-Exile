Chapter 87 - The Cursed Shafts

The rock was not what the survey said it was, which Ferra had told him on the quay, and he wrote it to her on the ninth day.

He wrote to her every evening, as she had asked, on the relay from the post above the tally house, in the Grayrock line's marks, and he wrote what had been done and what had been found and never what he had meant to do. Crosscut advanced four feet. Rock hard, grey, no water. Crosscut advanced three feet. A band of harder stuff, the survey calls it slate and it is not slate. Crosscut advanced one foot and a half. The survey is wrong about the band. She sent back, each morning, a single line acknowledging the day's figure and nothing else, and on the ninth morning her line said: Told you. He kept that one.

The crosscut ran from the foot of the nearest live working on the east side, the one the survey showed less than a stone's throw from the foot of the sealed shaft, which Hask's men called the Low Drift and had been working at its upper end for a dozen years and more without once going to the bottom, because the bottom of the Low Drift was the nearest place on the hill to the cursed one, and nobody went nearer than they had to. Now forty men were at the bottom of it, in shifts, driving a hole through the rock toward a place none of them could see, with a lamp on a stick held out ahead of the face every hour and watched until it was pulled back.

There was nothing wrong with the air in the Low Drift. It had two mouths, as every working Hask's men went into had two, a shaft and an adit, and the air walked in at one and out at the other without anybody asking it to. That was the whole of it. It was what the hill did on its own when you let it, and it was why men could breathe in every working on Grayrock except one.

The sealed shaft had one mouth, and the mouth was covered.

"You don't fight dead air," he said to Hask, on the first evening, at the grassed-over head of it. "You move it. And the cheapest thing in the world for moving air is a fire."

He drew it in the dirt with a stick.

The sealed shaft, going down. The Low Drift beside it, going down. The crosscut joining their feet, the thing they would spend five weeks on. And at the head of the sealed shaft, a chimney of boards, a tall square flue, twice the height of a man and then again, with a fire at the foot of it.

"The fire burns. Hot air goes up the chimney. It has to be replaced from somewhere. So the chimney pulls." He drew an arrow up the flue. "It pulls on the shaft. The shaft pulls on the crosscut. The crosscut pulls on the Low Drift, which is full of good air from the hill. Good air goes in at the Low Drift and walks down it and along the crosscut and up the sealed shaft and into the chimney, and it pushes the dead air ahead of it all the way. It's a river. You don't carry the water out of a pond. You cut it a channel and let it go."

Hask looked at the drawing.

"The fire's at the top," he said.

"The fire's at the top."

"Then what's burning it? You said the air coming up the old shaft is the dead stuff. You put a fire in the dead stuff, it goes out." Hask was an old man and a careful one, and he had worked the east side before it was closed, and he was not stupid in any direction Emeric had yet found. "Same as a lamp."

"Same as a lamp." Emeric drew it again, bigger, the chimney and the fire and the shaft's head. "So the fire doesn't breathe the shaft. It breathes the hill. There's a door in the side of the chimney at the bottom, at the fire, and the fire draws its own air from outside, off the grass, clean. The shaft comes into the chimney here." He drew a second opening, higher up, above the flame. "Above it. The dead air comes up out of the shaft and into the chimney where it's already hot, and the heat takes it and carries it up and out. The fire never touches it. It's a stove with a second pipe."

Hask looked at that for a long time. Then he held out his hand for the stick.

Emeric gave it to him, and scuffed the drawing out with his boot.

"Draw it back to me," he said.

Hask did not like it. He was old and a captain, and he had not been told to draw anything since he was a boy. But he took the stick and crouched and drew it, slowly, the shaft and the drift and the crosscut and the chimney, and when he came to the fire he stopped, and put the fire at the foot of the chimney, and then stopped again, and drew the second opening above it, and an arrow going in, and an arrow going up.

"There," he said.

"That's it."

"A man can't build a thing he can't draw." Emeric took the stick back. "That's the only rule I've got about drawings."

Hask looked at the lines in the dirt a while longer. Then he looked up.

"You drew this before," he said. "On your knee, up here. Two years back. Upside down, to me. I asked you what it was."

"I said it was a way to make it safe."

"You said one day." Hask got up, stiffly, a hand on his knee. "I didn't think you meant it."

That was all he said about it, then or ever.

The chimney took a week to build and the crosscut took five, and he spent most of the time between the two, standing in the bottom of the Low Drift watching the face, or standing at the sealed head watching the carpenters, or sitting in the tally house in the evening writing a line to Ferra in marks and reading her line back in the morning. Grayrock came to look at the chimney. Men who had not been up the east path since the working was closed walked up it after their shifts and stood at a distance, looking at a tower of boards on a grassy mound with nothing in it yet, and went away again, and he heard in the evenings, from Ward, what they were saying. They were saying the curse would not care about a chimney. They were saying the prince had come to open the door on something that had killed two men and would kill more. They were not saying it with any malice. They were saying it the way men say a thing they know.

He had Hask say one thing to them, at the tally house, at the end of the second week, so that it came from a man they knew and not from a prince.

"There's no curse in it," Hask said, to twenty men with their caps in their hands. "He says there's no mana in this and no stone and no prince. A fire and two holes and a man to feed it. That's what he says." Hask looked round at them. "I've drawn it. It's a stove."

Some of them believed him. Some of them did not. All of them went up the east path the next evening to look at the chimney again.

On the twenty-fourth day he told Ward what was in the shaft.

He had told him the shape of it before, on the road, in a sentence. Now he told him properly, sitting on the spoil bank above the sealed head with the evening coming down, because Ward was going to be at the top of that shaft for every hour of what came next and a man should know what he was standing over.

"It's not cursed," he said. "It's spent."

Ward waited.

"There's a part of the air that a body burns. The way a fire burns charcoal. You breathe it in and your blood takes it and uses it and you breathe out what's left, and what's left is no good to anybody. Out here it doesn't matter, because the wind brings more. In a still deep place with no draft, it matters. Every lamp that ever burned down there used some of the good part up. Every man who worked down there used some. And the timber." He nodded at the grass. "Renner sealed it with the timber still in it. Wood rots. Rotting's a slow fire. A dozen years and more of slow fire in a closed hole, eating the good part of the air and never giving any back. What's left is heavy. It's heavier than good air. So it sinks, and it lies at the bottom, and it fills up from the bottom like water filling a well."

"Heavy air," said Ward.

"You can't see it. It doesn't burn. It doesn't smell. That's the part that kills people." He picked up a stone and turned it over. "At the edge of it, a man pants. His head aches. He tells himself he's tired, it's been a long shift, and he sits down for a moment to get his breath. At the bottom of it he takes two breaths and lies down, and doesn't know he's doing it, and doesn't get up. That's the curse. Two men went in against Renner's order and did exactly that."

Hask, sitting a little way off on the spoil with his pipe unlit, did not say anything. Emeric had not asked him to go away, and did not now.

"The lamp's the instrument," Emeric said. "A flame needs the good part of the air the same way a man does. Lower a lamp into it on a line, and when it goes into the dead air it sags. The flame goes small and blue and lies down. Pull it back up, and it stands again. There's a line in the shaft, a level, like the surface of a pond. Above it you can breathe. Below it you can't. The flame draws it for us."

"And that's all."

"No." He put the stone down. "I don't trust it all the way. A flame can die where a man could stand for a while, because a flame wants more of the good part than a man does. That one's safe, it errs toward keeping you out. But there are kinds of bad air a flame doesn't mind and a man does, and if there's any of that down there the lamp will burn quite happily while the man holding it falls over." He did not know how he knew that, and did not pretend to himself that he did. "So I don't move on the flame alone. I move when the flame and the other thing agree."

Ward did not ask what the other thing was, and Emeric did not say.

It was the sense he had found in the hold of a boat on the Deep Channel, bound and hooded, when he had read two men's bodies through the dark by something that was not sight. It was fainter here and he had not known it would work at all until the first night he had stood on the grass over the sealed head and reached down. The dead air did not show in the medium the way a man did. It showed the way cold water shows to a hand put into a pond in the dark: a thickening, a change of weight, a level you could feel your way to the top of without seeing. It was faint and he did not trust it much. But it was a second instrument, and it read the same line the flame read, and where two instruments agree a man can move.

"Below that line," Emeric said, "you don't go. For anything. Whatever's happening. If I'm lying at the bottom of it, you don't go."

Ward looked at him for a long moment.

"Noted," he said.

They lit the fire on the thirty-first day and holed through on the thirty- third.

The fire went first, because it had to. The seal at the head of the shaft came off on the thirtieth day, timber and earth barrowed away from the mouth by men who worked fast and did not look down, and the chimney's second opening was built down over the hole and made tight with clay. Then the fire, in its own hearth at the chimney's foot, breathing through its own door off the grass. For two days it burned and drew and pulled on a shaft with only one mouth, and a lamp lowered on a line into the old shaft from the chimney's hatch still sagged and died a man's height below the head. The dead air had no way out. The chimney had nothing to pull through.

On the thirty-third day, at the bottom of the Low Drift, the crosscut face broke.

It broke on the morning shift, a pick going through into nothing, and the man with the pick stepped back so fast he fell over, and everybody at the face went back up the crosscut ten yards the way they had been told to, and Emeric went forward with the lamp on its stick.

He held it at the hole.

The flame leaned. It leaned away from him, toward the hole, toward the dark on the far side, the way a candle leans in a doorway when a room beyond has a fire in it. It stood tall and yellow and leaned, and it did not sag.

The air was going the right way. Out of the Low Drift, through the hole, into the foot of the old shaft, and up.

He stood there with the lamp for some time, feeling the draft on the back of his neck and the side of his face, a steady cool breath going past him into the dark. Behind him, ten yards back, forty men were watching a flame lean. Nobody said a word.

Then somebody at the back laughed, a short surprised sound, and somebody else said something about a stove, and the crosscut came alive with men talking all at once.

The fire ran four days. They fed it in shifts from the woodpile Hask had built beside the chimney, and every morning and every evening Emeric lowered the lamp down the old shaft on its line from the chimney hatch, and watched where it sagged, and wrote the depth to Ferra. The level went down. It went down faster than he had dared to hope on the first day and more slowly on the second, and as it went down a fire basket went down after it, an iron cage of burning coal on a chain from the hatch, hung in the shaft to heat the rising air and make the chimney pull harder. It never went below the point where the lowered lamp still stood tall. He checked it himself every time it was lowered. A fire in dead air goes out, and a fire basket that goes out in a shaft is a fire basket that has stopped pulling.

On the fourth evening he lowered the lamp to the full length of the line, to the foot of the old shaft, and it stood.

He went down on the fifth morning.

Not below the line. There was no line in the shaft any more. He went down on the ladders the carpenters had fixed as they went, with a lamp and a rope and Ward at the top, and Hask at the foot of the Low Drift with the crosscut men, and at the bottom of the old shaft he stood where no living man had stood since the closing, with good air going past his face toward the chimney, and looked at the gallery.

The shaft had come clean to the gallery mouth, and the flame stood there. Past the mouth the gallery ran downhill, and the draft went straight past it the way a river goes past a cellar door, and the flame sagged a pace in and died at three.

He stood at the mouth with the dead lamp in his hand and looked at the dark.

Of course it ran downhill. Renner had not sunk the deepest shaft in the district to reach somewhere a man could stroll into. The survey had followed the galleries down and down, into the foot of the hill, and the good iron had run down with them, and the thing under the iron had run down past everything. The deposit was not in the shaft. It was in the gallery, below the line, where the draft could not reach.

He drew it again that night, in the dirt outside the tally house, by lamplight, with Hask and Ward and three of the crosscut men watching.

A dead-end gallery running downhill was the worst ground in a mine to clear, and every miner on the hill knew it without being told. Dead air lay in it like water in the bottom of a jug, and a draft going past the mouth would never reach down into it on its own. You had to make it. You hung a screen, a wall of cloth or boards, down the middle of the gallery from the mouth to the far end, and you closed the gallery mouth with a door, so that the draft coming down the shaft had nowhere to go but down one side of the screen to the very end, round the end of it, and back up the other side. You walked the air to the end of the gallery and back, and it took the dead air with it.

"How long a screen?" Hask said.

"As long as the gallery."

"How long's the gallery?"

"The survey says eighty yards." He looked at the drawing. "The survey said the rock in the crosscut was slate."

Nobody laughed.

"And if it forks," said one of the crosscut men, "you need two screens. And you've hung the first one down the wrong side of the fork."

"Yes."

"So somebody has to go in and look."

"Yes."

There was a silence round the drawing. It was not the silence of men waiting to be told. It was the silence of men who had worked this hill all their lives and knew exactly who went into a gallery below the line, which was nobody, and exactly what happened to the ones who did.

"I'm going in," Emeric said.

He said it to the drawing. He did not look at anybody, because he did not want to see anybody's face while he said the rest of it, and there was a rest of it, and it was the only rule he held that was not an engineering rule, and he had held it since a night on a bench in the finishing shop, more than a year ago.

"I can feel where the line is. None of you can. I can feel where the dead air ends and where the rock is and where the gallery turns, in the dark, and you can't, and nobody in this barony can. That's not a boast. It's the only thing about this that isn't a drawing." He put the stick down. "I won't send a man into a danger only I can see. If I send one of you down there first, I'm sending a blind man into a room I've got the only lamp for. I've done that once already, on a bench, with a man's hands. It went where his hands would have been. I won't do it with a man's lungs."

The crosscut men looked at each other, and then at Hask, and Hask looked at the drawing and did not say anything.

Ward said something.

"No."

He said it quietly and without heat, the way he had said no in a corridor once to a request to watch a man sleep, and everybody round the drawing turned to look at him, because Ward did not often say anything in front of other men, and never that.

"You're the barony," Ward said.

"I'm not the barony. The Order."

"The Order's a sheet of paper." Ward did not raise his voice. "Everything in four baronies runs through you. The relay key runs through you. Half of what's in the notebook, nobody else can read. You wrote the Order because you know that, and you know the paper's not finished, because the old man asked you what happens if you die on a road and you didn't answer him." He looked at the drawing, at the gallery running down into the foot of the hill. "Everything you've built for ten years, you built so it wouldn't have one man in the middle of it that it couldn't lose. And you want to take the one man it can't lose and put him on the end of a rope in the only hole on this hill that kills everybody who goes in."

It was correct. Every word of it was correct, and Emeric sat by the drawing in the lamplight and looked for the answer to it and did not find one. Not because it was hidden. Because there was not one. The rule and the objection stood side by side in the dirt, both true, pointing in opposite directions, and he had built a barony on the principle that when two true things did that, you found the third thing that made them agree, and there was no third thing here.

"I know," he said.

"Then don't."

"I can't send anybody else."

"Then don't go."

"Then nobody goes, and the foundry stops next winter, and the ore's under that line with everything else."

Ward did not answer that. He did not say yes, either.

They sat by the drawing for a while longer, the six of them, and nobody said anything, and after a time Hask got up and knocked out his cold pipe and went off toward his cottage, and the crosscut men went off after him.

In the morning, when Emeric came out of the tally house, there was a rope lying on the grass by the chimney at the sealed head. A long one, new, hemp, coiled down in the careful flat figure-of-eight a miner uses when he means to pay a line out fast without a kink in it. Nobody had been told to lay it there. Nobody on the hill admitted to having done it.

Ward was standing beside it, looking at it, with his arms folded.

He had not said yes.
