Chapter 87 - The Holder of Grayrock

The river road to Grayrock ran north along the east bank for most of a day, through the barony's woods and then out of them, and Ferra rode it on a horse she disliked from the first mile.

It was a perfectly good horse. It was the quietest horse in the garrison's stable, a brown mare with a white foot whom Ward had chosen himself and who had never, so far as anybody knew, done anything to anyone. Ferra sat on it with her book in a satchel across her back and her hands too high on the reins and a face that said she was keeping a column on the horse, and the column was not going well. At the first halt she got down with her lips pressed together, and stood for a while with her hand on the saddle, and Emeric did not offer her his arm, because the last time he had she had told him the horse was the wrong shape, and he did not want to hear it again from this one.

"It's the wrong shape too," she said, to the mare, and got back on.

Ward rode behind them both, as he always did on a road, and said nothing for most of the day, and watched the far bank.

They had not been asked. Saban's man at Grayrock had carried the letter up the river in May, on a coal barge, and brought the answer down in June in the same barge, and the answer had been four lines in a careful clerk's hand, and the hand was not the old one. Grayrock would receive the prince of Kaldo, if the prince of Kaldo wished to come. It did not say what for. It did not say anything else.

The country changed in the afternoon. The woods thinned out to heath, and the heath went up into hills, bare brown hills with grey stone breaking through them, and the road climbed with them away from the river and came down again. Then there were spoil heaps. Long grey tongues of broken rock run out from the hillsides where men had tipped it from the workings for a hundred years and more, and the smoke of somebody's smelting hanging over a fold in the hills, and a cart track worn white, and the sound, carried down on the wind, of a hammer on stone somewhere out of sight.

Grayrock stood under its hill, where the river came closest to the workings. A town of grey stone, smaller than Kaldo and tidier, with a landing on the river below it and the barges drawn up at the landing, and a hall above the town, not a castle, a long plain stone hall with a slate roof and a yard in front of it.

On the east face of the hill, above the town and to one side of the other workings, there was a dark mouth in the rock, and it had been boarded across. Grey planks, gone silver with weather, nailed over the entrance from side to side and top to bottom, and a beam across the boards. Emeric looked at it from the road. Every other mouth on the hill had a track worn to it and a spoil heap under it. That one had grass growing up to the boards.

Ward looked at it too, and then at Emeric, and said nothing.



She received him in the hall.

It was a long room with a fire at one end and a table down the middle and nothing on the walls, and the light came in from windows along the yard side. She was standing at the end of the table nearest the fire when they came in. Forty, or a little past it. A dark dress, still, a year on. Her hair was grey at the front and nowhere else, and she held herself very straight and very still, the way people stand who have been told that they must not move and have decided to make a virtue of it. A tall girl sat at the side table under the windows with a pen and a book, her father's height and her mother's stillness, and did not get up.

The steward stood behind the girl's chair. He was the one who had not written to Orrin.

Bryda looked at Emeric for some time before she said anything. She looked at his face, and at his eyes, the crown's eyes, and then down at his hands, as if she wanted to know what they looked like.

"You killed my husband," she said.

"Yes."

She waited. He knew what she was waiting for, and did not give it to her. He had thought about it for most of a day on the road, and for most of a year before that, and he had known since he set out that he would not say he was sorry, because it would not have been true, and she would have known it was not true, and then they would have had nothing to talk about.

"He came to my town at night with fire," he said. "To a town full of people who had never done anything to him. He sat on his horse at the top of my ore yard and sent your men up my bridge, and they came, because he was there, and I knew they would stop if he wasn't. So I made sure he wasn't." He kept his eyes on hers. "I'd do it again. I'd rather not have had to."

Bryda did not look away. Nor did her face change. Behind her, at the side table, the tall girl's pen had stopped moving.

"Sit down," said Bryda, after a while. "All of you. There's food coming. You've been on a horse all day, and your clerk has been on one she doesn't like."

Ferra, who had been standing at Emeric's shoulder with her book against her chest, went very slightly pink under the freckles, and sat.



They ate first, because Bryda did not talk business over an empty table, and then the plates went away and Ferra opened her book.

Bryda held Grayrock in trust for her daughter. She said so plainly, as a fact about the table they were sitting at, and laid the writ on it to be read: the crown's seal, the crown's clerk's hand, a trust confirmed in the girl's name until her majority and in her mother's keeping until then. Emeric read the date. It had come back to her before the end of the autumn, inside a season of her writing to ask for it.

He put it down very carefully, so that his hands would not do anything else.

Her letter to the crown had gone north from Grayrock and come back with a seal on it inside a season. His letters to the crown had gone north from Kaldo by the same post, through the same sorting room at Wayford, and nothing had ever come back at all. Ferra did not look at him. She did not need to. She had the count in her book.

"Ore," said Bryda.

"Ore," Emeric agreed.

Not an ore barge had come down the river to Kaldo since the war, and Bram was casting lathe beds out of grates. Grayrock had been selling its ore elsewhere since the autumn, up the river and north, to buyers who paid in coin and asked fewer questions. Everybody at the table knew both of those things. Nobody said either of them.

What Kaldo had was grain.

Ferra laid it out. Kaldo's barony farm had come through two harvests under the rotation, and the second had been better than the first, and there was grain in Kaldo's granary that would not be eaten before the next one came in. Grayrock was hill country. Its fields were thin and high and wet, and its harvest failed one year in three; Saban had the tithe figures for twenty years, and in seven of them Grayrock had bought grain from somebody at the worst price of the year, in spring, when the granary was empty and every seller in the south knew it. It was June now. Grayrock's granary, Ferra said, without any change in her voice, would be about as empty as it got.

Bryda did not deny it. She looked at the steward, and the steward looked at the table.

So much grain for so much ore, seven hundredweight of ore to the cartload of grain, delivered by barge, at a rate Ferra had worked out on the road with her book on her knees on a horse she disliked, which Emeric had checked at the first halt and found nothing wrong with. A year at a time. Ferra wrote the terms out as she said them, in her small plain hand, and when she came to the party who would deliver the ore she wrote the holder of Grayrock.

Bryda read it upside down. She was quick at it. Most people who had grown up in a house where the important papers came across a table were.

"Why not my name?"

"Because your daughter will hold Grayrock one day," Ferra said. "And when she does, the terms should still stand." She looked up, for the first time, at the girl at the side table. "Whoever holds it."

The girl looked back at her. Then down at Ferra's hands on the book. She went on looking at Ferra's hands for most of the rest of the evening, Emeric noticed, the way Tess looked at Bram's hands at the anvil, and wrote something in her own book that he could not see.

Bryda sat back.

"No," she said.

Nobody said anything. Ferra's pen stopped.

"Not because of my husband," Bryda said. "I want you to know that. I've thought about my husband a great deal this year, and I'll think about him a great deal more, and none of it's going to be any use to Grayrock." She put her hand flat on the writ. "Because I can't price you."

"The rate's fair," said Ferra. "I'll show you every figure."

"I don't doubt the rate. I'm sure it's fair. That's the trouble." Bryda turned to Emeric. "My husband sold ore to Kaldo for a great many years. He knew to the copper what it was worth to Kaldo, because Kaldo turned it into nails and plowshares and sold them back to its own farmers, and that was all Kaldo ever did with it. I don't know what you do with it. Nobody does. Every bargeman who's been down your river this past two years has come back with a different story. Lights on your wall that burn all night with no oil. A machine in a shed that turns on its own. Mages in your pay who used to be in ours." She looked at the steward, who looked at the table again. "If I sell you ore at an ore price, and you turn it into something worth ten times that, then I've sold you my hill for bread. And I won't know I've done it until it's done."

It was the best argument anybody had made at the table. Emeric knew it as she said it. He had no answer to it that was true and that he was willing to give her.

He looked at Ferra.

He did not decide to. He did it the way he did it at the trestle every day, before he answered anybody about anything that had a figure in it, to see whether the figure he was about to give was the figure in her book. Ferra was already writing. She did not look up.

Bryda watched him do it. She had been watching him do it, he realized, all evening.

"Is that how it's done in the capital?" she said. Dry as dust. "The prince looks at his clerk before he opens his mouth?"

"It's how it's done in Kaldo," said Emeric.

"Seven and a half," said Ferra, without looking up. "Not seven. You said seven on the road. It's seven and a half at Grayrock's weights, because Grayrock's hundredweight is a little light." She wrote it. "I've checked."

The steward made a small noise. Bryda turned and looked at him, for long enough that he stopped making it.

Then she looked back at Ferra, and something in her still face moved, very slightly, for the first time since they had come into the hall.

"Stay the night," she said. "There's a room. I'll give you my answer in the morning, and it'll still be no."

In the yard, afterward, under the hall windows, Ferra said she meant it. "She's said it twice. She'll say it a third time to be sure we heard."

"Maybe," said Emeric.



It was not no. Ferra did not mention, then or afterward, what she had said in the yard.

She sent for them at first light, to the hall, and she had been up before them, because the fire was built up and there was bread on the table and a paper beside it in her own hand, not the clerk's, and she did not sit down.

"Grain first," she said. "Delivered at my landing, by your barges, weighed on my scale, before one sack of ore leaves my hill. Every time. Not ore first and grain after."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"Three years. Not one. Renewable by either of us at the end of the three, and not otherwise." She looked at Ferra. "So that if you turn my ore into gold I've only been a fool for three years and not for ever."

Ferra wrote it.

"The ore weighed at my pithead, not at your gate. Your man can watch it weighed. My man watches it unloaded at yours." She turned the paper on the table so that Ferra could read it. "And the rate is the rate your clerk said. Seven and a half."

"Yes."

She looked at him then, for a long breath, as she had looked at him the evening before when he came into the hall. Then she said what she had evidently decided in the night to say, and to say once, and not again.

"Your steward's letter said by whose order," she said. "When they brought him home. It said what had happened and when and where and by whose order, and it didn't say how. I had to ask the men how." She did not look away. "They walked home behind his cart. All of them. With their hands free. I asked every one of them, and every one of them said the same thing, that nobody laid a hand on them, and nobody took so much as a knife off them that they hadn't dropped, and somebody gave them bread for the road." She paused. "That isn't a reason to trust you."

"No."

"It's a reason to sell you ore." She picked up the paper and gave it to Ferra. "Write it fair. My daughter will copy it for us."

There was one more thing, and it was Ferra who raised it, at the end, with the copies signed and sanded and the tall girl blotting the last of them. Two mages had come down the river to Kaldo in Grayrock's pay last spring, and had been in Kaldo's ever since, and had been promised a wage by Grayrock's steward for the month of the march, half on signing and half on the day, and been paid the half.

"The other half," Ferra said, "is owed by the holder of Grayrock."

The steward opened his mouth.

Bryda held up one hand without looking at him, and he shut it. She went to a chest by the fire and unlocked it and counted out four silver, two and two, onto the table in two piles, and pushed them across to Ferra.

"My husband's debts are mine," she said. "Until they're hers."



On the way out of the town, at the foot of the hill, Emeric asked the steward about the boarded working.

The steward had walked them down to the horses because Bryda had told him to, and had not enjoyed a step of it. He looked up at the dark mouth with its silver boards, and his face did something careful.

"The east working," he said. "It's closed. It's been closed a long time."

"Why?"

"A lamp won't stay lit in it." The steward said it as if he were reciting. "You go in a little way and your lamp goes down, and goes blue, and goes out. Men came out of it sick. Two didn't come out. The men won't go in. They say it's cursed." He shrugged, as if the men were children, and as if he were not himself standing a long way from the boards. "The baron had it boarded. Years ago."

Emeric looked at the boards.

A lamp that went down and burned blue and went out was not a curse. It was air that had nothing left in it to burn. Old timber rotting in a working with no draught through it ate the air out of a gallery as surely as a fire did, slowly, over years, until a man walking into it with a lamp walked into nothing he could breathe. Any mine wright on his other world would have known it. Any mine wright in this one, he would have thought, with a hundred years of workings on his hill, would have known what to do about it: a fire lit in a chimney at the mouth, to draw the bad air out and the good air in after it, and a week of it burning, and the gallery was a gallery again.

Renner had had a hundred years of mine wrights. He had boarded it instead.

It was not Emeric's hill. It was not his business. He got up on his horse, and looked at the mouth once more from the saddle, the grass grown up to the silver planks and not a track worn to it anywhere, and said nothing to the steward, or to Ward, or to Ferra, who was arguing with her mare about which way was home.



They came in by the South Gate at dusk on the second day, and there was water in the aqueduct.

Not much. The masons had keyed the arch on the morning they left for Grayrock, the old master setting the last stone into the crown of it himself, with his new rod in his belt and his old one, Emeric heard afterward, left at home on purpose. They had let the mortar take for two days, and that morning they had begun to take the timber trough down from its trestles, length by length, and let the water through onto the new stone. It came across the gap in a thin bright skin on the floor of the channel, a finger deep, no more, because the masons would not trust it with more until the mortar had cured, and it ran on toward the town and down into the wall cistern with a sound that Emeric could hear from the road.

The old master was standing on the arch when they rode under it, with his hands on his hips, looking down at the water going past his boots. He saw Emeric and raised one hand.

"Not a stone dressed twice," he called down. "Not one."

He took the rod out of his belt and held it up, so that Emeric could see it from the road, and then put it back, and went on looking at the water.

Ferra, beside him, had stopped her mare under the arch without being asked to, which was the first thing the mare had done all day that Ferra approved of. She looked up at the new stone, and at the thin water running over it, and then she opened her book on the saddle in front of her, with some difficulty, and wrote the date.
