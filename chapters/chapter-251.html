Chapter 251 - Escheat

The hall was set for a sitting of the council, and it was set in grey, and the chair on the dais was empty.

Keller came in early, as he always did, and stood at the Crown's table and looked at the chair. Nobody had taken it away. Nobody would have dared. It stood at the top of the three steps where it had always stood, with its arms worn pale where the king's hands had rested on them for longer than Keller had kept the Crown's table, and there was nobody in it, and a pace behind it at its right hand, where he had always stood, the prince Corbin stood with one hand resting lightly on its back.

He had not sat in it at the first sitting of his lieutenancy either. Keller had watched him not sit in it then, and watched the benches see him not sit in it, and like him for it. It was the eleventh day since the north road. He had still not sat in it. Keller thought now that he never would, until he could sit in it without anybody at all being able to say he should not.

The grey young men put everybody in their places. The princess Maeve stood at the foot of the gallery stair with a single sheet in her hand and did not look at it. Keller did not look at her for long. He looked at the Crown's table in front of him, where a clerk of the lieutenant's had laid the morning's paper in the hour before the sitting, in three short rows, squared to the edge.

He knew what it was. He had read every sheet of it the night before, in his room over the chancery, twice. There was nothing on the table that was not true.

There were the priests.

Four sworn statements, one from each of the four priests of the close who had ridden with the king's carriage and the Church's on the north road, two on the steps and two inside. They agreed in every particular. That at the narrow place in the Coldharrow cut, at dusk, the rock had fallen before and behind. That men in black iron had come down the slopes on both sides, four of them, and put down the four knights round the king's carriage, and opened its door. That the king had said, in a loud voice, Who are you? I will not go with you. I am in the keeping of the lords spiritual. That he had been taken out of the carriage by the man at the door, and carried off up the slope, and had not been seen since.

Keller had read it twice and then sat with his hand flat on it for a long time.

I will not go with you. The loud voice. The voice for the door, dry and carrying, the voice Keller had heard the king use for rolls and summonses and the reading of instruments at every sitting he could remember. Four honest priests had heard it, because it had been meant to be heard, and had written it down, because they were honest, and it lay on the Crown's table this morning as the king's own sworn protest against his taking. Keller did not know what the king had meant by it. He thought he could guess. It did not matter. It was on the table.

There were the knights.

Nine statements from the escort, from men of the lieutenant's household and of Aldane's horse who had been on the road below the falls. Three of them swore to a thing in the air. Keller had read those three side by side, the way he read everything, and they did not agree about anything at all. The first swore to a shape like a great black bird with its wings out, going up off the road and over the rock. The second swore to a man in a cloak that burned, with a noise like a smith's fire, going up the slope in one bound, the way a man leaps in a dream. The third swore to smoke, a column of it, black, going up off the road into the snow, and a roar inside it, and nothing he could put a shape to. All three swore that the king had been in it. All three swore it had gone over the east shoulder of the cut and away. None of them had seen where.

Three honest men, sworn, describing three different things. It was not evidence of anything the law could name. Keller had said so to this hall once, of a scout on a hill: the Crown does not try the sky.

And there was the book.

It lay at the end of the last row, a small black book of the kind the Crown's officers carried, open at a page with a slip in it, and Keller knew the hand without looking at the name on the cover, because he had read that hand in every quarter's return from the licence office since the settlement: narrow, upright, a little cramped, the hand of a narrow grey man with a face like a closed door who wrote things down and went away. Oswin Fenner. The Crown's officer for the powder licence at Kaldo, with the right of entry to any place where powder was made or kept, at any hour, without notice.

The lieutenant had sent him south in the week after the vote on the king. Keller knew that because the order had gone through the chancery, as orders did, and he had seen it go. It had not seemed strange. It was a licence officer being told to exercise his licence, which the settlement allowed, as often as the Crown pleased. Keller had thought it was the lieutenant being thorough. He had thought that, and filed it, and not thought about it again.

Fenner had exercised it every night since, for six weeks. He had gone into the powder works at Kaldo at an hour of his own choosing, with his clerk and his lantern, and walked the powder house and the magazine and the nitre beds, and asked, as he always asked, for the lord of Kaldo; and written down the answer, as he always did, with the date and the hour and the place. Keller had read every line. Every line was the same few words it had always been. The powder house seen. The magazine seen. The beds seen. The lord at the palace, and was sent for, and came.

Except six.

Six nights among the forty, in Fenner's narrow cramped hand, the same line, six times: five together in the middle, and one alone at the end. The lord away on the barony's business. Asked where; not told. The last of them was the night of the north road.

And under it, where Fenner's clerk had gone back through the officer's older books at the lieutenant's request, a slip in the clerk's hand, copied out fair from the book of the autumn before: a night in the autumn, at midnight, the lord away on the barony's business; asked where; not told. The only line in two years of Fenner's books that had ever asked a question, until the new year. And now seven.

Keller had sat with that longest of all.

It was not proof. It did not say where the lord of Kaldo had been. It did not say he had been on the north road, or in the capital, or anywhere at all but not at home. A good advocate could have stood up in any court in the realm and said so. Keller could have. He had spent his life saying exactly that: that a pattern was a thing a clever man made out of the world by choosing what to count, and that he had never convicted anybody on one.

But this was not a court. This was the council. And the council did not try acts. It had found the king in breach of an oath by two parts in three on a record that named no act at all, and it could unmake a duke by the same count on the same kind of record, and nobody in the realm could say it had exceeded its office. Keller had told the lord of Kaldo so himself, on the west stair, the day he was made. It takes two parts in three to raise a duke. It takes two parts in three to unmake one.

And Keller knew it was true.

That was the thing he had sat with in his room over the chancery until the lamp burned down. Not that it was a pattern. That it was a true one. He had written to the lord of Kaldo himself, on the night the king went into custody, by a road he had not known existed, and told him everything. He had seen the young man count the voices at the elevation with his face arranged into boredom. He knew what the lord of Kaldo was. He knew, the way he knew the weight of a sentence, that the four men in black iron on the north road had been the lord of Kaldo's, and that one of them had been the lord of Kaldo, and that the king was at Kaldo now; and he knew that if he stood up at the Crown's table this morning and argued that there was no evidence, he would be arguing a thing he did not believe, in front of the council, into the roll.

He had never done that in his life. He found he could not begin now.

The lieutenant spoke from the dais.

He did it well. He did everything well. He did not raise his voice. He stood a pace behind the empty chair with his hand on its back, and spoke to the hall as a man speaks to a room of friends about a hard thing that has happened to all of them, and he did not once say the word brother.

The king, committed by this council to the keeping of the lords spiritual, had been taken out of that keeping on the king's own road, by armed men, by force, against his will, which the king had declared aloud in the hearing of four priests of the close. That was treason. There was no other word for it in any roll. Whoever had done it had done treason.

The lord of Kaldo had been forbidden the road north by the king's own word, in the day room, before the whole house. The lord of Kaldo had been away from his barony on the night of the north road, and on five nights before it, and would not say where, as the Crown's own officer had written in the Crown's own book. The lord of Kaldo's men had been seen, by a sworn scout of the duke of Aldane's, doing a thing in the air over a ford the night before a field; and a hanged man had sworn to it at Wayford before two thousand people. And three men of the king's escort now swore to a thing in the air over the north road, carrying the king away. It was on the table. Any lord could read it.

"I don't ask the council to find where my father is," the lieutenant said. "I pray he's alive. I believe he is. I believe I know whose house he's in." He looked along the benches, gravely. "I ask the council to find that the lord of Kaldo has committed treason against the Crown, and that his lands, which he holds of the Crown, are forfeit to it. All of them. Kaldo, and Grayrock, and Milford, and Wayford, and the duchy this council made of them. By escheat, as the law of this realm has always given it, when a man who holds of the Crown turns on the Crown." He took his hand off the back of the chair. "And I ask it by two parts in three. Because it takes two parts in three to unmake a duke, and I would not have anyone say, after, that it was done by less."

It was very well done. Keller heard the benches hear it. It was the law, every word of it, stated exactly, with nothing added and nothing claimed that the table did not bear; and it was generous about the count, in the way the lieutenant was always generous, by giving away a thing he did not need. He had the count. Everybody in the hall could see that he had the count.

The steward looked at the Crown's table.

Keller stood.

He stood because the Crown's table did not let a matter go by it in silence, and because if it went into the roll with no word from the Crown's law then the roll would say the Crown's law had had no word to say. He had stood in the winter for exactly that reason, and asked what act was charged, and been answered none.

"The Crown's table asks," he said, "what act of the lord of Kaldo is charged."

"The taking of the king," said the lieutenant, pleasantly, "from the lawful keeping this council ordained. On the north road. On the night the Crown's own officer writes that the lord of Kaldo was not at home." He inclined his head to the table, as a man inclines it to a colleague. "The Crown's table has the book. It's the Crown's."

Keller looked down at the small black book at the end of the last row. Fenner's hand. The Crown's book. A Crown officer, doing his office well, writing down the truth, with the date and the hour and the place.

"The Crown's table has it," he said.

He sat down. He did not square the paper in front of him. He put his hands flat on the table on either side of the rows, the way he had sat through the whole of the cardinal's case in the winter, like a man keeping a stall, and did not move them again until the sitting was over.

The voices went as he had known they would. He counted them anyway. He could not help it.

The lords spiritual first, whole. The cardinal gave his in one word, pleasantly, as a man agrees about the weather, and did not look at anyone. The old abbot at the far end of the bench, aye, and looked at nobody. The old blood: Aldane, aye, in the voice that expected to be listened to. The benches under the windows, one after another, the fathers of knighted sons and the men who owed Aldane and the men who would not be seen to shield a traitor, and among them the king's own household lords, most of them, in voices Keller had never heard them use, quietly, looking at the floor.

Grier stood when he was called.

He was still pale. He had not drunk since the vote in the new year; the lanes said so, and Keller believed them. He did not look left or right. He looked at the empty chair on the dais for a long moment, as though there were somebody in it, and said, "No," quite quietly, and sat down, and put his hands between his knees.

It made no difference. Keller entered it. It was one of nine.

Fifty-five to nine. Two parts in three, and a great many over.

The chancellor read the finding in his soft voice without inflection, as he read everything. That the lord of Kaldo had committed treason against the Crown in the taking of the king. That he was attainted. That the duchy of Kaldo and the baronies of Kaldo, Grayrock, Milford and Wayford, and all that was held of the Crown with them, were forfeit to the Crown by escheat, and that the lieutenant of the realm was to take them into the Crown's hand, in the king's name.

The clerk wrote it into the roll. Keller watched the pen go across.

At the foot of the gallery stair, the sheet in the princess Maeve's hand was still flat. She had not folded it. She stood with it held against her grey skirt and her face arranged into the face she wore for a sitting, and when the chancellor said attainted she did not move at all; and Keller, who had watched her fold her sheet at the verdict on the king and understood only now what he had seen then, looked at the flat sheet in her hand and understood that too, and looked away.

The three sworn statements about the thing in the air went into the close's keeping after the sitting, by the lieutenant's courtesy, because the lords spiritual had asked for them. Keller saw the cardinal's clerk come to the Crown's table for them, and take them, and carry them away across the square to the close in a plain wooden box.

He did not see what became of them there. He supposed they went where such things went at the close: into a press, beside a copy of the duke's file, beside the sworn statement of a scout called Garret Oakes who had seen a thing longer than a tithe barn pass between him and the stars, and a groom's statement about a face like the underside of a toad. A second rumour, laid beside the first. Not confirmation of anything. Three men who could not agree, and a scout who had been laughed at, and a hanged fool.

It was the kind of thing a careful man keeps. Keller would have kept it himself.

He did not suppose, because it did not occur to him to, that there was anything else at the close that the cardinal had not written down.

The princess Maeve did not sleep that night.

She sat in the window of her rooms in the west range, which looked down into the mews yard, with the lamp out and a shawl over her grey, and watched the yard. She had counted her household at supper: four, and the rest she did not answer for. She had set the council hall that morning for her brother, the lieutenant, as she had set it for her father, and he had thanked her for it, as he always did, and meant it, as far as he meant anything. She had held her sheet flat through the finding and gone back to her rooms and sat down in the window and not moved since.

Kaldo was forfeit. Her father was at Kaldo; she had had it in four lines, in her brother's hand, and had read them twice and burned them. Emeric was attainted. Her mother was at Emberfell, a treaty that had gone home when the treaty stopped needing her, writing letters that were not as private as she thought. Her full brother was at sea, past the edge of every map, and did not know any of it. Corbin kept the house. Aldane sat among the old blood with the most men. And the man in red across the square had given one word, pleasantly, and not looked at anyone.

She had been at this court all her life. She knew what a council did when it had found a king in breach and a duke in treason in one winter. It looked for the rest of the line.

A little before dawn, a lantern came into the mews yard below.

It was a groom's lantern, carried low, and the man carrying it was not a groom. He was tall, and broad in the shoulder, with a short beard and a riding coat, not a court coat, and he went across the yard to the end stall and hung the lantern on its hook and went in, and after a moment the stall door opened wider, and he came out again leading a grey mare, and at the mare's heel, very close, stepping high and uncertain on the frozen cobbles, a foal.

It was a filly. Maeve could see that from the window, even in the lantern light, by the way she carried her head.

Waylon walked the mare round the yard, slowly, in the cold, with the filly at her heel, once and then again, the way a man walks a horse he is pleased with when there is nobody to see him be pleased; and stopped under the lantern, and put his hand on the filly's neck, and stood there.

She had not thought of him. She had not thought of him all winter, in all her counting, and she thought of him now, all at once, the way you remember a door you have forgotten to lock.

Amara's other son. The youngest. Three the year his mother was killed in a road below a pass, and grown up in this house without her and without anybody ever quite saying where she had come from, the queen who was nobody's daughter; and with nothing of her in him, people said, but the jaw. He had not been in the hall for the elevation, or the verdict, or the finding. He never was. He stood by the south door in the yard, where you could hear the whole hall, and listened to the aye and the no, and then he rode out. He had nobody in this house now. His father was gone from it. His full brother was attainted in it. His half-brother kept it.

And the Church wanted the whole line. Maeve did not know why she was certain of that. She had never been told it and had never read it anywhere, and there was nothing on any paper in the kingdom that said it. She knew it the way she knew where every man on the benches would sit before the list was made: because she had watched the red on the east bench all winter, and it had never once hurried, and it had never once stopped.

Below in the yard Waylon took his hand off the filly's neck and turned, and looked up, straight at her window, as though he had known the whole time that somebody was there.

Maeve did not move away from the glass.

After a moment she put the shawl off her shoulders and stood up, and went to the door, and down the back stair toward the mews, in her grey, in the dark, without a lamp, to go and speak to her brother about his filly; which was a thing she had never once done in all the years they had lived in the same house, and which would be, she thought on the stair, the first thing in her life that nobody at all at that court would be able to count.

In the south, at Kaldo, in the dock at the head of the lake, the flagship lay on her stations under the long roof with the snow on it. Her keel was down, and her frames were up, and her iron was going on course by course over the frames, and she was nowhere near ready to fly. The men who worked on her went home at dusk, as they always did, and came back at first light.

On the morning after the finding, when the news of it had not yet come south by any road, they came back at first light, and there was a man standing at the head of the dock in a fur-collared travelling coat with his hands behind his back, looking up at her. He was old, with a white beard, and nobody on the dock knew who he was, and he stood there a long time in the cold without saying anything to anyone.

Then the foreman rang the bell for the day's work, and the gangs went up onto her, and the old man stepped back out of their way, and stayed to watch.

The war began that morning, in the capital, with a line in a roll. At Kaldo nobody knew it yet. They went up onto her in the cold, and the rivets began, and the flagship was still in the dock.
