Chapter 251 - Forcing It

The ration book hung on a nail inside the bakery's hatch, on a loop of twine, where the baker could reach it without turning round.

It was a long thin book with a grey board cover, ruled by Ferra into five columns: the date, the name, the household, the corners, and the hand that took them. One corner of the heavy bread a day for every soul in Kaldo who wanted one, and not two; and a household could send one of its own to fetch for the rest, as it sent one to fetch the water, because nobody was going to make a grandmother walk to the bakery every morning for a piece of bread the size of a thumb. The baker ticked each corner over the counter, and the hand that took them signed or made its mark. On Saturdays Ferra came down and copied the week's totals into her own book.

For three weeks it worked.

The town took the bread the way Maren had told it to, on the side, a corner with the soup, a corner with the eggs; Ferra's totals came up the hill at a little under two thousand corners a day, give or take the rain; and nobody came to the ward with a nosebleed that had not been punched there.

Then on the Thursday, the twenty-seventh of May, a little before five in the morning, a boy came to the ward door holding his right arm against his chest as if it had been broken, and there was blood on his collar, and it was not all his.



Emeric was awake when the runner came up the keep stair. He had been at his table since four with Bram's drawings for the boring mill's second bed, and he heard the gate, and feet on the stair two at a time, and knew before the door opened that it was the ward and that it was bad, because nobody took the stairs two at a time for a broken finger.

"The healer says come," said the runner, one of the dock-quarter boys Imani paid to sleep in the ward's porch. "She says bring stones. Empty ones. All you've got."

He did not ask why. He took the box off the shelf by the door, the one with the class's spent stones in it, thirty of them, grey and dull, emptied for the morning's drill, and went down the stair with it under his arm, and Ward was already at the bottom with his coat on.

They went down through the town at a run in the grey light. The lanes smelled of the night's rain and of the bakery's ovens, lit at three; and coming past the bakery's corner Emeric looked at the shut hatch, and did not know yet why he had looked.



There were two of them on beds in the main ward, and the third was in the passage on the floor with Rylan kneeling beside him.

The night nurse had sent the porch boy three lanes up for Rylan, because since Wren everybody in Kaldo knew Rylan's house; and Rylan had come down in his shirt and gone with two nurses and a handcart to the loft over a carter's stable on the back lane, and brought back what was in it. Two alive. One not. He had left the one who was not under a horse blanket on the loft's boards, because the living needed the cart; and then gone back for him on his own, and carried him down the ladder over his shoulder, and laid him on the floor of the ward's passage, because there was no bed left, and knelt beside him, and had not got up.

Emeric stopped in the passage door and looked down.

Everybody in Kaldo had seen the drawing on the leaflet and the bakery wall, and the third picture in it: a man on his back on a floor with lines drawn down his arms. The young men at the bakery wall had laughed at it, because the lines were four strokes of a pen.

The boy on the floor was perhaps seventeen, with a carter's shoulders and thick wrists, his sleeves pushed up or torn. From his wrists to his elbows, on both arms, and up under the sleeves, there were lines. Not a knight's silver, not the clean pale rings under Rylan's skin that had closed twenty years ago and never opened. These had started the same way, at the wrist, in rings, and had not closed. Each ring ran a little way round the arm and stopped, open, as if whatever had been drawing it had run out of hand; and from each open end a bruise's black-red had spread under the skin along some line of its own, so that each forearm was a mesh of half-made silver with black running out of it like ink into wet paper. There was blood dried at his nose and along his gums, where his lips had drawn back.

Rylan had pulled the blanket up to the boy's chin and left the arms out. Emeric did not know whether he had meant to.

"Eight names," said Rylan, not looking up, as a man reports a count at a gate. "In the book, for three weeks. Four of them, and their mothers and sisters and a grandmother who doesn't eat bread. Every evening, every name, signed for. They kept the corners in a crock in the loft and started on them Monday night."

Emeric did the sum without wanting to. A hundred and sixty corners; four boys; three nights. A corner filled like a meal. Three corners bled.

"Go in," said Rylan. "She's asking for you. There's nothing to be done here."



Imani was between the two beds with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder, and her hands were red to the wrist, and she did not look round when he came in.

"Drain them," she said. "Both. Now. Into the stones. Whatever's still in them, get it out."

He was already kneeling. He put the box on the floor between the beds and took out the first stone and put it in the left boy's hand and closed the boy's fingers round it, and put his own hand over the fingers; and put his other hand flat on the boy's chest, over the breastbone, on the wet shirt.

The boy was hot, the way a stone is hot at dusk after a day in the sun; and under the heat, in Emeric's sense, his note was a roar. A dock-quarter boy's note, which ought to have been a candle in a room, and it was a bonfire, ragged at every edge, and it was tearing. The mana had been forced down the thin threads every living body carries, faster and fuller than they had ever been meant to carry, and they had split; and it ran now where they had split, into places that were not paths at all, and everywhere it ran it broke something small, and the breaking was the black under the skin.

He pulled.

It came easily. That was the worst of it, afterward. It came out of the boy like water out of a split bag, as if it had been waiting all night for somewhere to go, and he sent it past his own wrist into the stone in the boy's fist, and the stone warmed and filled and stopped taking; and he put the second stone in place of the first and pulled again. A third. The bonfire went to a fire, and to a lamp. Five stones, and the boy's note was a candle again, thin and ordinary, as it should have been all along.

And under the candle, nothing had changed. The black under the skin was still there. The places where the paths had split were still split. Taking the mana out of a torn pipe did not mend the tear; it only stopped more coming through it.

He looked up at Imani.

She had her fingers on the boy's throat. She was counting with her lips. She did not stop counting to look at him, and he watched her face, and her face did not change, and that told him.

"The other one," she said.

The other one was smaller, and quieter, and further gone. Emeric drained him with six stones, and the bonfire in him went down to a candle as the first had, and the candle was very low, and when he lifted his hand off the boy's chest he could feel it, without trying, going lower.

"Bleeding inside," said Imani. She had pressed her hand flat on the boy's belly and taken it away and pressed it again, and her mouth was shut very tight. "All along the lines. Not just the arms. All through. I can feel it filling." She looked at Emeric across the bed. "You've taken out what was doing it. Can you mend what it did?"

He made himself think about it honestly: every thread in the boy split along its length, a thousand small bleeds from the wrists to the heart, too small to find, too many to tie.

"No," he said.

"No," said Imani. "Nor can I." She took her hand off the boy's belly. "Then we keep them warm and give them poppy and stay."



The fourth boy was the one who had come to the door.

He sat on a stool in the dispensary with a nurse's blanket round him and his right arm on his knee, and would not stop looking at it. He had stopped first, on the Tuesday night, at nine corners, when his nose would not stop bleeding and the fullness in his belly became a hand closing; and lain in his own bed all Wednesday telling his mother it was a bad egg. On the Wednesday night he had gone back up the ladder to tell them to stop, and they had not stopped, and he had sat in the corner and watched; and in the small hours the eldest had gone quiet, and he had run.

Emeric drained him last, with two stones, and the boy did not seem to notice. His note was his own again, thin and frightened; and the black on his right forearm was a hand's breadth from three rings at the wrist that had started and not closed, and it did not spread while Emeric watched. His left arm was clean. His right hand lay on his knee and shook, all by itself, a fine fast tremor, like a man's hand on a cold morning.

"Will it stop?" the boy said. He said it to the hand.

"I don't know," said Emeric. "Not soon."

The boy nodded, as if that were what he had expected. Then he said, still to his hand, "Am I a knight?"

Emeric did not answer straight away. He did not trust what his face was doing.

"No," he said. "You're alive."



The small one died at a little after noon, with his mother on one side of him and Imani on the other.

The other carter's lad, in the second bed, lasted until five. His father sat with him all afternoon, a carter with a carter's hands, who had come in at seven with his hat in them and had not put it down; and Rylan came and stood in the door twice, and went away, and came back; and at five the boy's breathing changed, and slowed, and Imani got up from the stool at the bed's foot and went and stood by the father, and put her hand on his shoulder, and kept it there, and the boy stopped.

The carter looked at his son.

Then he looked at Imani's hand on his shoulder, and up at her, and said, "There was a paper. On the bread. On the wrapping." He said it slowly, as if he were reading it off something in front of his eyes. "He read it to his mother. Out loud, at the table. She can't read. He read it to her and laughed."

Imani did not say anything.

"I'm not saying." The carter shook his head. "I'm not saying anything. I'm saying he read it."



Ivo came down from the glass house at half past five. Imani had sent for him at noon, and he had taken the afternoon to come, because he did not want to.

He said so at the door: that he ground lenses, that he had come south for the wage and not for this. He had the box under his arm anyway, dark oak the size of a small cupboard with his best lens in a brass ring on its front, and in a velvet case the glass plates he had been coating with salts of silver at the back of his shop since the winter.

"Their arms," said Imani. "All three. And the one who lived, if he'll let you, and his mother says yes."

Ivo looked past her down the main ward, at the sheets pulled up to the chin and the arms left out above them.

"Why?"

"Because they laughed at the drawing," said Imani. "A drawing's somebody with a pen making something up. Nobody can say that about yours." She looked at the box. "Can they?"

Ivo did not answer her. He looked at the box himself, under his arm, as if it had changed while he was carrying it.

"It takes a long time," he said at last. "With the plates I have. The light has to sit on the glass. Out of doors in sun, the count of thirty. In here, with lamps, a hundred and more. Nobody living can keep still a hundred." He stopped. He had heard what he had said.

"No," said Imani. "Nobody living."

She had asked the families, one at a time, the way she asked for a leg off: what it was, and what it was for, and that they could say no. The carter had said yes without looking up. The small boy's mother had looked at Imani across her son's body and said, "So the next mother can see," and Imani had written that down.

Ivo set the box on its three legs at the foot of the first bed and put his head under the black cloth, and came out with his face grey; and slid a plate in, and took the cap off the lens, and counted under his breath to a hundred and twenty, with the lamps drawn close and every person in the main ward standing still without being asked.

Seven plates: both arms of each of the dead, and the fourth boy's right, with the shaking hand held flat on a board by his mother so it would not blur. When it was done Ivo stood by the door with the case in his hands and did not go.

"I'll have them printed by morning," he said. He looked down the ward again. "I came south to grind lenses," he said, to nobody, and went.



Ferra came down at six with her book.

She had heard at nine, by the telephone, and had not come down, because there was nothing for a woman with a book to do in a room where boys were dying; she had spent the day doing the thing she could do. She found Emeric on the dispensary floor with his back against the press, and did not ask him how he was. She sat on the fourth boy's stool and opened the book on her knee and turned it round so he could see it.

It was the page she had begun on the day of the warning, headed in her square capitals, THE WARNING: a line for every time and place the warning had been given, with the count of those who heard it, where there was a count to be had.

The square, from the ward steps, on market day: about nine hundred, by Saban's men at the corners. The school, the morning after, read aloud by Sela to every class, the children told to read it again at home to anybody who could not. The bakery wall, the ward door, the north well, the water gate, the South Gate, the posting house, with the drawing. Every parish in the barony, from the step after the service, Doran's own first. Grayrock and Milford and Wayford, by the stewards, on the next post, four hundred leaflets each. The wrappers on the bread, every corner, printed, near forty thousand by the twenty-sixth. The ration book, with the rule written inside the cover in Maren's hand and Ferra's under it.

At the bottom of the page she had written, that afternoon, in a hand that was perfectly steady: 27 May. Three. And nothing else; not the names, which were in Imani's register, where names went.

"I went to the bakery at two with Saban and read the book at the counter," said Ferra. "Every name's real. Every corner's signed for. Eight names, twenty days, four hands, every one of them inside the rule. You can see it if you know what you're looking for. But a quarter of the town sends one hand for the house; I've a man in the north quarter fetches for eleven." She closed the book on her finger. "The baker kept it to the letter. He read me the rule off the inside of the cover while I was reading his book, because he thought I'd come to tell him he'd broken it."

"What did you tell him?"

"That he hadn't. That it was my rule and my book and if anybody had sold them bread outside it, it was me." She looked at him. "He didn't believe me. He'll be at the ward door tomorrow. He was crying into the flour when we left."

Emeric did not say anything.

"I'm not showing you this so you'll feel better," said Ferra. "I'm showing you so it's written down somewhere that isn't in your head. In your head it'll grow. On my page it stays the size it is." She held out the book so that he would take it. "Every way there is. That's the page."

He took it, and read the column through again, and gave it back.



Sela came at seven. She came because one of the three had been hers.

The small one, who had died at noon, had sat at the back of her school room from seven to eleven, slow at his letters and fast at his numbers, and gone to the dock at twelve; and she had not seen him in the room since, except on the morning after market day, when he had come to the door with two others to hear the warning read again, because they had missed it in the square.

"He read it for the little ones," said Sela. She stood in the dispensary door with her shawl crossed over her chest and did not come in. "I asked who'd read it again for the ones at the back, and his hand was up first, the way it always was." She looked at the floor. "He read it very well. He always read well when he was showing off." She was quiet. "He did all the hard words. Gums. Tracery. He knew all of them."

Emeric got up off the floor.

"Don't," said Sela, before he could say anything. "I didn't come for that. I didn't come so you'd say a thing to me or I'd say a thing to you." She looked at him, then; at his face, the way she had looked at it once in her own doorway, after Tam, and told him to stop avoiding her. "I came because I'd want to be told, if it were Arthur. That he'd read it. That somebody'd heard him read it." She turned to go. "So I'm telling his mother. On my way home."



Rylan was on the wall above the water gate at eight, where Emeric had known he would be, because it was where he went.

He was sitting on the parapet with his boots over the river, in his shirt, in the cold that came up off the water even in May; and he was not keeping himself warm. Emeric could feel it before he got to the top of the steps: a knight on a cold wall with nothing going into his arms at all, letting the chill have him.

Emeric sat down beside him. They looked at the river for a while.

"One of them asked me," said Rylan. "In March. The eldest. The one in the passage. He came to the yard one morning and stood at the gate with the others who watch, and when we'd done he asked me when the next class was." He looked at the water. "I said next spring. When the first one's two years in. I said come back next spring and I'll take you."

"You'd have taken him."

"I'd have taken him." Rylan looked at the rings on his forearms, pale in the last of the light. "He'd have had to wait a year. A year's a long time at seventeen." He was quiet. "I took my shirt off for him. I stood there and steamed like a pudding and told them what the floor did, and he was at the front. I saw his face."

Emeric looked at his own hands.

"I keep thinking," he said, "if I'd..."

"No."

Rylan said it at once, not loudly, the way he said "Sit down" to a man who had got up too soon after a blow to the head.

"There's no what-if," said Rylan. "I've been sitting here since six doing what-ifs. If I'd said a month and not a year. If I'd taken my shirt off twice. If I'd gone round the lofts. There's no what-if. We told them every way there is. Your Ferra's got a page of it. I've seen her page." He looked at Emeric, and Emeric saw that he had been crying at some point in the last hour and had stopped, and had no intention of starting again in company. "We told them, and they heard us, and they did it anyway, because they were seventeen and they'd watched sixty-one others get strong in a yard and thought they knew the half that mattered. That's not a thing you fix. It's a thing that happens to boys." He looked back at the river. "It happened to me. A captain put my name on a list and I was proud of it for a week."

They sat for a while longer.

"Keep yourself warm," said Emeric.

Rylan looked at him sideways.

"You're sitting on a wall in your shirt in the river damp with a knight's lines and nothing going into them," said Emeric. "Imani will have you in a bed by Sunday with your chest full and she'll blame me for letting you. Do it for me. I can't take her shouting tonight."

Rylan let out a breath that was very nearly a laugh. Then the heat came up off him, slowly, into the cold air, and a little steam with it, and he did not apologize for it this time.



He went back down to the ward at nine, and found Imani scrubbing the main ward.

It was her rule since the first winter: when anybody died in her ward, the bed and the boards under it and the wall beside it were scrubbed with lye and then carbolic, by her own hands, however late. The nurses knew the rule and did not offer. She had moved the boys to the cold room for their families to come for in the morning, and had the mattresses carried out to be burned; and she was on her knees between the two bare frames with a bucket and a brush, in a canvas apron, scrubbing.

She did not look up.

He hung his coat on the door and rolled his sleeves, and took the second bucket and the second brush, and got down on his knees at the other end of the space between the beds, and began.

She did not tell him not to.

They did not talk. They did the boards from the walls toward the middle, so that nothing went back under a bed, and then the frames, every slat, and the wall to a man's shoulder, and the floor of the passage where the first boy had lain all morning with Rylan kneeling beside him. The carbolic stung in every crack in his knuckles, and he let it. After midnight a nurse set a jug of tea on the windowsill and went out, and neither of them drank it.

At about two Imani sat back on her heels with the brush in her hand and looked at the wall in front of her, which was clean, and had been clean for some time, and went on looking at it.

"You'd rather it was yours," she said.

He stopped scrubbing.

"I've watched you all day." She did not turn round. "You've been looking for the place where it was yours. The drawing, or the bread, or the class, or the wrapper, or the day you said yes to Maren. Any of it. You'd rather it was yours. Because if it was yours, you could fix it. You'd build something and it'd never happen again." She put the brush in the bucket. "You told them. I told them. Rylan stood in the square with his shirt off. Ferra's got a page. They heard us and decided they knew better." She was quiet. "That's theirs. It's not yours to take off them because it'd be easier for you to carry than to put down. It's theirs. They were boys and they were stupid and they chose it, and it was theirs, and now it's done."

He knelt on the wet boards and looked at her back.

He had thought all day that he was looking for the flaw, as he would have looked for one in a weld.

"You're right," he said.

"I know." She picked the brush up again, and looked at it, and did not use it. "I've been right since nine this morning. I've been waiting for you to finish scrubbing so I could say it to somebody who wasn't a wall." She put the brush down. "It's mine too," she said, much more quietly. "That's the joke. I'll say all that and mean it and still lie down tonight and go through every word I said on those steps for a better one." She turned round on her knees, at last, and looked at him across the clean boards. "So we'll both not do that. All right? We'll both not."

"All right."

"Liar," said Imani, and got up, and held out her hand to pull him up off the floor.



Before they left she locked the register.

Not the book on her desk; Bettany's rat book, with the spring's figures: the fullness limit and the bleeding stage, and beside them the blood. Rested blood safe. Fresh blood from a high body, a knight's or a dense man's, an overdose, worse than bread, because blood did not stop at the stomach. She had shown the figures at the table and told the class, and nobody else; and she stood in the dispensary at a quarter to five in the morning with the book in her hands and looked at it the way she had looked at the carter's boy's arms.

"Somebody'll think of it," she said. "Not this week. Somebody. A boy who heard a knight's blood is strong. A boy who's read the leaflet and thinks bread's for cowards." She put the book in the press, on the top shelf, behind the poppy, and locked the press, and put the key on the cord round her neck with the other one. "If they're going to think of it, they'll think of it without my figures."



The light was coming up grey when they came out of the ward. Her house was across the lane. He had meant to walk her to her door and go up the hill; she took his wrist at the door and pulled him in without saying anything, and shut it behind them.

The front room was as it always was: low, the fire laid and not lit, the smell of vinegar and drying herbs, the shelf above the fire with the razor folded in its leather and the net needle worn bright at the eye, the lamp stone in its cup at the shelf's end; and the two chairs by the fire. Hers. And the other.

She let go of his wrist and stood in the middle of the room and looked at the cold grate.

"Sit," she said.

He went toward her chair.

"Not that one."

He stopped and looked at the other chair, with the cushion that had no dent in it. In all the years he had known her he had seen her sit in it once, on the night he told her about Ferra, to look at him; and he had known that was the most she would ever say about it.

"Imani."

"I know whose it is." She did not look at him. "Sit in it. I want to sit down and I can't sit down in mine. I'll be sitting with my hands in my lap waiting for the next knock and I'll not do that tonight." She was standing very straight, the way she stood at the foot of a bed. "He'd not have minded. He'd have told you to take your boots off first."

He took his boots off. Then he sat down in her father's chair, slowly, and it took his weight, and creaked, and the cushion went down under him for the first time in however many years it had been.

She looked at him in it for a long breath.

Then she came across the room and got into his lap, in her canvas apron with the lye still on it, and put her head on his shoulder, and drew her knees up, and was still.

He held her, one arm round her back and the other over her knees, the whole of her, close; and after a while she turned her face into his neck. She did not cry. She breathed against his throat, and every so often her whole body went tight in his arms, from her shoulders to her feet, as if something had gone through her, and then let go; and he held on through each one and said nothing.

The light came up round the shutters.

"He used to hold me like this," she said at last, into his neck. "When I was small. When something died. A gull with a hook in it. My mother's patients, sometimes, when I'd been there and wasn't supposed to have been." She was quiet. "In this chair. He'd say, you hold on till it's gone through, and then you put it down. You don't carry a thing that's gone through."

He did not say anything.

"Has it gone through?" she said. "Yours."

He thought about it.

"No."

"Nor mine." She did not lift her head. "Then hold on."



They slept in their shirts, with the shutters closed and the day going on without them. Neither of them decided it. Some time after seven she got up out of his lap and dropped the apron on the floor where she stood, which he had never seen her do with anything, and went into the low room at the back and lay down on the narrow bed facing the wall; and he lay down behind her, because there was nowhere else in that bed to lie, and put his arm over her, and she pulled it tighter, and they were both asleep before either could think of anything to say.

When he woke there was a line of gold round the shutters, the long late gold of evening, and the room smelled of her soap, and faintly of lye.

She was awake, on her back beside him as much as the bed allowed, watching him; he could not tell how long she had been doing it.

"You talk," she said.

"What did I say?"

"Nothing I could use." She did not move. "You said 'level' twice. And a word I didn't know. And my name, once, very cross, as if I'd moved a tool."

"You'd moved a tool."

"Probably." She lifted her free hand and put two fingers on the inside of his wrist, where it lay on the blanket between them, the way she always did; and he lay still and let her. "Sixty-four," she said, after a while. "Asleep it was fifty-eight. I counted." She did not take her fingers away. "I've been lying here an hour counting your pulse in the dark like a madwoman because it's the only thing in this house that's going the right speed."

He turned his hand over under her fingers and took hold of hers.

She looked at their two hands for a while, on the blanket.

"I want something," she said. "I don't want to be held. I've been held. I'm grateful for it." Her mouth moved. "I want to be somewhere that isn't the ward. In a thing that's alive and going the right speed and isn't anybody's son on a board." She looked at him straight, with the line between her brows he knew from a hundred difficult cases. "Is that a terrible thing to want. Tonight."

"No."

"You'd say no."

"I'd say no if it were," he said, "because you'd know I was lying, and then you'd be angry, and you'd go and scrub something." He held her hand. "It's not. It's what bodies do. You told me that yourself, at Grayrock."

"I said it about you."

"You said it about bodies."

She looked at him a while longer. Then she let go of his hand and took hold of his shirt instead, a fistful of it over his chest, as she had in the cabin at Grayrock; and did not pull. She held it, and waited, and he understood that she was asking.

"Yes," he said. "If you want it. If you stop wanting it, say so and it stops."

"I know it stops." She pulled, then, a little. "That's why I'm asking you and not anybody else."



It was slow. Neither of them had anything left in them to be anything else.

The bed was too narrow for two to lie side by side without one on the edge, and they found that out at once, and she laughed at it, the short dry laugh through her nose, the first since the ward door. He took her by the waist and moved her, the whole of her, and laid her against the wall in the middle of the mattress, and came over her on his forearms. She lay back with her hands on his arms, testing them, as if to see what was holding him up.

He had pulled the tie off his hair in the ward in the night, wet through with lye, and not tied it again; and now it came down round her face on both sides, the whole long fall of it, and shut out the brown room and the gold line at the shutters, so that there was only his face and the dark of his hair round them both like a tent. She put her hand up and took one of his locs between her fingers and turned it, slowly, as if she were reading it.

"There," she said. "Nobody can see in."

"Nobody's looking."

"I know nobody's looking. I'm saying I can't see out." She let go of the loc. "Good."

He kissed her: not as he did on the slip in passing, quick and grinning with somebody about to come in, but slowly, for a long time, with nobody to come; and she kissed him back the same way, her hand in his hair at the back of his head. Neither of them was counting.

She undid her own shirt. He would have done it, and she knew it, and did it herself instead, button by button from the throat down, without looking away from his face; and when it was open she took his hand off the mattress beside her and put it on her, flat, over her heart, under her breast, where she had once put the cup of her stethoscope on him in her surgery and lost count.

"Count," she said.

He did not count. He felt it, hard and quick under his palm, and her skin warm, and her breath going in under his hand and staying there; and then he bent his head and put his mouth where his hand was, and she let the breath out all at once and said something that was not a word.

He took the rest of her clothes off her slowly, and she took his, less slowly, with ward hands that had no patience with buttons. There was no room in the bed for any of it; things went on the floor, and his elbow went into the wall twice, and the second time she laughed properly, with her head back on the pillow, and he put his mouth on her throat while she was laughing and felt the laugh turn into something else.

He took his time. She was tired; he could feel it in the long muscles of her back and in the way her hands moved on him and stopped and moved again. He went where her breathing told him. The inside of her elbow, where the lye had reddened the skin and she hissed at him for finding it and then did not tell him to stop. The hollow of her hip. Lower, when she put her hand on his head and moved him there, without a word, with her fingers in his hair; and he stayed there a long time, until her heels were pressing into the mattress and her hand had gone from moving him to holding on, and she said his name, twice, once in the cross voice and once in a voice he had heard from her only in the dark; and then her whole body went taut under his hands, from her shoulders to her feet, the way it had gone taut in the chair, again and again, and this time when it let go it let go all the way, and she lay back in the narrow bed with her arm over her eyes and her breath coming in pieces, and laughed, and the laugh broke, and she was crying.

He came back up the bed and held her.

She cried, with her face in his shoulder, openly, the way she had not cried in the chair; not for long; and he held her and felt it go through her, and go out.

"There," she said at last, into his shoulder, wet. "That's gone through." She wiped her face on his skin, deliberately, as if he were a towel. "That's what that was for. I didn't know." She lifted her head. "I'm not done."

"No?"

"Don't sound so pleased." But her mouth had gone crooked, and her hand had come down between them, and was not being clinical at all. "I'm not done," she said again, lower, watching his face while her hand moved. "I want you. Now. And I'll not have careful. I've had a careful day."

He was not careful. He was slow, which was not the same thing, and she knew the difference. He moved her, because the bed left no other way to manage it, with his hands under her and the strength in his arms, and she let him take her weight as she let nobody else take any weight of hers, and came to him; and they were both still a long breath, joined, with her eyes open on his and his hair round her face.

"There," she said, very quietly. "Alive."

Then neither of them said anything.

He would remember it afterward for being ordinary: two tired people in a bed too small for them, in a house that smelled of vinegar, at the end of the worst day of the spring, going as slowly as they liked because nothing was waiting for them outside the shutters. She set the pace. Her hands were on his back, and in his hair, and then on his wrist; and he felt her fingers settle there, on the inside, where they always went, and press, and felt her lips move against his jaw, counting. He felt her get to fifteen and lose it, and start again, and get to four and lose it, and give it up, and laugh against his mouth, and hold on to his wrist anyway. Her heels came round behind his knees. Her breath went short, and shorter, and she said his name into his ear, and something else after it that he did not catch and did not need to; and when she went over the second time she pulled him with her, hard, by the wrist and the hair both, and he went.

Afterward they lay as they had to, with her half on top of him because there was nowhere else for half of her to go, her head on his chest and his hair everywhere. The gold line at the shutters had gone red, and then grey. Out in the lane a woman called a child in for supper, and the child did not come.

Imani lay with her ear on his chest and listened.

"Ninety," she said, after a while. "And coming down." She did not lift her head. "I counted that one. I wanted to know I could still count something."

"Could you?"

"Badly." She was quiet. "I'll tell Ferra in the morning. First thing, before the table." She moved her hand on his chest, flat, over the bone. "You tell her too. I don't want her to have it from one of us. I want her to have it from both."

"I'll tell her."

"She'll ask if you ate."

"I haven't eaten."

"Nor have I, since yesterday morning, and I'm the healer, and I've just..." She stopped, and he felt her face go warm against his chest. "There's bread in the crock," she said, with dignity. "Ordinary bread. And cheese. And if you say one word about corners I'll put you out in the lane in your shirt."

He did not say a word about corners. He found the bread and the cheese in the dark by her directions from the bed, which were exact, and they ate sitting up in the narrow bed with the blanket round both of them and the crumbs going everywhere, and did not talk about the ward, or the three carts that had come to the ward's yard that morning while they slept. Once he caught her looking through the door at the lamp stone in its cup on the shelf, which he had hung in the air of that room for her once, in a year when everything had been simpler.



Ferra was in the counting room at seven on the Saturday when Imani came up the stair, and she put her pen down before Imani had got through the door.

Emeric came up behind her. He had meant to be first, and had stopped at the pump to put his head under it, and Imani had gone past him on the stair and said, "Too late," over her shoulder.

Ferra looked at the two of them in the door. At Imani's hair, which was down and not even pretending to be up, and at Emeric's, which was wet, and at both of their faces.

"I know where you were," she said. "The ward porch rang at five yesterday to say the ward was scrubbed and you'd both gone across the lane. I wrote the time." She folded her hands on the book. "Go on."

Imani told her. Plainly, standing in front of the counting room table, in her own words, the way she gave a report on a patient at the table on a Monday: the chair, and the bed, and the evening, and that it had been her asking. Not all of it. Enough. Ferra listened without moving and without looking away from her once, and when Imani had done she looked at Emeric.

"And you."

He told her the same thing, shorter, and worse, because he did not have Imani's gift for reporting a thing as if it had happened to a patient.

Ferra looked from one of them to the other.

"Good," she said. Then, to Imani, much more gently than she said most things: "You had the worst of it. I'm glad it was you that had him." And to Emeric, not gently at all: "Did you eat?"

"Bread and cheese."

"In a bed," said Imani.

"In her bed," said Ferra, "which is the width of a ledger." She picked up her pen. "There'll be crumbs in it till midsummer. Sit down, both of you. Maren's sending up eggs. I asked for three plates at six." She did not look up. "Before anybody came up the stair. I'll have that known."



Hester Mull came down from Milford on the grain train on the last day of May, in the guard's van, with a crock in her lap wrapped in a sheepskin, and would not let the guard carry it.

All winter Pell's hundred pairs of dishes at the Seers' Table had shown the broth with the mould going bad slower, in a clear ring round it; and in April Imani had said at the table that she would try it on a wound, half her festering wounds with it and half without, the way she had tried the boiled water in the first summer. But the broth would not grow. In the ward's cool dispensary it went cloudy with the wrong things; on the kitchen hearth it cooked. In the end she had written to Milford.

Hester looked at the dispensary, and at the cool press, and at the jar on the bench with the grey skin on it, and sniffed.

"Too cold," she said. "On bread in the cellar, it'll grow. But the broth's a baby, my mother said, it wants keeping warm." She put her crock down and took the sheepskin off it. "In the winter she kept it in the bed."

Imani looked at her.

"Against her back, under the blankets, all night, like a hot brick." Hester unwound the cloth from the crock's mouth. "She said it wanted what a baby wanted. Blood heat. A bit more and it cooks. A bit less and it sulks." She looked round the dispensary. "Where's your bed?"

"I'm not sleeping with a crock," said Imani.

"Then it won't grow," said Hester. "And you've wasted my morning on the train."

Emeric, at the door, with his shoulder against the frame, said, "She's already got something in her bed."

Imani turned her head and looked at him in a way that would have stopped a ward full of drunks. Hester looked at him, and then at Imani, and then at him again, and her mouth went up at one corner, slowly, all the way; and she said nothing whatever, which in Hester Mull was worse than anything she could have said.

"What it wants," said Emeric, with his face perfectly straight, "is a box that's always blood heat. All night. Without anybody in it. I can make you one."

"He can make you one," said Hester to Imani.

"I heard him."

"I'd let him," said Hester, "if I were you. Before he says anything else."



He made it with Tess, in bay three, over three days, and Tess did most of it, which was the point.

The heat was the easy part; every wire carrying current got warm. What they wanted was a wire that got warm and stayed warm, did not rust in the damp of a box, and gave the same warmth on the hundredth night as on the first. Tess had tried copper and iron before lunch on the first day and had a burnt finger and a box that smelled of hot rust to show for it.

"Nickel," said Emeric. "And the chrome Grayrock sends for the stainless. Four parts to one." He had the ingot out of the cellar by evening, with a set of drawing dies, each a hair narrower than the last, as the cellar had made them for the bulbs' tungsten. "It'll fight you. It's hard."

It fought her. She drew it through every die on Bram's bench the next day, with Osric at the winch, and by night had a hundred feet of grey wire as fine as a hair; and a yard of it with current through it went a dull even red on the bench and stayed there for an hour and did not change colour or smell of anything.

"Nichrome," said Emeric. "Nickel and chrome. Call it that."

"You'd call a horse a horsemeat," said Tess, and wrote it on the coil's tag.

The hard part was holding it at blood heat all night with nobody watching. Tess thought about it for most of the third morning, chin on her fist, and then said she wanted a switch that knew how warm it was.

He gave her the strip: brass and iron rolled together into one, brass down one face and iron down the other. He held it over a candle and it bent, because the brass grew more than the iron in the heat and the strip had nowhere to go but round; and away from the candle it straightened as it cooled.

Tess watched it bend three times. Then she took it out of his hand.

"That's the switch," she said. "It's a switch that's a thermometer." She was already holding it against one of her relay contacts. "It touches when it's cold and lets go when it's warm, and the wire cools, and it touches again. On and off all night by itself." She looked up. "And you set the gap with a screw, and the screw's the heat."

"A bimetal strip," said Emeric. "Two metals. That's its name."

"That one I'll have."

By evening it was a cabinet of Fletcher's pine, double-walled with sheep's wool, two shelves inside and a coil of nichrome on porcelain pegs under the bottom one, and the strip on its screw behind a glass window on the side wall, and one of Ivo's thermometers through the lid. Tess set the screw with Imani beside her, a quarter turn at a time, until the mercury stood at the heat of a body's blood and stayed there, and the strip clicked off and on and off, and the wire's red glow came and went under the shelf as steady as a sleeper's breath.

"An incubator," said Emeric.

Hester put her hand in through the door, flat, palm down on the bottom shelf, and held it there while they waited.

"That's a bed," she said, and took her hand out. "That's my mother's bed, that is. Without my mother." She put her crock in it.



The broth grew in two days, a grey-green skin going velvet on the top of the jar. Imani laid it on the first wound on the fourth of June: a dock man's hand, torn on a hook three weeks before, red to the wrist, with the red line starting up the forearm. Half the festering wounds in the ward had the broth on a pad, morning and evening, and half had the pad without it, and Imani's own hand in her register every morning wrote down what the red had done in the night.

She would not say anything about it until she had the register to say it for her. But on the morning of the sixth Emeric came into the dispensary and found her standing over the dock man's hand with the dressing off, looking at it, with her own hand flat over her mouth.

The red line had gone back down the forearm an inch.

She saw him in the door and took her hand off her mouth and wrote it down.



"Can it remember?" said Tess.

It was the sixth of June, late, in bay three. She had the click of the incubator's strip in her head, she said, on and off all night like a clock with something wrong with it. She was at Bram's bench with two relays and four of the works' small battery lamps in a row on a board, and Osric on the stool beside her, watching her rather than the relays, the way he did.

"You said next month," she said. "In March. It's June."

"I was busy."

"You were afraid I'd do it first." She turned the board round. "So I've tried. The strip doesn't remember. When it's warm again it's forgotten it was ever cold. A relay's the same: it only knows what you're telling it this instant." She put her finger on the first relay. "Abel's hand has to know what it was doing a moment ago. So has anything that counts. You can't count if you forget the last number every time you say the next."

He pulled Bram's other stool over and sat down.

"Wire it to itself," he said.

She looked at him.

"When it closes, let it feed its own coil through its own contact." He took her pencil and drew it on the edge of her slate: the coil, the contact, the line back round. "Then it holds itself closed till somebody cuts the line. It remembers it was pressed."

Tess looked at the drawing for a long breath. Then she took the pencil back and drew a second relay beside it, with a line from each one's contact to the other's coil, crossed; and sat back.

"Two," she said. "One remembers on. Two remember which." There was colour in her face. "A switch with two sides that stays where you put it."

"A latch," said Emeric. "That's what it's called. Or a flip-flop."

"I'm not calling it that."

"It's a good name."

"It's a sandal," said Tess. "I'm not putting a sandal in Abel's hand." She was already wiring it; and when it was wired she pressed the left button, and the left relay clicked shut and the left lamp came on and stayed on when she took her finger away. She pressed the right, and the left lamp went out and the right came on, and stayed.

Osric said, "Well, that's the sentry and the relief."

Tess looked at him.

"One on, one off," said Osric. "Whoever's on stays on till he's relieved. You don't need to keep telling him." He shrugged. "That's all a gate is."

She wrote OSRIC in the margin of the slate beside it, small, the way she had in March, and did not say anything to him about it, and he did not say anything back, and Emeric looked at the battery lamps very hard for some time.

She built four of them by midnight, each with a third relay to steer it so that it turned over at every click instead of holding, in a row along the board, each feeding its lamp; and she wired the works' telegraph sounder to the first, so that every click on the line would turn it over, and the first, turning back, would turn the second, the way one man passing a bucket passes it on.

"Send," she said to Osric, and pointed at the test key. "Just clicks. Slowly."

Osric sent. One click: the first lamp came on. Two, and it went out, and the second came on. Three: the first and the second. Four: only the third. At seven she said "Stop," and looked at the board without blinking. The first three lamps were lit. The fourth was dark.

"It's not counting like a man," she said. "A man would put up seven fingers. This is putting up one and two and four." She was very still. "Each lamp is twice the one before."

"Binary," said Emeric. "That's what I said in March. Counting in twos."

"Then four lamps don't count to four," said Tess. She did not seem to have heard him; he could see her doing the sum behind her eyes. "Four lamps count to eight and four and two and one. Fifteen." She looked up at Osric. "Send me fifteen."

Osric sent fifteen. On the fifteenth click every lamp on the board was lit, all four in a row, and Tess sat with her hands in her lap and looked at them as if they were a thing she had seen in a church.

"And one more," she said, quietly, "and they all go out, and it starts again. With five lamps, thirty-one. With eight..." She stopped. "You knew it went this far."

"I knew it went a long way."

"You knew it went further." She pulled her data book toward her. "I did it in four hours, because you told me to wire it to itself and Osric told me it was a sentry." She began, without another word to either of them, to rule a page.



Rylan took the photographs into his drill on the seventh.

Ivo had printed them on Kaldo paper, seven sheets; Imani had pasted one set into the register on a leaf of its own, and given the other to Rylan without his asking.

He did not make a speech. The class stood in the yard in their long line, sixty-one of them, waiting for the wall; and Rylan gave the seven sheets to the first seven and let them go down the line, hand to hand, and stood at the end and waited.

Nobody laughed at these.

The sheets came back to him in silence, and he put them inside his coat and said, "Twist," and they ate their weighed portions, slowly, as he had shown them on the first morning; and he said, "The wall," and they jumped, and fell, and got up, until he said the fill was out of them; and then he took them down to the river, and they went in.

Noll Finch came last out of the water, as he always did, blue to the lips. He stopped by Rylan on the step with the water running off him and said something to him that Emeric, on the wall above, could not hear; and Rylan looked at him, and then put his hand on the back of the boy's wet neck, once, and took it away.



The lane transformers went up on the west half that same week, one on a pole at the head of every lane, grey iron boxes the size of a cask that took the works' high pressure in at the top and gave the houses the low out of the bottom; and the west half's children followed the gang from pole to pole and were shouted off every ladder.

Emeric walked the line of them on the Tuesday evening with Ferra, from the head of the first lane to the last, because she had asked him to, and because she wanted to count them herself and not off Bram's sheet. At the bottom of the bakery's lane she stopped.

The baker was at his hatch, with the shutter half down for the night. The ration book was on its nail inside, on its loop of twine, and he had it in his hands, and was reading it, standing up, by the last of the light; and he did not see them in the lane.

Ferra watched him for a while.

"He's done that every night since," she said. "Saban's man says. He reads the day's names through before he shuts the hatch. Every one. Then he goes in." She did not move. "Nobody told him to."

They stood in the lane a little longer. Then Ferra opened her own book against her arm, at the page headed THE WARNING, and took her pencil, and under the last line, 27 May. Three., she wrote a new one, small: The bakery hatch, every night, by his own hand. And looked at it, and left it, and closed the book.
