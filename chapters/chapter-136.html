Chapter 136 - Kaldo Black

They came down off the thorn hill in the last of the dark, with the lamp shuttered in Ward's hand and the ship already gone up behind them into nothing, and walked the mile into Wayford along the edge of Lisle's wood with the dew coming through their boots.

Nobody was on the south road. A dog barked at them from a yard at the edge of the town and was told to be quiet by somebody inside who did not come out to see why it had barked. The inn above the junction stood dark except for one window at the back, and the yard door was on the latch, the way Saban had said it would be, and they went up the back stair one step at a time, keeping to the wall end of each tread where the boards did not speak.

Hobb was on the landing.

He was sitting on a stool outside the door of the cold room, with a candle on the floor beside him and a book open on his knee, and he had been reading aloud, quietly, to a shut door, since a little after the second bell of the night before. He stopped when he saw them. He shut the book on his finger and stood up.

"Master Saban had the possets sent up at the second bell," he said. "And again at the fourth. I drank them."

"Both?"

"It seemed wrong to send them back full, Your Highness." He considered. "The second was better."

Emeric went in and took his boots off and got into the bed in his clothes, and Ward went along the passage to his own room, and after a little while, through the door, Hobb opened his book again and went on reading from where he had stopped, in the same low even voice, to a man who was now there to hear it.

He kept the chill two more days.

It was the cheapest time he was ever going to get, and he knew it. Nobody in Wayford expected a sick man to do anything, and nobody in Wayford came into a sick man's room, and a man lying on a bed with his eyes shut and a stone in his fist looked, from a doorway, exactly like a man with a fever lying on a bed with his eyes shut.

The stones were in a leather bag under the pillow. Forty of them, plain lamp stones, the charge-and-hold crystal Nevin cut for the mine lamps and Saban bought at the coast at four Copper the piece. Emeric had taken the bag off the shelf in the pattern store the night before, between the end of the table and the ship, and Ferra had written it in the book before he was out of the door: forty lamp stones, to Wayford, for the ink.

He took them one at a time.

In the delve a lamp stone was a small plain room with nothing in it, the way a new notebook is a stack of nothing, and what he put into each one was a knock. That was the nearest word he had for it. The ink boiled beside a charged stone gave off a falling pulse as it dried, and the pulse was the stone's charge going home to the air, and it carried, faintly, the shape of whatever had given it. So he gave each stone a shape. Not a pretty one. A short figure of beats, like a man knocking on a door in his own way, two and a long, or three and a pause and one, and he made them by a rule and not by ear, counting them out the way a machine in the old world would have counted, so that no two of the forty could ever be taken for each other however faint the pulse or however bad the hearing.

It took about a quarter of an hour a stone, and then he filled each one, the way Orrin would have filled it, and it went dim and heavy in his hand, and he put it down on the blanket beside him and took the next.

Hobb wrapped them. He came in when Emeric asked for him and sat on the end of the bed and wrapped each stone in a twist of the standard sheet and wrote a number on the twist in Ferra's hand, which was the only hand he wrote any more when he was working, because he had been copying it for two years and his own had gone somewhere and not come back.

"They're for the ink house," Emeric said.

"Yes, Your Highness."

"They make the ink set."

"Yes, Your Highness." Hobb wrote seventeen and folded the twist over and put it in the box. He did not ask anything else. He had kept the Grayrock tally house for two years under Ferra's eye, and he had learned from her that a clerk who wrote down what he was told was a clerk who got told things.

By the evening of the second day there were forty twists in the box, numbered one to forty, and two more that Emeric had written last and wrapped himself and put in his own coat, marked with a T.

The chill broke in the night, conveniently, and in the morning he went down to breakfast.

Saban was at the table under the window with the inn's accounts of three weeks spread in front of him as though they were his own, which in a sense they were, since he had been paying them.

"You look well," he said. "The town will be disappointed. There's a woman at the well who told me yesterday it was the water in Kaldo. That you'd been poisoned slow, years back, and it had come out in you."

"What did you tell her?"

"That I'd heard it was the drains." Saban turned a page. "Placit's man has called twice. He'll call again. He doesn't write, he calls. He wants to know if His Highness is well enough to name a night."

"I'll see him after the ink house."

"He'll be here before that." Saban did not look up. "He's in the yard."

Emeric saw him in the yard, and was pleasant, and a little pale, and said that he was much recovered and very grateful for his lordship's concern, and that a matter at the works called him home for a while, the kind of matter that did not wait for a man to feel like it, and that he would be back in Wayford inside the month and would count it an honour if the invitation still stood when he was. Placit's man was a grey, soft-voiced person who had very clearly been sent to see whether the prince was dying, and could now report that he was not, and he said that his lordship would be delighted, and that the other houses would be delighted, and looked once, while he said it, at the cart standing empty in the corner of the yard.

Then he went, and Emeric went down the junction road to his ink house.

It stood two doors up from the square on the Milford side, a narrow stone building three storeys high with a yard behind it and a smell in front of it that reached the road: galls and iron and gum and old wet wood, and under all of it something sour that had soaked into the stones over three generations and would not come out now if the house stood another three. There was a new board over the door with KALDO on it and, under that in smaller letters, AMBRY, because that had been in the terms.

Old Wendel Ambry was in the boiling room with his sleeves up.

He was a big man, bigger than Bram and softer, with a white beard he had tucked into his shirt to keep it out of the coppers, and he had sold the house and its coppers and its stock and its lease to Kaldo for fourteen Gold Rand and four Silver, and stayed on as master at two Silver a week, and been very plain with Emeric in the counting house about which of those two things he minded more. It was not the money. His son was a clerk in the post at Milford now, and his daughter had married into the post at the Wayford gate, and neither of them had ever wanted the ink, and he had not wanted to be the Ambry who sold it. He had sold it anyway. He stood at his copper now in the morning steam with the look of a man working in a house he had just been told was not his, and was trying to decide whether that made any difference to the work.

"The back room," Emeric said.

"The gall store."

"It was the gall store."

Wendel looked at him. Then he wiped his hands and took him through.

It was a long low room at the back, with one window onto the yard and one door onto the passage, and its walls were black to the height of a man with the dust of a hundred years of oak galls, and it still had the bins down one side. Emeric walked the length of it and back. He put his hand flat on the outer wall, and on the wall to the passage, and stood a moment in the middle of the floor looking at nothing.

"I'll want it cleared," he said. "Scrubbed, and limed. The bins out. A new door, and a lock on it that I'll send up from Kaldo. Two of our clerks will work in here, and the stock for the shop will come through it, and nobody else goes in. Not your men, and not you. I'm sorry for that."

"Kaldo's business."

"Kaldo's business."

Wendel nodded, slowly, the way a man nods at weather. "My grandfather put those bins in," he said, and then did not say anything else about it, and they went back to the boiling room.

The copper was on. It was a big pan, old and thick-walled and patched twice at the base, and the ink in it was Wendel's own receipt from the galls to the gum, with only the salt changed, the green iron salt out of the barrel that had come up on the cart from Kaldo instead of the salt the Ambrys had scraped off a wet heap for three generations. It was coming to the point where he would take it off.

Emeric took the twist marked one out of the box under his arm, and opened it, and held out the stone.

"Put that in," he said. "Before you take it off. Leave it in till it's cold, and then fish it out and give it to the clerk, and he'll put it in a cup with its number on. One stone to a boiling. Never the same stone twice."

Wendel took it and looked at it. It was a small grey crystal, dull, filled, a little heavy for its size.

"What does it do?"

"It makes the ink set."

That was true. It was not all of what the stone did, and Wendel was a man who had made ink for fifty years and knew when he had been told a true thing that was not the whole of a thing, and he weighed the stone in his palm a moment longer and then dropped it in. It went under without a sound. He took up his long stick and stirred, three times round and once across, the way he had been stirring since he was twelve.

He lifted the stick out to look at the colour.

The ink ran down it in a black sheet, and slowed, and stopped, and on the pale old wood where it had run it went from wet to dry in front of them, a dull black skin drawing in along the grain as quick as breath off a window, until the whole of the end of the stick was dry and black and he was standing in the steam holding it up like a man holding a torch.

Wendel Ambry laughed.

It came up out of him all at once, a great big laugh, loud enough that the two lads at the far copper turned round with their mouths open, and he stood there with the stick in his hand and his beard coming loose out of his shirt and laughed at it, and put his thumb on the black and took it off clean and laughed again.

"Forty years," he said, to nobody. "Forty years I've stood waiting on it."

Emeric turned to Hobb and asked for the book.

Hobb had it ready. It was a new book, quarto, bound in plain board, with its columns ruled out in the keep before it ever came north, and on its first page, in Ferra's hand, which was Hobb's hand now, he wrote the house and the day, and under that: Boiling one. Stone one. Master W. Ambry. He blotted it. He held it out for Emeric to see, and Emeric looked at it and nodded, and Wendel came and looked at it too over Hobb's shoulder, and read the line, and went back to his copper.

"The case that came up on the cart," Emeric said to Hobb, quietly. "The Kaldo black out of the finishing room. None of that is sold here. It goes home with me."

"It's the same ink."

"It isn't from this house."

Hobb wrote that down too, on a separate slip, and put it in the back of the book.

He left Saban in Wayford, and Hobb with him, to see the gall store cleared and the bins out and to sell paper across a counter to anybody who would buy it, and he left Wendel a box of thirty-nine stones in their twists and the rule that went with them. He and Ward rode out on the fourth morning with the carter, the cart empty but for the case of finishing-room black under a sack, and the town watched them go the way it had watched them come, from doorways, at the width of one step more than the street needed.

It was five days. The relay posts stood on their ridges one hard ride apart with their keepers' cottages under them, and at each one Ward stopped long enough to be seen and to see that the keeper was sober, and on the third day it rained, and on the fifth they came over the last rise and down to the Left Bridge in the evening with the island in front of them and its smoke going up into a clear sky.

Ferra opened the strongbox in the morning, at her own table, with Wenna at the other end of it copying the week's tallies and not looking up.

The green silk was on top. It had been the lining of a comb case once, a lady's, cut from its box and folded small, and it still held the shape of the combs in two soft ridges down its length. The two stones lay in it where Ferra had put them. Grey, dull, carved over every face in the fine repeating lattice that nobody in recorded history had ever made do anything at all. Under them lay the treasury clerk's slip, and Emeric did not need to read it, because he had read it the night Ward opened the box: Grey stones, carved. Two. Of no value.

"I entered them at the treasury's figure," Ferra said.

"Nothing."

"Nothing. It is the only figure anybody has given me." She took up her pen. "Which one?"

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters to the book."

He looked at them. They were the same stone, as near as two carved stones could be; the same size, the same lattice, the same grey. One had a chip out of a corner, small, old, worn smooth, as if it had been dropped on a floor a long time ago and picked up and kept anyway.

"That one," he said, and took the other.

Ferra wrote one taken, for the root, the whole one; the chipped one remains, and blotted it, and folded the silk back over the chipped stone and shut the lid and turned the key, and put the key back where it lived. She did not ask him anything else. Wenna, at the far end of the table, went on copying tallies.

He took it up to his room and put it on the table under the window and did not touch it for the rest of the day.

His mother had put one of these in his hands when he was small. His brother had set seven of them on a banquet table in front of two hundred people and told him they were unchanging, like him. And somewhere in his father's treasury a clerk had counted what was left of her things after she died and found two grey stones in a comb case and written them down as nothing. He did not know whether Corbin's seven had come out of the same drawer. He did not know who had bought them, or from whom, or what she had meant to do with them, and there was nobody left he could ask who would tell him, and he put that down where he put the other things he could not do anything about, and went down to the works.

He bound it that night, alone, the way he had bound the board's stone.

It was slow. It was always slow. A stone that had never had anybody in it did not take a man the first time he knocked; it took him the way a strange dog takes a hand, a long while after the hand was first held out, and only if the hand stayed still. He sat at the table under the window with the lamp out and the stone under his palm and went in and waited in the empty grey room it made, and came out when his head began to ache, and drank some water, and went in again. The bell went for the middle of the night. Somewhere below, the lake went on slapping at its wall. Some time before the first grey, without anything happening that he could have pointed at, it was his, the way the board's stone was his, and would answer him and nobody else, and he sat back in the chair and found that his hand on it was cold.

The reason it had to be one of these, and not a lamp stone, was simple enough to put in a sentence, and he put it in one in the notebook before he slept. A lamp stone was told a thing once, and kept it. The printer's page stones each held one page forever, and would print it for as long as anybody filled them and never anything else. But the thing in the back room at Wayford would have to make a new page every time anybody in five houses picked up a pen, and a stone that could be told something and then told something else was a stone of this kind, and every one he had owned until the box came was bound already, to somebody or to something that needed it. His own rode in the breast of his suit and in the silver star. The board's stone ran the wall. The rest were on six wrists. He was not going to leave either in a gall store five days away.

He wrote the rest of it into the root over four nights.

The names of places first, because the listening stones would be named for where they hung and not for who lived near them. Then the forty knocks, one to forty, and the two marked T, and the rule that went with them: a pulse that knocked in a way the root knew was a page to be made, and a pulse that knocked in a way it did not know, or did not knock at all, was nothing, and was let go. Then the head of the page, which was the name of the stone that had heard it, and the number of the knock, and a count the root would keep of its own, from the hour it woke, which Ferra's clerks could turn into a date. And under all of it the page itself, which was the hardest thing and the thing he had been doing without a word for it since the hold in the boat: the sense laid over a wet line and reading where the ink lay, drop by drop, and putting a dot on a grid wherever it lay, a hundred to a division of the master foot, exactly as he had once sat in the plain light for four nights and decided every point on a sheet by hand.

Forty knocks. Forty boilings, and then the root would have to be told more, and it could only be told by him with his hand on it, in Wayford. He wrote that down as a cost, and went on.

Bram made the casings.

He made them in brass, thin, in two halves the size of a man's fist that screwed together on a fine thread Jorrin cut, with a seat inside for three stones and a ring of pitch in the seam so that no light would ever come out of it, and when Emeric asked for them thin, so that the stone inside could hear through the wall of them, Bram made the next one thinner and did not ask why it had to hear. He had said build it, then, at the table, and he was building it. That was the whole of what he had said.

Inside each casing were three stones. One was a lamp stone Emeric had written from his own sense, as near as he could put the thing he did when he laid the sense over a wet line into a stone that could not think, which was not very near, but was near enough; it listened, and it could tell the root where the ink lay, and nothing else. The other two were beads out of the forge, dense, the same beads that went into the bands and the suits, and they took turns. One ran the listening stone and spent itself doing it. The other sat by and drank, slowly, from the air around it, the way a lamp stone left on a sill will fill a little in a week, and when the first ran low the listener went over to the second, and the first sat by and drank. It would not last forever. Nothing that spent faster than it drank lasted forever. But it would last a long time up under an eave where nobody would ever put a hand, and it would not need a man to come and fill it.

When they were done Tobe blacked them with soot in pitch, and they looked like nothing. Like a lump of old swallow's nest, or the paper end of a wasp's house, or the knot of a gutter bracket.

They tried them in the yard.

He boiled two small pots of ink in the finishing room with the stones marked T, one each, and Pim wrote with the first of them at a bench while Emeric walked away from him with a casing in his hand and the root in his coat and his eyes shut, and called back the distance each time he heard, and Osric paced it. Ferra stood at the finishing-room door and wrote down what they called.

Through lath and plaster, the finishing room's own partition and the wall of the glue house beyond it, the stone heard Pim's pen to thirty yards, clear, every stroke. At thirty-five it heard one word in three. Through the wall of the keep, which was six feet of rubble between two faces of dressed stone, it heard almost nothing at all standing hard against the wall, and nothing at a pace off it. Pim let the page dry and read it aloud, slowly, with his finger on the line, and the stone heard him read it and heard nothing of the page, because dry ink was silent, and always would be. And in the evening Declan rowed a second casing across the lake to the far shore and sat on a stone with it on his knee, and the first casing heard the second call to it plainly across open water at a quarter of a mile, and not much further.

Ferra wrote the numbers down once, in the order they had come, and closed the book on them. Nobody had to say them again.

Jorrin turned rails for two.

The printers were the printer from the winter before last grown twice: the same flat iron bed and the same two rails and the bridge and the carriage no bigger than a fist, and the same head that Bram had drilled under a lens and sworn at, and he swore at the second head exactly as much as he had sworn at the first. What was different was on the bridge. On the first printer, the one that printed Orrin's primers, a page stone sat in its socket and told the head everything it would ever print, one page, forever. On these two there was a socket and a lead of fine brass wire to a board at the end of the bed, and the thing on the other end of the board was the root.

The ink went in plain. Ferra carried the jar down herself, as she had the first time, and said, with no expression at all, that the Kaldo black was on the shelf at the far end of the room and would stay there, because a printer that wrote in the ink the stones could hear would hear itself.

They set the first of the two frames up at the far end of the finishing room, with the root in its socket, and Emeric took a bottle of the second T ink and a pen and a sheet and went out through the glue house and across the yard and up the ladder into the drying loft, which was thirty paces off through two walls of lath and one of board, and sat down on a stool under the louvres with the sheet on his knee.

He wrote one line, about the weather, because he could not think of anything else to write. He stopped halfway through it to decide whether the wind was in the west or the south-west, and the pen sat on the sheet while he decided, and then went on.

When he came back across the yard the printer had stopped. There was a sheet on the bed.

At the top of it, small, in square letters the root had made out of dots: YARD. T2. and a number, which was the hours since it had woken. Under that was his line.

It was his hand. It was not like his hand; it was his hand, the long tail he put on a g, the way his s sat a little below the line, and in the middle of it, where he had stopped to think about the wind, a small round blot where the pen had rested on the sheet, exactly the size and shape of the blot on the page upstairs in his coat.

Ferra took both sheets to the window.

She held them one over the other against the light, the way Jorrin had once held the first two printed pages up against the same window, and moved them until they lay true, and looked at them for a long time. The dots sat inside his pen line all the way along it. The blot sat on the blot.

"It stopped where you stopped," she said.

"Yes."

She lowered the sheets and looked at the printer, and at the root in its socket, and then at him, and did not say anything else, and gave him back the one he had written and kept the other, and went up to enter it.

The pads came last, because they were the part he liked least.

A man placing a stone under the eave of a house at night had to stay there long enough to place it, with both hands, in the dark, without a sound, and the suits could hold a man in the air, but a man held in the air was a man with his stones lit and his cape open and every part of him working, and a watchman who looked up at the wrong moment would see a shape against the sky that did not belong there. A man stuck to a wall with his stones dark was a shadow. So the pads.

Each was a shallow cup of turned brass the width of a palm, with a rim of Rowena's pitch round its lip, warmed soft and pressed on, black and tacky and smelling of tar and of something sharper under the tar. Behind the cup sat a small air stone in a housing of its own, the same stone that drove the palms and the calves of the suits, and it did one thing: when the cup was pressed to a wall, it drew the air out of it through a vent the width of a straw, and when it was told, it pushed the air back in. One went on the sole of each boot, turned in on a hinge so a man could walk on the plate and not on the pad. One went on the off gauntlet, set into the heel of the palm below the palm's own stone. They were the first thing Rowena's pitch had ever done for Kaldo that was any use.

Nevin cut the valves for the masks in the same week, because the pads and the masks went on the same belt and Emeric did not want the suits opened twice.

The masks were oiled leather, cut close over the nose and mouth by the saddler, with a flap valve at the cheek that Nevin cut from thin horn and fitted by hand, fourteen times before he had one that did not stick, and a hose of waxed gut that ran from the mask down the back of the neck and looped once through the warm channels at the small of the back before it came round to the hip, so that whatever came up it came up warm and did not freeze the breath in a man's throat. At the hip, clear of the front heart and clear of the pack, on the belt, was a rack of three tin tubes in a row, like the pipes of a small organ, empty.

Ward fetched the candles.

He went down to the old smoke house under the lake wall with his own key in the late afternoon, alone, and before he went he walked through the pattern store and the forge room and the store behind the forge and looked on every bench and in every corner and the bottom of every box, and then went and stood in the foundry door and looked at the dry store from across the yard without going into it. Emeric watched him do it and did not say anything. The saltpeter jar and the charcoal sack were in the dry store, on the far side of the yard, and there was nothing in the pattern store but iron and pitch and brass, and Ward knew both of those things perfectly well. He looked anyway. Then he went down to the lake wall.

He came back with the candles in a box against his chest, eighteen tin cylinders the length of a finger with their strikers in their caps, and put fifteen of them into five racks, three to a suit, one suit at a time, and closed each rack, and tried each catch. The last three went back into the box and the box went back into the smoke house, and he locked it, and came back and hung the key on its nail inside his coat.

Nobody tried one. There was nothing to try one on. They were a quarter of a glass of breath each, for a man sitting still, and less for a man working, and there were eighteen of them in the world and would be no more until there was lead for the acid, and every man who wore one knew that, because Emeric had told each of them at the rack with his hand on the tube, the same words each time. They were for when the air went and a man could not come down. They were not for when the air was thin and a man could.

Ward tried the pads on the keep that night.

The east face of the keep ran straight up out of the yard for fifty feet of dressed stone laid in good mortar, and there was no moon, and the only light was the lamp at the forge door behind them, and Ward walked to the foot of the wall in his suit with the cape furled and the helm off and his stones dark, and put the pad on his left hand flat to the stone at the height of his head, and pressed.

There was no sound. He took his hand away from it and the hand did not come away. He put his weight on it, carefully, then all of it, and lifted his feet, and hung.

He went up the wall like that, pad and boot and boot and pad, slowly, with the stone doing nothing but sitting in the dark behind each cup, and at each step when he wanted a pad to let go he told it, and there was a small soft pop in the dark, like a cork out of a jar, and it let go. At the top he stayed there a long while with his cheek to the stone and his stones dark and his breathing even, and from the yard he was not there at all. Osric, standing beside Emeric, looked for him for a count of ten and could not find him, and said so.

The second night they took him to the Western Gate.

The gate towers were older than the keep by two hundred years and had been patched more times than anybody had counted, and the mortar in the old courses on the outer face had gone to something between sand and chalk, and they went out over the Left Bridge in the dark to the foot of it with nobody on the bridge to see them. Ward went up the first ten feet the way he had gone up the keep. At ten feet he moved his left boot to a new place on the old face and set it and put his weight on it, and the pad took the face of the stone and the face of the stone came away from the stone behind it, a flake the size of a plate, with the pad still stuck to it, and he dropped.

He dropped about a foot. Then the right boot, which had not moved, took him, with a jerk that went up through the whole of him, and he hung there on one boot and one hand with a flake of the Western Gate stuck to the sole of his other foot, and did not say anything, and did not light a stone.

"Old mortar," Osric said, below.

Ward reached down with his free hand, slowly, and peeled the flake off his pad, and passed it down to Osric, and Osric passed it to Emeric, and Emeric turned it over in his hands in the dark. It had pitch on one face and nothing on the other, and it was lighter than it had any right to be.

"Sound stone," Emeric said. "Only sound stone. If a pad goes, it's the wall, not the pad. You feel the wall first."

"With what?"

"Your hand. Before you trust your weight to it."

Ward looked down at him from the wall for a moment. Then he put his free hand flat on the old stone above his head, and moved it, and moved it again, and found a place, and set the pad there, and went on up.

Ferra entered the whole of it on the fifth evening, at the long-room table, with Bram and Armand at the far end of it arguing about a gear and the lamp between them.

She did it in two places. In the barony's book, in the fund's line, she wrote one line that was true and told nobody anything, and blotted it. Then she took the key off the cord at her neck and opened the box Bram had made for her, and took out the itemised sheet, and wrote the rest on it, reading each line aloud as she went, as figures, not as anything else: a hundred lamp stones at four Copper, forty for the setting and twenty for the listening and forty for the forge to drink and press into beads, the forge's time entered at nothing because the forge was Kaldo's; twenty casings in brass, Bram's shop, at two Silver apiece; two printers complete, rails and beds and heads and carriages, at four Gold and five Silver each; twenty air stones off the etching floor for the pads; the cups, the saddler, the gut, the horn, Nevin's time. Pitch, from the duchy of Emberfell, a gift, entered at nothing, and she paused over that one before she wrote it, the way she paused over every gift, because a gift was a figure somebody else had chosen not to tell her.

"Nineteen Gold Rand," she said, "six Silver, and four Copper."

It was under the line. It was not a great deal under the line. She did not say so. She turned to the schedule at the back of the box, where the five conditions were written out and signed, and beside the first of them, in the space that had been left blank at the table, she wrote two names, Wenna and Dace, and blotted them. Then, because she did not need to say anything about the line, she turned the sheet round on the table so that he could read it, and he read it, and turned it back, and she locked it in the box and put the key back on the cord and the cord back inside her collar.

Bram, at the other end of the table, had stopped arguing about the gear, and was looking at the box.

They went out to the keep one more time that night, because Ward wanted it done once more before he called it learned.

He went up the east face in the dark as he had the first night, pad and boot and boot and pad, the stones dark, the only sound the small soft pop of each cup as it let go. Two thirds of the way up he stopped. He took his left boot off the wall, and then his right, with a pop and a pop, and hung there by the pad on his off hand alone, fifty feet up the Baron's Keep in the dark with his legs hanging and his whole weight on a brass cup the width of a palm and a rim of pitch from the other side of the world, and stayed there. He stayed there for a slow count of thirty. Emeric counted it. So, beside him, did Osric, under his breath.

At thirty Ward reached up with his free hand and set it flat on the stone above the pad, and felt the wall, and was satisfied with it.

Then, in the dark, above them, very small, the pop as he let go.
