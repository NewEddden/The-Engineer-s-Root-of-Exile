Chapter 136 - Isolde

Isolde Sallow came in at the South Gate on the twenty-ninth of January in a closed carriage with her house's arms on the door, green and silver, behind four matched greys, with eight riders round her in the same colours and her aunt's steward on a tall bay at the carriage window, and the whole of the high street came out of its doors to watch her go up it.

Nothing like her had come up the high street in living memory. The town looked at the greys and the silver harness and the riders' cloaks and the carriage's glass windows, and then at the girl behind the glass, and Emeric, standing at the foot of the keep stair with Ward on one side and Ferra on the other, watched the town look, and watched it decide, all at once and all together, that this was what a prince's wife looked like.

The steward got down first. He was a tall grey man with a long face and very good gloves, and he came across the gate yard to Emeric and bowed exactly as deep as the law required to the son of a king and not a hair deeper, and presented the compliments of his mistress the first queen, and of her brother the lord of Sallow, and their hopes for the holder's health, and their great pleasure in sending to him, to be his guest for a season, the lord of Sallow's daughter.

Then he stepped aside, and the carriage door opened, and she got down.

She was nineteen, as Saban had said, and tall, nearly as tall as Emeric, with fair hair coiled and pinned beneath a little green cap that did nothing to diminish her beauty. Her face was striking, with high cheekbones, a delicate nose, and clear, luminous eyes set beneath finely arched brows. There was a cleverness to her expression, a quiet confidence in the way she held herself, and a fullness to her lips that hinted at a smile she had learned to suppress more often than indulge. She was beautiful enough to rival Ferra and Imani, though in a manner entirely her own, less striking at first glance than she was difficult to forget once one had looked. She was dressed for the capital, in green wool and grey fur, and stood in the gate yard of a barony on the edge of the kingdom, with mud on the cobbles, smoke rising from the coke ovens beyond the wall, and the entire high street staring at her. She paid none of it any mind. Her eyes were fixed on Emeric.

Then she looked, quickly, at Ward, and at Ferra, and at the keep door behind them, and up at its windows, one after another, counting.

"Highness," she said, and curtseyed, very well, exactly as deep as the law required and not a hair deeper.

"Lady Isolde."

"My aunt sends her love." Isolde straightened. "She doesn't, of course. But she told me to say so, and I've said it, and now we needn't talk about her any more, if that's agreeable." She smiled. It was a real smile, quick, and it went away again almost before it had come, as if she had remembered whose gate yard she was in. "It's very cold. Is it always this cold?"

"Only in winter."

"Then I've come at a bad time," said Isolde Sallow, "for everything," and went past him up the keep stair before the steward could stop her, with her skirts held up out of the mud in one gloved hand.



She was better company than anybody had expected. That was the first thing, and it was the thing Emeric could not forgive her for, afterward, because it made everything harder.

He had expected the capital. A girl taught by the first queen's household to sit at a table and say the right thing to a prince, and listen to him as if every word were a pearl, and count the doors behind his back. She did count the doors. He saw her do it, every room she went into, quickly, without moving her head. But she did not do the rest of it.

At dinner on the first night, in the hall, at the high table that Kaldo had not used since Wexler and had brought out of the store for her, she sat on Emeric's right as the steward had made very sure she would, and ate Maren's food, all of it, and asked Maren afterward through a kitchen girl how the pastry had been made, and listened to the answer. She asked Ward how many men were in the garrison, and when Ward told her the number exactly, as he told everybody the number exactly, she looked at him in surprise and said that in her father's house nobody would have told her, and Ward said that in her father's house nobody would have been asked. She laughed. Ward did not, but something at the corner of his mouth moved.

She asked Emeric almost nothing. That was the thing he noticed most. She asked Ferra.

Ferra was at the high table because the Chancellor sat at the holder's table, and the steward had looked at her when she sat down, at her plain dress and her red hair pinned up any way at all and the ink on her fingers, and Emeric had seen him decide what she was and dismiss her. Isolde had not. Isolde had looked at Ferra once, the way she looked at the doors, and then leaned across Emeric at the end of the first course and said, directly, to Ferra, that she had heard in the capital that the barony's books were kept by a woman, and was it true.

"Yes," said Ferra.

"All of them? Four baronies?"

"Yes."

"Alone?"

"With three clerks," said Ferra, "and an apprentice, and a clockmaker's widow who argues with me about carrying." She did not look up from her plate. "And a machine."

Isolde looked at her.

"I'd like to see it," she said. "The machine." She paused. "And the books. If that's allowed." She sat back. "At home nobody lets me near the books. My father says a woman who can read a ledger is a woman who'll want to." She smiled her quick smile. "He's quite right."

Ferra looked up from her plate then. At Isolde. And Emeric, sitting between them, saw something pass from one to the other across his place at the table that he was not part of and did not understand, and was not meant to.

"Tomorrow," said Ferra. "At nine. In the counting room. Wear something you don't mind getting ink on."



Isolde went to the counting room at nine, in a grey wool dress that was the plainest thing she had brought, and spent the morning there, and Emeric did not go near it, on purpose, and heard about it at noon from three different people.

Ferra had shown her everything. Not the file, which was in Ferra's room in the left-hand box in a cipher Isolde could not have read. Not the left-hand box at all. But the books. The barony's books, the four baronies' now, open on the counting room table, Kaldo's and Grayrock's in Ilsa Renner's new hand and Milford's in Paxton's steward's and Wayford's in Aster's. How they were ruled and why. How a line got into them and how it was checked and by whom. Ottoline's machine, which Isolde had turned the crank of herself, four times, and laughed at, and then turned again with her face perfectly serious and done a sum against it in her head and lost by a second.

And when the steward had come to the counting room door before noon, to take his mistress to see the wall, Ferra had said that the lady was busy, and the steward had looked at Ferra, and Ferra had looked back at him, and the steward had gone away.

"She was kind to her," said Hale, telling it. He said it as if it surprised him. "The Chancellor. She didn't have to be. That girl's here to take her place at the table, or what the town thinks is her place, and everybody in the counting room knew it, and the girl knew it, and the Chancellor knew it." He was quiet. "And she sat her down and showed her how to rule a page."



The press was the other thing Isolde saw, and it was the press that made Emeric understand what she was.

It had been coming since the first winter. Tess had carved letter blocks out of pear wood for the school, then: one block for each letter, cut backward on the end grain, so that a child could ink them and press them onto a slate or a sheet and see the letter come out the right way round. A, and B, and all the rest. Sela had used them for four years. They were worn nearly smooth now, and the A had split.

Tess had grown them up. Over the winter, at the works, at night, she had made moulds of every letter, in brass, from the pear-wood blocks: a little cup the shape of the letter backward, sunk in the end of a brass bar. And Bram had cast type in them in the foundry, out of lead and tin and a little of the hill's antimony, which ran thin into the fine lines and set hard: a little metal bar for every letter, the height of a finger joint, with the letter standing up backward on its end. Hundreds of them. Every letter many times over, in a wooden case with a box for each letter, so that a man could pick them out one at a time and set them in a row in a frame, face up, backward, and make a word, and a line, and a page.

And the press. Bram's. A great heavy oak frame with a flat iron bed at the bottom and a flat iron plate above it, and between them, driving the plate down, a screw as thick as a man's arm, cut on the lathe, with a long bar through its head to turn it by. You inked the type in the frame with a leather ball. You laid a sheet of the new sized paper on it. You slid the frame under the plate and pulled the bar round, and the screw drove the plate down, hard, and pressed the paper onto the inked type, and you pulled the bar back and slid the frame out, and lifted the sheet.

A printing press. The plainest one there was. The kind any shop in the kingdom could build, if it had a smith and a foundry and somebody who had thought of it.

The first sheet off it, on the thirty-first of January, in the lower floor of the mill beside the stamper, with half the works crowded round, was the school's alphabet. All of it, A to the end, in Tess's letters, in a row across the top of a sheet of Kaldo paper in plain black ink, and under it the numbers, one to ten. Exactly what Tess had cut in pear wood in the first winter.

Tess lifted it off the type herself. She held it up. And then she gave it to Sela, who had come down from the school for it, and Sela held it in both hands and looked at it and did not say anything, and took it back up the hill to nail on the school door, where it stayed until the rain got it.

Isolde was there. She had asked to come, at dinner, the night before, and Emeric had said yes because he could think of no reason to say no that would not be worse. She stood at the back of the crowd in the mill's lower floor in her grey wool with her steward beside her, and watched the type set and the bar pulled and the alphabet come up off the frame, and while everybody else was looking at the sheet in Tess's hands, Emeric saw her look at the press.

Not at the sheet. At the press. The frame, the screw, the bed. The case of type beside it with its hundreds of little boxes. She looked at it all for some time, the way she looked at doors, and then she came forward through the crowd to where Emeric stood beside it.

"How many can it make?" she said. "In a day."

"A thousand sheets, perhaps. With two men. More, when they're quick at it."

"A thousand." Isolde looked at the sheet in Tess's hands. "The same sheet."

"Or a thousand different ones. You change the type."

Isolde was quiet.

"In my aunt's house," she said, "there are twelve clerks. They copy. All day. Every letter my aunt sends, every proclamation my uncle wants read in the villages, every notice, every list. By hand. Twelve of them. And they make perhaps a hundred sheets between them in a day, and every one of them can be changed on the way by a clerk who's paid to change it." She looked at the type in its case. "And this makes a thousand. All the same. That nobody can change once it's set." She looked up at Emeric. "Who decides what it prints?"

The mill's lower floor had gone quiet round them. The steward, at Isolde's shoulder, had gone very still.

Emeric looked at her.

It was the right question. It was the only question. It was the question nobody in Kaldo had asked yet, not Ferra, not Tess, not Sela with the alphabet in her hands. A press that made a thousand sheets a day that nobody could change once they were set was not a machine for making alphabets. It was a machine for making the same thing true in a thousand places at once. Whoever decided what it printed decided what a thousand people read. And on his other world, the day the first one like it was built, the people who had decided what everyone read had found that they no longer did, and it had taken them a century of war to stop trying.

"Right now?" said Emeric. "Tess."

"And later?"

"I don't know yet."

Isolde looked at him for a long breath.

"That's honest," she said. "My aunt won't believe it." And then, quietly, so that the steward at her shoulder would have to lean in to hear it, and Emeric saw him lean: "I'll tell her anyway."



The telegraph went up to Grayrock that week along Linus Vance's route, forty miles of it, on poles from the baron's woods set every hundred paces and a single wire of Tess's drawn iron between them on glass knobs from Ivo's glasshouse, with Linus walking ahead with his theodolite and his book, marking every pole, and writing down every gradient and every bend in the margin of the weather, and saying nothing about it to anyone.

And Ambrose's rods went up on the towers.

Ambrose Lark had come south in the spring of the third year to look at the dynamo and stayed for the lightning. In the third summer he and Tess had flown a paper kite the size of a door off the wall into a storm on a wire, and built Kaldo its first lightning arrester between them in two days while Emeric stood in the door and said nothing. Now there would be more: iron rods, pointed, on the top of every tall thing in Kaldo, with a cable from each down the outside of the wall into the wet ground at the foot. A road for the lightning that went round everything Kaldo cared about instead of through it.

They went up that January on the wall towers, and the works chimney, and the water tower's blue roof, which made the pigeons furious. Ambrose went up every ladder himself. He was nearly sixty and he went up the water tower's ladder to its conical roof in a north wind with a rod over his shoulder, and stood on the top rung with his arm round the roof's iron finial, and looked out over the whole of Kaldo under the grey sky, and Emeric, at the foot of the ladder, saw his face, and understood that he had wanted to stand exactly there for thirty years.



The chair failed for the third time on the second of February, in the cart shed, and nobody laughed.

It was the heating. The chair was for Tess, and Tess could not feel her legs, and in the winter they went cold and blue, all the way up, and she did not know it until Bram or the girl from the north well looked at them and saw. So the chair would warm her. A fire stone under the seat, set low, a gentle heat, the warmth of a hearthstone, coming up through the cushion. And in the summer the same stone the other way, pulling the heat out, so she did not cook in the sun.

He had set the stone under the seat that morning, and filled the cushion with horsehair, because horsehair was what cushions were filled with, and sat on it himself for a quarter of an hour, in the cold shed, to feel how warm it was.

It was warm. It was pleasant. He sat on it and made notes on his slate about the setting.

And after a quarter of an hour he smelled it.

The sharp brown smell of scorching hair, of a blanket too near a hearth, the smell he had smelled at the powerhouse door the morning Tess had cut a fire sign on a chip of stone out of boredom and it had woken in her pocket. He stood up. He turned round. The cushion under where he had been sitting had gone brown in a ring the size of a plate, and in the middle of the ring, where the stone was nearest, the horsehair was smouldering. A thin grey thread of smoke was coming up out of it.

He had felt warm. He had not felt it burning. He had been sitting on it, writing, and his body had felt the warmth, and the warmth had been pleasant, and the cushion had been charring under him for some minutes before the smell reached his nose, because a man sitting on a warm seat does not look under himself.

He pulled the cushion out of the frame and threw it on the floor of the shed and stamped on it.

When he straightened up Imani was standing in the shed door.

She had come about Tess's legs. He learned that afterward. She had heard from the girl from the north well, who had heard from Tess, that the prince was making the chair warm, and she had come down from the hospital ground to tell him how warm, because she was the one who had looked after Tess's legs for two years and knew exactly how much heat they could take and for how long, and had thought he might want to know before he built anything.

She stood in the door and looked at the cushion on the floor. At the brown ring. At the thread of smoke still coming up from the horsehair. At the fire stone under the seat, still warm, still set.

She did not say anything at first.

Then she said: "She wouldn't have."

Very quietly.

Emeric stood in the middle of the shed with his boot on the cushion and did not move.

She was right. He had smelled it. He had stood up because he had smelled it, after a quarter of an hour of warmth he had felt as pleasant. Tess would not have felt it at all. Not the warmth. Not the burning. Not anything, from the waist down. She would have sat on that cushion and written on her slate, as he had, and the stone would have gone on warming the horsehair under her, and the horsehair would have charred, and then the cloth of her dress, and then her, and she would not have known until somebody smelled it.

Bram was in the shed. He had been at the lattice. He did not say anything either. Nobody laughed. Nobody said anything at all for a long breath in the cold shed with the smoke coming up off the floor.

Imani came in. She bent and picked up the cushion and looked at the ring, close, the way she looked at a burn on a child's arm. Then she put it down on the bench.

"A shield," she said. "Between the stone and the cushion. Something that spreads it. A plate. Copper, if Bram has it." She looked at Bram, who nodded. "And it doesn't go on the setting you'd like. It goes on the setting her legs can take, which is less than you'd think, because her blood doesn't move in them the way yours does and they can't carry the heat away." She looked at Emeric. "And it doesn't go on for longer than an hour without stopping, by itself, and starting again when it's cooled. Because she won't know."

"Yes."

"I'll do the heating," said Imani. "And the cooling. All of it. How warm, how long, where. You'll make it do what I say." She did not make it a question. "You don't know what her legs can take. I do."

"Yes," said Emeric.

Imani looked at him a little longer.

"You sat on it," she said. "To feel it. Yourself. For a quarter of an hour."

"Yes."

"That was the right thing to do," said Imani. "With the wrong legs." She picked up the cushion again. "I'll take this. I'll want to show Tess."

"Show Tess?"

"She'll want to know," said Imani. "It's her chair. She'll want to know what it nearly did and why it won't." She turned to the door with the charred cushion under her arm. "She'd rather know than be protected from it. So would you."

She went out. Emeric stood in the shed. Bram, at the lattice, picked up a sheet of copper off the bench and looked at it, and began, without a word, to mark it out for a plate.
