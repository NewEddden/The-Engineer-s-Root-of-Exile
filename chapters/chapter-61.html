Chapter 61 - No Rings

Armand ran the whole set again on the Monday, in a different order, against a fresh standard, with two of the foundry men swapped for two he had not measured before.

On the Tuesday he came up to the long room and put the sheet down and stood over it with both hands on the table, which was not how he usually stood.

"Eleven of the twelve," he said, "are inside the range I would expect of eleven grown men taken off any street in this kingdom. The spread is a little narrow and the middle is a little high and neither of those is worth a sentence on twelve people."

"And the twelfth."

"The twelfth is not inside that range." Armand's finger came down on a figure near the bottom of the sheet. "The twelfth is inside the range of a made knight. Not the top of it. The lower third of it. He would not be a distinguished one." He straightened up. "He would be one."

Emeric looked at the name written beside the figure in Armand's crabbed hand.

"Tobin."

"That is what he is called, yes."

They had him up within the hour, which Emeric regretted afterward, because it meant the man came in off the floor with the grey still on his hands and no idea what he was walking into, and stood in a room with a prince, a mage of the tower and a bench in it, and was asked to take his shirt off.

He did it. He was not a man who made difficulties.

Armand went over him properly, the way he did everything, which took a long time and involved a lamp held close and a good deal of turning of the arms. Forearms first, inner side. Then the upper arms. Then the hollow of each elbow, then the shoulders, then the neck on both sides and the line down under the collarbone.

There was a burn scar across the back of one hand from a foundry job three years before that Imani had dressed. There was an old white line down one shin from something he could not remember. There was nothing else on him at all.

"Turn to the light," Armand said, and then, after a while, "again," and then he stopped and stepped back and said nothing for long enough that Tobin began to look worried.

"Am I ill?"

"No," Armand said. "Put your shirt on. Thank you. You may go back to work. Nobody is going to trouble you about this."

Tobin went. At the door he stopped and said, to Emeric, "Is it the answer?"

"It's a piece of one."

"Right," said Tobin, and went back down to the floor, and told nobody anything, which Emeric only learned much later and was grateful for.

Armand sat down after that, which he did not often do in another man's room, and he gave the lecture. Emeric did not ask for it. He had worked out inside about four seconds that the old man needed to say it out loud in order to refuse to believe what he was about to be told, and had the sense to let him.

"You have seen the marks," Armand said. "On your father's knights. On Rylan, who lives in this building. Describe them to me."

"Raised lines under the skin. Forearms mostly. Following the veins, or near enough. Paler than the skin around them. Rylan has them up the inside of both arms to the elbow and one that goes up the side of the neck."

"Bands in the north. Rings in the capital. The Church calls them the tracery and the Circle does not call them anything, because the Circle regards naming a thing as the first step toward being sentimental about it." Armand put his hands flat on his knees. "Do you know what they are?"

"No."

"Then I will tell you, and you will understand that what I am telling you is not a theory. It is the settled description of the oldest working in this kingdom, and it has not changed in four hundred years, and I have taught it to boys who are now old men."

He held up one finger.

"A man is born with pathways. Call them channels, call them what you like, the word does not matter and every generation invents a new one. They are narrow. What flows through them is what he can move, and how wide they are is what he can hold, and both of those are fixed at birth in the overwhelming majority of men, which is why the assessment is made once and never repeated.

"The rite takes a candidate and forces pure mana into him. Not his own. Delivered, from outside, by eight men working in relay, in waves, for between nine and fourteen hours depending on the house and the year and how obstinate the candidate's body is. The pressure is very great. The pathways widen under it, because they must widen or they tear, and those are the only two things that happen."

"And if they tear."

"Then he dies in the chamber, or he dies in the week after it, or he lives and is ruined, and those three are not rare." Armand's voice did not change at all. "The marks are what widening at that speed leaves behind. Tissue that has been made to do in a night what it was never built to do at any speed. Every knight in Randillia carries them. There has never been one who did not."

"And the capital reads them as proof of what he is."

"The capital reads them as proof of what he survived, which it treats as the same statement, and for practical purposes it is. A man with the tracery has stood in the chamber and come out. You cannot counterfeit it. You cannot buy it. It is the one credential in the kingdom that is written on the body." He spread his hands. "So. There is your answer. Whatever is happening on your etching floor, that man has not been made a knight, because he has no marks, and a man without marks has not been through the rite, and capacity is what a man is born with."

Emeric had spent the entire lecture deciding whether to say it and had been certain for most of it that he would not.

"How many die?" he said.

"In the rite?"

"Yes."

Armand opened his mouth, and stopped, and Emeric watched a man of seventy-two reach for a number that he had assumed all his life was somewhere and discover that it was not.

"It is not counted," he said, at last. "Not centrally. Each house keeps its own and the houses do not compare, and the Circle does not ask, and the Crown does not ask, because the Crown receives knights and does not audit the making of them."

"Then how do you know it isn't rare?"

"Because of the liturgy," Armand said. "The dead of the chamber are named. Every one of them, by name, going back to the founding. It is read on the Feast of the Ascent."

"How long does it take to read?"

There was a pause that went on rather too long.

"It is read in portions," Armand said. "Across the year. It cannot be read through in one sitting."

Emeric said nothing. Armand looked at the far wall for a while, and then said, with some irritation, "That is not evidence of anything. Four hundred years is a great many years and a great many men."

"It is."

"Say what you are going to say."

So he said it.

"The marks aren't a grade," Emeric said. "They aren't a credential and they aren't a verdict and they aren't proof of anything except one fact. They're scar tissue. They're the record of an injury. The rite delivers the right total at a rate the body can't take, and about so many men a year come apart under it, and the ones who don't come apart heal, and the healing is the tracery, and the kingdom has spent four hundred years reading the scar as the achievement."

He put a hand on the sheet on the table.

"And that man has been carrying charged stone in his bare hands eleven hours a day for two years, and his hands are stained and his skin is clean, and he measures where he measures. Same total. Different rate. That's all I'm saying. That's the whole of it. It's a parameter."

Armand stood up.

Emeric had expected anger and had prepared for anger, and what he got first was something much quieter and much worse, which was that the old man went and stood at the window with his back to the room for a considerable length of time.

"Do you know what you have said," he said finally.

"I know how it sounds."

"You do not, or you would have said it differently, or not at all." Armand turned round. "The rite is the oldest instrument the Circle and the Church hold in common. It is not a workshop procedure. The Church consecrates the chamber. A cardinal attends the making of every knight in the royal service and the liturgy of the dead is his office, not ours. Four hundred years of men have gone into that room, and their fathers have stood outside it, and their names are read out on a feast day by priests. And you have sat in a chair in a southern barony and described the entire apparatus as a mistake in a setting."

"I've described it as a rate problem."

"You have described it as a rate problem," Armand agreed, "which is precisely what a heresy sounds like from the inside. It always does. It is always a small technical correction and the men making it are always surprised."

He came back to the table.

"And there is a second thing and it is worse than the first, and you have not thought about it for one moment, because you never do." His voice had risen for the first time in fourteen months. "If you are right, then every man named in that liturgy did not fail. He was not weak, and he was not found wanting under the Light, and his father was not told the truth at the door. He died of a method. He died because eight men in a room were running a procedure at a rate nobody had ever thought to question, and it could have been run slower, and nobody tried, for four hundred years, because there was nothing wrong with it, because the ones it killed had failed."

"Yes," Emeric said. "That's what it means."

"I have attended," Armand said.

The room went entirely quiet.

"Twice. Once as a young man because I was told to, and once thirty years later because a former student asked me to stand for him. I watched a boy of nineteen come apart in the fourth hour of the second one and I stood in the gallery while it happened and I thought it was solemn." He said it without any self-pity whatsoever, which made it much harder to sit through. "I thought it was terrible and solemn and necessary and that I had been present at something. I have thought that for thirty-one years."

Emeric had not known. It had not occurred to him that there was anything to know.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't know that."

"No. You did not ask." Armand sat down again. "You never ask what a thing costs the person you are saying it to. It is the single worst habit you have and you have had it since you were fourteen."

The fight proper took another two hours, and it was the first real one they had ever had, and it was not about any of that.

Armand fought it on the ground he was strongest on and he was entirely right about every step of it.

"One man," he said. "You have one man."

"I have one man with no marks and a knight's capacity, which by your own account cannot exist."

"By my account it is very unusual and not impossible. Natural capacity varies. It varies more than the assessment admits, because the assessment is one old man's hand on a boy's wrist, and I have said for forty years that it is crude. A man in the lower third of the knightly range, born that way, working in a barony where nobody has ever measured anybody, is not a miracle. He is a man nobody measured."

"Eleven others on that floor are where you'd expect."

"Which is my argument and not yours. If the room does it, the room should have done it to twelve." Armand held up a hand before Emeric could answer. "You will tell me that men differ, that some take it and some do not, that two years is not long for some and long for others. All of that may be true and every word of it is unfalsifiable, and the moment you start explaining why your effect appears in one case out of twelve you have stopped doing arithmetic and started doing apologetics."

"The sick-day column is all twelve."

"The sick-day column is a good question and it is not this claim. Do not weld them together because they happened in the same week."

And then he said the thing that ended it, and he said it quietly.

"You have no reading of that man from before he began."

Emeric opened his mouth and did not have anything to put in it.

"None. Not one. You do not know what Tobin measured three years ago, because nobody in the history of this kingdom has ever measured a labourer for anything, which is your point and which is also, at this precise moment, entirely against you." Armand tapped the sheet. "So what you have is a measurement with nothing to compare it to, and a hypothesis you formed four years ago in a hole in the ground, and a result that agrees with it. And when I put a stone on that bench eighteen months ago you were confident about a number for four years, and you were wrong by a sixth, and you were wrong because you asked a mirror and it told you yes. I corrected you for concluding before measuring. You thanked me for it. You are doing it again in front of me."

It was the worst possible thing he could have said, and it was true, and Emeric sat with it.

"You're right about the method," he said eventually.

"I am."

"You're wrong about the answer."

"I may be," Armand said. "Prove it, or do not say it again."

What came out of that room was not a conclusion. It was a programme, and it was Armand's, and he wrote the terms himself on two sheets before he would agree to any of it.

Every person in the barony who would consent to it, measured. Not the etching floor. Everyone. Farm, foundry, garrison, school, kitchen, boats, the men on the drainage crews and the women in the soap works and the children in Orrin's class, with the children's parents asked first and separately. Three readings each, on three days, at different hours. Recorded before anybody went anywhere near the etching floor, so that the baseline existed independently of what it was going to be compared against.

Then again, every year, at the same season.

Controls, by which he meant crews matched for age, food, hours and housing against the etching floor and doing work with no charged stock in it at all. Movements between crews recorded as movements and not quietly folded into whichever column suited. Nobody moved onto the etching floor because of the study, and nobody moved off it because of the study, and the wage stayed the same either way so that money never became a reason.

And Armand ran it. Not Emeric. Armand held the sheets, set the order, and had a written right to refuse any request that would have let the answer be known in advance.

"It will take years," Emeric said.

"Five. Possibly seven, if you want it to be worth anything to anybody who is not already persuaded." Armand signed the bottom of the second sheet. "You have an entire population, on a payroll, who are already counted, in a barony that writes everything down, run by a man who cannot be sacked for finding an unwelcome answer. I do not believe such a place exists anywhere else in this kingdom. If you are right, this is the only ground in Randillia where it could be shown."

Emeric signed it under his name.

It was, he understood perfectly well, the largest thing that happened that month. He had held a hypothesis for four years with nowhere to put it, and the only two roads out of it had been a volunteer or a corpse, and he had refused both and had therefore not moved an inch. And now there was a third road, and it was slow and dull and honest and ran through seven years of other people's ordinary bodies, measured with their permission, by a man who wanted him to be wrong.

He told Rylan that evening, because he was not going to have this argument under a roof Rylan lived beneath without telling him, and because the argument was about Rylan's arms and Rylan's dead.

He told it straight, without softening the part about scar tissue, and it took about four minutes.

Rylan listened to the whole thing sitting on the edge of the table with his boots off, which was how he listened to everything. He did not get angry. He did not thank him for the honesty, which Emeric had half braced for and would have hated.

When it was finished he looked at the inside of his own left forearm for a while, at the pale raised lines he had carried since he was twenty, and asked one question.

"Would it have worked ten years ago? On the men in that room with me?"

Emeric thought about giving him the version with the qualifications in it, and did not.

"I don't know."

Rylan nodded slowly, put his boots back on, and stood up.

"That's the right answer," he said, and went out.

Armand raised the last problem the following morning, before Emeric had got to it, which Emeric noted and did not comment on.

This was not a technical finding and would not be received as one. The chamber was consecrated ground. The liturgy of the dead was a cardinal's office, and the cardinal in question had been Augustine for nineteen years, and Augustine had blessed the third son of Harrold on his way south out of the capital. A barony that produced a paper saying the tracery was an injury was not correcting an engineering parameter. It was telling the Church, in writing, that four hundred years of the dead had been named as failures under the Light when they had in fact died of a procedure, and that the men reading the names had been wrong every year since the founding.

Emeric did not argue with any of it, because there was nothing in it to argue with.

He wrote nothing down. He told nobody outside that room. The programme went into the barony's papers as a measurement of the working population by crew, which is what it was, and Armand's sheets went into Armand's own box with Armand's own lock on it, and the word tracery did not appear anywhere in any document Kaldo produced.

He had the beginning of something that would take seven years to prove and that would make him a heretic on the day it was proved, and the only man in the kingdom who could finish it for him did not want it to be true, and had agreed to do it anyway.
