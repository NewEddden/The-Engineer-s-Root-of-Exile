Chapter 61 - Barges at Night

The day was the longest he had ever spent doing nothing.

Kaldo had never been so full or so quiet. Every house that had stood empty since the plague had people in it, and smoke coming out of it, and children on its step who did not know the lane and would not leave the step to learn it. The school was shut. The market was not held. The garrison slept in the afternoon on the wall walk in their iron, in rows, with their bows beside them, and the townsmen Ward had given spears to sat on the stairs below them with nothing to do yet and tried not to look at the spears.

He went round all of it, because he could not stay still.

Imani's ward had its doors open and its floors scrubbed and nobody in its beds. The kit was laid out on a clean cloth on the table by the side door: the boiled needles in their jar, the thread, the spirit, the stoneware bottle with its stopper pushed in hard, and at the end of the row, folded into its strip of leather, a razor he had seen once before, on a shelf. Imani was rolling bandages and did not stop. She told him the ward was ready, and went on rolling, and he left her to it.

The mages slept in the room behind the exchange, all of them, in their clothes, the way people sleep who have done it on decks. The woman with the grey plait opened one eye when he looked in, and shut it again.

Ferra sent a boy across the east bridge at four with a loaf and a jug for bay three, and the boy came back with the jug empty and a message that Bram had said thank you, which the boy reported with some surprise, and Ferra wrote nothing down.

At dusk the needle gave its one slow swing. All well. Ward put the wall dark. Every lamp on the battlements stayed cold in its hood, and down at the powerhouse the belt ran idle on the loose pulley, and Tess sat at the lever with her hand on it, waiting for three swings of a lantern that might not come for another night, or another week.



Emeric was in the line room when the bell came. He had not meant to be. He had climbed up to look at the needle at midnight because it was something to look at, and had stayed, because going down again meant deciding where to go.

The needle went three times to the right.

The man on it was awake this time. He said, "Bell," out loud, quite calmly, and then, as it swung again, "Boat," and then the needle came back to the middle of its glass and stopped there.

It did not move again. It did not tick.

Emeric sent say-again up the river. Nothing came back. He sent it again. The needle hung where it was and did not move.

He thought of the wall-crew man in the doorway of the hut with his bread, and of the second man, who had gone back up the road with Ward's message the morning before, and then he made himself stop thinking about it, because there was nothing in the line room he could do about any of it, and he went down the keep stair at a run.



Ward was at the corner of the wall above the east bridge, where the first lamp hung dark in its hood over his head. Emeric told him.

"They have found the hut," Ward said. "Or the wire."

Emeric did not answer. Ward handed him the lantern, shuttered, still warm, and turned back to the river.

Two miles. Emeric had walked it, pole by pole, the week the line went up. A barge on the river at night, with the spring water still high and the current under it, would come down in less time than he had taken to walk it. He stood at the parapet with the lantern hot against his leg and listened, and there was nothing to listen to but the river.

Then a sound came down it that did not belong to it. A knock, hollow, wood on wood, very small and very far. Then another. A pole finding the bottom, and a man swearing at it under his breath, too far off to be words.

The river tower stood out over the water a little way north along the wall, a black shape against a sky that was only a little less black. Beyond it the river went up into the dark, and the dark moved.

He saw them first by where the river stopped shining. Long flat shapes came down out of the north on the current, darker than the water and lower than the banks, one, and another behind it, and another, and the knocking of the poles came with them, more of it, and the creak of loaded timber, and once, clear across the water, the stamp and blow of a horse that did not like boats.

Somebody on the wall walk behind him let out his breath. Ward turned his head, and the man stopped.

The first barge came level with the river tower. Ward did not move. The second came level, and the first was past it, sliding down toward the bridge, near enough now that Emeric could see the men in it as one solid darkness, packed shoulder to shoulder, and something taller in the bow that was a man standing. The third came level.

"Wait," Ward said.

The fourth. The fifth. The first was almost at the bridge, and turning, the poles going in on the far side, swinging it toward the dock quarter's bank and the pale new deck of the wharf.

"Now," said Ward.

Emeric opened the lantern and swung it toward the mill, once, twice, three times.

Nothing happened. He had known it would not, not at once. Down the bank Tess would be leaning on the lever, and the belt walking across onto the fast pulley. He counted, and across the water in the dark the first barge's bow touched the wharf with a long grinding bump, and men began to come over its side.

The lamp over his head hissed, and came on.



The river went white from bank to bank.

He had seen it in March, with nothing on the river. Now there were nine barges on it that he could see, and more coming round under the river tower, and every one of them was full. The light came down on them all at once, out of the dark, from the corner and from the river tower, and found every face in them, turned up, white, with the hands coming up in front of them against the glare. A horse on the third barge reared and came down and reared again and went over the side, and the men nearest it went with it. In the bow of the first barge, at the wharf, the tall shape was a knight in plate, and the plate threw the light back in a white flash that hurt to look at, and he had his arm up over his eyes like everybody else.

Then the bows started.

Ward had told them not to shoot until there was light, and they had not, and now there was. The sound went along the wall walk from the corner to the river tower and beyond it, a dry flat clatter, like a handful of sticks thrown down on a floor, and then the creak and grunt of men bending to the stirrups to span again. In the barges men went down. Not many, at first. Then more.

The arrows came back up out of the barges late and all wrong. They went up into the glare and broke on the parapet or went over it or did not reach it at all, and the men who had loosed them could not see what they were loosing at, because the men they were loosing at were standing in the dark behind the lamps. Emeric heard one strike the hood of the lamp over his head with a clang, and fall.

At the foot of the east bridge, behind the stone parapet, the woman with the grey plait stood up.

He saw the ring of light come up in the air in front of her hands, small and steady, with its mark in the middle, and the river above the bridge humped up under the bow of the barge nearest it and shoved. Water came over the gunwale in a sheet. The barge swung, broadside, heavy, and the current took it and laid it against the middle pier of the bridge with a crash that Emeric felt through the stone under his feet, and held it there, and men went off it into the river in the white light, and some of them came up.

Out at the river tower, another ring came up, and another barge turned and slewed and went aground on the near bank under the wall, and stopped, and the bows on the wall walk above it did not stop.

Then a ring came up that was not Kaldo's.

It came up in the middle of the fourth barge, out on the river among the packed heads, hard and bright, and something went off it at the lamp on the river tower. The hood rang like a bell somebody had kicked, and swung on its iron, and settled, and the lamp under it went on burning.

"The rings," Ward said, not loudly. The word went along the wall walk from man to man faster than he had said it. Every bow that could reach the fourth barge went to the place under the ring, and the ring went out, and the man who had made it went down among the others, and the men round him pushed away from where he had been.

A second came up at the end of the wharf, where the first barge's men were still coming over its side, and whatever came off it hit the corner hood over Emeric's head hard enough that he felt it in his teeth. It lasted as long as it takes to span a bow and loose it. A third came up in a doorway on the far bank, back under the eaves where the lamps did not reach, and the ring was all the light the bows needed.

There were two more, later. He saw them come up, one in a barge going in to the moorings and one in a lane behind the wharf, and he saw both go out again before they were finished, as if the men under them had seen what happened to the others and thought better of it.

The rest of the barges went for the far bank, because the far bank was the only bank they could reach, and it was lit too. Every roof in the dock quarter had gone the color of bone, as it had in March, every chimney with its black shadow beside it. The barges ran in along the moorings and the wharf and the old piers, one after another, and the men came over their sides into the shallows and up the bank, many of them, more than he could count, and the light found every one.

Two of the barges did not turn for the far bank. They went straight on down the middle of the river under the bridge, in the confusion and the noise, and he saw the stern of the second one go into the shadow of the arch, and did not see where it went after that.

"Ward," he said.

"I saw." Ward did not turn his head. "Below us."



The long thin mage was behind the ore yard wall on the far bank, where Ward had put him, with orders to throw the river once at whatever came to the wharf first and then run for the bridge and not stop for anything.

He threw it. Emeric saw the ring come up behind the ore yard wall, wavering, and the river came up over the end of the wharf in a long brown slap and took the legs from under half a dozen men coming ashore and put them back into the water. Then the ring went out and the long thin mage came out from behind the wall and ran.

He ran badly, with his elbows out, the whole length of the ore yard and out onto the road to the bridge, in the full light, and the men coming up off the wharf saw him and came after him. One of them was fast. He was faster than anybody had a right to be, and Emeric knew what that meant before he saw the plate on him: a knight, coming up the bank in great raking strides, closing on the thin man as though the thin man were standing still.

The cart barricade was at the town end of the bridge. Rylan went over it.

He went out onto the bridge alone, at a run, in his helmet and Bram's breastplate, with his sword out and nothing else, and he met the thin mage halfway across and went straight past him without a word, and met the knight at the far end of the bridge, where the road came onto it. Emeric saw them come together under the lamp, the two of them moving faster than anybody else on that bank could move, and heard it from the wall: a sound like a dropped anvil, and then another. Rylan gave a step. Then he took it back.

A bolt from the wall went into the Grayrock knight under the arm, where the plate was not, and he went down on one knee, and Rylan put his foot on the knight's shoulder and shoved him over the parapet into the river, plate and all, and turned and walked back across. He did not run. He walked, in the full light, with the whole far bank shooting at him, and the arrows went past him and over him and one rang off the back of his helmet, and he reached the carts and climbed over them, and sat down on the other side with his back against a wheel.

The thin mage was already on the ground behind the carts, on his hands and knees, being sick.

"Still seasick," Rylan said, to nobody, and took his helmet off, and looked at the dent in the back of it.

They dragged the last cart across the gap. Ward watched it done from the wall, and then turned back to the river.

"Now it is held," he said.



They tried it twice.

The first time was knights, five of them, or six, coming up out of the dock quarter onto the far end of the bridge together, and across it at a speed that made the men on the wall swear, under the full light of the corner lamp. The bridge was narrow and stone-walled, and there was no getting off it. The bows on the wall walk shot down into it, and the spears at the carts came forward, and Ward went down the wall stair, which Emeric had not known he was going to do, and met them at the carts with Declan and Osric on either side of him. Emeric saw very little of it. He saw Osric's sword go up and come down twice, and a man in plate fold over it the second time, and he heard Declan shouting, not words, and he saw Ward step back, and the knight in front of Ward go forward into the space Ward had left, and not come back out of it. Then the rest were going back across the bridge, three of them, one of them carried, and the men at the carts were standing up behind them and shouting, all but one of the townsmen, who was sitting against a cart wheel holding his side with a surprised look on his face, until two others got him up and took him away up the lane toward the ward. Ward came back up the stair with his sword in his hand and blood to the elbow and did not say anything.

The second time was levies, a great many of them, with shields over their heads against the bolts, and they did not get as far as the middle of the bridge.

Then nothing came over the bridge at all, and Emeric understood, watching the dock quarter, that nothing was going to. Men were moving in the lanes over there, a great many of them, in and out of the doors of the empty houses. Some of them had torches.

The first thatch went up a little after that, at the river end of a lane, a roof he had looked down on in March. It caught all at once, the whole slope of it, and the flame stood up off it twice the height of the house, yellow in the white light of the lamps, and then the tarred shed beside it went, with a sound like a breath drawn in, and then the next roof along.

They were firing it from the river back. House by house. He could see the torches going from door to door.

"He will burn it," Ward said beside him. He had said it at Ferra's table. "Whatever he cannot hold."

"Fire's mine," Emeric said.

Ward looked at him.

"The buckets can't get to it," Emeric said. "Not across that." He nodded at the bridge, with its carts and its dead. "It's mine or it's nobody's."

Ward was quiet for the space of a breath.

"Behind the carts," he said. "Not on the bridge. Take the coast men." He turned back to the river. "And when I say come back, you come."



He went down the wall stair to the bridge foot, where the woman with the grey plait was waiting behind the parapet, and crouched behind the carts beside Rylan, and looked across.

The nearest burning roof was a little more than a hundred yards away across the water. He could feel the heat of it on his face from there. He could hear it: a steady roar, under the shouting, like a forge with the bellows going.

He had never pulled a fire from so far. He had never pulled one so big. He put his hand up over the edge of the cart, and the glyph came up over his fingers, and he reached.

It was like taking hold of a rope with a horse on the other end of it. The fire did not want to come. It wanted the thatch, and the thatch was there, and he was a long way off across cold water. He pulled. He felt it in his teeth, and behind his eyes, and in the middle of his chest, and then all at once he felt it give, and a long ragged sheet of flame came up off the roof and out over the river toward him, so bright that the men behind the carts flinched down, and he took it, and turned it, and sent it straight up into the black sky over the river, higher than the river tower, and let it go.

It went out up there, in the dark, all at once. The roof across the river smoked, black and smoldering, and did not burn.

Behind the carts, somebody laughed out loud.

Beside him the coast woman had her hands up, and her ring was up, and a long slop of brown water came up out of the river below the dock quarter and fell on the next roof along, hissing, and then another, and the steam went up white into the white light. The thin one was up beside her, swaying, with his ring wavering over his hands. Further along, at the river tower, another ring came up, and the river went over a third roof.

His nose had begun to bleed. He wiped it on his sleeve, and put his hand up again, and found the next fire, the tarred shed, and took hold of it.

It came easier the second time.
