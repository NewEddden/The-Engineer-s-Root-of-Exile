Chapter 94 - The First Page

He had said it once, to a cold room in Wayford with nobody in it, and not again. And then a printer. It had gone into the notebook the next morning, one line at the foot of the second column, with nothing under it, and in the first week of winter he took the notebook down to the works and showed the line to Bram, and Bram read it and asked what a printer was.

"A thing that writes a page," Emeric said, "and then writes it again. The same. As many times as you like."

Bram looked at the word for a while.

"Like the plates on the blade," he said.

"Like the plates on the blade."

It was built in three places at once, and only one of them was the works.

The frame was Bram's: a flat iron bed the size of a table, planed true against the three plates, with a square of polished iron let into the middle of it where the sheet would lie. Along both long sides of the bed ran a rail, and across the bed between the rails ran a bridge, and along the bridge ran a small iron carriage no bigger than a man's fist. Jorrin turned the rails on the second lathe, round rods of iron as long as a man's arm, ground and lapped until the carriage slid on them at a touch and did not rock. Neta, who had made every sheet the barony wrote on since the summer before last, cut the first ream to a size and a squareness Ferra had specified to the width of a hair.

The head was the pen grown up.

It rode on the carriage, underneath, pointing down at the bed. It had a reservoir like a barrel, and a feed like the pen's feed, and at the end of the feed, where the nib would have been, a point of steel with a hole through it smaller than a hair, which Bram had drilled under a lens with a needle and sworn at for most of a week. When the ink was pushed, a drop came out of the hole. When it was not pushed, nothing did. That was the whole of the head. Where a pen would have drawn, it blotted, once, at a point, and then the carriage moved, and it blotted again.

The ink was the pen ink, from the summer before last, strained four times and poured off the top, because it was the only ink in the world that would go through a hole that small a hundred thousand times and not silt it. Ferra brought the jar down from the keep herself and put it in Bram's hands, and said, with no expression at all, that the book ink was on the shelf at the far end of the room and would stay there.

The rest was stones.

There were four of them, small, cut by Nevin from the plain charge-and-hold crystal that went into mine lamps, which Saban bought at the coast at four copper apiece. One sat at the end of each rail and pushed the bridge along it in steps of a hundredth part of a division of the master foot. One sat at the end of the bridge and did the same for the carriage. And one sat in the head, behind the reservoir, and pushed the drop out. Each did one thing, a small push, when it was told to, and nothing else, and they were charged in the evening by Orrin, who poured into them for a quarter of an hour apiece and said it was less than holding a lamp.

And then there was the deciding stone, and that was the part that was built somewhere nobody else could go.

He made the page in the delve.

It took him four nights. He sat in the plain light with a sheet the size of Neta's sheet laid out in front of him, and a grid over it of points so fine that a hundred of them stood in a single division of the master foot, and he decided, for every point on that grid, whether it would be ink or paper. That was all a page was, in the end. Every letter anybody had ever written was a question asked at every point on a sheet, answered yes or no. A pen answered it by moving. The head would answer it by stopping and blotting and moving on. It was the most tedious work he had ever done in the delve and he had once spent a week there taking eleven slices through a propeller.

When the page was made he took a crystal into the room, a fifth one, a little larger than the rest, and bound the page into it.

He did it the way he had bound the six stones, and wrote nothing into it but the page. It went in once. It would not come out, and nothing else would ever go in after it; a stone told a thing is told it for good. He had learned that on Armand's bench with a thumb of gravity stone rising past his face, and he did not need to learn it twice. One stone, one page. Written once. Read for as long as it held its charge, which was as long as somebody poured into it, which was forever if anybody cared to.

He set the deciding stone in its socket on the bridge, and closed his hand over the socket, and felt it go live, and stepped back.

The first page came off the bed on the ninth day of winter, in front of Bram and Jorrin and Neta and Orrin, and Ward at the door, and it was a field of blots.

Not letters. Not even the ghosts of letters. The carriage ran as it should, back and forth across the bed a division at a time, and the bridge stepped down the sheet, and the head blotted where the stone told it to blot, every time, exactly. And every blot ran into the next. The ink came out of the hole as a drop and landed on the sized paper as a drop, and spread, as a drop will, to three times the width of the point that made it, and met its neighbours, and joined them. By the time the bridge reached the foot of the sheet the page was a grey-black mottled field like the belly of a trout, with here and there, if you held it at arm's length and squinted, the suggestion that something underneath it had once been trying to say something.

Nobody laughed. There was a wall at the works for that, and everybody present had written on it.

"Too much ink," said Bram.

"Too much ink," Emeric agreed. "And too far to fall."

It took eleven days. The head came down to the paper until it nearly touched, so that the drop was laid on the sheet rather than dropped onto it. The push behind the reservoir came down, and down again, until what came out of the hole was less a drop than a bead that barely left the steel. Neta sized the next ream heavier, with the glue bath a third stronger, so that the ink sat on the skin of the sheet and did not wick into it. Every change was one change, and every page was entered in a book Ferra kept for the purpose, headed as the trial's book had been headed, WHAT WENT WRONG, with the sheet pinned to the facing page so that anybody could see what the words meant.

On the eleventh day a sheet came off the bed with letters on it.

They were not beautiful. They were made of dots, and if you put your eye close to the page you could see every dot, the way you can see the stitches in a sampler. But at the length of an arm they were letters, black and even and square to the page, every one the same as every other of its kind, and a line of them ran across the top of the sheet in the largest hand Emeric had given the stone, and said:

A B C D E F G H I J K L M

And under it the rest of the alphabet, and under that the twelve divisions of the master foot numbered one to twelve, and under that, in a smaller hand, one sentence, which he had chosen on the fourth night in the delve after a great deal of thought and which was the first sentence ever printed in Randillia.

This page is the same as every other page like it.

He ran it again. It came off the bed the same. He ran it forty times, which took the rest of the day, and laid the forty on the bench in a stack, and they were the same, and Jorrin took one off the top and one off the bottom and held them together up to the window, one over the other, and every dot on the top sheet lay exactly over a dot on the bottom one, and the light came through nowhere.

Jorrin looked at them for a long time and did not say anything. He had made the rails.

Orrin had the first of them.

He did not ask for it. He stood at the end of the bench with the forty sheets and did not touch them, and Emeric took one off the stack and put it into his hands. Orrin had taught the barony its letters for six years off slates and chalk and a few sheets of Hesketh's wrapping paper and the backs of old tally bills, in a room that had once had eleven people in it and now had more than he could seat, and every child in it had learned to read off a letter Orrin had drawn himself, once, on a board, and rubbed out in the afternoon.

He held the sheet at arm's length, the way a man does whose eyes have begun to go. Orrin had talked more or less without stopping since the day he came to Kaldo. He said nothing at all for some time.

"Every child," he said.

"Every child. And every one of them can take it home."

"Take it home." Orrin said the words over as if they were in a language he was learning. Then he folded the sheet carefully in half and in half again, and put it inside his coat, against his chest, the way Rudd kept his sheet, and went back up to the school without another word, and Emeric heard from Ward that evening that he had taught the whole of the afternoon's lesson with his hand on it.

Armand came down the next morning.

He looked at the machine for some time without touching it. He looked at the rails, and at the head under a lens, and at the four small stones and the one larger one on the bridge, and he put his palm flat over the deciding stone in its socket for the length of a breath and took it away. Then he took a sheet off the stack and read the sentence at the foot of it twice.

"Who makes the stones?" he said.

"Nevin cuts them. I bind them."

"And the page?"

"I make the page."

Armand looked at him, and did not ask where. The old man put the sheet down on the bench and took from inside his coat a folded paper, soft at the creases, that Emeric knew before it was open. It was the sheet from the winter before last, from the week of the Standing Order: four questions in the crabbed hand nobody in Kaldo could read at speed, and three answers written beside them, and one line blank. The fourth question. What happened if Emeric died on a road.

"When I brought you this," Armand said, "you answered three and left the fourth. You did not say you would answer it. I assumed you would. Is this it?"

"It's the start of one." Emeric put his hand on the stack. "Everything I know that anybody can use is in my head, and in the notebook, which nobody else can read. That was the problem you wrote down. This puts it on paper. Not once. As many times as there are people to read it. A drawing, a rule, a method. A primer. A way to pack a gland, a way to clear a shaft. When it's printed, it's out. It doesn't need me to be anywhere."

Armand considered that.

"And the next page?"

Emeric looked at the deciding stone.

"Every page is a stone," the old man said, "and every stone is bound by you, in a place I can enter only on your arm, doing a thing you do not describe. If you die on a road tomorrow, perhaps the stones you have bound will go on printing and perhaps they will not. I cannot measure it and neither can you. Either way there will never be another page." He took a pencil from his coat and unfolded the old sheet on the bench and wrote on the blank line, in the crabbed hand, one word. "That is half an answer. I will take half. It is more than I had."

He folded the sheet and put it back in his coat, and went out.

Emeric stood by the machine for a while after he had gone. Then he took out the notebook, and turned to the second column, and under And then a printer, where the line had sat alone for most of a year, he wrote a second line.

Press and type. Letters cast in lead, one to a piece, set by hand in a frame, inked, and pressed on the sheet by a screw. No stone. No page made anywhere but in a man's hands. Any man.

He looked at it for some time. It was a worse machine in every way he could measure. It was slower and dirtier and it needed a room full of people and it would take years to build, and every piece of it could be made by somebody who had never heard his name. That was the only way in which it was better, and it was the only way that counted. The thing on the bench in front of him was the first of something, and only the first. He had said it to himself so often it had stopped sounding like a rule: magic builds the first of a thing, and the barony lives on the second.

He closed the notebook.

Ferra ordered five hundred.

She came down in the afternoon with a sheet of her own under her arm, which she laid on the bed: a ledger page, ruled by her own hand, with a column for the date and a column for the entry and three for the figures and a narrow one at the right for the initial of whoever made it. She had ruled that page, by her own reckoning, something over nine thousand times in ten years. Every one of them had been a little different from every other, a line a hair off here, a column a hair narrower there, because a line is only as straight as the hand that draws it and her hand had been tired for a good many of them.

"Can it do that?" she said.

"It can do that."

"The same, every time."

"The same, every time."

He made the page that night. It was the easiest page he had ever made, nothing but lines, and the next morning the bed ran it, and Ferra stood by the machine and took the first ten sheets as they came off and held them up to the window two at a time, one over the other, the way Jorrin had held the primer, and looked at the light through them for a long time.

"Five hundred," she said.

Neta cut five hundred sheets. They took two days to print, and nobody at the works minded. When the last of them came off the bed Ferra counted them herself, and squared the stack, and carried it up to the keep in both arms, and nobody offered to carry it for her because everybody knew better.

Vantic was in Ferra's room when the stack came in. She had come about the quarter's service, as she did on the first day of every quarter, and she watched Ferra set the stack on the table and square it again, and took the top sheet off it without asking, and held it to the window, and then took the second and laid it over the first and held both.

She looked at the light through them for some time, the way the others had.

Then she put them down, very precisely, one on the other, and squared them to the stack.

"A thing that can make five hundred sheets the same," Vantic said, to nobody in particular, "can make one that is the same as somebody else's."

Ferra did not look up from the stack. Emeric, in the doorway, did not say anything. It was the kind of thing that was plainly true, and plainly important, and nobody in the room had the least idea what to do about it, and so nobody answered.

Vantic opened her book and did not write anything in it, and after a moment closed it again.
