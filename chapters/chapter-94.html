Chapter 94 - The Hold

Ferra brought the two sheets down to the works herself, flat between two boards so that neither of them would take a crease, and she did not say what they were until she had carried them to the window.

"The first," she said, "and the five hundredth."

She laid one over the other and held them up to the light, the way she had held the first ten in the keep a fortnight ago, and Emeric came and stood at her shoulder and looked.

In the keep the lines on every pair had lain on each other as one line. Now every line on the page wore a faint grey fringe down one side of it, a hair wide, where the line on the sheet underneath showed past the line on top. Every column. Every row. The whole way down. The five hundredth sheet had been printed with a fatter line than the first, and the fat had come on so gradually that nobody had seen it arrive.

"It's a hair," he said.

"It's a hair." Ferra lowered the sheets. "There's a page pinned on the wall of this room that says it's the same as every other page like it."

There was. It was the first sentence ever printed in Randillia, and it had been true for something under four hundred pages.

Bram had the head off the carriage before she was out of the door. He put it under the lens and looked for a long time, and then held the lens out to Emeric without a word.

The hole had grown. When Bram drilled it, its lip had been a sharp little ring of steel round a point of dark. Now it was rounded over at the edge, like the mouth of a well that a hundred years of buckets have been let down past. The ink had done some of that; there was gall in it, and gall bites steel, slowly in his own ink and quickly in anybody else's. The push had done the rest. Something over a hundred thousand drops had been shoved through a ring of steel no thicker than a nail paring, and every one of them had taken a little of the ring away with it.

"I can drill another," Bram said. "It took a week and six needles, and it'll go the same way at the same count."

"Every four hundred pages."

"Near enough."

Emeric gave him back the lens. "What wears is the mouth," he said. "The drop's the size of the mouth. If the hole ran back into the steel a long way, the same width all the way down, you could stone the face flat when the mouth went, and there'd be the same hole waiting behind it. You could do that twenty times before you got to the reservoir."

Bram thought about it. "You can't drill a hole that fine that deep. A needle wanders after three hairs. You'd know that if you'd broken as many as I have."

"I know it."

"So nobody can make it." Bram turned the old head over in his fingers. "It's a pencil, what you want. A thing you sharpen."

"It's a pencil."

"And nobody can make it," Bram said again, and looked at him.

Emeric could make it. He had made a nib in the summer before last, with the door barred, and the nib had been harder than this in every way but one, and the one was size. A head that could carry a bore that long, with a barrel behind it, had to come out of a billet of steel as long as a hand. He had worked out that summer why he could not make anything larger than a fingernail. Three ropes at once, the hold and the heat and the form, and the hold the weakest of them, because the hold was really him, pushing up from underneath exactly as hard as the steel pushed down. On a fingernail that was nothing. On a bar it was a man holding the bar out at arm's length all night and trying to engrave it with his other two hands.

Not bigger. Not yet.

But since the autumn the hold was the one rope in the world he knew how to take off himself and give to something else.

He went to Armand for stone.

The old man was in the room off the foundry office, where the bench stood with its legs let into the floor. He heard what it was for without getting up, and then he took the strongroom ledger out of the drawer under the bench and opened it at the page that had begun with two pounds.

"How much?"

"Four blanks. An ounce apiece."

"For?"

"A bench that holds its work."

Armand wrote it with a pen out of the tray of forty, which he had taken to using in the autumn and would not discuss. Four ounces, for a bench. Leaving twenty-four.

"How long does it hold?"

Emeric had two numbers, both in Armand's hand, both in that book. Six hours and a quarter for a blank carrying its own weight; a little over five for one carrying a little more. Two points made a line if a man was willing to call them one, and he meant to ask each blank to carry three times its weight. "Two hours. A little more."

"A little more," said Armand, in exactly the voice he used for the constant. Then: "It is cut in a room I can lock."

"Yes."

"The room is swept into a pail with a lid, and the pail goes across with the others."

"Yes." Since the study began, every room in Kaldo with charged stock in it was swept into a lidded pail, and the pails went over to the etching floor on the seventh day to be sieved and weighed. Dust that had been stone was still stone, and Armand would not have the columns lie about who had been beside what.

"And you go on the sheet."

"I'm on it."

"You are on it," Armand said, "as a man who does his work at a table with a pen. You are about to do it a hand's width from a stone that has never once been cut in a closed room in the history of the tower. I will not have the column wrong." He turned to a clean sheet and ruled a heading across it. Room. Stone. Hours. Name.

Emeric could have refused. It was in the terms, which Armand had written himself, that every soul on the sheet was there by consent. He had held those sheets over other people's lives for years, and watched Tobin come up off the floor with the grey still on his hands and take his shirt off for a stranger.

"Put me down," he said.

Armand put him down. It took him no longer than any other name.

Bram built the bench on the ladder, which meant that every part of it that could be cut on a lathe was cut on one and measured against the gauges in their felt-lined box, and nothing in it was made by eye.

It was a small thing when it was done. A frame of iron like a low table, with a short stand at each corner. A plate of iron no thicker than a slate and a little wider than a spread hand, scraped flat on the three plates. And riveted under the plate's four corners, four housings, each a shallow iron cup with a threaded collar that screwed down over it, to hold a blank inside with one face bearing on the plate above.

Emeric had drawn the housings himself, and he had not drawn them in figures. Every dimension was given against every other: the collar so much of the cup, the thread so many turns to the width, the wall such a part of the blank it held. And at the foot of the sheet, in the small hand he kept for notes to himself, which he had not meant to leave there and had left there anyway: any size.

Bram read the drawing twice, as he read every drawing. The second time his eye went to the foot of the sheet and stayed there. Then he rolled it up and took it down to the second lathe, and gave the collars to Jorrin, and cut the cups himself, and did not say anything about it at all.

A room above Kaldo, two winters ago, and a bearing turning in the air. How long have you known. Bram had not asked it again, and every season he did not ask it, it cost a little more not to have answered. Now there was one more thing he had not asked. Emeric watched him set the first cup in the chuck, and did not offer an answer either, and they were both very busy for the rest of the afternoon.

When the frame was finished Bram added two things that were not on the drawing.

The first was a brass dish the size of a palm, with a ring scribed round the inside of it a finger below the rim, that sat in the middle of the plate. When the water in it touched the ring all the way round, the plate was level. When it did not, it told you which way.

The second was four iron fingers, bolted to the frame at its corners and bent up and over, so that each stood a hand above the place where a corner of the plate would sit.

"They go up," Bram said, when Emeric looked at them. "I carried the box."

They put it in the old pattern store at the back of the finishing shop, which had one high window, and Bram hung a new door on it with a lock that took two keys, one of them Armand's. Nevin cut eight blanks there over two days, off the larger of the two black pieces in the chest. Armand stood over every stroke of the saw and entered every hour of it on the new sheet as a movement off the etching floor, because the study required a movement to be written as one. Nevin was a quiet, square man who could not read and did not need to, and he swept up after himself into the lidded pail without being asked, because it was the habit of the floor.

On the third evening Emeric set the first four.

Ward was at the door. Armand sat on a stool against the wall with his sheet on his knee. Bram stood at the far end of the bench, where he had stood every night of the spring before last, with his arms folded, and did not come any closer, and nobody asked him to. The plate sat on its four stands with a blank in each cup under it and the collars screwed down, and on the plate, on three hardened points no bigger than pinheads that Bram had set into the iron so that it touched nothing else, lay the billet: a bar of Bram's best steel as long as a hand and as thick as two fingers.

He gave each blank the same.

He had had eleven mornings in the yard and two nights on the bench in the autumn to learn what a weight felt like from inside a stone. He gave each blank a quarter of the plate and the billet, as near as his feel could make it, one after another, and let go of each without drawing anything back. The plate came up off its stands one corner at a time. When the fourth was set it hung a finger's width clear, touching nothing, and the water in Bram's dish sat on its ring all the way round.

Emeric took up the heat and the form, and nothing else.

For eight years the hold had been underneath everything he did at this size, the way a man's own weight is underneath everything he does with his legs, and he had long since stopped feeling it. He felt it now by its going. It was like putting down a pack at the end of a road and finding he stood a hand taller than he remembered. The chip of fire stone on its copper wire went to the one spot on the steel where he wanted it, and the spot went dull red. The form went in behind the heat, less an edge than a pressure. He had two hands for two ropes, and a third hand, somewhere inside him, with nothing to do.

He went down into the delve with the two ropes. The true head stood on the plain table where he had built it over the fortnight: barrel, feed, point, and running back into the point from its tip, straight as a ruled line, the bore, no wider than a hair and as long as the top joint of a finger. He held the true head and the real billet at once and moved the one towards the other a pass at a time, and with nothing on his back the comparison was easier than it had ever been. He could look at one wall of the barrel for what felt like a morning, and in the pattern store the lamp would burn down by the width of a straw.

He was watching the steel, which was the whole point of the plate.

The corner by the window had been going down for most of an hour. It went a hair at a time under the push of his work and did not come quite back up when the push came off, and the water in the dish had left its ring on that side by a thread and then by a straw. He did not see it. The three points held the billet as long as three points will hold anything on a slope, which is exactly until they do not, and then it went off them the way a cup goes off a tilted tray, all at once after a long time of not going.

He had heat on it and the form pressing into it, and in the space of a breath there was no steel under either.

The chip on its wire hung red where the steel had been. The form went on down through the place where the steel had been, into the lip of the plate and past it, and into the oak. The billet went off the low corner of the plate with one end still dull red and rolled once on the bench and lay there.

He let go of both ropes and came up out of the delve with his ears ringing.

There was a groove in the oak the length of a finger, black and smoking, where the form had gone in, and beside it the shape of the billet's hot end, burned into the wood like a brand. It had a good deal less behind it than the one in the finishing shop. It was the second scorched bench in two years, and the first lathe was standing on the other one, forty feet away through the wall.

Bram was at the far end of the bench, where he had been all evening. He looked at the scorch. Then he looked at Emeric, for rather a long time. He did not put his hand on this one. He had put his hand on the last one because his hands would have been there, and his hands had been nowhere near this one, and they both knew why.

Without the billet the plate was carrying less than it had been set for, and it went up, slowly and crooked, the window corner still low, until three of its corners met Bram's iron fingers and the fourth hung a straw short. It would hang like that until the stones were spent. Nobody could take them back.

It took three of them to get the stones out. Ward pushed the plate down off the fingers and held it flat on its stands with both palms while Bram unscrewed the collars, and each blank came up out of its cup into Bram's fist and pulled there, upward, like a small live thing that wanted to be let go. They went into the iron box from the autumn, with a flagstone on the lid, and all night the box ticked now and then on the bench as they pressed against it.

Armand wrote it in the ledger before he went to bed. He did not ask what to write. Four ounces, set alike. Held unlike. No way back.

The pail went across to the etching floor on the seventh day, as every pail went.

Vesk sieved it. He sieved all of them, at the long table under the north window after the floor had gone home, because it was the part of the work he trusted to nobody. Most weeks the pails were grit and ink stone and floor dust, and the fine stuff left in the pan was grey.

The pail from the pattern store had something in the pan that was black.

He looked at it through the lens for some time. It was finer than the ink-stone dust, and when the lamp moved over it, it caught the light in a great many points at once, the way the blank had caught it on the night on Armand's bench, when an old man had stood with his hands out over a thumb of rock hanging in the air, afraid to take them away.

Nobody had told him that anybody was cutting black stone in the pattern store. Nobody had told him there was a lock on it. And nobody in Kaldo, nor in the tower so far as he had ever heard, knew what that dust did to a man who breathed it, because nobody had ever cut enough of it in a closed room to find out.

He weighed the black dust on the fine scale. There was very little of it. From the weight, and what he knew of a saw kerf and the size of a blank, he worked out roughly how many blanks had been cut, and wrote that down too, with a mark beside it to say it was a guess.

He did not go to Armand with it, or to the prince. In five years he had asked a great many careful questions in Kaldo and had most of them answered, and he had noticed which kind were not. He folded the paper round the dust and put it in the drawer, and opened the notebook nobody had ever read, and wrote the date and the room and the weight and the guess, in the hand he used only there.

Armand read the four blanks that were left on three days, at three different hours, the same way he had read every soul in the barony who consented to be read, because a number taken once was an anecdote.

Each blank read the same as itself every time, to the width of the pointer. No two of them read the same as each other.

"Same piece," Armand said, laying the sheet on the bench. "Same saw, same man, same day. Same weight to a grain. Four numbers." He tapped them. "I do not know why. If you ask me whether the tower knows why, the tower has never cut four blanks from one piece in one day, so the tower has never had occasion to be puzzled."

Emeric looked at the four numbers for a long time.

It was obvious, once it was in front of him. It always was. The deposit was a body in a hill, and nobody had poured it into a mould. It had grain, the way oak had grain, and a saw went through the grain wherever the grain happened to lie. He had treated four pieces of it as four of the same thing because they weighed the same.

"Then they don't get the same," he said. "They each get their own."

The fix, at that size, was careful rather than difficult. He set each blank by its own reading and not by his feel, with a fresh billet on its three points. And before he let go of each thread he trimmed it, a hair in and a hair out, watching the water. When the water sat on its ring, Bram took the master pound out of its case, which he had never before let out of his sight, and set it down on each corner of the plate in turn and lifted it off again. Each corner went down under it by the same hair and came back to the same place. Only then did Emeric let go.

It took most of an evening. Armand wrote down each setting as it was given, in the divisions of his own scale, because that was the only scale anybody had.

Four ounces, for a bench. Leaving twenty.

The plate sat dead level under the billet and stayed there, and Bram pressed each corner with a finger, the way he tried a bearing, and the water in the dish trembled and came back to its ring every time.

He made the head that night.

It took a little over two hours by the glass, and a good deal longer than that in the delve, and nothing moved under him the whole time. He did the barrel and the feed and the point, and last the bore, which was the whole reason for the head and the part no needle in the world could have made: a channel the width of a hair, running back into the steel the length of a finger joint without wandering from the true one by so much as its own width. He cut it with the form alone, carrying the true bore up out of the delve in his eye the way he had once carried the slit of a nib.

When he lifted the finished head off its three points and set it on the cloth, it was the largest thing he had ever made the way he had made the nib, and it was a part for the printer. The first thing the new machine had made was a spare for the last new machine. He found he did not mind that at all.

Two ropes instead of three. It was the difference between a nib and a bar, and he had just made a bar. He had written not bigger, not yet, in the summer before last, and he had meant both halves of it, and it had moved exactly one step.

With the head gone the plate was carrying less than it had been set for, and it went up. It went up slowly, level this time, until its four corners met Bram's four iron fingers, and there it stopped, a hand above the oak, pressed up against them as lightly as a leaf against a window.

Bram looked at it, and at his fingers, and said nothing, in a way that was very close to saying something.

It would hang there until the stones were spent. Four ounces of the only stone in the barony, which the Crown would have paid five Gold Rand for without blinking, spending itself on an empty plate in a locked room with nobody to see it. Every set the bench ever used would go like that. It was the first of a thing, and the first of a thing was allowed to be dear.

He sat down on Armand's stool with the notebook and opened it at the second column.

Two summers ago it had gone into the delve as a note the size of a shed, at the far edge of the plain light: a chamber with the stock laid in it, the true object on a table in the delve, and in place of a man holding everything, crystals doing what he did. He had written one line beside it. Armand. Can it be told, or only cut?

He went down for the length of a breath and found it where he had left it, and put a line through the question, and wrote under it: Told. Once. And under that: the hold is off.

Then he came up and wrote a name for it in the notebook, a word from nowhere, like most of his words. Fabricator. Put in the stock, have the finished thing in the delve, and it makes it. The pen, with the ropes taken off one at a time. The bench is rope one.

And against it, in the column for things he could not answer himself, the next question he would have to take to the room off the foundry office, to an old man who would want to know how he knew it might work.

Heat and form. Can they be taken off me the way the hold was?

He closed the book.

Armand's sheet was on the bench where the old man had left it, with the four settings on it in the crabbed hand, and before he went Emeric copied them out large on a clean sheet, one to a line, and pinned it to the wall over the bench, where anybody who ever used that bench would have to read it first.

Four stones, and four numbers, and a plate that sat dead level under anything the bench would ever carry. Any four stones in the world, read first and trimmed under their load, would do what these had done. He stood by the door for a while with the lamp in his hand, looking at the empty plate hanging level over the scorched oak with nothing under it, and at the four numbers above it, and he was very pleased with them.
