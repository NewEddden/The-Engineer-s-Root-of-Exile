Chapter 94 - Boiler

For a fortnight after the river stair, Ward put two men on the water gate every night.

They stood, one at the head of the stair and one at the gate itself, in the dark, with a lantern hooded at each man's feet and a crossbow in the crook of his arm, from the time the lamps came on until the light came up grey over the far bank. They did not talk. Ward had told them not to, and they did not, which Emeric thought was the clearest thing anybody could have said about how the garrison felt about a nailed boot in the mud of its own river.

Nothing came.

The river went past, low and slow, and the herons stood in the drain mouths at dusk and went away at dark, and the board in the line room sat with its twelve shutters up and its twelve needles lying still, night after night, and the bell did not ring once. On the fourteenth night the man at the head of the stair saw a boat. It was a fisherman from the reeds below the bridge, going down to his lines with a lantern in the bow, singing, and it went past the foot of the stair without slowing, and the needle for the river stair did not stir. The man at the head of the stair told Ward so in the morning. Ward listened to it without any expression at all.

At the end of the month he took them off.

He did not say why. He did not have to. The garrison was thirty-two men, and the harvest was in and the farms had given back the eight they had borrowed, but thirty-two men did not stretch to two at a gate every night for nothing, and the board had sat through fourteen nights without a twitch and caught the only thing that had come. The men talked about it in the guardroom, Emeric heard, the way they talked about Ward: as a thing that did not sleep and did not miss anything and was best not argued with. One of the gate crew had started calling it the Captain's Eye. Declan said that if Ward heard that he would have the man walking the river stretch until midwinter.

Ward heard it. He did not say anything about it either. He went up to the line room every night at ten, and stood in front of the board for a while with his hands behind his back, looking at the twelve shutters up and the twelve needles lying still, and then went down again.



The plate came down on the third barge of the month, with the ore.

It came flat, in a stack in the middle of the barge with straw between the sheets and a tarred cloth over the top, and the bargeman had been told it was precious and treated it as he would have treated a load of eggs, which was to say that he had stood on it only twice. There were fourteen sheets. Bram had them off onto the wharf with a sheer-legs and four men, one at a time, and laid them on trestles in the shed at the wharf head, and went along them with the rod.

It was the rod Emeric had sent up the river in July. Grayrock had sent it back down with the plate, wrapped in the same oiled cloth, with a slip of paper tied to it in the same tall upright hand as the tally, which said that it was returned with thanks and said nothing else. Bram laid it on the first sheet, along the edge, and then across it, and then took the gauge and tried the thickness at each corner and in the middle, and then did the same to the second sheet, and the third, and all fourteen, without saying anything, while Emeric stood at the end of the shed and did not help.

When he had done the fourteenth Bram put the rod down on top of the stack.

"They've a man up there knows his business," he said.

That was all. But he said it in the voice he used for good iron, which was not a voice he used often, and he stood looking at the stack for some time with his thumbs in his belt. Every sheet was the same thickness at every corner as the gauge said it should be. Every edge was straight to the rod. A rolling mill two days up the river, run by men who had never been to Kaldo and never would, had taken a foot of iron with twelve lines on it, and made fourteen sheets of plate to it, and sent the foot back.

It was the first Kaldo inch that had ever left Kaldo. It had come home as iron.



He drew the boiler for Bram on the bench in bay three the same afternoon, from the sheets on the trestles and not from the drawing on the wall, because now he knew what he had.

A drum. Five feet across, twenty feet long, lying on its side on a bed of brick. And running through the middle of it from end to end, under the water, a second drum, a tube two feet across, with the fire in it. The fire would burn at one end of the tube and the heat go along it through the water, and out at the far end into brick flues that brought it back round the outside of the drum before it went up the chimney, so that the water was heated from inside and out and the coal gave up as much as coal could give.

"A Cornish boiler," he said.

"Why Cornish?" said Bram.

"It's its name."

Bram looked at him the way he looked at a man who said a horse had always been a good horse, and let it go.

It was round because round was the strongest shape for plate that was not very strong. A flat side would bulge under pressure until it found out where the weakest rivet was; a round side pulled the same on every rivet all the way round, and none of them had to carry more than its share. And even round, it would not carry much. Emeric meant to run it at a few pounds over the weight of the air, no more, and get the rest of the engine's push from the cold side, the condenser, where the steam went back to water and left a hole that pulled. That was how the first good engines on his other world had run, for the same reason, on iron no better than Grayrock's. It made a weak engine. It made one that did not kill anybody.

Orrin, who had come in to look because Orrin came in to look at everything, stood at Bram's elbow and read the drawing upside down.

"Vesk could make you that without a seam in it," he said. "Shape it in one piece. A week. Ten days." He considered the size of it. "Two weeks. And I'd want a word about the rate."

"I know he could."

"Then why rivet it?"

"Because when it cracks," Emeric said, "and one day it will, Bram has to be able to cut the bad plate out and rivet a new one in. If Vesk makes it, Vesk has to mend it. And Vesk might be at Grayrock, or asleep, or dead."

Vesk, at the back of the bay with his hands in his sleeves, did not look as though he minded being any of the three.

"Riveted," said Bram, who had been going to say so anyway.



The riveting took most of a fortnight, and the whole of bay three went deaf.

Bram rolled the plates on bending rolls he had built for the purpose that month, a sheet at a time, each one taken through a dozen times and a little tighter at each pass until it lay in the curve of the drawing; and Orrin warmed them where Bram pointed, and was paid by the job, having lost the argument about the day rate. Then the holes, punched in a row along each edge, the punch worked by a long iron lever with two of the yard boys hanging their whole weight on the end of it. Then the plates offered up one to the next, with the holes matched and pinned, and the rivets.

A rivet was a short fat iron bolt with a head on one end and nothing on the other. It went into the hole red hot, and a man held the head from the inside while another man hammered the plain end flat from the outside, into a second head, and as it cooled it shrank, and pulled the two plates together tighter than any bolt could have pulled them. It wanted three people. Four, if the rivets were to arrive hot.

So there was a heater, a big yard boy at a little charcoal fire in an iron basket, who took the rivets out of the coals with tongs and threw them, red, underhand, in a long low curve across the bay. There was a catcher, who caught them in an old iron bucket and plucked them out with his own tongs and put them in the hole. There was a holder-up inside the drum with a heavy iron dolly against the head. And outside, Bram, with a hammer.

The heater missed four times on the first morning. One rivet went into the straw and had to be stamped out, and one went down the back of the catcher's collar, and after that the catcher stood with the bucket held out in front of him like a man expecting the end of the world, and caught everything. By the second day they were not missing. By the third the heater was showing off, and throwing them round the back of the drum to the catcher on the far side without looking, and Bram told him that the next one he threw without looking he would be catching himself, with his teeth.

The holder-up was the smallest of the yard boys. He was the one who had thrown a hot nut at Orrin in March to see what the shield would do, and he had been given the inside of the drum because he was the only one who fitted, and he went in through the manhole at the end every morning with a candle in a tin and the dolly and did not come out until noon. Inside the drum, Emeric knew, every blow of Bram's hammer would come through the iron and through the dolly and through the boy's arms and up into his head like the inside of a bell. On his other world the men who did this all their lives had gone deaf by forty. He had Tess make up plugs of carded wool, rolled tight, and made the boy put one in each ear before he went in, and the heater and the catcher too, and Bram.

Bram looked at the wool.

"I've been hitting iron for thirty years," he said.

"Then you've lost enough."

"What?" said Bram, and the yard boys laughed, and Bram did not, and put the wool in.



They filled it with cold water from the race on the last Thursday of the month.

It took all morning. A chain of buckets from the race, a man to each yard of it, and the buckets going up a ladder to the manhole at the top of the drum and in, one after another, and every man in the chain wet to the knees by ten. When it was full to the top Bram closed the manhole and bolted it down, and screwed onto the drum the last two things that went on it before it ever saw a fire.

The first was the safety valve.

It sat on top of the drum, at the middle. A brass seat with a brass plug lying in it, and across the plug an iron lever, pinned at one end, with a weight hanging at the other. The weight held the plug down. The steam pushed it up. When the steam pushed harder than the weight held, the plug lifted, and the steam went out of the hole with a noise like a bull, and the pressure in the drum fell until the weight could hold again. It was the oldest thing on the whole engine. Somebody on his other world had put one on a cooking pot thirty years before anybody built an engine to put one on, for the same reason: so that the pot would scream instead of burst.

The second was a row of three little brass taps in the end of the drum, one above another, a hand apart. Try-cocks. With no glass in Kaldo to make a gauge of, a man would know where the water stood inside the drum by opening each tap in turn. If the top one gave steam and the bottom one gave water, the level was between them, where it ought to be. If the bottom one gave steam, the water was too low, and the fire tube was standing bare in the steam, getting hotter than iron should, and the man should do something about it at once.

For the test, Bram hung a second weight on the lever, heavier than the first. Twice as heavy. And then he put his own hands to the force pump he had built for it, a little brass barrel with a plunger and a long handle on a stand beside the drum, its pipe going into the bottom of the boiler, and began to pump more water into a drum that was already full.

"Why water?" said Vantic.

Emeric had not heard her come in. She was standing at the end of the bay by the door, out of the way of everything, in her grey gloves and her grey coat, with Hale a pace behind her holding a book. She had come down from the east tower to see what the fifth had bought. She had recorded the engine in her book in August under the cap, and now she wanted to look at it, and she had said so to Ferra in those words, and Ferra had told Emeric the night before, and he had forgotten.

"Because water hardly squeezes," he said. "You can push more into a full drum, a little, and the pressure inside goes up and up, as high as you like. But there's nothing in the water to spend. If a seam gives, the water comes out of it and runs on the floor, and that's all. Steam's different. Steam's a spring wound up. If a seam gives with steam behind it, the whole spring lets go at once."

Vantic looked at the drum.

"So you're trying to break it," she said, "with the thing that can't hurt anybody."

"Before the thing that can."

She nodded once and did not write anything down. That was unusual enough that he noticed it.

Bram pumped. The handle went down hard and came up easy and went down harder. Tess sat in her chair by the drum with her bench book on her knees, watching the safety valve and the lever, because the moment the lever lifted under the doubled weight, the drum was carrying twice what it would ever carry in steam, and she meant to write down the stroke of the pump it happened on.

It did not get there. At about two thirds of the way, the drum began to weep.

A seam along the bottom, halfway down the drum, went dark, a line of wet along the plate edge between two rivets, and then the wet gathered, and a bead of water swelled out of the seam and ran down the curve of the drum and dropped onto the brick. And then a second one, near the front, high on the side. That one did not bead. It found a fine crack between the plates and came out of it in a thin hard jet the width of a hair, sideways, exactly at the height of a man's face, and the man whose face was at that height was Bram's, bent to the pump handle.

It took him in the eye. He stood up with his eye shut and water running down his beard, and looked at the seam that had done it, and wiped his face with his sleeve, and said nothing whatever for long enough that the whole bay held still.

Then he said that it was a good thing it was not steam. Tess wrote it down.

They let the pressure off and marked the two seams with chalk and drained the drum to below them, and Bram caulked them. Caulking was the other half of riveting, and older: a blunt chisel with a narrow edge, set against the edge of the outer plate where it lay over the inner one, and struck, along the whole length of the seam, so that the edge of the plate was driven down and in and closed hard against its neighbour, a bead of iron hammered into the joint like a cooper hammering in a hoop. Bram did both seams in an hour. Then the buckets again, and the drum full again, and the pump.

Vesk had been standing at the back of the bay through the whole of the first test, with his hands in his sleeves, as he stood through everything. When Bram bent to the pump handle the second time, Vesk took one hand out of his sleeve and went down the side of the drum to the far end, and stopped by the seam that ran round the end plate, near the bottom, and looked at Bram, and pointed at it with his chin.

The seam was dry. It was as dry as every other seam on the drum.

Bram looked at it. Bram looked at Vesk. Then he went on pumping, and at about three quarters of the way the seam Vesk had pointed at went dark along the plate edge, between two rivets, and a bead of water swelled out of it and ran down the end plate and dropped onto the brick.

Bram let the handle go.

"Could have said," he said to Vesk.

"He did," said Orrin, from the door.

Vesk put his hand back in his sleeve.

The third seam took another hour. The third time Bram pumped, the drum did not weep anywhere at all, and the lever on the safety valve lifted under its doubled weight with a small hiss of water, and Tess wrote down the stroke, and Bram let go of the handle and stood back with his hands at his sides and looked at the whole twenty feet of it, and then, very briefly, put the flat of one hand against the plate, the way a man puts his hand on the neck of a horse he has just decided to keep.



Vantic had stayed through all of it.

She had moved once, when the water hit Bram in the eye, back a pace, and then forward again to where she had been. She had not spoken since the first test. She came down the bay now, with Hale behind her, and stood at the side of the drum and looked up at the lever on the top of it, with the two weights hanging off the end.

"What happens," she said, "to a boiler that isn't tested?"

"It's tested anyway. The first morning it's fired. If a seam's bad, it finds out then, with steam behind it instead of water."

"And then."

He told her. He told it plainly, because she had asked plainly. A seam opened, and the spring let go, and a drum of water at a heat well past boiling became steam all at once, in the room, and the drum went one way, and everything in the room went another, and the steam went everywhere. He had read about it on his other world, in an old account of a mill, and the account had been written by a man who had gone in afterward, and he had not forgotten it.

"How many," said Vantic, "does it kill?"

"Everybody in the room."

"How many will be in the room?"

"Three. When it's running. A stoker. A man for the engine. A boy for the coal."

Vantic held out her hand, and Hale put the book in it, open, and a pen, and she wrote. One line, short. He could see from the side that it was a number.

Then the manhole at the end of the drum opened on its bolts and the smallest yard boy came out of it backward, the way he came out every noon, with his candle in its tin and the wool still in his ears and the dolly under his arm, and stood up on the brick in the light and blinked. He was the size of a ten-year-old and wet to the skin and very pleased with himself.

Vantic looked at him for a while.

Then she looked down at her book, and wrote again, under the first line, and Emeric saw that she had crossed out the number and written another one beside it, one more.

"When it's mended," she said, to the page.

He did not say anything. He watched her blot it.

"The lever," she said, and looked up at it again. "With the weight on it. Who decides the weight?"

"I did. It's set at what the drum will carry in steam, with room to spare. The second weight comes off. The first stays."

"And who decides that it stays?"

He opened his mouth and did not, for once, have the answer ready.

He knew the answer. He had known it on his other world. A man who stoked an engine for his living stood beside it all day and listened to it labouring, and wanted it to go faster, and knew that the only thing between him and faster was a weight on the end of a lever. And one morning he hung his coat on the lever, or his hat, or a second weight he had found in the scrap, or simply put his boot on it, and the engine went faster, and he was pleased with himself, until the seam that had been waiting for exactly that went. It had happened so often on his other world that there had been laws about it.

"Nobody," he said. "Yet."

Vantic nodded, as if that were the answer she had expected and was not pleased to have been right. She looked at the lever, and the pin, and the place where the weight hung from the end.

"Is it hot there," she said. "When it's running."

"Very."

"Then not wax." She turned to Hale. "The House's lead. And the pincers. From the strongbox."

Hale went. He went up the hill to the east tower and back, which was most of half an hour, and in the half hour Vantic had Bram drill a hole through the end of the lever and another through the hanger of the first weight, and asked him three questions about the pin, and stood with her hands folded while he answered them. When Hale came back he had a small coil of soft iron wire and a twist of lead pellets in a paper and a pair of iron pincers as long as a man's hand, with a die cut into the faces of the jaws.

Vantic did it herself, in her gloves. She ran the wire through the hole in the lever and through the hanger of the weight and twisted the ends together, close and tight, and slid a lead pellet onto the two ends, and closed the pincers on it. The lead squashed flat between the jaws and came away with the House's mark struck into both faces of it, small and exact, the same mark that was on the strongbox in the keep that she and Ferra had counted together on the first day.

The weight could not come off the lever now without the wire being cut, and the wire could not be cut without the lead coming away, and the lead could not be put back.

"If that is ever broken," Vantic said, "the House will want to know who was last in the room."

"It's a good idea."

"It is a precaution." She gave the pincers back to Hale. "The House lent against this barony as it stands. A barony with three men boiled to death in it is not the barony it lent against." She took the book from Hale and closed it, and looked once more at the boy on the brick with his candle, who was turning a wet lead pellet over in his fingers as though somebody had given him a medal. "Or four."

She went back up the hill with Hale a pace behind her.

Emeric stood by the drum and looked at the little flat seal of lead on its wire at the end of the lever.

She was pricing his risk to the House's security. That was true. It was the whole of what she had said, and he did not doubt that every word of it would be in her letter to the House on Thursday, in her small grey hand, under the date.

It was not the whole of what she had done. She had counted the boy.
