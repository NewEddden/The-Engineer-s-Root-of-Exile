Chapter 307 - What Happened on the Road

His father had taken to the counting room in the evenings.

Nobody had suggested it. In his first week Harrold had walked every street in the town in a borrowed hat that fooled nobody past the first corner, and in his second he had found the one room in Kaldo where nobody stood up when he came in. Ferra did not stop work for anybody, and had not stopped for the king. She had said, "Majesty," and pointed with her pen at the chair at the far end of her table, where Saban sat on quarter days, and gone back to the column; and he had sat in it and read the Wayford tape as it came off the reel, every yard of it, until she put the lamps out at ten.

He had been in that chair every evening since, with the price of oats at Wayford and the north gate's water and the hospital's beds, and once, without asking, Doran's book of the dead, which Ferra let him read and wrote down afterward that she had.

On the twenty-fourth of July Emeric came up the keep stair at nine with the drawing office's week for her to cost, and sat at his own end of her table with the sheets, and his father said, without looking up from the tape:

"Twenty-two days."

Ferra's pen stopped.

"Since the river." Harrold let the tape run on through his fingers, a yard of figures, and did not read it. "You've a thing you haven't told me. You had it on the ferry when you came over the side. You had it on Sunday with the soup in front of you, when Armand said the word Church, and you looked at your spoon as if it owed you money." He turned the tape over and looked at the back of it, which was blank. "I don't ask. I wait. I've waited. That's as near as I come."

Emeric put the sheets down.

He had meant to tell it every day of the twenty-two. His father had been out of a churchman's keeping three weeks, and he had not wanted to put the Church back into the room with him so soon. It had been a poor reason, and Harrold had been counting it.

"Not at this end of the table," he said. "Ward should be here. And Armand. And Imani."

"Then send for them."

"The clerks went at six," said Ferra. "There's nobody to send."

"I'll ask them," said Emeric.

He did not get up. He let his sense go out, thin, and found Ward's note on the gate tower where it was every night at nine, and Armand's in the dome beside the tree, and across the hospital's roofs Imani's, beside a note he knew for Rylan's by the irritation in it. He opened the door to each of them the width of a hair, and said three words, and shut it.

His father's head came up.

He was looking at the air between Emeric and the window, where a draft would have been if there had been one. He was a knight, the kingdom said, and had never been a mage in any room Emeric had seen him in. But he had something. He looked at the empty air with his hand gone still on the tape, the way a man looks at a door he is sure he heard open.

"What was that?"

"That was me asking."

Harrold looked at him.

"I'll tell you that too," said Emeric. "It's part of it."



Ward was first, because Ward was always first. He came in without knocking and looked at the king in Saban's chair, and at Emeric, and at Ferra, and went and stood with his back to the shelves of ledgers by the door, where he could see all of them. Armand came up the stair two at a time, which he still did on purpose, and was not out of breath, and minded that nobody remarked on it. Imani came last, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a smell of the ward's lye soap on her.

"He wants his pole back," she said, to the room. "He says he'll lie still if he can hold it. I said I'd break it over him." She looked at Emeric. "Well? You said now."

Ferra got up and shut the door and dropped the latch, and turned the lamp over her table up a little, and sat down again. She did not pick up her pen.

So he told them the ferry.

They knew the parts that could be told in a long room: the fourth man who did not run, the pressure over the bank, Ward's voice in the reeds saying the channel. They had not heard the rest. He found when he began that his right hand had gone flat on Ferra's table and his forefinger was tapping on the wood, slowly, as it tapped on a bench when a figure would not come right. Ferra looked at it once. He let it tap.

"Every person has a note," he said, to his father, because the rest of them knew. "It's what I hear when my sense is out. Each one's different, and it doesn't change, any more than a voice does. When he opened on the bank I heard his. And another under it." The finger tapped. "Two strings, in one man. I've never felt that."

"I felt something," said Harrold. "On the ferry. In the carriage. Like weather coming over a hill." He was quiet. "I thought it was weather."

"It was him." Emeric turned his left wrist over on the table, so the band lay up in the lamplight, and the small grey bead in it among the others, worn soft at its edges from being carried. "This was hers. The stone she put in my hands when I was small. A stone like this learns whoever carries it; it had her most of her life before it had me. And when the second string sounded on the bank, this answered it." He touched the bead. "It rang. The way a glass rings on a shelf when somebody hums its note in the room."

Nobody said anything. Down in the inner ward somebody was walking the gate with a lantern; he heard the boots go past and turn.

"I want to say this first," he said. "So you know where I stand when I say the rest. I don't believe in this. I don't believe the dead are anywhere. I never have. I don't believe in the Light, or what Doran says over the bread, though I'd sit through Doran for the bread." The finger had stopped. He looked at it, and left it where it was. "And she was there. Not like a ghost. Not standing on the bank in a white dress. She was in it. In the second string. With him, somehow, in his right hand, which he had closed. I can't tell you how. I'd say it was one stone answering another, except that it sounded like somebody."

His father sat very still in Saban's chair with the tape across his knees.

"She would be," said Harrold.

He said it quietly, the way a man says a thing he has no doubt of; and Emeric, who had no faith at all, found he did not want to argue with it.

"I felt it in the channel," said Ward from the shelves. "Twenty-eight years ago. On my back, running. I'd no name for it." He did not move. "I said on the bank, that's him. I'll say it again. That's him."

Harrold looked at his knight. Then at his son.

"What is it?" he said. "What he does. What you do, that you can hear it." He put the tape down on the table, folded, square. "And what you did just now, to bring them up a stair without a word."



"There's a room," said Emeric.

"Inside. Everyone has one, I think; most people never find the door. The grey stones are the door. The court calls them Hollow Crystals. We call them control stones." He touched the bead again. "Hers is mine. You sent me the rest of hers, the year Ward asked. The treasury wrote of no value on the slip."

"I let it," said Harrold. "It kept them out of the inventory."

"The room's whatever you are. Mine's a long table and shelves. Ward's is a guardroom." Ferra did not look up. "Time runs slow in it, forty minutes inside to three out here. Everything you've looked at properly is on the shelves, and you can take it down and look again." He stopped. "And if somebody lets you, and you've a hand on them, you can see what they remember. As if you stood behind them and looked over their shoulder. Only what they give you. If they push, you're out."

"And people who do it feel each other," said Armand.

He was at the window with his back to the dark glass, tall and lean and thirty-two to look at, with the old man's voice, dry as a slate.

"The tower has a word for nearly everything, Majesty, down to the way a stone sweats in a warm room. It has no word for this. I was in it sixty years and never heard of it." He looked at the floor. "I've thought since that a word is a thing somebody has to be allowed to keep."

"And the thing just now. The draft."

"A sliver of it. The door held open the width of a hair. I can say a word into somebody's head across a yard, if I know their note and they've said I may, or lay a picture over what they see." Emeric looked at his father. "May I?"

Harrold's eyes narrowed very slightly. "Go on."

He opened it. He felt his father feel it a breath before the word, the way they all did, a quiet like a room when somebody has just stopped speaking; and said into it, plainly, with no picture at all: Father.

The king sat back in Saban's chair.

"Don't," he said, after a while, "do that at dinner."

Imani laughed, once, through her nose, and put her hand over her mouth.

Harrold did not smile. He looked at the place where the word had been, and then at the grey bead; and Emeric watched him go back along twenty-eight years, slowly, the way a man goes back along a ledger with his finger for the line where the figures first went wrong.

"She did this," he said. It was not a question.

"She used to come to the room. Mine. The first night I went in, I knew it was hers, and I'd never been in it in my life." Emeric's finger had begun to tap again.

"She never told me."

Nobody said anything to that.

"Twenty-three years beside her," said the king. "Eight of them married. She told me when the cook was stealing sugar. She told me the night you were born that you'd have my temper and her hands, and she was right about the temper." He was quiet. "She never told me this."

"Somebody taught her very young never to let anybody see how good she was," said Armand.

The king turned his head.

"She did everything I set her too well, Majesty, and then spoiled it at the end with one small fault. Always at the end. I called it carelessness for four years, while you waited at the bottom of my stair with two horses." He looked at his hands, which were steady. "It wasn't carelessness. Somebody had taught her that before she could read. I told your son so in a ship and thought I was telling him a story about a girl at a slate. I think now it was the whole of her."

"That's half," said Emeric. He made himself look at his father, and not at the table. "Whatever this is, the Church buried it a long time ago, and has made sure since that nobody does it but its own. And a man who did it stood beside you every day of your life. If she could feel him the way I felt him on the bank, she knew what he was from the first feast day she sat at your table." The finger tapped. "And she knew you. If she'd told you, you'd have looked at him once. One dinner. One look, the wrong way, across a cup. And he'd have seen it."

The king said nothing for a long time.

"Yes," he said at last. "I would have."

He turned in the chair, then, slowly, and looked at Ward by the shelves.

"Thirteen years of letters," said Harrold. "One a month. You wrote me the wells and the war and the debt and the price of a pound of stone." He paused. "You wrote me a forge."

"I wrote you a forge."

"Below the keep. That made fine steel and true parts, and you didn't understand it and hadn't asked." The king's voice did not change. "I read that letter three times. I knew it was smaller than what you'd seen. I thought it was a better furnace." He looked at Ward. "You never once wrote me a room."

"No."

"Why?"

"It wasn't mine." Ward did not look away. "I'm your man. I'm his. When those pull different ways I make a thing smaller and I don't lie. A room was his to tell you. He hadn't."

"And if you'd written it, I'd have read it at my desk with the cardinal in the next room, and gone in to supper, and looked at him." Harrold turned back to the table. "Her reason. You had her reason."

"I didn't know her reason," said Ward. "I knew mine."

"It's the same one."

Emeric's finger had stopped. He looked at his father across Ferra's table, and remembered a small room and a bed's edge and a man holding a pleasant face over something enormous.

"You told me when I was eight," he said. "Not a man. Not a house. Something funded, and patient, and old enough to be patient."

"I said that."

"You were right."

The king looked at his own hands on the arms of Saban's chair.

"I was right," he said, "and it did me no good at all." Then: "There's more. You didn't bring them all up a stair to tell me about a bead."



"There's a way to show you the day," said Emeric.

He had not needed to ask Ward. Ward had come away from the shelves while Armand was talking, and stood at the corner of the table, a pace from the king's chair.

"I have it," said Ward. "All of it, till the castle gate. I put it in the room the first winter after I learned the room. I've never taken it out." He looked at the king. "I'll give it."

"And mine," said Harrold.

"Yours I'd have to read. You've never found your room, so it's the old way, with a hand on you, and you'd have to let me." Emeric looked at his father's wrist on the chair's arm. "It doesn't hurt. If it hurts I've done it wrong and I stop. You'll be awake, in that chair, with Ferra's lamp on, the whole time. I'll look where you take me and nowhere else. If you want me out, push, the way you'd push a man off your arm, and I'm out." He stopped. "I'd take you from the gate, when Ward comes in. To where you found her. That's all."

"Will I see her?"

"In Ward's. As Ward saw her."

"Then yes."

He said it at once, and held out his left hand across the corner of the table, palm up, the way he would have held it out to have a ring put on it.

"The wrist," said Emeric. "Only two fingers."

"How long?" said Imani.

"Ten minutes. Not more. A day held in a room isn't the day. It's what he kept of it." He looked at her. "It plays the way he put it away. Not the way it happened."

Imani opened her watch in her palm. "Ten," she said. "Then I stop you." She went and stood behind the king's chair, without asking anybody.

Ferra had not picked up her pen. That was what he noticed most, in the last breath before: the pen lying across the column where she had put it down at twenty-two days, and her hands flat either side of the book, and that she was not going to write any of this. Armand had turned back to the window. Only the three of them, then, himself and Ward and his father, and the others outside it to watch their faces. Each one he carried cost him, and a moving picture most of all, and he would not spread himself thinner over this than he had to.

He put two fingers on the inside of his father's wrist, where Imani put hers. The pulse was slow and very even. It was the pulse of a man who had trained it.

"Shut your eyes if you like," he said. "Some do. You'll see it either way."

The king kept his eyes open.



The room was where it always was. The long table, and the shelves, and the slow clean light.

He held the door open to both of them, wider than a hair, and felt Ward go down through his own band into the guardroom, and cross it to a chest under the bench that Emeric had never seen opened, and open it without hesitating at the lid. Ward brought what was in it to the door between them and held it out; and Emeric took it, and laid it on his table, and laid it out across the counting room for his father to see.

It came faint, as the overlay always came, a pattern on a window: through it he could see Ferra's ledgers and the lamp and Imani's hand on the back of the king's chair. Over them lay a castle yard at morning.

Four carriages and a cart. Grooms at the horses' heads. Knights in plate, mounted, twenty of them, their crests up, and a gate standing open on a bright sky. Ward's hands on a girth strap, younger hands. The sounds came as if through a wall: a horse stamping, a man laughing at the gate, somebody calling for a second blanket.

A boy at the window of the second carriage.

He was seven, perhaps, and small for it, in a blue coat with the buttons done up wrong, and he had his face against the glass so that his breath came and went on it, and his eyes in the bright morning were gold. He was looking at the gate. He was looking at it as if it were the best thing he had ever seen.

Emeric had not known he would be there. He should have; it was his birthday. He looked at the boy with the buttons done up wrong and felt nothing he could name, only a great flat quiet, as when a sum carried for years comes out to a figure he cannot argue with. His finger on Ferra's table tapped once, twice.

A woman came down the steps from the keep door with a riding cloak over her arm.

Ward's eye went to her, because Ward's eye always went to her: that was his post. A grey travelling dress, plain as a clerk's, and her hair put up under a net; brown skin, and a long face that did not care to be beautiful, and a mouth about to say something dry to somebody. She stopped on the last step to say it to a groom, and the groom laughed. Then she turned her head and looked straight at Ward, across the yard, through twenty-eight years and a counting room in Kaldo, and her eyes were a light brown, almost amber, and she was smiling.

That was all. A woman on a step. He had her name and had had it all his life; he had never once had her face, and now he had it, and it was the face of a stranger, and it was his. He knew it the way a man knows his own hand in a glove.

Beside him in the real room his father made no sound. Emeric felt the pulse under his two fingers go once, hard, and come back to even.

Ward's eye went round the yard after, because the yard was his post too: the grooms, the gate, the household come down on to the keep steps to see the birthday off. Rowena on the bottom step with the small one on her hip, Waylon, three, red in the face and howling at being left behind, and Amara stopping on her way to the carriage to kiss the top of his head and tell him he could have the falls next year. A step above them the eldest prince, thirteen and grown out of his coat at the wrists, sent down by somebody to wave, and not waving.

The yard went. Ward had not kept the road out, as a man skips the pages he does not need. Then a hill road high up, the carriages strung along it, a stream below in a cut, and a long bare ridge on either hand with grey rock coming through the grass. Ward on his horse at the second carriage's door. The boy's voice from inside, asking how far to the falls, and a woman's voice answering him: two more bends, and if he asked again it would be three.

Her voice. He had dreamed it for years, a voice above and behind him at a slate, and never once heard what it said. It was lower than he had thought. It was teasing a seven-year-old about the number of bends in a road.

A rider came back down the column at a canter: a lean grey man in plate, with his helm under his arm and a face like a shut gate, who said something short to Ward and went back up the road at the gallop.

"Halden," said Ward, in the counting room. It was the first word anybody had said. "Captain of her guard." And after a moment: "He turned us at the gate that morning. Off the river road on the board, on to the falls. He always did. Never took the road on the board in his life."

Then the slopes moved.

It was not like the stream ground, where the volleys had gone out in ranks and the thing had a shape a man could see from a hill. This was the ridges getting up. Men in grey hoods came up out of the grass on both sides where there had been nothing, more than he could count, in no hurry, in lines somebody had drawn on a table who knew his work; and the first thing off the slope was glyphs, opening along both ridges like a row of lamps being lit, and the road behind the last carriage went up in a wall of earth and stone, and the road ahead was open.

That was the cleverness of it. They left one way out and drove the convoy down it, with the slopes burning behind and the horses screaming, at the gallop, into the only gap there was.

The channel.

A cleft where the stream had cut down through the ridge, grey rock going up on both hands three times a man's height, and the road along the bottom beside the water. Wide enough for a carriage. Narrow enough at its far end, where the rock came in close, for one man on foot to hold it against anything that came at him one at a time. The carriages went into it with their hubs striking sparks off the rock, and the hooded men came down behind and over the lip on both sides; and at the far end there were hooded men already, waiting, because of course there were.

Halden went up the channel on foot past the front carriage, alone, and put himself in the narrow place with the rock at both his shoulders. Ward's eye went to him once, and away; and when it next found the far end, a long time afterward in that cut and broken memory, the captain was down on one knee with a heap in front of him, and still there.

The rest went in pieces, the way Ward had kept it.

A carriage on its side in the stream with the water going white round it. Ward's sword. A hooded man with a glyph half drawn on his palm and no time to finish it. Knights round the second carriage with their shields up, two deep, and red light coming down on them off the lip, and a knight going down. Another. The cart burning. Ward pulling the carriage door off its hinges with one hand because the frame had bent shut, and the boy inside on the floor with his hands over his ears.

And her.

She was out of the carriage before the door was off, through the other side. She stood in the channel with her back to the second carriage and her cloak gone and her two hands up, and she was the most frightening thing in it. Glyphs came off her hands one after another, small, very fast, each one exactly the size of the thing it was for: a hooded man on the lip went backward, and another, and a third who had been drawing something large on the rock above the knights stopped drawing it. She did not shout or seem to hurry. She stood at the carriage's wheel and put down the men who came over the lip in ones and twos, with the boy under the carriage at her feet; and every glyph was small.

"She never once opened the big ones," said Ward in the counting room. His voice was quite flat. "She could have taken that whole slope off the hill. Not with us in it."

By the window, Armand said, very low, "No. She wouldn't have." Nobody else said anything.

The far end. Halden on his knee in the narrow place, and then not on his knee.

The knights fewer. Ward's head turning back down the channel toward its mouth, where they had come in, where the line of hoods was thin. Ward's hand on the boy's coat, hauling him out. Her voice, close, saying Ward, the mouth, go, and the three of them going down the channel along the rock, the boy between them, and two knights with them, and then one.

The mouth of the channel. Grey rock, a split pine growing out of a crack in it at a man's height with half its roots in the air, and the road going out between the rock and the open hillside and the thin line of hoods across it.

And on the slope above the mouth, off to the left, one man in a grey hood stood quite still on the hillside while all the others moved.

She saw him. Ward saw her see him. Her right hand came off the boy's shoulder for the length of a breath, and something went up the slope from it, small and very fast, no bigger than all the rest she had thrown that day; and the still man turned half round, the way a man turns when a cart clips him in a lane, and his right arm dropped to his side and stayed there.

He did not go down. He brought his left hand out of his cloak instead, and held it, while the three of them came on along the rock toward the mouth with the boy between them; held it until she had her hand on the boy again. Then, from the left hand, a line of white.

It was thin as a wire. It came down off the slope too fast to see and Ward saw it anyway, because he was a knight and that was what the rite had bought him: a line of white light, very straight, and at its end the boy.

She turned. She did not draw anything. There was no time to draw anything. She turned her back to the slope and went down over the boy, all of her, in one movement, the way a woman catches a cup going off a table.

And under Ward's memory, under the table, under the wall, something of Emeric's own came up.

It did not come through Ward. It came from below and much closer, from where a seven-year-old could have been and a grown knight could not. A hand on the back of his neck, small and very strong, pressing his face down into grit and the smell of horses, harder than anybody had ever pressed him; and then, after the crack, which was not in it at all, a smell. Sharp and clean and wrong, like the dynamo house when a brush sparked. That was all. No face. No voice. A hand on the back of his neck, and a smell.

He knew it was his because nothing else in the whole of Ward's day was that close to the ground.

The wall did not give. It stood there smooth, as it always had, with the scrap lying at its foot like something pushed out under a door; and he put the scrap on the table beside the boy in the blue coat, and did not try the wall.

In Ward's day the white line was gone and she lay over the boy in the road at the mouth of the channel, face down, not moving. Ward was on his knees. His hand was on her neck, at the side of it, under the net and the put-up hair; and Emeric felt what Ward had felt there, because Ward had kept it, exactly, the way a guard keeps the thing he failed at: the note going out of her. Not slowly. Like a lamp. There, and then the place where it had been, and nothing in it at all, though his hand was on her and her skin was warm.

On Earth every hospital had kept a machine that put a current through a stopped heart to start it. She had taken the other kind in her back. He put that on the table in his own head, where nobody would see it.

Ward did not wait. That was in it as exactly as the rest. He turned her over, enough to get the boy out from under her, and the boy came out white and with his eyes open and not crying, and Ward put him up over his shoulder, one arm round his legs, and stood up with his sword in his other hand and looked once at the woman in the road; and went at the thin line across the mouth.

Two hoods. Ward went through them. Then the open hillside and the road going down it, and Ward running.

And the boy over his shoulder, looking back.

Emeric had this. It was the only thing he had ever had from before seven: being carried, and Ward younger, and his own eyes fixed on something a long way past the man holding him. He had had it twenty-eight years from the inside. Now he had it from Ward's side, the boy's weight on Ward's shoulder and the stones in Ward's plate going hot one after another under the run; and he knew at last what the boy had been looking at. It was the mouth of the channel, getting smaller.

The run went in pieces too. Ward had kept almost none of it. A milestone. A ford. A gauge on his forearm with its needle down past the half, and then past the quarter. The boy's weight. The light going round the sky. And all the way down the hill and across the ford and on, behind him, from the mouth of the channel or near it, a pressure in the air, in the teeth, like weather standing still over one place; and Ward with no name for it, running away from it with the boy, hour after hour, because there was nothing else on earth he could do.

Emeric knew that pressure. He had stood under it on a riverbank twenty-two days ago.

His finger came down on Ferra's table, and went through it.

He heard it: a short flat crack like a nail driven into wet wood. He felt the grain give round his fingertip, an inch and more of old elm, and the splinters go out underneath on to his knee. It did not hurt. He looked down through the overlay and saw his forefinger standing in Ferra's table to the second joint, and the hole round it very neat.

The door did not shut. That was what he made sure of, in the breath after: that it stayed exactly as wide as it had been, his father's pulse still under the two fingers of his other hand, Ward's day still across the room, the castle's towers coming up grey at the end of the road. He held it. He drew his finger out of the table, slowly, and laid his hand flat on the wood beside the hole, and kept it there.

Ferra did not move. Out of the side of his eye he saw her look at the hole, and at his hand, and at his face; and then put her own hand out across the table and lay it on his wrist, above the bead, and leave it there, and say nothing.

The castle gate. A sentry's face. Ward's own voice, very hoarse, saying a word over and over, and the word was the king.

Ward's day ended there. He had put it away at the gate, and there was no more of it.



His father's was another thing.

He went along his own mana into the note under his two fingers and asked, and felt his father give, which was not clumsy at all. Harrold did it the way he did everything he had decided on: completely, at once, and without the least sign on his face that he was doing it. He was looking at the gate. Because he was looking at it, it was there.

The same gate, from inside. A yard full of men and a lamp being lit. A knight coming in under the arch on foot, at a stumbling run, in plate gone dull with dust and blood, with a boy over his shoulder; and the knight's face, which was Ward's, young, grey-white and wet, with his mouth open. The boy being taken off him by three people at once. Ward standing in the yard with his empty arms a little out from his sides, as if he were still carrying something, and saying it again.

It was not cut like Ward's. Harrold had never had a room to put it in, and it came the way any man's memory comes when he is asked, in bright pieces each lying by itself: a horse under him, its neck dark with sweat. Torches. Every man in the castle who could sit, on the road behind him, going up into the hills at night. No sound in any of it but hoofs.

Then the channel, by torchlight.

The carriages were in pieces. The cart was ash. The knights lay among the hoods along the rock, where they had stood round the second carriage, and some of the hoods had been pulled back, and the faces under them were nobody's, men nobody would ever put a name to; and at the far end, in the narrow place with the rock at both shoulders, a heap, and in front of it, on his face, Halden.

Harrold went down the channel with the torch held low and looked at every face, knight and hood alike; and each time it was not her, and he went to the next. He stood over Halden a while, and did not turn him over. Then he came back the whole length of it, and out at the mouth, past the split pine with its roots in the air, and stood in the road.

She was not there.

Ward's memory lay on the table beside his father's, faint, and Emeric turned it, as he had turned a lamp on a hill once in a tower room, until its ground lay along the ground of the other. The split pine. The same crack in the same grey rock. The road. They were the same place seen at noon and at midnight, and in one of them a woman lay face down in the road at the foot of the pine, and in the other there was nothing in the road at all but the marks of a great many feet.

Torches going out across the dark slope in a line and coming back, a long time; Harrold had kept the length of it if nothing else. Then a man shouting from above the road, back the way the convoy had come, a long way off.

A gully. A dry cut going up into the hill a quarter of a mile back from the mouth, with thorn along its sides and a lip of rock at its head, overhanging, like a door with no house behind it. Harrold climbing it with the torch. The men below him stopping, and not coming on.

Under the overhang, on the dry ground, she lay on her back.

She lay straight. Her legs were together and straight out, her dress drawn down over them to the ankles and smoothed. Her arms were crossed on her breast, the hands laid one over the other, the fingers straight, as hands are laid in a crypt. Her face was turned up and her eyes were closed. Somebody had taken the net out of her hair and laid her hair straight too, on either side of her face, on the stone.

Harrold knelt down beside her with the torch in his hand, and did not touch her, and looked at her hands.

It was the sharpest thing in all of it: the light shaking on her hands, because the torch shook; the right over the left. Then the torch going down on the stone, and his own hand with the king's ring on it going out, and stopping short of hers, and staying in the air an inch above them all night. The torch burned down and a man came up with another. The stars went round. In the grey before morning a cart came up the road below with a long shape on it under canvas that took eight men to carry up the gully, a great clear crystal the length of a woman, faintly lit from inside, a cocoon, as the court called them, that the old houses bought to keep their dead, and a small anxious man with it in a crystal-seller's coat, who looked at her, and at the sky, and said something to the king that made Harrold look at him; and then the man put his hands on the crystal and it opened, slowly, like water drawing back from a stone, and they laid her in it, and it closed.

Harrold had kept what the man said. He had kept it twenty-eight years. A day is a long time, Majesty. It keeps what it's given.

That was where his father's memory ended. He had asked to be taken to where he found her, and he had been taken there.

Ward spoke first.

"That's not where she fell."

"She went down at the mouth. On the road. At the foot of that pine. I had my hand on her." His voice did not rise at all. "I'd know that ground in my sleep. I've had it in my sleep. She was in the road."

"She was up the gully," said Harrold. "Under the rock. A quarter of a mile."

"Then somebody carried her there."

Emeric had already looked; it is the first thing a man looks for in two drawings of the same part. He had thought first of Grier, who had given him the right courier and the wrong day and believed it, because a memory is what a man remembers and not what happened. But a man could mistake a Tuesday for a Thursday. He did not mistake a road for a gully a quarter of a mile up a hill, when he had knelt in one with his hand on a woman's neck and in the other all night with his hand an inch above hers.

"They're both right," he said. "She fell where Ward says. She was found where you say. She was moved."

"Why?" said Ward. "There was a fight on. There were your father's riders coming, and they knew it, or they'd not have gone before the torches. Why move a dead woman a quarter of a mile up a hill in the middle of that?"

Emeric looked at the gully, faint over the shelves, and the thorn along its sides that a man on the road would never see past.

"Because whatever he wanted with her," he said slowly, "he couldn't do on the road. Not in the fight, with knights still on their feet and Ward coming back for her, for all he knew. It took time. And it took quiet. So he took her up a hill, under a rock, where nobody would come on him." His hand was flat on Ferra's table beside the hole. "The pressure Ward ran away from for four hours. It wasn't at the mouth. It was up that gully. That was him, doing it. Whatever it was."

"Doing what?"

"I don't know." He knew what had rung like somebody in the closed right hand of a man on a bank, and he would not say more in this room than he knew. "He took something from her. Up there. Something that took him hours, and that he still carries. I can't tell you what it is. I can tell you where he got it."

His father had not moved.

"And then he laid her straight," said Emeric.

The king looked at him.

"When he was done. Her legs and her dress and her hands." He made himself go on. "You've thought for twenty-eight years that one of Halden's knights did that for her. Dying. Carried her up out of the road, and laid her straight, and lay down somewhere himself."

"Yes," said Harrold. Only that.

"There was nobody. Look." He laid Ward's day over his father's again, gently, Ward going out at the mouth with the boy past the last knight, and the last knight going down behind him. "When Ward went out, there wasn't one standing. Nobody of hers carried her up that gully." He stopped. "He did. And then he laid her straight, and crossed her hands for her, and went away."

"And left her there," said Ward. "He'd carried her a quarter of a mile up a hill. Why not the rest of the way?"

Emeric looked at his father.

"Because you were coming," he said. "You felt him on the ferry, like weather over a hill. It goes both ways. He'd have felt you on that road with every man in the castle behind you, a long way off, getting nearer. Whatever he was doing up there took hours. He stayed for every one of them he had. Then he couldn't carry her and keep ahead of you, so he laid her out, and went."

The king said nothing. His hand on the arm of the chair had closed.

"There's one more," said Ward. "I never had the sense to ask it. Twenty-eight years and I never asked it." He was looking at the faint road over the shelves, where the overlay had been. "Halden turned us at the gate. Nobody had the falls road till the yard. And they were on it, both ridges, waiting."

"He was at the hill house that week," said Harrold slowly. "His retreat. I sent a rider up to him the next morning with the news, and he came down for the office." He stopped. "The hill house is above the falls."

"Then somebody in that yard sent him the road," said Ward.

Nobody had an answer to that. Emeric still had the yard on his table, faint, the way a drawing stays on the eye after the lamp is turned down: the grooms, the gate, the household on the keep steps. Half the faces he could not have put a name to. Ward did not ask it twice.



Imani said, "Nine minutes," and closed her watch.

He let the two days go off the table slowly, both together, so neither of them would feel the pictures snap away; and the counting room came back whole, and with it, the instant the door was shut, the ache behind his eyes, the overlay's price, like a thumb on each eyeball from the inside. It was no worse than a long night at the drawing board. He let go of his father's wrist.

Nobody moved.

Harrold sat in Saban's chair with his hands on its arms and his eyes open on the dark window, past Armand, at nothing; and when he spoke, it was in the voice from a small room twenty-seven years ago, the voice that died at arm's length, so low that Emeric had to lean to catch it, though there was nobody at the door and nobody on the stair.

"Again."

Then he heard himself. Emeric watched him hear it: the habit of twenty-eight years, a king in a room with nobody listening, whispering. He shut his mouth on it. He sat up a little in the chair.

"Again," he said, at an ordinary volume, quite steadily. "Ward's. The end of it. Where she goes down."

Imani looked at Emeric. She was not looking at his face. She was looking at his throat.

"Only the end," said Emeric. "I can do the end."

"Two minutes," said Imani, "and then you're done, both of you," and opened the watch again.

Ward said nothing. He brought it to the door a second time, and Emeric laid out only the last of it: the split pine, the thin line of hoods, the still man on the slope, the white line, and her turning. His father watched it with nothing at all in his face, the way a man hears a sentence read out in court that he has already read, to be sure it is the same sentence.

It was the same.

"He had the boy clear from that slope," said Ward, when it was gone, "from the moment we came round the rock. And he held it." He was quiet. "He waited till she was beside him."

Nobody answered that either.

"Ten and a half," said Imani, and shut the watch, and put it in her pocket, and put her hand on the king's shoulder from behind, and left it there.

Harrold sat for a while under it. Then he said, without turning his head:

"He said the office over her."

Nobody answered.

"In the crypt, when the crystal was in its place and they'd shut the grille. The cardinal said the office for the dead over her, the long one, and I thanked him after." His voice was perfectly level. "I remember he blessed her with his left hand. I remember thinking it was his age. That his shoulder had gone. He was old then, I thought. He's never got any older."

"It had gone," said Ward, from the shelves. "She put it out for him. At the mouth." He did not raise his voice. "You saw it."

The king was looking at the window. Emeric saw him go back along the ledger again, quicker now, a man who has found the first wrong figure and is running down the column for every one after it.

"His left hand. Every feast day after. On the book at the council table. On the stair, when he takes my arm for the people at the foot of it to see. Always the left." He was quiet. "And at your farewell. I was on the dais. I watched him stop in front of you and lift his left hand over you and say something about the Light that the hall thought was gentle; and his right at his side, closed." He turned his head at last and looked at his son. "I watched him do it and I thought nothing at all. I've never thought anything at all about that man's right hand. Twenty-eight years. He's stood at my elbow with it shut every day of them."

"With her in it," said Emeric.

"With her in it." The king looked at his own hands on the arms of the chair; at the right one, with the ring on it, and then at the left. "I've stood beside my wife every day for twenty-eight years. And never once known it."

He did not shout. He did not get up. There was no colour in his face at all. He sat in the chair with Imani's hand on his shoulder and turned the king's ring once on his finger, all the way round, and stopped it where it had been; and Emeric, who had been angry himself in a cellar once in a way that still frightened him, understood that he had never seen anybody as angry as his father was now, and that nobody else at that table would have known it from his face.

Harrold looked down, then, at Ferra's table, between them. At the hole in it, a finger wide and very neat, with the lamp shining into it. He looked at it for a long breath. Then he looked at his son's right hand, lying flat beside it, and then up past Emeric at Ward, at the corner of the table; and Ward looked back at him, and neither of them said a word, and the king looked away again at the window.

"It wasn't Aldane," said Ward.

They all looked at him.

He had his flat grey eyes on the king, and he spoke as he spoke at the dawn drill, in the fewest words that would do.

"I told him it was Aldane," he said. "On the westerlies, in the winter of the tenth year. I said it to you the night she died, with her blood on my coat. You looked, and you cleared him, and I didn't. I've looked at that man across every hall I've stood in since, for a hole." He was quiet. "There wasn't one. You were right. It was the churchman. He was on the slope, and I ran past him with the boy, and I spent twenty-eight years looking at the wrong man because I wanted it to be one I could reach." He looked at Emeric. "I take it back. All of it. I want that said in this room, with the king in it."

"It's said," said Harrold.

"Then I've done." Ward went back to the shelves by the door, and stood with his back to them, and said nothing else at all.



The others went down at eleven. Armand went first, without a word, and Emeric felt his note cross the inner ward into the dome and stop beside the tree, and stay there. Imani went to the ward, because Rylan had found a way to sit up and she wanted to be there to stop him, and at the door she put two fingers under Emeric's jaw, unasked, and held them there, and said "Hm," and went. Ward went to the gate tower. He always went to the gate tower.

His father stayed in Saban's chair.

Ferra put a cup of the kitchen's tea by his hand, which he did not drink, and picked up her pen at last, and opened the drawing office's week and began to cost it. She did the castings first. She always did the castings first.

After a while his father unfolded the yard of Wayford tape he had folded square, and read it from the beginning, the oats and the water and the beds, line by line, as if any of it were something a man could hold on to.

At half past eleven Ferra reached across the table without looking up, and put the tip of her forefinger into the hole in it, and took it out again, and went on with the castings.



On the first of August his father asked them why not.

It was the counting room again, in the afternoon, with the windows open on the inner ward. Imani had come up with the ward's sheet for the month and put it down beside Ferra's column, and sat on the corner of the table to wait, which Ferra allowed nobody else to do. Emeric was at his end with the tape. The king was in Saban's chair. He had begun to bring his own pen.

Nobody had mentioned the hole in the table. Ferra had put her inkstand over it on the second day, and moved it back off on the third, and left it.

"You're not married," said Harrold.

He said it to the window, in the tone a man uses to read a figure off a tape. Emeric looked up. Ferra's pen did not stop.

"Either of you," said the king. "To him, I mean. Ward wrote me so, in a line at the bottom of a letter about a road, and every so often after in the same place, the way he put the wells at the bottom: not yet." He turned his head and looked at the two women, the one at her column and the one on the corner of the table, and did not look at his son at all. "I'd like to know why not. I'm asking you. Not him. He'll give me a reason that's about himself, and it'll be wrong."

Ferra finished her line. She drew the rule under it, and laid the pen down across the book, and looked at the king across her table, as she had looked across a trestle once at a prince who had come to buy grain.

"Because a wedding is paper, Majesty," she said. "A page in Doran's register, with the duke's name on it, and mine, and hers, and the date, in Doran's hand. Two women and a man in a church that says it has one door for one woman. There's a cardinal's man who has gone up the Lieutenant's stair twice a month for two years with a portfolio, and I know what goes in it. Our paper." She did not look away. "There's a case being built against this town, and I won't give the man building it a page with my name on it to read out in a hall. Not while there's a portfolio. Not for anything."

"There's still a portfolio," said the king.

"There's still a man who carries it. We watched him go up into the air from a riverbank with your wife in his hand."

Harrold looked at her. Then at Imani.

"And you."

"He's already got a page with me on it." Imani had her hands round her knee. "Everything I do in the ward, he'll call craft if he wants to. Mana medicine. The dosed water. The cartridges. If there's a case, I'm in it already, as the duke's healer who does strange things to people with stones." She shrugged. "If I marry him, I'm in it as the duke's wife. And so's the ward, and every soul in it. Rylan's in it this minute, asking for his pole." She looked at the king, very directly, the way she looked at a man on a bed who was going to be told something. "I won't hand him my ward. Not for him. Not for anything."

The king said nothing for a while.

"Good," he said.

Ferra's eyebrows went up, very slightly.

"They're the right reasons. They're the only ones I'd have taken." He looked at his hands. "I've taken a great deal less from my own council."

"And we told him first," said Ferra. "Before he asked. On the keep leads, on the first Hollow Night, both of us, in one breath." She picked up her pen again. "So he'd never have to hear us say it to a question."

Harrold looked at her.

Then he looked, at last, down the length of the table at his son, who was sitting at the far end of it with a yard of Wayford tape in his hands and nothing whatever to say; and something happened to the king's face. It started at the corner of his mouth, the way it had in a small office in the capital with the fire gone to coals, and this time it did not stop there. It went all the way across. He put his head back against Saban's chair and laughed.

It was not a loud laugh. It was rusty, as if it had been put away and not taken out, and it went on longer than he seemed to have meant it to, and at the end he put a hand over his eyes and sat like that, with his shoulders going. Emeric had not heard it in twenty-eight years. He found he had not known what it sounded like. He felt Ferra not look at him, very carefully, and Imani not look at him either.

"Before he asked," said the king, through his hand.

"Yes," said Ferra.

"Both of you."

"In one breath," said Imani.

Harrold took his hand down. His eyes were wet, and he did nothing about it.

"She'd have done the same," he said. "She did. To me." He looked at the window. "I asked her at the foot of Armand's stair, the week she came home from the tower. She was eighteen. She told me not yet, and why: that a fosterling with no house who married a prince with two wives living would be the court's supper for a year, and she'd not be eaten. And then she said she'd tell me when." The corner of his mouth went again. "She told me three years after. At the foot of the same stair. Before I'd opened my mouth. I'd been going to ask her again that morning, and she knew it, and she'd not let me."

Nobody said anything. Ferra's pen had stopped again.

The king looked at Imani on the corner of the table, at her hands round her knee: long brown hands, with the nails cut short for the ward and a small burn on the back of one from the steam tray, that had held a great many things that morning and would hold a great many more before dark.

"You have my wife's hands," he said.

Imani looked down at them.

She did not say anything for a while, which was not like her. She turned the right one over in her lap and looked at the palm, as if somebody had just told her it was not hers; and then turned it back, and closed it on her knee.

"They've been in a lot of people," she said at last, gruffly.

"So had hers," said the king. "That's how I know."



The old pear by the gate was carrying that summer more than anybody could remember, and the squirrel was eating it.

Ferra had the figures, on the back of April's envelope in a second column under the blossom: eleven pears, off the kitchen's best tree. She stood under it that evening with the envelope in her fingers and her face turned up, and the squirrel looked down at her with half a pear in its hands and no shame in it at all.

"Twelve," she said. "That's twelve."

Emeric had come down with her because she had asked him to. The ache had been gone a week. He stood beside her on the grass with his sense out over the inner ward, the gate man in his lodge, Armand's note in the dome beside the tree as it was every evening now; and the pinprick in the pear, quick and warm and busy, which he had known since April. He had had it in his hands and laid the wall-top road on a table for it. He had its note.

"Watch," he said.

"What are you going to do?"

"Ask it something it can't refuse."

He opened the door to it. Not a read, not a hand on it; only the hair's width, from the grass, as he would have opened it to Ward on the wall, and down it, faint as a pattern on a window, he laid a picture: a hazelnut. One. Brown and ripe and whole, with its little rough cap still on it, sitting in the crook of the pear's farthest branch, out over the ward wall, where nothing at all was sitting.

The squirrel stopped eating.

It looked along the branch a long time, its tail going in small jerks over its back. Then it dropped the pear, which fell between Ferra's feet, and went along the branch in three long flat runs and out on to the thin end, which bowed under it, and put both its hands down into the crook.

There was nothing in the crook.

The squirrel took its hands out and looked at them. It looked into the crook again. It turned round on the swinging end of the branch and looked at the trunk, and the grass, and the two people on the grass; and then it sat up with its tail straight behind it, and stared at Emeric.

He had never been looked at like that by anything with fur. It was the look Ferra gave a column that had been added up wrong by somebody who should have known better.

"It knows," said Ferra.

"It can't know. It doesn't know what I am."

"It knows there was a nut and there isn't." She was looking at the squirrel, which had not stopped looking at him. "And it knows you were standing there when there was."

The squirrel turned its back on him. It did it slowly, as it had turned its head away from a crust in April, the whole small body going round on the branch until there was nothing toward him but a tail, and sat like that, facing the wall, with great dignity, and did not move again.

Ferra laughed.

It came out of her all at once, the laugh she did not let anybody hear in the counting room; she put her hand flat on his back between his shoulder blades to hold herself up and laughed into his shoulder, and he found that he was laughing too, for the first time since the counting room, at a squirrel's back.

When she could, she took the pen out of her hair and wrote on the envelope, under the twelve pears, and turned it so he could read it in the last of the light.

1 August. Showed September a nut. There was no nut. September knows.

It did not come down the pear when he was in the ward for a week, and when it did, on the ninth, it took the crust Imani left on the kitchen step and ate it on the wall-top with its back to the keep, where he could see.
