Chapter 349 - Golem

Bram had put the head back on with eleven bolts, and he wanted it known that it was eleven.

"Twelve holes," he said. "The old makers drilled twelve. One of them's cracked through to the seat, so it gets nothing, and the other eleven carry it. If it falls off, it's not the bolts."

"Nobody's said it'll fall off," said Ward.

"You were going to."

It stood in the proving gallery on the third of September, past the bend, under four lamp stones on the ceiling and Dov's battery cart, which had been wheeled in at seven and sat humming at its heels like a dog that wanted feeding. Emeric had last seen it whole years ago, at the inmost door of a city under the Isle, a head taller than him and nearly twice as broad, faceless, its hands open at its sides. Since then it had been a shoulder on a bench and a chest block on sleepers and an arm he had ridden for four minutes before his nose itched. Now it was a figure again. The shoulder he had broken in the quarry had a band of Kaldo steel round it where the old cast had been, plain and grey against the pale dust ceramic of everything else, and the chest plate over the seat had a new piece let into it in the shape of his fist, which Bram had made a little bigger than his fist out of spite.

There were two stones in it. In August Bram had given up the two he'd been keeping for the fifth and sixth sentries without a word, which for Bram was a speech, and Emeric had written both over four nights on his stool in the Motion Shop. The first lines of each were in his hand: change nothing not signed by a listed hand, stop on a listed note, and if anything tries to cut these lines out, lock. Below them, in the old makers' blocks he had spent a winter learning to read, the routines for a knee, a hip, a foot that felt its own weight. He had read the old guard's lines four times, the ones he'd once taken for ornament, and left them out.

His mother had asked to go first.

She had asked on the last night of August, by the tree, with his father sitting on the edge of the mattress and her own hands folded on the blanket beside him, and she had asked very reasonably, the way she asked for everything, as if it were already agreed and she was only mentioning it. He had said no. She had said she had been dead for twenty-eight years and was not afraid of a statue. He had said that was exactly why. She had looked at Harrold, and Harrold had looked at the roof of the dome, and that had been the end of it, though she had told him on his way out that he was his father's son and she didn't mean the good part.

Ward had not asked to go first. Ward had come to the gallery to say Emeric shouldn't.

"You're the king," he said. He said it the way he would have said you're wet. "A king doesn't walk out on the bridge before the carpenters have jumped on it."

"The carpenters have jumped on it. Bram's been jumping on it since July."

"Bram's been jumping on the frame. Nobody's been inside it." Ward looked at the faceless head, which was a foot above his own. "I'm on the list. Your list. The six can ride anything you can. Let me take it to the bend and back, and if it goes wrong it goes wrong on a man you can spare."

"I can't spare you."

"You can spare me better than you can spare you. Ask Ferra. She'll have a column."

Ferra, on the bench by the wall with her book open on her knee, did not look up. "I do have a column," she said. "He's right."

"Thank you."

"I said he's right. I didn't say I'd stop you."

Emeric looked at the figure with its eleven bolts and its steel shoulder. He had argued this with himself already, on the stool, on the fourth night, and the argument had not got better for being had twice.

"If something goes wrong in there, it goes wrong in the stone," he said. "In lines I wrote. If I'm in it, I'm reading them from inside while it happens. If you're in it, you're reading my hand and guessing." He turned to Ward. "That's the reason. It's not a good enough one for you, and I know that."

Ward thought about it for long enough that Bram began to tap a spanner against his own boot.

"No," Ward said. "It isn't." He went and stood by Dov's cart with his hand on the dead-zone stone in his belt, where he'd have stood anyway. "Go on, then."

Tess had a stool, and her bench book, and a tin cup of tea on the floor by her foot, a long way from the golem. She saw Emeric see the cup.

"I moved it," she said. "I've lost one cup to that thing. I'm not losing two."



Imani took his pulse before he went in, which she did now before anything, and wrote it on the back of Ferra's page.

"You'll be sitting on this stool," she said, "for however long you're in there, and I'll be sitting next to you. If your breathing goes wrong I pull you out."

"You can't pull me out."

"I'll shout very close to your ear. It's never failed."

He sat on the stool by the gallery wall, three paces from the golem's left foot, and put his hand flat on the plate of its thigh, and went down.

His room was where it always was. The door stood open. He found the golem's head stone through his own the way he'd found it in the winter, through the arm, a second door in a wall he'd thought was solid, and he went through it and stood in the head seat, and then he let go of the thigh plate, outside, with his real hand, and held the door by the hair-width only.

It came up round him all at once.

In the winter it had been an arm. He'd known the arm was a body by the end of the four minutes, from the way the joints reported, and from the itch. This was all of it. There was a weight standing on two feet, and the weight was his, all of it, more than a ton of it, pressed down through two ankles that each felt the load arrive and said so, quietly, the way a man says yes under his breath when he's carrying something. There was a back that knew it was straight. There were hands at the ends of arms, open, that knew the air was there and nothing else. He had no eyes. He'd known he wouldn't, and still went looking for them, and found instead what the head stone had in place of them: a sense of every living note in the gallery, near and bright, and of everything dead as shape. The gallery was a long tube of shape. The battery cart was a box of shape with Dov's note beside it, and Ward's, steady, and Bram's, which was cross. Tess's cup was a small round shape on the floor. Ferra and Imani were two notes very close together on his left, beside a third, slack and a little foolish, sitting on a stool.

That was him.

He moved the left foot.

The old makers' muscles were all stone, rods along the thigh and stacks of thin plate at the calf that swelled and shrank when they were told, and nothing under them to take a share. They did what he meant. They did all of it at once. The foot came up the height of a stair when he'd meant an inch, and came down a yard ahead, and the gallery floor took it with a sound like a dropped flagstone. Somebody said something sharp. The weight went forward over the foot and the knee reported it and the hip reported it and he was, for the length of a breath, a ton of somebody else's stone deciding whether to fall over.

It didn't. The chest stone had balance in it, written by people who had walked these things before anyone he knew was born, and it caught him. He stood. He made the next step smaller. He made the one after that smaller again, and it came out as a shuffle, and the gallery floor took that with a scrape.

By the bend it was walking.

It walked the way a man walks on ice in borrowed boots, every step decided twice. He turned at the bend in three tries and a long grinding pivot that Bram would hear about from the floor, and came back toward the lamps. Ward's note had moved so that the cart was between Ward and the golem. Tess's had not moved at all.

He stopped two paces from Tess and bent down, slowly, the back reporting every inch of it, and put out the right hand.

"Don't," said Tess.

He picked up the cup.

It was the hardest thing he'd done in either life with a hand. The fingers were coarse stone in coarse joints, and they knew how to be open and how to be shut and very little about the long country in between. He put them round the cup as if it were a bird. The cup was a round shape with a warmer shape in it, and he could feel it through the stone, the slight give of the tin, and he stopped closing the hand at the place where the tin began to talk back.

He lifted it a foot off the floor and held it there. Tea slopped once over the rim and then lay still.

"Put it down," said Tess. "Put it down. Put it down."

He put it down. It was dented on one side where his smallest finger had been, a little dent, the size of a thumbnail.

Tess looked at it for some time.

"That's better than last time," she said, and wrote something in her book, and did not drink the tea.



He should have come out then. He knew it while he was deciding not to.

The pull was his own. That much the winter had settled: the golem's stones were the golem's, and the things that were his stayed his and went with him, the way a man takes his own hands into a borrowed coat. He turned the faceless head toward the two notes beside the stool, which were bent over the third, the slack one, and talking about it.

"His mouth's open," Imani was saying.

"It's always open a little when he's asleep."

"Not like that. That's open like a fish."

He took hold of them both by their weight, very gently, and pulled.

It should have been the gentlest pull he had, the coat-off-a-peg one. It went into them and stopped, the way it had stopped the first time he'd tried it on Imani at her desk, because the two of them were heavy in the pull now, heavier every month, and the pull met them as it would have met two women made of something denser than women. He leaned on it. It was like leaning on a door with people behind it who were thinking about whether to open it.

They looked round.

A faceless stone figure a head taller than Ward stood two paces off with its hands open, and they could feel exactly who was in it, because it was him and they always could.

"Is that you?" said Ferra.

He couldn't answer. It had no mouth. He pulled a little harder.

Imani planted her feet and folded her arms and let him pull, and didn't move an inch, and her face did something that was trying very hard to be stern and wasn't getting there.

"You could ask," she said.

He didn't know how to ask. He bowed instead. It was a bad bow. The back reported it in some detail and the head came down a long way and nearly kept going, and the chest stone caught it, and Bram said a word he didn't use in front of Tess.

Ferra put her book down on the stool next to his own knee.

"Well," she said, to Imani, "he did bow."

And they came. Not because he'd pulled them; he could feel that they could have stood there all morning. They let the pull have them and walked into it, the last two paces, one on each side, until they stood against the chest plate with Bram's fist-shaped patch between them, and he put the two great arms round them and did not close them. He didn't trust the hands. He held them open behind the two women's backs, an inch off, the stone fingers spread, and they knew he was doing it and why, and Imani leaned back against nothing to see if he'd catch her, and he caught her with the pull and not the hand.

"If those brakes go," said Ward, from behind the cart, "they'll bury the three of you in one box."

"Not in my gallery," said Bram. "Not in my gallery, not on my brakes, I'll not have it said."

Ferra had her cheek against the dust ceramic. "It's warm," she said, surprised.

"The joints run warm," said Tess, writing. "Who's got his body?"

They all looked at the stool.

His body had leaned. With nobody beside it, and its mouth open, it had gone slowly over sideways from the hip, as a sack of meal goes over when you take the next sack away, and Dov had got to it from the cart just in time and was holding it up by one shoulder against the gallery wall with an expression of great patience.

"I've got it," said Dov.

Emeric came out.

The hitch was worse than the arm's had been, three breaths in which he was in both and neither, a ton of stone and a man on a stool, and then the stool won and he was looking at the gallery ceiling with Dov's hand on his shoulder and the lamps much too bright. His own body was very light and very small and smelt of the gallery. His jaw ached from hanging open. Across the floor the golem stood where he had left it, braked, perfectly still, its arms still open round the place where two women had been and now weren't, because they had come across to him.

Imani took his pulse again. It was higher than the first time. She wrote it down under the first without comment, and then looked at it, and then at him.

"That's not the ride," she said.

"It might be the ride."

"It isn't."

Tess had come over with her book. She didn't look at any of the three of them. She had the look she wore when people were being foolish near her bench and she'd decided not to see it, and she held the book where he could read the page.

3 Sept. Full body, E. riding by the door, about 14 minutes. Walks. Turns badly. Coarse in the hand (cup). Stone muscle all or nothing. Rider's own casting works from inside (seen). Body by the wall alive throughout. And under it, underlined: So this proves nothing about the other case.

He knew which case she meant. They had asked it in the winter, the three of them, with the arm on the sleepers: what happens to a rider whose body dies while he's out. He'd been in the golem for fourteen minutes with his heart going on the stool the whole time, Imani's fingers on it. It answered nothing.

"No," he said. "It doesn't."

"Good," said Tess. "I wanted it written."



They talked about it in the dark, which was where Ferra and Imani talked about most things they didn't want written down.

He was nearly asleep. He'd been nearly asleep since nine, because the ride had taken more out of him than the walk home admitted, and they were on either side of him in the knot they made every night now, Imani with her back to his chest and Ferra behind him with her knees in the backs of his, which left him the middle and no say in it.

"Did you mind?" said Imani, over him.

"Mind what?"

"Being pulled across a room by a statue."

"It wasn't across a room. It was two paces. We walked most of it." Ferra thought. "No. Did you?"

"No." Imani shifted. "That's what's odd. I'd mind if it were anyone. I'd mind if it were Ward. I'd break Ward's statue."

"Ward wouldn't."

"I know Ward wouldn't. I'm saying if." She was quiet. "And I didn't mind you standing there either. In front of it. With your cheek on it."

"Why would you mind that?"

"I don't know. I'm saying I didn't." Imani rolled a little so her voice came from further over his shoulder. "Is that him? Or is it us?"

Ferra took a long breath through her nose, the one she took before she started a column she knew would run long.

"We've slept like this," she said, "for a year. More. Every night. I know when you're cold before you do. I know which foot you put out of the blankets." She considered it. "It's possible I've developed a slight fondness for women."

"For women."

"For one woman. Somewhat."

"Somewhat."

"I'm being careful. It's dark and I haven't checked my figures."

Imani laughed. It started small and went on, and she put her face into the pillow to stop it, which shook the bed, which shook him.

"I think I've got one too," she said, into the pillow. "Somewhat. For you."

"Well."

"Well."

"It's probably him," said Ferra, after a while. "It's probably all just him, and we're standing near each other when it happens."

"It's probably him."

"Should I write it down?"

"Don't you dare write it down."

"I wasn't going to write it down."

He lay between them with his eyes shut and his breathing kept slow and even, and did not move, and did not smile, and knew perfectly well that neither of them believed for one second that he was asleep.

"He's listening," said Imani.

"He's always listening. It's his worst quality."

"After the coffee."

"After the coffee," Ferra agreed, and put her cold foot on his ankle, deliberately, and kept it there.



They brought it to the dome on the tenth on the crawler's flatbed, under a sheet, at six in the morning, because Saban said a sheeted statue going through the town at six was a curiosity and the same thing at noon was a story.

Amara watched them walk it in.

Her son did it from a stool by the door with his hand on its leg, then without, and it came up the gravel and in under the glass one great careful step at a time, and Bram walked backward in front of it the whole way with his hands out as if he could catch a ton of stone if it fell, and Emeric's two women walked on either side of the stool carrying it between them each time he moved, which nobody had asked them to do. It stopped by the tree. The braked joints went quiet with a series of small clicks, like a house settling at night.

She had been looking forward to this, and she had told nobody, because it was not the kind of thing a woman of her age said. She had been looking forward to it the way she'd looked forward to her first ball.

Harry was on the mattress's edge by her own body, where he'd been since half past five. Since the summer, since his own long sleep, he could see her without Emeric's help, and the first week he'd kept looking away and back, quickly, like a boy with a sweet he hadn't eaten yet. He'd stopped that. Now he just looked.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I know."

"It's ugly."

"It's not ugly. It's old. You're old." She looked at him. "You were. You've got young again and it's gone to your head."

Emeric stood up from his stool and came to her.

"It'll come up all at once," he said. "The weight. Don't fight it. It's very heavy, and it'll feel like it's you, and the joints talk. Don't answer them. Make the steps small, smaller than you mean."

"Did you?"

"No."

"Then I won't either," said Amara, and went into it.

It was the weight.

She had not had weight in twenty-eight years. In the stone she'd had nothing, not even dark; by the tree she'd had the dome's air to stand in and that was all, a shape of herself held up by mana the way a leaf is held up by a wind that never drops. She'd forgotten. She'd thought she remembered, and she hadn't. It came down through her like water poured down a well, and she stood on two great feet on the floor of the dome and the floor pressed back, and she was so heavy, and the heaviness was so much like being somewhere that she would have wept, if the thing had had anywhere to weep from.

It had no face. She knew where the faces were anyway. Harry's note was the one she'd have found in a crowd of ten thousand; she found it in the dark of the head as if somebody had held a lamp up beside it.

She took a step toward him. It was too big. Emeric had told her it would be.

The second one was better.

She stood over Harry. He got up off the mattress, and had to look up to where her head would have been, which he had never in his life had to do. She put out her right hand.

The hand was a great coarse stone thing, and she knew how to open it and how to close it and that was all. She knew, from her son's face across the dome, that he'd taken half an hour over a cup. Harry held out his own hand to meet it. He held it out palm up, the way he had at a hundred doorways, and it was the hand she'd held on the road, and she began to close the stone fingers round it, and felt the place where they'd stop if she told them to, and was not sure they would.

She stopped.

She left it open. She turned it over instead, slowly, the joint grinding at the wrist, and put the back of one knuckle against the back of his hand, very lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child to see if it was warm. It was the only part she trusted.

"Well," said Harry. His voice had gone. "That's a start."

"It's a rock."

"It's a start."

She took the hand away before she could do anything stupid with it.

"Now outside," she said.



The dome's door was wide enough. Bram had measured it twice in August and it cleared her shoulders by a hand. She went through it sideways anyway, because she didn't trust the shoulders either, and came out onto the gravel in the September morning, and the cold came up off the moat and she felt it on the stone, faintly, like a draught on a cheek.

Behind her, inside the glass, the air was thick and gold and green. Out here it was thin.

She'd known the line was there. Every evening for a month she had walked to it inside the dome and stood at the glass with her own projected hand flat on nothing, a hand's width from the pane, and gone no further, because a step past was a step into ground too thin to hold her up and she'd be back by the tree with her teeth aching. Now there was a canister in the chest of this thing with mana in it, and every one of its joints drank from that and from the ground under it, and she rode in the middle of it like a woman in a carriage with the windows up.

She went past the line. It held.

"Count," she said through the stone, to nobody in particular. Only the delvers could hear her out here. Emeric, walking beside her on the gravel at the distance of a man beside a horse he didn't know, had already begun, under his breath, for her.

"Three. Four."

Ferra was by the door with her book. Imani was by the door with nothing, her hands at her sides, watching the joints. Ward stood on the bridge's end with his hand in his belt. Harry had come out and stood on the step of the dome and did not come any further. She thought he had been told not to, and she thought he was obeying it about as well as he'd obeyed anything.

"Nine. Ten."

It was getting thinner.

She'd thought the canister would carry her, and it was carrying her, but it was carrying everything else too. Every step the great stone muscles of the thighs swelled and shrank, rods and plates, all at once, all of them, and every swell was paid for, and she could feel it being paid, out of the canister and out of the ground and out of the very air she was standing in, as though she'd come out to walk in a cart that ate the road. In the dome there had been so much that the eating didn't show. Here it showed. The gold went out of the air round her a little with each step, like the colour out of a cloth in the wash.

"Fourteen."

The left knee spoke. It had been speaking all along, low; now it ground, a long dry turning, an old joint that had stood at a door for a thousand years and had not been told it would ever have to walk on gravel, and she felt it in her own knee, which she didn't have. She didn't answer it. She made the step smaller.

"Seventeen. Mother."

"I heard you."

"Eighteen."

The thigh rods came on all at once and off all at once and didn't come on again when she asked, and the chest stone caught the weight and held it, and she was standing on one leg and a half on the gravel with the moat in front of her and nothing under her. The air was water now, cold water, a long way down. It hurt. It hurt in the places she didn't have, which was all of them.

"Nineteen. Twenty."

She let go.

She didn't decide to. It went, the way a cup goes when the fingers forget it, and she went with it, back up the gravel and in through the glass all in one long sliding instant, and came down by the tree on her hands and knees on the dome's floor, in no body at all, with her teeth aching and her whole self ringing like a bell somebody had struck and then put a hand on.

She stayed like that for a while. The tree was over her. Her own body lay on the mattress beside her with its hands folded, breathing.

When she looked up through the glass the golem was standing on the gravel exactly where she'd left it, twenty paces past the line, braked, rigid, mid-stride, with its left knee bent and its right foot not quite down, like a man stopped in a children's game. It had not fallen. Bram was walking round it. Ward had come off the bridge and stood looking up at the faceless head with his arms folded, as if he expected it to apologise.

Harry was coming in. So was her son. Harry got there first, and knelt on the dome's floor beside her, and couldn't touch her, and didn't try.

"Are you hurt?"

"Yes," said Amara. "Not much. It's going off." She looked past him at Emeric, who had stopped in the doorway with Ferra just behind him and that look on his face, the one he'd had as a small boy with something of hers in pieces on the floor, that said he'd already worked out what had gone wrong and was only waiting to be allowed to say it.

She got up. Projected, it was easy. It had always been easy, and now she resented it a little.

"That," she said, "was somebody else's."

"I know."

"It was made for somebody else. A big somebody, who didn't hold hands." She brushed her skirt, from habit. There was nothing on it. "Make me one that fits."



He wrote it out that night at the long table, the canister and the drinking joints and the twenty paces, and in the middle stopped with the pen up.

She couldn't leave the dense ground. Out past the last islands, in the open ocean Zale had never crossed, there was a line that the world's people couldn't go into, because it was too dense, and Kaldo had spent fourteen years making itself a little more like the far side of it; and her wall and the world's were the same line, looked at from the two ends.

He wrote that down too, small, on the inside of the cover, where nobody but Ferra read.



The six tried it on the thirteenth, because Saban asked.

They'd learned it in the sleep, all of them, under the stones: how to stand up out of the body and go about in the mana like a draught through a house. None of them had done much with it since. Ward said it made him feel he'd left the stove on. Bram said a man who wanted to go somewhere could walk. Armand had done it twice and called it the most interesting thing he'd learned since he was seventy and the least useful.

Saban had done it every night for a week.

"I want to know where it stops," he said, "before somebody else finds out for me."

So they sat round the long table in the castle hall with the doors shut and the lamps low, the six and Emeric, and went.

It was easy in the castle. It was easy in the town. Emeric went with them because he could, and because he wanted to see what Saban did with it, and what Saban did with it, first, was go straight through the wall of the hall into the records office next door and read the open ledger on the clerk's desk upside down.

"Ferra," he said, from the records office. They could hear him. In the mana, anybody who could hear could hear. "You've got the coal bill wrong by a shilling."

"I have not."

"The ninth. You carried a seven."

There was a short silence while Ferra went and looked.

"Get out of my ledger," she said.

He didn't. He went on, down through the floor and out under the inner ward, past the old pear, where September was asleep in the trunk and noticed nothing, which disappointed Imani; down the hill; into the Motion Shop, where Tess was working late with her back to the door, and they stood round her in the warm air watching her solder, and she didn't know they were there, and Bram said it was the worst thing he'd ever done and wanted to go back.

"You can go into any room in Kaldo," said Saban, standing in the middle of the Motion Shop without a body. "Any room. Through any door. Hear anything. I could stand in Vantic's office while she's writing to her house and read it over her shoulder."

"Don't," said Ferra.

"I won't. I'm saying I could." He turned toward the west. "Now the other end."

They went out along the Milford road in the dark, seven of them, a little above the ditches. It was thinner there. Kaldo had stones in its walls and its water and its people, fourteen years of them, and past the last houses the stones stopped and the fields went on without them, and the air began to give, the way it had given under the golem's feet.

Ward went first, because he always did. He went three yards past the last mile post and came back so fast they didn't see him go, and was sitting up straight in his chair in the castle hall with his hand flat on the table, and swearing, quietly and with care.

Saban went to the post and stood at it, the way Amara had stood at the glass, and put out a hand that wasn't there, and felt the line under it.

"Well," he said, and came back on his own.

Round the table they opened their eyes, one after another. Armand had his fingertips pressed together. Bram had got up and was walking about the hall touching things to see if they were solid. Imani was looking at Ward's hand, which he let her.

"Inside Kaldo," said Saban, "it's a spy's dream. I could know everything said in this town by morning, and nobody would ever see me do it." He folded his own hands. "And outside the last mile post it's nothing. I can't hear one word in Ostrel. I can't hear one word in Milford." He looked round the table. "I wanted to know. Now I know. Nobody else is ever going to be told."

"Amara's was the dome," said Imani.

"Ours is the town. Same wall. Farther out." Armand pursed his lips. "She's riding on what the tree gives her, and we're riding on what we carry and what the streets carry. Something like that. I'd not put it in a book."

"You'd put it in a book by Thursday," said Bram, from across the hall.

"By Wednesday," said Armand. "But I'd not like it."

Emeric sat with his hands on the table.

He had been thinking it since the gravel: a body that carried its own ground, so she rode inside it like a lamp carried out into the dark with its own glass round it. He had nearly said it at the long table twice, and both times looked at his mother's twenty paces in Ferra's book and kept quiet, because it was his mother, and he didn't trust a thought that led exactly where he wanted to go.

He didn't say it now either.



Tess said it, the next afternoon, without being asked, over a drive with its cover off.

The Motion Shop smelt of hot oil and the coffee Tess made, which was good, which he'd stopped mentioning. Dov Kettle was at the far bench grinding a disc and swearing at it in a steady undertone, as he did. Wren had gone home. Tess had Ferra's copy of the golem's twenty paces open on her bench under a weight, and had read it, he could see, more than once, because she'd written on it.

"It's all stone," she said.

"The golem?"

"Every muscle in it. There's nothing under the stones. No fiber, no drive, nothing." She tapped the page. "So every step it takes, it pays. Mana for every inch. She's riding in it, and she's riding on the same mana it's eating to walk. Of course it ran out. It's like trying to read by a lamp that's also cooking your dinner."

He waited.

"So you put motors under it," said Tess. "Drives at the big joints, like the sentries. Fiber in the hands. Then walking's free, or near enough, the battery does it, and the stones only push when she wants more than a woman's strength. And then all of the canister's hers. She takes it with her." She looked at him. "And you make it her size. Not that. Not a door guard. Her height. Her hands."

"Her size," he said.

"She said one that fits." Tess turned the drive over in her hands without looking at it. "When I woke up from the stone I couldn't stand. You know that. Weeks. I'd tell my leg to go and it would go somewhere, and it was mine, and it was wrong, and the worst part wasn't that it didn't work. The worst part was that it nearly did." She put the drive down. "Something that nearly fits is worse than nothing. You keep reaching for the bit that isn't there. That golem is never going to fit her. Don't make her ride it again."

He had had it for four days. She'd had it since dinner.

"Yes," he said.

"Yes what?"

"Yes, that's what we'll build."

Tess looked at him with some suspicion, as if she thought he'd been about to say it and was letting her have it, and he didn't tell her. She decided not to mind, and pulled a clean sheet toward her across the bench.

"It'll want a name," she said. "On the drawings. Bram won't sign a drawing that says her."

"A humanoid."

"A what?"

"A humanoid. A thing in a person's shape."

Tess wrote it at the top of the sheet in her square capitals, and looked at it, and said it under her breath twice, the way she tried out a new word for a part to see if it would wear.

"Fine," she said. "Bram'll call it the body anyway."



Bram called it the body, and argued about it for a week.

He argued about the frame, which had to match a woman lying on a mattress under a blanket by the tree.

"I'm not measuring the queen," he said. "I'm a smith."

"She's not been the queen for twenty-eight years," said Imani.

"I'm still not measuring her."

So Imani measured her, on the sixteenth, with a tailor's tape Abloh lent her and Ferra writing it down, the long bones first and then the joints, wrist to elbow, shoulder to shoulder, the hand spread and the hand closed, while Amara stood at the head of the mattress in the air with her arms folded and watched herself being measured, and corrected Imani twice.

"The left's longer," she said. "Half an inch. My dressmaker always said so."

Imani checked. It was a quarter.

"Your dressmaker flattered you," she said.

"She was paid to."

The figures went down the hill in Ferra's hand to the Motion Shop and were pinned on the wall by Bram's drawing board, and the body began.

It began on paper, with arguments. Bram drew the frame in Kaldo steel, hollow in the long bones to save weight, the ribs a cage round a double-cased canister like the sentries carried, filled through a glass port under where a collarbone would be. Tess drew the joints three times and threw two away. The hips, knees, shoulders and elbows she gave cycloidal drives: Dov's discs with the lobed rims that rolled round inside a ring of pins, so a small fast motor turned a joint slow and very strong. Dov cut them two to a drive, set half a turn apart so they didn't shake each other off the bench, because the first one he'd ever cut had walked itself onto the floor and he wasn't going to be told about it again. Ottoline took the bearings, every one, from the hip to the last knuckle of the smallest finger, and sat at the end of the bench with her loupe in her eye, grinding the races true and speaking to nobody, which was how she was happy.

The hands were Tess's other drawing: the artificial muscle she and Bram had drawn for Abel's hand years before, hair-fine sealed tubes of a fluid that carried no current, with a charge along them that dragged the fluid one way and shortened one side as the other let go. One strand did nothing. A hand needed thirty bundles, some as thin as a bootlace, and Tess laid them down the forearm the way the real ones ran, off a wired skeleton of a hand Imani had lent her from the ward.

"The stones go on top," said Tess. "At every joint. For when she wants to lift a cart. The rest of the time she's running on the battery like the rest of us."

"And in a dead zone?" said Emeric.

"In a dead zone the stones stop, and she slows down, and the brakes catch anything the drives can't. She doesn't fall. She just has to walk home like a woman." Tess considered it. "Very like a woman. Slowly, complaining."

Abloh came up on the eighteenth with a roll of something under his arm and an expression of enormous reluctance, because he'd been told it was a body and had decided, on the walk up, that he was being asked to make a doll.

"I don't make dolls," he said.

"It's a covering," said Emeric.

"For a doll."

"For a woman who's going to walk about the town in it."

Abloh unrolled it on the end of Tess's bench: the close black wool he'd cut for the stealth suits, over its iron mesh, and beside it a pale knit that gave under the thumb and came back, which he'd been working on for the mill for a year and told nobody about because it wasn't finished.

"That," he said, "on the hands and the face. It's not skin. Don't tell her it's skin. It's the nearest I've got." He looked at the drawing on the wall, at the figures pinned beside it in Ferra's hand. "And she'll have a face. I'm not covering a thing with no face. I'll want a picture."

"Harrold has a picture," said Emeric. "He has a lot of pictures."

"Then he can bring me the good one," said Abloh, "not the one he likes."

Emeric wrote the first lines on the twentieth.

He wrote them on paper, because there were no stones for it yet, at the top of a clean sheet on the wall above Tess's bench, in his own hand, and the sheet stayed there with nothing else on it for two days while the rest of the body went on below it. Change nothing not signed by a listed hand. Stop on a listed note. Anything that cuts these lines, lock. Tess read it every morning when she came in and every evening when she went home, and on the second evening she wrote a fourth line under his, in her own hand, smaller.

Unless she asks.

He looked at it for a long time.

"She'll want the stop," said Tess. "She'll want to be able to stop it herself. From inside. Her own note. Not just yours and the six." She didn't look up from the drive. "I would."

He crossed out nothing. He added her line to the list in the morning, and that was the fourth of the first lines, and it stayed.



The stones were the one thing nobody in the Motion Shop could give it.

He'd thought they could. He'd thought, on the night of the twenty paces, that the tree's crop was the tree's and he'd write two off it for the body the way he'd written two for the sentries, and say so at the long table, and that would be that. He had got as far as saying so at the long table.

"No," said Ferra.

He'd looked at her.

"It's a body," she said. "For a person. That's not a sentry's stone. That's a person's stone. That's the board."

"The board sits once a fortnight and takes three weeks over a dyer's hip."

"The board took one sitting over the dyer's hip, and it took that sitting because Master Aubin wanted the hip explained to him twice." She didn't raise her voice. "You wrote the charter. You put the stones clause in it. You said, in my hearing, at that table, that nobody gets a stone because they're somebody's son." She turned a page. "She's somebody's mother."

Imani had said nothing at all, which was how he'd known Ferra had the table, and Armand had said Quite into his cup, which was how he'd known it was over.

He had been wrong, and had known it a quarter of a mile before he admitted it to the horse. He had been about to take two stones for his mother out of a crop every family in Kaldo had a name in the book against, because she was his mother and he was king and nobody would have stopped him.

Ferra had.

So it went through the book.

The Stone Board sat on the twenty-second in the records hall, with the doors open on the square as the charter said, and a dozen people on the benches at the back who came every sitting because it was free and better than the theatre, and the clerk with the book. Master Aubin had the chair. Prudence Fairley sat on his left in her good brown with her hands in her lap, and the holdings steward on his right, and Armand at the end. Imani's chair was empty. She'd entered the case, so she sat on the front bench with the patients' folk, with a folder on her knee, and didn't sit at the table.

It was the fourth case of the afternoon, after a ropewalk foreman's lungs, a Wayford girl's heart, and a farmer who asked because his father had had one and was refused in nine words.

Reading the cases aloud had become Prudence's job because she had taken it. If the book was open, she'd said, it ought to be heard as well as read, since half the benches couldn't, and nobody had stopped her since.

She read Imani's case as it was written.

"Entered by the ward. Two stones. Not for eating." She looked up. "That's a first." She went back to the sheet. "For a body. A made body, of steel and drives and fiber, with a canister, for a patient of the ward whose own body cannot walk and will not be made to walk. Healing case. The patient is known to the board." She put the sheet down. "No name."

"No name," said Imani, from the bench.

"The book's open, healer. That's the point of it. We wrote it on the door."

"I know." Imani stood up. "The patient is known to the four of you. I told you on the twentieth, in the side room, with the clerk there. Her name goes in the book on the day she can sign it herself." She looked at the benches, and at the open doors, and the square beyond them with the tram going past. "With her own hand. In the made one. That's what the stones are for."

Prudence looked at her for some time.

"Then put that in the book," she said to the clerk. "Word for word. Name to be entered in the patient's own hand, when she has one." She waited while he wrote it, and leaned over and read it, and nodded. "Now. It's not a person, this made body?"

"It's not," said Imani. "She is. It's what she'll walk in."

"Does it eat?"

"No."

"Then it's cheaper than my husband," said Prudence, and the benches laughed, and the holdings steward put his hand over his mouth. Master Aubin did not laugh, because he never laughed in the chair, but he wrote something in the margin of his own copy that he didn't show anybody.

Armand asked one question. He asked whether the stones, if the made body were ever unmade, came back to the board, and Imani said they did, and Armand said he'd like that in the book as well, and it went in the book as well.

Imani went and stood in the doorway with her arms folded while the four decided, the way Prudence had once done for the carter with the bad heart, and the clerk wrote that she had.

They took eleven minutes. Emeric, on the back bench among the people who came because it was free, counted them on the clock over the door, and for eleven minutes he did not look at anybody and nobody looked at him, and he minded much less than he'd expected, sitting there, waiting, like the ropewalk foreman's wife, for four people to tell him yes or no about somebody he loved.

Granted, Master Aubin wrote. Healing first. Two stones, returned to the board if the body is unmade. Name to follow in the patient's hand. And then, because Prudence leaned over and said something: She can't walk without them. That's the reason.

Prudence read it out to the square.



He told his mother that evening, by the tree, with his father on the mattress's edge and Ferra at the east bed with her readings.

"They asked if you eat," he said.

"Do I?"

"Prudence Fairley said that made you cheaper than her husband."

Amara laughed, the laugh that was exactly as he'd remembered it, and Harrold, who had heard it every day since the spring and still looked up every time like a man hearing his name across a street, looked up.

"I'd like to meet Prudence Fairley," she said.

"She'd like to meet you. She wants your name in her book."

"She'll have it." His mother looked down at her own hands, folded on the blanket, and at the ones she was standing in, which were the same hands and weren't. "Tell the girl with the drives she can have her line. The stop. I'd want it."

"She thought you would."

"She's right." Amara looked across the dome to the east bed, where Ferra had stopped writing and was listening with her pen up, not hiding it. "And your Ferra keeps turning back to a page in October and turning away again. Whatever it is, she's been writing soil readings for an hour instead."

Ferra went faintly pink and turned back a great many pages, to a date in October ringed twice, and the column under it headed Not yet decided, which was shorter than it had been in August and still not short. Doran, ask was still in it. So was Rowena, ask.

"Tomorrow," Ferra said.

"You said that last week," said Imani, who had come in behind them with two cups from Tamsy's.

"I meant this tomorrow."

In the Motion Shop that night the body's first long bone, a hollow steel thigh, lay on Bram's bench under the lamp, with Tess's hip pinned above it and above that the sheet of first lines, four of them now, in two hands. Out in the yard of the long shop, where Ward had ridden it back the day after the gravel, badly and saying so the whole way, the golem stood under its sheet, braked and faceless, its hands open at its sides the way they'd been at the door under the Isle, waiting for somebody who had never come back for it.
