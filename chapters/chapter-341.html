Chapter 341 - Free Wheel

Tess had the News before the town did, because Wren brought it.

She was already up. She had been up since three, at the kitchen table in the forge house with a lamp and the Seam Table's notebook, writing the same line about the dew seam three ways and liking none of them. When the knock came at a quarter past five she knew it was Wren, because nobody else in Kaldo knocked like somebody trying to get in before the door had finished making up its mind.

"It's out," said Wren. She had run. She was seventeen and had run everywhere since she was four, and she had the paper flat against her chest under her coat to keep the damp off it, and the ink had come off on her shirt anyway in a neat backward column. "Hobb gave me one off the stack. He said if I touched the others he'd set me in type and print me."

The Kaldo News, four pages, a copper. Wren turned past the trains and the price of flour without reading either.

The engraving took up the top half of the second page. The printing works' engraver had cut it from Tess's own side drawing, the one she had done for the jig: the bicycle side on, two wheels of a size, the diamond of the frame between them, every spoke ruled in.

"The chain's on the wrong side," said Tess.

Wren leaned over it. "It's a picture."

"It's a picture of a bicycle with the chain on the left. He's drawn it from the other side and not turned it round."

"Nobody's going to build one off it, Tess."

"There's always somebody," said Tess.

Under the engraving was the price, in figures big enough to be argued with, and under the price was the box.

Ferra had written it on the first of April at the long table, on one side of a card, and handed it to her clerk with a line on the back: When the Avenue's surfaced. Not before. The roller had made its last pass on the Gate Avenue at dusk two days ago.

THE SAFETY BICYCLE. Built at the vehicle works, Kaldo. Three frame sizes and a child's. Each sold with a lock and its own numbered key. At the works' shop on the Gate Avenue from seven.

Outright, at the price above. Or by the week, on a card at the Works Office: a fifth down, the rest over forty weeks, stamped. It is yours on the last stamp. Miss three weeks running and it goes back to the works, and what you paid was rent. One to a house until the list is through. Not on the quay, the tram rails or the records hall steps.

And under that, smaller: Tricycles for those who would rather have three wheels. Ask.

"One to a house," said Wren. "That's ours. Mam's, I mean, but she said I could have the card if I paid the stamps." She was doing it on her fingers. "Forty weeks. I'll be eighteen." She stopped. "Da's going to be furious he slept through it."

"You've ridden the works' ones two years. You don't need to buy one."

"Those are the works'," said Wren, with enormous patience. "I want one with a key."

Out of the window, over the forge house roofs, the dome on the hill still had its faint violet in it, the way it had every night since the fourth. Tess looked at it the way she looked at a gauge across a room, and folded the paper in four, and put it in her apron.

"Go and stand in the queue, then. I'll come down at six."

"There won't be a queue at six."

"There'll be a queue at six," said Tess.



There was a queue at a quarter to six. By the time Tess came down the Gate Avenue in her chair it went from the shop door past the tram stop and round the corner toward the gate arch, and people were still joining the end of it at a trot.

She came down with the wheels down and the stones lightening her, on the motor, which was the rule in the streets, and she noticed that she could hear the motor. On the old setts she never had. Now the chair ran on the new black like a boat on a still morning, and she could hear its whine, and the hiss of the tyres, and somebody's kettle in a window overhead.

The asphalt had come up from the Pitch Lake over the winter in barrels, and the road crews had cooked it in kettles at the works' end of the Avenue all through March: the pitch melted soft, stirred hot into crushed stone and sand, barrowed out steaming, raked, and rolled while it was still too hot to stand on. The Avenue had smelled for a month like a ship's bottom being caulked. It would go soft in July; Tess had told the road foreman so, and he had said he knew.

The ring path had been done the same way after it, all the way round. The tram streets had a lane painted along the kerb in white, a bicycle's width, that the carters had already started calling the wrong things.

The queue was in a good mood, the way a queue is before anybody has been told no. Two men from the steelworks had come straight off the night shift in their leathers and were asleep standing up, propped against each other. A woman near the front had been knitting since four. A yellow dog of no family went up and down the whole length of it, being fed.

One of Maren's girls had come up from the coffee house under the South Gate arch with a can on a handcart and was selling coffee by the tin cup, at a price that went up every time Tess looked at the queue's tail.

"Tastes like a fence," said one of the steelworks men, waking enough to drink it.

"A burned one," said Maren's girl. "And you'll have another."

He had another.

Wren was ninth. She had a stool, and was explaining the chain to the eighth, who had not asked.

The shop was the vehicle works' front on the Gate Avenue, a long room that had sold lorry parts and lamp oil until February. The works' foreman, who had built the frame jig with Tess over a cold January, stood in the doorway and nodded at her the way he would have at anyone off the line, which she liked.

"Two hundred and forty," he said. "And thirty of the three-wheelers. That's the lot till June."

"They'll be gone by noon."

"I've been out the back and counted the queue," said the foreman. "They'll be gone by ten."

Inside, the bicycles stood in racks down both walls, black and gleaming. The drawings had been Emeric's, four sheets done last summer and left on Tess's bench before he went off to a war. Everything after that had been theirs: the steel drawn into tube at the works and bent cold in Tess's jig, the roller chain and sprockets off the Motion Shop's line, the ball bearings in their cups and cones, and the tyres, which had been the worst of it.

The first tyres were Isle-sap rubber over canvas, blown up hard with a pump, and the first one pumped hard had climbed off its rim and burst with a noise that brought the whole works into the yard. The foreman's frame crew had solved it while Tess was at the island. They ran a ring of steel wire into each edge of the tyre, so that the air pushed the wire out against the rim's lip, and the harder you pumped the tighter it held. Pneumatic tyre, Emeric's sheet said. Tess called the wire a beaded edge. The frame crew called the whole thing the fat one, and went on calling it that.

By the far door was the counting house's table, and at it, with a ledger and a ruler, sat Ferra's clerk, the same one who had read the Church's men onto Saban's wagons. Her columns were already ruled. Name. House. Frame. Outright or card. Key number. A space for the stamp. At the top she had written the date and the hour, small, at the right, where Ferra put them; and Tess looked at the hour in somebody else's hand in Ferra's place, and then at the racks.

By the near door, at a little table of her own, sat Ottoline Fesk with the locks.

There were two hundred and seventy of them on trays, steel shackle locks the size of a fist, each key on a string with a numbered paper tag. Ottoline had a jeweller's glass pushed up on her forehead. In front of her on a square of green baize lay one lock by itself with a window ground into the side of its body and glass let in, so that you could see in.

She showed it to every single customer, all morning, and did not tire of it once that Tess could see. Tess, who had heard it in March, listened every time.

Five little brass pins stood in a row of holes across the round plug of the lock, each pin cut in two, each pair pressed down by a spring. Ottoline put a wrong key in, and the pins rose to it, and under the glass you could see the breaks between the halves come up all over the place, high, low; and every one that missed the line where plug met body pinned the plug there like a peg. "Turn it," she said, and it did not turn. Then the right key. Each notch lifted its own pin just so far, all five breaks came level on the line at once, and the plug turned in her fingers as if it had never been locked.

"Pin tumbler," said Ottoline. "Five pins, eight heights each. That's a lock."

"How many keys is that?" said the knitting woman.

"Thirty-two thousand seven hundred and sixty-eight," said Wren from the door, before Ottoline could open her mouth. "Eight times itself five times."

Ottoline looked at her over the glass. "Less the ones I won't cut, because they're too easy to rake. Who's this?"

"She's ninth," said Tess.

A carter who had come in to look and not to buy, and wanted everybody to know it, leaned over the baize. "So you've all the numbers in a book. Then you can open any one you like."

"I can," said Ottoline.

He looked round at the queue to make sure it had heard.

"So can a man with a hammer and no book. It's to stop a boy with a free afternoon. It's not the end of the world." She set the cut-open lock square on its baize. "And when you lose your key, which you will, by Thursday, you'll bring me the number off your card and I'll cut you another in ten minutes, and you'll be glad I kept the book."

The carter said something about castle toys, not quite loud enough, and went out.

The first bicycle went at seven by the shop clock to the baker from the lower town, who had shut his ovens at four to queue and was still white to the elbows. He paid outright out of a cloth bag, and Ottoline made him hang the key round his neck, and he wheeled the bicycle out between the queue's two lines like a man walking a horse he was not sure of. At the door he rang the bell by accident, and jumped.

Wren had hers by twenty past. Frame two, on the card, key one hundred and four. She did not get on it. She wheeled it over to Tess's chair and stood holding it, and looked at it, and at the key, and at the bicycle again.

"Ride it, then," said Tess.

"In a minute."



The punctures started at nine, where the new black ran out at the river end of the Avenue and the old setts and their nails began.

By half past there were eleven bicycles leaning against the shop front with a flat wheel each,, and eleven owners with the look of people who had been sold something. The foreman had a tin of rubber cement and a sheet of patches and could do a patch in five minutes once he had found the hole; finding it took him twenty, ear to the tube, pumping and listening.

A boy solved it. He was eleven or so, with no bicycle and no money. The third time he took the tube out of the foreman's hands without asking, walked it to the horse trough at the corner, pushed it under a hand's length at a time, and pointed at the thread of bubbles coming up.

The foreman looked at the bubbles a while.

"There's a copper a tube in that," he said, "if you'll do it all day."

The boy did it all day. By noon he had a line of his own and a chalk mark on the trough's rim for each tube, and had begun charging two.

The foreman pulled the shutter down at a quarter to twelve and went out and told what was left of the queue, and the queue said what queues say. Ferra's clerk ruled a line under the last name and wrote the hour, then turned to a fresh page, wrote The list at the top, and took names for June. When Tess left at one it ran to four hundred and eleven, and the clerk had sent to the counting house for another pencil.



Nobody in Kaldo could ride. The works' people had ridden the works' bicycles inside the works' fence, and the campus children had theirs on the school paths; and now two hundred and seventy more had bought one, and every one of them had a family, and every family had come out on the Gate Avenue to watch, and to help, which was worse.

All afternoon the Avenue was full of people falling off very slowly. They fell sideways at a walking pace with a look of great surprise, and lay with one foot still on a pedal, and got up, and fell off the other way. Everybody had advice, and all of it was different.

Arthur had come off the wall at two, after four hours' sleep, and was holding a saddle for a woman from the cutting tables at the Clothing Works. He walked beside her with a hand on the back of it, and then ran, and said the same thing every ten yards in the same quiet voice.

"Look where you want to go. Not at the wheel. You go where you look."

"I'm looking."

"You're looking at the wheel."

"I'm looking near the wheel."

The trick of it, which nobody could tell anybody, was that you did not balance a bicycle. You steered it back under you. When it leaned you turned the bars a little into the lean, and the wheels ran back under your weight; and the faster you went, the less you had to do it, which was why every beginner went too slowly and fell. Tess had written all this on the board for the frame crew. It did not help anybody.

Past the tram stop Arthur took his hand off the saddle and did not say so. The woman rode on, wobbling, thirty yards, a good deal faster than before, and called over her shoulder to ask if he was still there; and when he said from a long way back that he wasn't, she looked round to see, and fell into a privet hedge.

Arthur ran up and got her out. "You were doing it," he said, picking leaves off her shoulder.

"I was not doing it. You were doing it."

"I was back by the tram stop."

She looked down the Avenue at the tram stop for a long time, and then got back on.

The coaster brake caught out half the town. Pedal backward and a split sleeve inside the back hub spread against the shell and stopped the wheel; there was a hand lever too, working a spoon brake on the front tyre, but nobody in a fright remembered they had hands.

The children learned in an hour, and then stood on the kerb and taught their parents, which went less well.

"Pedal, Da. Pedal. You can't fall over if you're pedalling."

"I am pedalling."

"You're not. You're thinking."

The baker from the lower town rode into the fountain at the top of the Avenue at half past three. He had been doing well. He had got all the way up without falling once, and looked up, and the fountain was in front of him, and he went where he looked. He sat in the basin with the water to his waist and the flour coming off his arms in clouds, and the yellow dog stood on the rim and barked at him, and he laughed so hard he could not get out.

Sela had said for a month that she was not falling off anything at her age. She had put her name down for a tricycle at five past seven, outright. It was Tess's own design from March, for anyone old or unsteady or who did not see why they should have to balance: two wheels on a short axle at the back and one in front, a low step-in frame, a basket. Tess had thought of it as a slow thing.

Sela came down the Avenue at three faster than anything else in the street, with her hat tied on and her basket full and her bell going and the yellow dog flat out beside her back wheel, and overtook her son, who was running beside a saddle, without slowing down.

"Mam," said Arthur.

"Out of the lane, Arthur," said Sela, and was gone.

Mira was holding saddles too, up at the flatter gate end. She held them one-handed. Her other hand stayed flat against her side, under her jacket, where she thought nobody would see, and when she let go she let go a little sooner than Arthur did and walked the last steps instead of running them. Tess watched her do it twice and said nothing, because Mira would not have thanked her.

Under all of it ran a sound Tess had not expected. Every back hub had a freewheel in it, a ring of little teeth with two spring pawls riding over them, so that you could stop pedalling and the wheel would go on turning without you; and every bicycle on the Avenue that was not being pedalled was ticking. From the gate to the river, all afternoon, two hundred and seventy small dry clicks came and went among the voices and the bells.

Ottoline came out onto the shop step at four with her glass still on her forehead and listened to it.

"Sounds like my shop," she said. "When I've wound everything."



Tess's own bicycle had stood in the Motion Shop's back bay behind the Seam Table, under a sheet, since the end of March.

It came off the same jig, with one thing changed. To get on a frame like the engraving's you swung a leg over the back wheel or stepped across the top tube, and Tess could do neither; so she had dropped the top tube in a long sweep down almost to the bottom of the frame, doubled it for stiffness, and now you stepped through the frame instead of over it. Wren had called it a step-through the first morning she saw it, and the name stuck before Tess could find a better one. Then she had put twenty-two teeth on the back sprocket against everybody else's eighteen, so that every turn of the pedals took her a shorter way and less out of her legs: porridge on the flat, nothing on a hill.

She had not ridden it. She had not told anyone she meant to.

Mira came for it at six, off the wall again, without being asked.

"Wren said," said Mira.

"Wren talks."

"Wren does talk," said Mira, and pulled the sheet off, and looked. "That's a good shape. Lower than mine. Campus path; it's flat and there's grass both sides. Come on before it gets dark and I change my mind."

The campus path ran between the schoolrooms and the playing field, freshly surfaced and nearly empty. A few children on the school's own bicycles stopped to watch, and Mira looked at them until they went back to riding.

Tess got up out of the chair with her stick. Since the summer her legs had carried her five or six steps at a time, with the stick doing a good deal of the work, and these were five of them: chair to bicycle, step through the frame, turn, sit. The saddle took her. Her feet found the pedals. Mira took hold of the back of the saddle.

"Pedal," said Mira.

She pedalled. What she had known on paper and not in her body was that she was sitting down. The saddle and the bars had her weight. Her legs did not have to hold her up at all; they only had to go round. They did not go round well, and the left was slower than the right and wanted to stop at the top, but the slow gear forgave it, and the bicycle moved, and Mira walked beside it.

"Look up," said Mira.

Tess looked at her feet.

She went over sideways onto the grass at about the speed of a coat falling off a hook. She put her hand out, as everybody does, and the heel of her right palm came down on the path's edge and lost its skin, and her left knee slid off the grass onto the asphalt and lost some too, through the stocking.

Mira stood over her holding the bicycle.

"You looked at your feet."

"I know I looked at my feet."

"I said look up and you looked at your feet."

"I heard you," said Tess, from the grass, and started to laugh, and could not stop; and Mira started too, and sat down on the path beside her and laughed until she had to stop because of her side.

The second time she looked up. She looked at a lamp post at the far end of the path and pedalled at it, and the bicycle wobbled and then stopped wobbling. Mira ran with her hand on the saddle ten yards, fifteen, and Tess felt the hand go and knew it had gone and kept pedalling, because the lamp post was still there.

She got all the way to it. She pedalled backward and the coaster brake took, gently, and the bicycle stopped; and she remembered too late that she could not put a foot down fast, and the bicycle and she leaned over together very slowly into the hedge, and stayed there.

Mira came up at a walk. "Your knee's bleeding."

"Your side's hurting."

"My side's fine." Mira lifted the bicycle off her. "Your knee's bleeding through your stocking."

Mira held out her free hand, and Tess took it and got out of the hedge.

Coming back toward the chair there was a slope. Not much of one. Tess's left leg wanted to stop at the top again, and she let it.

The bicycle went on.

The back wheel ran on without her, ticking under the saddle, quick and dry. Her legs hung on the pedals and did nothing at all. Mira was ten yards behind, walking, with her hand on her side. Nothing held Tess up but the speed and the two wheels running back under her of their own accord, and she went down the slope toward the chair with the evening coming cold past her face and the bicycle carrying her the whole way, and nobody's hand anywhere.

She stopped by the chair and did not fall over. She put the stick down and leaned on it, still on the saddle, and sat there.

Mira came up and did not say anything for a bit.

"Osric'll fall off one of these in a day," she said then. "He's all knees. He'll go in the fountain like the baker."

Tess bent and looked at the coaster brake's arm where it clipped to the frame. She looked at it all round, and tightened the clip a quarter turn with her finger and thumb. It had been perfectly tight.

"He'll want a frame three," she said.



Doran had been back from Wayford since the fourth, and on the eighth he refused.

He refused all morning from the bench outside the shop's shutter, with his bad knee out straight in front of him and his patched coat over it. He had come to watch. He had a knee. He would not have it out on the Avenue among the young, and he had seen men killed by less than a wheel.

Then the trough boy came by on a borrowed bicycle and rang the bell at him.

"Do that again," said Doran.

The boy did it again. Doran got up, with his knee, and went and looked at the bell: a little dome on the bars with a striker on a spring under it and a thumb lever. He flicked the lever himself and listened.

"Who tuned that?"

"I did," said Ottoline, from her table in the doorway, where she was cutting a key for a woman who had lost hers the first evening. "They're all within a quarter-tone of each other. I'd have done better with longer than a fortnight."

Doran looked at the bell some more, and then at the bicycle under it. "How does a man with a knee get on one of those?"

Arthur, off the wall again, put his coffee down on the bench and came and held the saddle. It took a quarter of an hour, with the old man's hands white on the bars; and on the third length Doran stopped gripping quite so hard, and rode the last twenty yards with Arthur's hand an inch off the saddle, ringing the bell the whole way. He stopped by pedalling backward, put his good foot down, and sat there with his face gone very strange.

"It doesn't hurt," he said.

"It's not carrying you," said Tess, from the chair. "You're sitting down. Your knee only has to go round."

Doran thought about that. "Forty years I've walked on this knee," he said. "Nobody told me I could sit on it."

At noon he went in and put his name on the clerk's list for June, frame one, and asked Ottoline whether the bells could be had in a lower note.



Armand came down into the back bay at two, when Tess had been at the Seam Table an hour, and looked at the knee of her stocking.

"I heard," he said.

"Mira told the wall."

"And the wall told Bettany, and Bettany told me at the six o'clock hour." He sat on the stool beside hers in his cardigan. "When I was a boy a thing with two wheels was a barrow with ideas. Ottoline wants me to have one. She says it would be good for the lock."

"I'm too old inside to fall off anything," said Armand, "which is worse, because it doesn't show," and picked up the dew-seam jar, beaded again overnight, and turned it to the light.

Tess wrote a line in the square for air over water and did not look up. "How are they?"

"Asleep. Mending. Bettany reads them every hour and the lines come and go on their arms the way they should." He set the jar down and looked at the dockhand slice under the flat lens, not at her. "Osric's stopped going through the tree. The first night he stood up out of himself too fast and went halfway into the trunk, and Amara was cross. Every night since, a little less. Last night he stood up and stayed where he was, and she didn't have to say a word to him." He picked up the chalk she had put down and gave it back to her. "He's learning to be small. It's not easy for a big lad."

"Good," said Tess.

She drew a line under dew seam, gently, and the chalk did not break.



She rode the ring path on the evening of the ninth.

Wren had wanted to come. Tess had said no, and then, because Wren's face did a thing, not this time; and Wren had said fine, and taken her own bicycle off down the Avenue toward the river with her bell going, which was how Wren said she minded.

The ring path went the long way round under the keep's hill, new black all the way, and came up to the inner ward from the east. The slow gear took the hill as she had drawn it to, her legs going round and the bicycle creeping up the slope under her, and on the flat stretches she let the freewheel have it and sat, and the keep's wall went by on her left with the last of the light along its top.

At the inner ward gate she pedalled backward and leaned, and had the stick out first this time, and did not fall. She leaned the bicycle against the railing, and then, because she had it, she took Ottoline's lock out of the frame bag, put the shackle through the back wheel and round the rail, and closed it, and heard the pins drop.

Arthur was on the path between the two sentries at the outer door. He had watched her lock a bicycle to a railing under two seven-foot golems in the most guarded yard in Kaldo, and his mouth moved, and he said nothing. He nodded to her and walked on round the inner ward the long way, and left her the path.

The left-hand sentry's slot lit as she passed, a thin violet line across the helm, and its head turned a hand's breadth toward her with the small sound of its gears, and went dark, and turned back. She had fitted the gears in that neck.

She did not go to the door. She went along the outside of the glass, five steps and a rest against the frame of a pane and five more, round to the north side, and stopped where she had stood on the fourth, where she could see.

The violet came through the pane onto her hands. Inside, the dome was full and still. Nine people lay on new oak boards round the trunk with their cuffed hands on their blankets, and on the south side Amara on her old mattress; and somewhere among them, Armand said, a woman who taught school was standing with her arms folded. Tess could not see her and did not try.

Osric was on the nearest board. He lay on his back as Imani had told them all to, his big hands loose on the wool, his face turned a little toward the trunk, and his ears, which went red at nothing, were not red now. A faint silver came up along his forearm in the lamplight as she watched, a thread that branched and sank. She watched it come and go twice.

She put her hand on the glass.

It was the right hand, the one she had scraped on the campus path. The pane was cold, and the cold found the scrape at once and stung there. She left it where it was.

She did not say anything. Behind her the sentry's gears went once, faintly, and stopped. Out past the gate a bell rang on the ring path, somebody going home.

The inner door opened on the hour and Bettany went in with her watch and her lamp. She went round the boards in Ferra's waking order, Emeric first, kneeling at each, two fingers to a neck, the lamp held close to an arm, a line in her book. At Osric's board she looked at his arm a little longer than she had at Declan's, and wrote, and went on to Ward. She did not look up at the glass. If she saw a hand on it, she let it be.

Tess stayed until Bettany's lamp had been all the way round and the door had shut on it. Then she took her hand away. It had left a print on the pane, a small grey fog with the shape of the scrape in the middle of it, and she stood with her stick and watched it go, from the edges in, until the glass was clear.
