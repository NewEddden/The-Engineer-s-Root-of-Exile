Chapter 152 - Lanterns Again

It snowed on the fourth of March, late and wet, the last of the winter coming down the valley in the night as if it had forgotten something; and on the sixth, when the town went up onto the river wall at dusk for the second Lantern Night, the snow was gone from everything that faced the sun and still lying on everything that did not.

So the roofs showed it. Emeric stood at the top of the water stair with his lantern folded flat under his arm and looked back over the town while the wall walk filled up behind him, and the roofs showed it the way a ledger shows a year.

Red. That was the first thing. A year ago, from this same stair, Kaldo inside its walls had been a brown town: thatch on nine roofs in ten, grey-brown and sagging and green at the eaves, with the keep and the church and the works standing up out of it like rocks out of a field of dead bracken. Now it was red. Tile, from the kilns out past the brickfield, the long tiles and the ridge tiles and the half-round ones for the hips, laid all winter by every man in Kaldo who could be got up a ladder, paid for over fifty weeks out of the wage the way the House's paper had said it would be. Red on the south faces where the snow had gone. White on the north faces where it had not, every roof a two-coloured thing in the dusk, a whole town of roofs with one cheek pale.

The thatch that was left was in the low rows below the churchyard. The plague houses. Nobody lived in them and nobody had for years, and nobody would put a tile on a house that was coming down, and so they sat there in the bottom of the town under their old grey hats, dark, with snow in the holes, the last of what Kaldo had looked like when he came.

The rest was lit.

The arc lamps on the wall, round the whole circuit, as they had been for three years tonight. The works across the river, every window of the long sheds yellow, because the night shift was on and the rolling mill did not stop for lanterns. The hospital on its slope with its three rows of windows. A long plume of white over the baths' roof where the warm water met the cold air. The wire from the gate tower going away north over the fields on its poles toward Wayford, and the second wire going west to Milford, and the third, the oldest, up the valley to Grayrock, with the new thing running under it all winter: the Grayrock line, the rails coming down the river road from the landing, forty miles of them on Linus's survey, worked since January by horses, because the engine that was meant to work it was still standing in pieces in Bram's shed.

And one roof dark on purpose. The long shed under the north wall, with its big doors shut and no lamp in it and no lamp near it, where the night shift did not go.

"There's no snow on that one," said Arthur.

He was on the stair below, with his lantern, in the coat of Ward's department that he had been given at Christmas and had not taken off since. He was seventeen and as tall as Emeric now, and he had been looking where Emeric was looking.

"Every other roof on the north wall's white on the north side," Arthur said. "The armoury. The stores. The tower. Not the shed." He frowned at it. "Both sides are clear."

Ward, two steps above them, did not turn round.

"Watch the river," said Ward.

Arthur looked at Ward's back for some while. Then he turned and watched the river, as he had been told. Emeric felt him do it, in the small bright note of him on the stair, and felt him go on thinking about the shed the whole time, the way a boy goes on thinking about a door he has been told not to open.



Sorrel's balloon was on the quay below the water stair, lying on its side on the cobbles like a dead whale made of paper.

She had built it in the mill loft over the winter, with Tess and the paper mill's largest sheets and a great deal of shouting. The shouting had mostly been about glue. Fifty-two panels of the mill's heaviest paper, each one the height of two men, cut to a shape Tess had drawn on the loft floor in chalk and glued edge to edge to the next with a seam two fingers wide, and the whole of it brushed inside with a wash of alum from the dyehouse that Abloh said would keep it from catching if a spark found it, and Sorrel said would not, and Tess had tested on forty scraps over a brazier until Sorrel stopped saying it. Under the mouth of it hung an iron ring, and under the ring on four chains a fire basket of iron bars, and under that, on four ropes, a basket of willow from the basket-maker in the dock quarter, big enough for one woman and a sack.

And a rope. A long one, from the ring down to the drum of the old wool crane at the end of the quay, which Bram had put a new pawl on and a second crank, so that two men could let her up and four could wind her down.

"It's a bag," Sorrel had said at the table in February, when Ferra asked her what it was. "A bag of hot air. Hot air goes up. You make enough of it and put it in a bag, and the bag goes up." She had been very patient about it, the way she was about nothing else. "Nothing pushes it. Nothing holds it. No stone. No mage. You could do it with a cooking fire and your grandmother's bedsheets if you had enough of either." She had looked round the table. "Which is why nobody in the Circle ever has."

That was the part Emeric liked best about it. The Circle flew. The Circle had flown for four hundred years, mages holding their weight off with gravity and shoving themselves along with air, a man at a time, at a cost, and charged for it. And here on the quay at Kaldo, on the second Lantern Night, in front of the whole town, a woman the Circle had called an embarrassment was going to go up into the sky on a bag of paper and a fire, and anybody who wanted to could watch her do it and go home and think about it.

They filled it at half past four.

Sorrel lit the fire basket herself, from one of the wall lamp's brass doors with a taper, the way the town lit its lanterns. It was full of Tess's cakes. Not the lantern cakes; bigger ones, the size of a loaf, pressed from the same wax and sawdust with a little coal dust in them, that burned an hour each and hot. Four men held the paper mouth up on poles over the basket while the heat went in. At first nothing happened but smoke and the smell of hot alum. Then the paper on the cobbles stirred. A fold came up off the stones, and lay down again, and came up again and stayed. The whole flat grey heap of it began to breathe.

The quay went quiet. Then the wall walk above it went quiet, and then the bank.

It rose. Slowly, the way bread rises, filling and rounding and lifting off the cobbles a panel at a time until it stood up on its own on the quay over the fire basket, taller than the water tower's roof, a great round pear of grey paper with the light of the fire coming up into it from beneath so that every seam showed, glowing, in the dusk. The men let go of the poles. It stood there. It tugged on the basket ropes, gently, the way a horse leans on its tether when it smells water.

Somebody on the bank began to clap. Nobody else did. The town was not ready to clap yet. It was looking.

Sorrel climbed into the basket with her limp and her cropped hair and her ring on the outside of her glove, and the sack of spare cakes at her feet, and looked up into the glowing mouth of the thing over her head, and then down at the men on the crane.

"Let her go," she said.

They let her go.



She went up off the quay as smoothly as a lantern, rope paying off the crane drum behind her with a long rattling clatter of the pawl, out over the edge of the quay and the black water and up past the river wall, past the wall walk and the faces on it, so close that people on the wall could have touched the basket, and up, and up, until she was standing a hundred and fifty feet over the river in a basket of willow under a burning paper moon.

Then Kaldo clapped.

The wall walk, the bank, the bridge, the far bank and the dock quarter's moorings, all of it at once, clapping and shouting and whistling, and somebody on the bridge beating a pan. Sorrel, a small dark shape against the glowing paper, put up one arm and waved it, and the noise doubled.

Emeric did not clap. He stood at the head of the water stair with his hands at his sides and his face turned up and found that he could not do anything at all with them.

Ferra was beside him. She had her book under her arm, because the wind was hers tonight, and she was not looking at the balloon. She was looking at the vane on the water tower, which still pointed up the valley.

"She's on a rope," Ferra said. "Over the river. And the wind hasn't turned."

"It's a strong rope."

"I know. I bought it." She did not look away from the vane. "I want her down before the lanterns go. Two things in the air full of fire on one wind is one too many."



It went wrong at a quarter past five.

He did not see it go wrong. He smelled it. The wind was coming down the river toward them, still up-valley, and on it, faintly, from above, came a smell that was not wax and sawdust burning hot. Wet smoke. The thick yellow smell of something smouldering that does not want to burn.

He looked up. The glow inside the paper had gone dull. Not out. Dull, red, the light of a hearth an hour after the last log. And in the basket Sorrel was bent over the fire basket with a cake in each hand, feeding them in, and smoke was coming up off it, white and heavy, into the mouth of the balloon, and the balloon was not rising any more.

It was coming down.

Slowly. The rope from the crane, which had stood out from the drum taut as a bar, began to sag in a long curve over the water. The great pear of paper, which had been round and tight, had a crease in its shoulder that had not been there before. Then another. The basket dropped past the top of the water tower's roof, and the crowd on the wall walk, which had been laughing and pointing, stopped laughing.

"The cakes are wet," said Tess.

She was at the foot of the water stair in her chair, with her data book open on her knees and her face turned up, very white in the light from the wall lamp. She said it flatly, the way she said the readings off a dial.

"The sack was on the cobbles. Where the snow was. I told her." She shut the book. "They'll not catch. They'll smoke. She'll put the whole sack in trying to make them catch and it'll smoke the more."

The men were on the crane already, four of them, winding. The pawl rattled. But a crane drum winds a rope at the speed four men can turn it, and the balloon was coming down faster than that, and not straight down. The wind had her. The up-valley wind was laying her over, the basket swinging out under the sagging paper, and the line of her fall ran down and across the river toward the quay below the water stair, and the quay below the water stair was packed shoulder to shoulder with three hundred people holding paper lanterns.

And the crease in the balloon's shoulder was a fold now, a long slack belly of paper hanging in toward the fire basket, a hand's width from the smouldering cakes, and coming closer as the air inside her cooled.

Emeric went down the water stair.

He did not run. He walked down it fast, between the people, and out onto the quay to the crane, where Bram was standing with his hands on the drum's frame shouting at the men to wind, and he did not say anything to Bram. He stood beside the crane and looked up at the basket coming down over the water and the dull red smoulder in the fire basket under the paper, and put up his right hand.

Fire was his. The kingdom knew it. The whole of Kaldo had seen him bend a candle flame in the schoolroom and a forge flame at the works, and there was nothing on the quay tonight he could not be seen to do.

The glyph came up off his palm, small and red-gold, and closed. He reached up through it.

The smoulder was a long way off. A hundred feet and more of dusk, and the fire at the end of it was not a fire at all, only heat, dull and sullen in a mass of wet wax and sawdust that was boiling its water off before it could burn. He found it anyway. He could not feel it the way he felt a living note, because fire carried nothing, it came back to the sense as nothing at all; but he could see it, and he could reach it through the glyph the way a man reaches a thing on a high shelf with a long pole, and he did. He took the heat that was in the cakes and told it where to go. Not out. Up. Into the wax, all at once, hard, so that the water in the sawdust went off in one hiss of steam and the wax under it caught, and then the next cake, and the next.

The basket lit up. A clean yellow flame came up out of the iron bars, and up into the mouth of the paper.

He held it.

That was the work. Lighting it was nothing. A wet cake that has been made to catch wants to go out again the moment it is let go of, and these were all wet, the whole sack of them, and they went on wanting to go out. So he did not let go. He stood on the quay with his hand up and held the flame in that basket a hundred feet over the river as he had held a candle flame bent on the schoolroom table, steady, the same height, the same colour, while the wet came out of the cakes one after another and they burned.

The balloon stopped falling.

It hung. The belly of slack paper over the fire basket filled, and rounded, and lifted back out of the heat; the creases went out of her shoulder one at a time; and she stood up again in the dusk over the river, round and glowing, and the rope that had sagged over the water drew up straight. The crowd on the quay made a sound like a long breath let out.

"Wind her," said Emeric. "Slowly."

Bram looked at him. Then at the men. "You heard," he said. "Slowly."

They wound her down.

It was slow work. The crane drum went round at a walk, a turn at a time, and the rope came in a turn at a time with it, and the balloon came down out of the sky over the river with a steady flame in her fire basket that nobody in the basket was feeding any more; Sorrel had stopped feeding it the moment it caught, and stood holding the basket's rim with both hands, looking down over the edge at the quay and at the man standing by the crane with his hand up. Down past the water tower. Down past the wall walk and its faces. The wind laid her over once more as she came to the quay's edge, and the men on the crane checked her, and she came in over the cobbles and settled, and the basket touched, and tipped, and stood.

Emeric let the fire go.

It went out in the basket at once, as he had known it would, in a puff of wet steam, and the paper over it began to sigh and lean and come down on the cobbles in great slow folds, and the men ran in to catch it.

He put his hand down.

That was when he noticed it. He stood by the crane with his hand at his side and waited for it, the way he had learned in five years to wait for it after any long hold: the ache behind the eyes, the ringing, the shake that came into the fingers a breath after the work stopped, the feeling of a cistern pumped down to the mud. It did not come. His hand hung at his side and was a hand. His head was clear. He had held a flame a hundred feet off for the time it took four men to wind a crane drum down, and he could have held it again.

He had held a bent candle flame for a count of ten, behind the shutters, in the first summer, and lain on the floor after with his eyes shut.



Sorrel was out of the basket before the paper had finished coming down, and limping across the cobbles toward him, and the crowd on the quay, which had begun to cheer, saw her face and let her through.

"You poured into it," she said.

"Into the fire. Not the bag."

"It's the same thing." She was furious. She was white with it, under the smoke on her face, and her eyes were wet from the smoke and from something else. "It's a bag that flies on fire and nothing else. That's the whole of it. That's the point. The whole of Kaldo watching, and three thousand people going home tonight to say they saw a mage hold it up."

"Three thousand people going home tonight to say they saw it nearly come down on them," said Emeric. "And didn't."

Sorrel opened her mouth. She shut it. She looked back at the balloon lying on the cobbles in its folds, with the men going round it lifting the paper off the fire basket and Tess in her chair at the edge of it, already, with her data book open, looking at the wet sack of cakes.

"The sack was on the stones," Sorrel said. "She told me. I heard her. I thought it'd dried." She wiped her face with the back of her glove and made the smoke worse. "It's not the bag's fault."

"No."

"It's mine."

"It's the cakes'," said Tess, from the edge of the paper, without looking up. "And it's the fire basket's, for not having anything in it that keeps going when the cakes don't. That's two faults. Neither of them's yours." She turned a page. "Come here and look at this."

Sorrel looked at Emeric once more, hard, the way she had looked at him in the gate yard a year ago with a paper plane in her hand. Then she turned round and limped across to Tess's chair and bent over the data book, and the two of them began to argue about it in low furious voices with their heads together, and he left them to it.



The wind turned at twenty to six. Ferra watched the vane come round, and gave it a minute, and nodded once, and the word went down the wall faster than the wire.

Imani was on his left. She had come up the water stair while the balloon was coming down and had stood at the head of it the whole time with her lantern under her arm and her other hand on the parapet, and she had not said anything when he came back up, and had looked at him the way she looked at a man coming into her ward who said he was quite well.

She had her father's name to write. She had written it last year where the town could read it, and she would write it there again, and she held her lantern out to him by its hoop before she took up her pencil.

"Hold it," she said.

He held it. She wrote. Her father's name, large and plain on the side that faced the town, with the pencil going round the curve of the paper the way a needle goes round a curve of skin, and when she had done it she did not take the lantern back at once. She looked past it, at his own lantern on the stone in front of him, which he had written while she was coming up the stair: two names on the outside, and a third, small, on the inside, by the hoop, where it would face the flame.

"Same one?" she said.

"Same one."

She did not ask whose. She looked at the paper a little longer, as if she could see through it, which she could not; then she took her lantern back from his hands and lit it at the lamp.

Ferra was on his right, at the parapet, with her lantern on the stone and her pencil out. Last year she had written one name and put her hand over it flat while she found her taper, so that nobody could see it, him least of all.

She held her lantern out to him by its hoop.

He took it. She bent to write, with the paper between his hands, so close that her hair brushed his wrist; and he turned his head and looked at the river.

He looked at it for as long as it took her to write a name. The water going by black under the wall, and the lamps on it, and the dark far bank where the dock quarter's lanterns were being lit one after another, small and yellow, like a row of houses with their doors opening. He did not look back until she took the hoop out of his hands.

"You could have looked," said Ferra.

"I know."

She lit it. She stood with it filling in her hands, and he saw her face over the top of it in the light coming through the paper, and it was not a face she made at a column.

Then the three of them let go together, on her count, three lanterns off the top of the water stair at once into the dark over the river, and the wall let go of its lanterns all down its length behind them, three hundred and more, and the downriver wind took all of it.



The fireworks went up at nine, from a barge.

That had been Ward's rule. Ward had made Fennick a great many rules in the year since the hayrick, and Fennick had kept all of them with every sign of enjoying it, and the barge was the best of them: an old coal barge anchored in the middle of the river below the bridge, two hundred yards from the nearest roof in any direction, with a water cask at each corner and nothing on it but Fennick and two of his quarry boys and the boxes. Every ounce of powder that had gone onto the barge that afternoon had been weighed out of the powder store by Ward himself, on Harl's old beam, and written in Ward's book, and would be weighed back in again tomorrow, what was left of it, and the difference would be what Fennick had fired, and it had better be.

They were better than last year's. Everybody said so. Emeric could not have said how, except that they were, the way a man's second bridge is better than his first: higher, and cleaner, and stranger. A red that burst and then burst again inside itself, three times, like a box in a box in a box. A gold that hung over the river and came down slowly in long weeping threads, so slowly that the town had time to say oh. A thing that went up with no light at all, so that people on the wall said it had failed, and burst at the top of its climb into a great sudden ring of green that stood in the air over the water, perfectly round, and went out all at once.

The last one was white. It lit the valley from end to end. And out of the middle of it, as the town's breath went out, a single burning star came down, drifting, on the downriver wind, toward the far bank, exactly as it had a year ago.

The whole of Kaldo turned its head to watch it fall.

It fell into a field. An empty one, ploughed, black and bare under the last of the snow, below the dock quarter where Farris's neighbour's hayrick had stood a year ago, and where there was now no hayrick, but a post, with a board nailed to it, and on the board, in large white letters that the white star lit up very clearly for the length of a breath as it came down beside it and fizzled out in the furrow: NOT THIS YEAR.

The far bank laughed first. Then the wall. Then the whole of the river, from the bridge to the marsh, laughing in the dark at a board in a field, and out on the barge in the middle of the water, small and black against the glow of his own spent tubes, Fennick Crake laughing louder than any of them.



Imani found him at the foot of the keep stair a little after ten, when the town was going home.

She did not say anything. She took his right wrist in her fingers, the way she had taken it on the slip at dawn last May, two fingers on the inside under the bone and her thumb on the back, and stood with her eyes on nothing and counted, in the lamplight at the foot of the stair, with the last of the crowd going past them in the lane.

He let her.

She counted for a good while. Longer than she needed to. Then she let go of his wrist and looked at him.

"Seventy," she said. "A man who's walked up a stair." She did not look away. "I watched you hold that. All of it. On the quay. The whole time it took them to wind her down." She folded her arms in her cloak. "That should have cost you."

"It used to."

"When did it stop?"

He thought about it honestly. "I don't know," he said. "I didn't notice it stop. I noticed tonight that it had."

Imani looked at him a little longer, in the way she had of looking at a thing she meant to write down later and had not decided how. Then she said goodnight, and went off down the lane toward the hospital with her hands in her cloak, and he stood at the foot of the stair and looked at his own right hand, which was steady, and went up.



Ward weighed the powder back into the store at seven the next morning, on Harl's beam, with Fennick standing beside him with his hands behind his back and an expression of the most complete innocence.

It came out to the ounce. Ward checked it twice. He wrote it in his book and looked at it.

"Exact," said Ward.

"I know," said Fennick, and grinned. "I knew you'd count it. So I counted it first." He rocked on his heels. "Did you see the green one?"

At noon Sorrel went up again.

The balloon had dried overnight in the mill loft, every panel of it, and the fire basket had a new thing in it: a lamp. A plain brass spirit lamp from the still, with a glass chimney Ivo had blown that morning and a wick as thick as a thumb, wired into the middle of the iron bars, burning the still's strongest spirit with a steady blue flame that a wet cake could not put out and a gust could not get at. Tess had drawn it on the back of a page of her data book at half past six. The cakes went in round it, from a tin box with a lid, and if they would not catch, the lamp would go on burning under them until they did.

There was no crowd. A few dozen people on the wall walk with their dinners in their hands, and the crane crew, and Tess in her chair on the quay with the data book, and Emeric at the head of the water stair with his hands in his pockets.

She went up on the rope a hundred and fifty feet over the river and stayed there for an hour in the cold bright noon, a small dark woman in a willow basket under a great grey moon of paper, with the blue flame of the lamp steady under her, and no stone in her, and no mage, and nothing pouring into anything.

When she came down she looked up at the water stair before she climbed out of the basket.

"Nobody touch it," she said.

"Nobody did," said Emeric.
