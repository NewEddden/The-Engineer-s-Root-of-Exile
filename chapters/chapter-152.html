Chapter 152 - One Crossing

The bell in the church tower did not ring the hour. It rang anyhow, too fast, a man hauling on the rope with his whole weight and no count in it, and Ward was out of his bed and into his boots before he knew he was awake.

He knew the sound. Every man who had kept a garrison knew it. There were only two things a bell rang like that for, and there was no army in the south that could have reached the bridge in the dark without the board telling him first.

From the passage window of the keep he could see it. Behind the market, low, a dirty orange light under the roofs, and the smoke going straight up in the still frost and then flattening out over the island like a lid.

He was on the keep stair when Emeric came out of the east room behind him with his coat over his nightshirt. Neither of them said anything. Ward went down first, because he always went first.

It was the tannery lane.

Ward had stood above it on the wall a week ago and counted the windows. He knew the house before he reached it: the third from the end, on the side that backed onto the tannery yard, where he had looked down through a window at a row of people asleep on a floor and a woman with a rush light mending something. The house was gone already. Not fallen. It stood, with its shape still there, but there was nothing inside the shape except fire, and the fire came out of the window he had looked through and went up the front of the house and under the eaves of the next one.

That was the whole trouble with the lane, and he had known it on the wall and not thought it. The houses touched. They were old timber houses with shingle roofs and wattle between the posts, and they had been built one against another along the lane so long ago that nobody knew which had been first, and they shared their end walls like men sharing a coat. The fire did not need to jump from one to the next. It walked.

People were in the lane. Too many, and not the right ones. The people who lived in it were coming out, in blankets, carrying children and pots and a chair, and the people who lived round it were coming in to look, and between them they filled it wall to wall. A woman was screaming a name at the burning house. Nobody was doing anything.

Ward took the first four men he saw who were his, which were two of the gate watch and Declan, in his shirt, and a boy from the stable, and made them into a line across the lane below the fire, arms out, and walked the line down the lane pushing people out of it. He did it without shouting. A man shouting in a crowd at night made the crowd into a bigger crowd. He said, "Back. Back to the market. Back," over and over in the same voice, and walked, and the line walked with him, and the lane emptied behind them except for the people who had to be in it.

"Water," Declan said at his shoulder.

"I know."

"The carts. Bram's barrels."

Ward was already looking the other way, down the long street to the Western Gate, and past the gate at the bridge, and he had seen it the moment he looked and not let himself believe it.

The bridge was full.

It had been full every morning since harvest. The carts came in off the Milford road before light, to be first over when the gate opened, and they queued on the bridge because there was nowhere else to queue, nose to tail, with their owners asleep under them in the frost. Tonight they had come early because it was market day, and there were more of them than there had been all winter, and the first cart in the queue was against the gate and the last was off the far end of the bridge and onto the road, and between them there was not a yard.

Across the water, at the foundry, there were lanterns moving. Bram's men. Ward could see them in the glow off the slag heap, and he could see the shape of the two big water barrels on their wheels that Bram kept by the furnace house, which held more water between them than every bucket on the island, and he could see men trying to bring them up to the far end of the bridge.

And stopping. Because the far end of the bridge was a cart with a man asleep under it, and behind it another, and behind that the whole of Milford's market day.

One crossing. Water on every side.

"They'll not get over," Declan said.

"No."

"We could clear it."

"We could clear it by noon." Ward turned back to the fire. "Lake. The water steps under the lake wall. Everybody with a bucket. Everybody without one, find one. Go."

The water steps went down from the lane end of the lake wall to a stone landing where the fish boats had tied up before the new racks. The smoke house stood at the top of them, dark, locked, with Ward's key on its nail inside his coat, and he did not look at it. The lake at the bottom had a skin of ice a yard out from the stone, and the first man down broke it with his heel and went in to the knee and swore, and filled his bucket, and passed it up.

That was the line.

Ward built it himself. He stood on the steps and put men into it one at a time as they came, by the shoulder, here, you, here, and he did not ask any of them who they were. Knights from the gate, in their shirts and their boots. Island men who had come to look and been made to stop looking. And the new people, the ones from the carts and the lofts and the lanes, men he did not know and had never counted, who had come out of houses that were not theirs to houses that were burning and stood in the frost and waited to be told. He told them. They went into the line beside the knights, and the buckets went up hand to hand from the lake to the lane, sixty yards, up the steps and along the foot of the wall and round the corner, and the empties came back down the other side of the line, thrown.

It was not enough. He had known it would not be enough when he said lake. A bucket held what a bucket held. A line of forty men moved one every few breaths. The fire in the tannery lane was drinking a house a quarter of an hour.

But the line held, and it was the only thing on the island that was holding, and he walked it.

About the middle of it, on the steps where the pass was hardest because a man had to turn and lift, there was a young man in his shirtsleeves with the sleeves pushed up, big through the shoulders, who took every bucket that came to him and turned and put it in the next pair of hands and turned back for the next without once looking up. He did not stop. Men either side of him stopped, to shake their arms out or to cough or to look at the fire. He did not. Ward watched him for a count of fifty and he did not stop once.

Ward knew the face and took a moment to find it. Sela's boy. Sela's other boy, the one the fever had taken down in the first year and Imani had sat three nights with. He had been a thin lad then, all neck. He was not thin now. He would be past twenty.

Ward did not say anything to him. He noted him, the way he noted a man on the practice yard who kept his feet when he was put down, and went on up the line.

Armand came down the steps with Orrin behind him, both of them in their robes over their nightclothes, and Orrin talking.

Ward had not sent for them. He did not know who had. Orrin was saying something about the wind, and Armand was saying nothing, and they went down past the line to the bottom step, to the stone landing, and stood at its edge above the broken ice.

Ward had seen a mage throw water once before, in the capital, at a granary fire, years ago. He had never forgotten it and he had never much liked it. It was not like a bucket. The water did not get carried. It came up out of the lake in a body, black in the night and then orange as it rose into the light of the fire, a great slow twisting mass of it the size of a cart, and went up the lake wall as if the wall were a slope, and over the top, and it did not fall until it was over the lane.

Then it fell.

It came down on the roofs at the lower end of the lane, the ones the fire had not yet walked to, and it came down hard, and the steam went up off the shingles in a white sheet, and men in the lane cheered.

Orrin threw the next one. Then Armand. Then Orrin.

They did not throw it on the fire. Ward saw that after the third one and understood it. The fire was too big to put out with anything two old men could lift. They were throwing it ahead of the fire, on the houses it would reach next, soaking them, shingles and wattle and posts, so that when the fire walked to them it would have to stop and dry them first. They were buying time a house at a time.

It worked for as long as they lasted.

Orrin lasted the longer. He was the younger of them by fifteen years, and he talked the whole time, to Armand, to the lake, to nobody, and threw, and talked. Armand threw in silence. By the eighth time Ward could see his hands from the top of the steps, and they were shaking, and the water came up slower and smaller, and on the ninth it came up a barrel's worth and fell short, into the lake, and Armand stood on the landing with his arms at his sides and his head down.

Then he put his hand inside his robe and brought something out.

It was an iron flask the size of a man's two fists, with a screw lid and a black seal round it. Ward had heard about the one like it that had slipped its lid a foot from Armand in the autumn. He had heard Armand, at the table, afterward, say what it had done to him and at what rate. He had not heard any of the others understand it the way he had understood it.

Armand held it close against his chest and turned the lid.

Ward saw his face.

He saw it from forty feet away by firelight, and he knew what he was looking at, and it put him back somewhere he had not been in a long time. A stone room with eight men standing round it. A table in the middle. The fourth hour, when the thing that was being poured into a boy stopped being warm and started being something else, and the boy on the table could not tell anybody because there was nothing left in him to tell them with.

Armand's face did exactly that, for one breath. Then it stopped. Then he straightened.

He knew the rate. Ward had heard him name it at the table in his own voice, in front of Emeric and Imani and all of them. He had stood on that landing knowing it and turned the lid anyway.

The next throw was the biggest of the night. It came out of the lake like something alive and went over the wall and fell on the corner of the lane where it met the market, on the three houses that stood between the fire and the market stalls and the lofts over them, where Ferra had said people were sleeping four to a loft, and it soaked them to the posts.

Armand threw twice more. Then he sat down on the landing where he stood, quite slowly, the way an old man sits, and Orrin stopped talking for the first time and went to him.

It bought the island.

Ward understood it by first light, when he could see the lane whole. The fire had walked down the tannery side of the lane and taken every house on it from the third to the end, and turned the corner, and come to the three soaked houses at the market corner, and stopped. It had tried them. Their fronts were black to the eaves. Their shingles had steamed and curled. They had not caught. Behind them the market stood, and the lofts, and the rest of the island, and on the other side of the lane the houses were scorched and smoking and standing.

It did not buy the row. The row was gone.

Nine houses. They stood in the grey light as nine black shapes with their end walls still up, sharing them, the way they had always shared them, with nothing between the walls but ash and fallen shingle and smoke going up into the still air. The tannery yard behind them had its drying frames burned down to stumps and its vats boiling in the heat, and it stank.

Ward stood in the lane and looked at it and thought, very plainly, in words.

Two old men with water magic bought ten minutes. Ten minutes, and the market, and everything past the market. And one of them had paid for it with a thing Ward would not have asked a man to pay in any war he had ever fought, and the other was sitting on the water steps with a blanket round him and a cup of something in his hands that he had not drunk.

A brigade with pumps bought all night. A brigade did not need either of them. A brigade did not need anybody alive in particular. It needed forty men who had done it before, and a place to stand them, and a pump that a boy could work, and water somewhere the fire was not, and a road to bring the water down that was not one bridge with a hundred carts on it.

He had built this garrison for eleven years on the idea that the island was a thing nobody could come at. And the fire had not come at it. It had started inside, in a house with three families on its floor and one hearth and one rush light, and it had found out in one night that the island was a thing nobody could get out of, and nothing could get into, and the water was everywhere and could not be brought.

Emeric had gone into the fourth house.

Ward had not seen him go. He had been on the steps with the line. He learned it afterward from Declan, who had been in the lane, and from a woman whose name he did not know, and from Emeric himself, who told it in three sentences.

The fourth house was the one the fire had come to first after the house it started in. The people on its ground floor had got out. The people in its loft had not, because the loft ladder came down beside the shared wall and the shared wall was burning. Emeric had gone in under the eaves at the back where the wattle was still whole, and had found the loft beam down across the ladder, and had lifted it.

Ward did not ask how. He knew the prince could make a thing light when he had his hand on it and his mind on it; Bram had told him once about a nib held up in the air over a bench with nothing under it. He had not known it could be done with a burning beam the length of a room. He supposed Emeric had not known either until he tried.

Under the beam there had been a woman, alive, with her hair burned. Emeric had got her out through the back. Then he had gone back in, and come back out without anybody, and said to Declan, there's nobody else alive up there, and sat down in the tannery yard in the mud with his back against a vat.

There had been three more in the loft. A man and his wife, and the man who owned the house.

They were brought out when the ash was cold enough, a little after noon.

Ward had them laid in the church porch because Doran asked for it. Doran came down the nave to look at them with Bram beside him, and said nothing for a long time, and then said their names, because the woman Emeric had got out had told them to Imani, and Imani had told Doran.

Nat Brayley, a tanner's man, forty-four, of the tannery lane, born in it. Who had a house with a loft, and in the autumn had let the loft for nothing to a couple off the Milford road who had come over the bridge with a copy of the notice and a handcart and no cousin to go to.

Joss Carrow and Ailie Carrow, of a holding on the east pasture of Milford, lately. Who had owed eleven years' rent against a tenancy that had been let to Joss's father, and been held on it, and had read in a printed paper nailed to a Milford church door that a debt could be claimed against a man's goods and his wages and not against his freedom to leave. And had left.

They had been free for three months and eleven days.

Nat Brayley's wife had lived, with her hair burned off on one side, because she had been asleep under the loft ladder and not in the loft, and a beam had come down over her and not on her, and a man had lifted it.

Ward stood in the porch and did not let himself make it into anything. It was what it was. They had come to Kaldo to stop being held, and Kaldo had had nowhere to put them but a loft in a lane where the houses touched, and the crowding had killed them. He did not soften it in his own head. There was no one there to soften it for.

Emeric found him in the ash at the end of the afternoon.

The lane was empty. The garrison had a rope across each end and a man at each rope, and the people who had lived in it had been taken into the church and the long room and three houses on the east side whose owners had opened their doors without being asked, which Ward had noted too. The smoke had nearly stopped. The end walls stood up out of the black like the ribs of something, and in the third house, where it had started, you could see the hearth still, a stone hearth, perfectly whole, with a kettle hook over it.

Emeric had washed his face. He had not changed his coat. There was a burn across the back of his left hand that Imani had dressed and that he was holding a little away from his side.

They stood in the ash together and did not say anything for a while.

"How's Armand," Ward said.

"Sleeping. Imani's with him." Emeric looked at the hearth. "She says his hands haven't stopped. She says he'll live." He was quiet. "She says he knew what it would do. She asked him, when he could talk. He said he did."

"I saw his face."

Emeric looked at him then, and Ward saw him understand, and saw him decide not to ask.

Ward looked down the lane, at the nine black shapes, and at the soaked houses at the corner with their fronts burned to the eaves, and at the market behind them standing whole in the low sun.

"Build it," he said.

Emeric did not answer.

"The city." Ward said it without any weight on it, the way he would have said which gate to double. "Build it. I was right about the wall. I'm still right about the wall. A wall on flat ground's a wall anybody can walk round, and I'll tell you that again when you've drawn it, and I'll build it anyway and put everything I've got into it. But I've spent eleven years keeping people from getting in here, and last night nobody could get out, and nothing could get in, and there was water on every side of us and none of it on the fire." He looked at the hearth with the kettle hook. "There's no plan I can make for that on this island. There's no wall I can build that makes a lane wider."

"I know," said Emeric.

"So build it."

"I will."

"And until you do," said Ward, "I'll hold this one." He did not say what he meant by this one. He did not need to. "It's going to happen again. It's winter and they're burning anything they can find to keep warm, three families to a hearth. It'll happen again before your wall's a foot off the ground."

"Yes."

"Then it'll happen to men who've done it before." Ward turned from the hearth. "A fire company. Knights first, because they drill already and they'll do what they're told in the dark without asking why. Two watches of twenty. A post at the market and a post at the gate, with pumps if Bram can make them and buckets till he does. A cistern at the market corner, filled, covered, that doesn't depend on the lake or the bridge or anybody's barrels. A rule that the bridge is cleared of carts every night and they queue on the road." He paused. "And a drill. The drill's the thing. Men who've never done it will stand in a lane and look at a fire. Men who've done it forty times in the dark with nothing burning won't."

"You've thought about it."

"Since about the fourth bucket."

Emeric looked at him for a long moment, with the burn on his hand held out from his side and his face washed and the ash on his coat.

"You'll want the freed men in it," he said. "Not just knights."

"Later. Knights first." Ward considered it. "There was a lad on the steps. Sela's boy. He didn't stop once all night. I'll want him in the second watch."

"Then have him."

Ferra entered it that evening.

Ward was not in the room when she did it, but he knew how it would read, because he had seen her enter the dead of the war and the dead of the lower water's first winter and she did not have more than one way of doing it. A line for each name, with the age and where from. A line for the houses, by count, and whose. A line for the tannery's frames and vats, and a figure against them, and a figure against the houses at the corner, soaked and scorched and standing. Nine households on the parish, to be housed out of the barony's purse until the spring, and a figure against that. And under it all, in the same hand, a line that said what had happened and nothing about why.

He heard afterward that she had put one more line under that one, and that it was a count of the new people in the lane on the night, as near as the clerks could make it, against the number of people the lane had been built for. He heard that the second figure was a little under a third of the first.

Ward sat up with the garrison book after the last bell.

It was the old book, the one he had begun in the first year, with the watch hours in it and the gate rules and the drills, every drill the garrison had, each on its own page, in his own hand and nobody else's. He turned through it to the back, past the rules for the board and the rules for the relay, to the dead-crystal drills, the three of them, which he had written the winter the gatehouse board flickered and there was nobody in the chair. They took three pages.

He turned to the next page, which was blank, and smoothed it flat.

He wrote at the top of it, Fire.

Then he sat for some time with the pen over the paper and did not write anything else, because he found that he wanted the first line to be right, and he did not yet know what the first thing a man should do was, when he woke to a bell rung with no count in it and smoke over the island like a lid.

In the end he wrote, The first man to the bell rings it until he is relieved, and the second man goes to the bridge.

Then he went on.
