Chapter 359 - Guests?

September was awake before Ward.

Emeric came across the inner ward at a quarter to five with the sky going white over the sea wall and the gravel still dark with dew, and the squirrel was sitting on the low branch of the old pear with his tail over his back, watching the kitchen door. The kitchen door was where the crusts came from. Emeric was not the kitchen door. He had a hazelnut in his pocket anyway, because he usually did, and he stopped under the branch and held it up on his flat palm.

September looked at the hazelnut. Then he looked at Emeric, for long enough that it was clearly a decision, and came down the bark head first in three short runs, and took the nut off his palm with great dignity, and went back up without a word of thanks. He sat on the branch and turned it over in his hands as if checking it for a hidden overlay.

"It's only a nut," said Emeric.

The squirrel showed him the back of his head.



Ward's yard was the old one behind the barracks, with the pump in the corner and the post by the gate, and on the post the slate. Ward had put the slate up when Emeric was crowned. He had not said why. He had chalked two columns on it, K and W, and every morning after the drill he made his marks, and on the first of each month he wiped it, without comment, and started again.

It was the twenty-fourth of June. The W column was longer.

Ward was at the pump with his sleeves rolled, washing his face in his hands, and when he heard the gate he didn't turn round. "You're late."

"It's a quarter to."

"Quarter to's late for a man who has to warm up." Ward shook the water off and took the two blunt swords down from the rack, the old ones with the leather wrapped over the edges, and threw one across the yard handle first. Emeric caught it. Ward's rule in this yard had not changed in years: legs and arms until the bell, nothing else. No stones. No pull. Emeric's stone was inside him and could not be left on the rack, so it was a rule about what a man chose to do, and Ward had never once had to remind him of it.

They went round the yard twice at a jog, which Ward did in silence and Emeric did breathing, and then squared off on the packed earth in the middle where the grass had never grown back.

The first touch was Ward's, and it took about four breaths. Emeric came in high and Ward wasn't there, he was a step to the left and inside, and the leather tapped Emeric's ribs on the open side almost gently, the way a man knocks on a door he knows is unlocked.

"You did that last Thursday," said Ward.

"I know."

"You did it the Thursday before."

"I know, Ward."

The second was Emeric's. He didn't deserve it much. Ward's foot went in a soft place by the drain, and Emeric saw it and was there, and the blade went across Ward's forearm, and Ward said, "Drain," in the tone of a man filing a complaint against the earth itself, and then, "Fair."

The third was long. By the end of it they were both blowing, and over at the far end of the yard the morning class had come in through the side gate: a dozen of them in their shirts, the newest names on the Crestless roll and three of the base's young knights who had asked, with Arthur at the front. Arthur had the mornings. He started them on the ropes and paid the two men in the middle of the yard no attention at all, which Emeric liked about him more than he could have said. Ward took the third when Emeric's arm came down a hair slow on a cut he'd made a thousand times, and the leather went in under it to the belt.

"Tired," said Ward.

"Old."

"Same thing, in a yard."

The fourth Emeric won with his hands. Ward came in close for a bind, and Emeric let the sword go, which Ward hated, and took Ward's sword arm at the wrist and elbow and turned with it, the way he had once been taught to turn in a ring by a man with a broken nose, in a life nobody here had seen, and Ward went over his hip and landed on his back on the packed earth and lay there.

The class at the ropes had stopped. Arthur said something short and they started again.

"That's not a sword," said Ward, from the ground.

"You said legs and arms."

Ward looked at the sky. "I did say that." He put up a hand and Emeric pulled him up. "Again. With the sword in your hand, if Your Majesty doesn't mind."

The fifth was over almost before it started, and Emeric had known it would be from the way Ward settled his feet. The leather touched the side of his neck and stayed there.

"Three," said Ward.

"Two."

"Two's more than Monday."

"Monday was one." Emeric bent over with his hands on his knees. "There are kings who win at this, you know."

"There are kings who've never been in a yard with anybody who'd hit them." Ward went to the post and chalked three marks under W and two under K, neatly, and stood back to look at them with his head on one side. "There. That's honest."

He put his head under the pump again. At the ropes Arthur had a boy halfway up and was telling him, without raising his voice, that his feet were doing the work and his arms were only there to complain about it.

"The base wants the yard on the twenty-eighth," said Ward, with his eyes shut and water running off his chin. "Duvrenne's second year's coming down on the train. Rylan's taking them."

"Do you need me?"

Ward opened one eye. "No," he said, as if the question were odd. "Why would I need you?"



Armand was on the stair.

The real one: the castle's old west stair, worn hollow in the middle of every step, that went up from the inner ward to the long room. He was sitting on the sixth step with a letter on his knee and the cane laid along the step beside him, in a cardigan, and he looked like what he was, a lean man of about thirty-two reading his post in the sun. Nobody would have taken him for a man past ninety. People who didn't know him took him for one of Tess's engineers. He enjoyed that more than he let on.

"You smell of Ward," he said, without looking up.

"I lost."

"I'd assumed." Armand folded the letter. "The north-west tower's answered. Their death register, the last two hundred years. They wanted to know why I was asking, and I told them I was an old man interested in how other old men die, which is true, and nobody in a tower has ever been able to resist that. It's coming south on the House's next ship, copied. Tess will have it on a sheet in a week."

"And then?"

"And then I'll know whether my register's lying to me or I am." He put the letter away inside the cardigan. "You're going to the dome."

"I'm going to see my mother."

"Good. See her." Armand set the cane's end on the step below him, and didn't get up. He looked at Emeric the way he had in the years when he still had white hair, patient and not at all soft. "Don't go down the stair."

Emeric stood two steps below him. They were on a stair. Both of them knew it, and neither did anything with it.

"I haven't," he said. "Not since the winter."

"I know you haven't. I'd know." Armand's eyes went to Emeric's wrist, where the root bead sat in its band, warm against the skin. "That's a latch, you know. Not a lock. A latch keeps a door shut from your side. It says nothing about who's leaning on the other."

"You've said."

"I'm saying it again. I'm old enough to repeat myself and young enough to be right about it." He got up then, all at once, the way he could now. "When we know more. When the far thing has a name, or a shape, or a number on a sheet. Not before. I'm asking as your teacher."

"You asked as my teacher last time."

"I'll ask as your teacher every time," said Armand, "until one of us is dead, and I've had the stone, so it isn't going to be me," and went on up the stair with the cane under his arm, reading his letter again.



The dome was cool. It always was in the mornings, before the sun got onto the glass, and the air under it had the faint pressed feel against the skin that Emeric had stopped noticing years ago and noticed again now, coming in from the heat.

The tree stood in the middle of it, braided and pale, humming. Under it, on Armand's old mattress on its low frame by the roots, his mother's body lay asleep in a clean grey gown, as it had lain for two years, with her hands on the blanket and her face turned a little toward the trunk. Imani had been in before dawn; the sheet on the board at the mattress's head had the night's figures in her hand, and under them, No change. Good colour.

His mother was on the bench.

The bench was new that spring, oak, from Bram's shop, because Harrold had said that if he was going to sit by the tree every morning for the rest of his life he wanted something with a back to it. Amara sat at one end of it in the made body, in a summer dress the colour of wet sand that Abloh had cut for her with long sleeves and a high neck, and gloves, and the brimmed hat in her lap. She had her ankles crossed. Tess had said the drives didn't care how she sat, and Amara had said she did, and that was the end of it. When she turned her head, Emeric heard the small dry sound of them, like a drawer closing, and then she smiled at him with Abloh's careful face and Ivo's glass eyes, and it was her smile. Nobody had been able to explain that to him, and he had stopped asking.

Harrold was at the other end of the bench with his arm along its back behind her. He was in his shirt with the collar open, a big gold-eyed man who looked in his early thirties, with a cup of coffee going cold on the gravel by his boot. The town still called him the old king. He answered to it.

And between them, sitting on the gravel with his back against the bench and his knees up, eating a pear, was Waylon.

"You're dusty," Waylon said.

"I've been on my back in Ward's yard. When did you come down?"

"The night train. There's a man in the third carriage who snores like a rasp on a hinge, and I'm going to find out who he is and have him moved to the goods van." Waylon held up the pear. "The duchy sends its accounts. Alina sends a letter for Ferra, which I'm not allowed to know the contents of, and a jar of something for Imani that I'm not allowed to open. And I want your examination."

"My what?"

"Milford. The clerks." Waylon finished the pear and looked round for somewhere to put the core and, finding nowhere in the dome that would be forgiven, put it in his pocket. "I've got forty clerks in the north who were Aldane's clerks, and thirty of them are the sons of men who were Aldane's clerks, and I'd like to know which of them can add." He said it lightly. He had not been light about the north at all, for two years. "If it works in Milford I want the papers. I'll ask her at the table."

"Ask her before the table," said Harrold. "She likes to have the answer written before she gives it."

Amara laughed. It came out of the made body a little late, the way it did, and she put her gloved hand over her mouth the way she always had, and Harrold watched her do it. Emeric had seen him watch her do it every morning for two years, and it had not worn thin yet.

"Sit," she said to Emeric. "You're making the tree nervous, standing."

He sat on the gravel beside Waylon with his shoulder against his mother's knee. Through the stone in his chest and the tree's hum he could feel her the way any delver in the dome could: not in the made body, which was very good and was a machine, but in the air of the place, close and warm and quick, the woman who had taught nine people on boards how to sleep and wake. She put her hand on his head, through the glove. The made hand was a little too heavy. She knew it, and kept it light.

"Tess says three hours on the new canister," she said. "Then I'm to come back and sit by myself and be good. I thought the river market. Harry's never bought anything with his own hands in his life."

"I have."

"Name one thing."

Harrold thought. "A horse."

"A steward bought you a horse and you nodded at it." She looked at Emeric. "He hasn't got a purse. All his life a king, and he walks round the town with nothing in his pockets but his hands."

"I've got a card," said Harrold, with dignity.

"Ferra gave him a card," Waylon told the dome. "She told me. She said if he spends more than what's on it at the river market she wants to know what on."

Somewhere in the middle of that his mother had started humming.

She did it without seeming to know, as she did most mornings, under the talk, low, and not really in the made throat at all: it came out of the air of the dome, out of the place where she actually was, and anybody with the sense heard it before they heard it with their ears. It was the tune. Emeric had had it in his head all his life. He had had it in a corridor in the capital and on a dark road in the first year, and in another life altogether, and it went the way it always went, up, and round, and up into the long high note at the top.

And stopped there.

She stopped where he always stopped. The note hung in the dome a little longer than a note should, and the tree's hum came up under it and took it, and then there was only the tree.

Harrold had heard it too, in his own way now, since the sleep. He moved his thumb on the back of the bench where it lay behind her shoulders.

"One of these days," said Waylon, who had heard it only with his ears, "somebody's going to finish that, and I'm going to be very annoyed with them."

"Why annoyed?" said Amara.

"Because I'll have spent thirty years wondering, and it'll turn out to be the same as the beginning, the way they all do." He stood up and brushed the gravel off his trousers. "I'm going to find Ferra before Harrold tells her I'm coming."

"I'd never," said Harrold.

When Waylon had gone, Amara turned her glass eyes toward the east bed, and Emeric followed them.

There were two small trees in it. They had been seeds, a knuckle deep in the turned earth with a scratch on each where Tess had filed them; then, for most of a year, nothing; then a pale bent stem in each, like a finger crooked out of the ground, which Ferra had written down to the hour. They were as high as his knee now. They had the tree's bark already, braided in a small way, two strands each, and they hummed very faintly, a long way under the big one's note, the way a child hums along with a song it half knows.

"Go and look at the left one," his mother said.

He got up and went and looked.

At the foot of the left-hand tree the gravel edging had been pushed up in a little ridge, and the plank that held the bed's edge had lifted at one end, a finger's width, and under the lifted end, coming out of the dark earth and going back into it, there was a root. White. As thick as a pencil. It had gone out sideways under the plank as if there were somewhere it particularly wanted to be.

He crouched and looked at it. Behind him Harrold had stood up and come over, and stood at his shoulder looking down at it too, with his hands in his empty pockets.

These two had come out of Sabina's press, out of the twelve the treasury had written down as of no value. But they were the same grey stones as the nine in the box he had carried down the long hall of the palace on the night of his exile, her dead stones, Corbin's farewell gift, with the court laughing on both sides of him, two hundred people, and a box of carved soldiers under his other arm; and at the end of the hall there had been the door, and the steps, and the dark, and the road south.

He put two fingers on the root. It was cool. Under the cool, very faint, it hummed.

"I watched you carry her stones down the hall," said Harrold.

"I know." Emeric pressed the earth back over the root with the side of his hand, and set the plank down on top of it, gently, so the root could go on under it if it wanted to. "You didn't laugh."

"No," said Harrold. He didn't say anything else.

His mother picked up her hat from her lap and put it on and settled it, and looked up at the two of them from under the brim.

"Tell Ferra the plank wants moving," she said. "Not today. And give your father a purse."



He went down into the town by the hill road, because there was time before the table and he wanted to walk it.

The trams were running. One went past him at the turn below the castle, a gyro car with its flywheel singing under the floor, full of the morning shift from the dye house, and somebody on the back step lifted a hand to him and somebody else didn't bother, because it was half past eight and he was only the king. The street smelt of bread and hot iron and nothing else. He still noticed that every day and never mentioned it, the way a man who has once been very hungry notices every full plate: there was no smoke. The stacks of the old coal station stood up over the lower town against the blue, clean at the lip.

Orrel Marrick was on the station's steps with his cap on the back of his head and a can of oil, and three of the reserve crew behind him with rags.

"Majesty," he said, and didn't get up, because he had a valve on the feed line half done and you didn't get up from that. "Turn-over. Boilers two and four." He gave the valve a quarter turn and listened to it. "Still cold."

"Still able to light?"

Orrel looked up at him with real offence. "Four hours from a cold grate. Same as the night of the fire. Same as it'll be when they bury me, if anybody's still got the sense to send for us." He went back to the valve. "Nobody's sent for us. That's the point of us."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm telling the lads." He jerked his chin at the three with the rags, who were grinning. "They think we're a museum."

Down by the chapel, Corbin was on a ladder.

He was clearing the gutter over the south door in an old shirt with the sleeves cut off, throwing handfuls of wet leaf down into a barrow, and his watcher was sitting on the low wall opposite with a newspaper, as if the two things had nothing to do with each other. The anklet showed above his boot when he reached. Doran stood in the doorway underneath in his cassock with his arms folded, telling him where he had missed some.

"There," said Doran. "To your left. No, your other left."

"I've only got one left, Father."

"Then use it."

Corbin saw Emeric in the street and stopped with a handful of leaf. Then he dropped it in the barrow.

"Majesty."

"Corbin."

That was all it had been, in two years: the word each way, in the street, like a coin passed and passed back. Corbin went back to the gutter. Doran came down the chapel step and walked with Emeric as far as the corner, which he did sometimes.

"He mends things," Doran said, when they were out of earshot. "He's mended every bench in my chapel. He asked if he could. He doesn't talk while he does it, and I find I don't need him to." He stopped at the corner. "Wednesday's confession is still Wednesday. In case you were wondering how he is."

"I was a little."

"Then he's the same," said Doran, "which, for that man, is a great deal," and went back to tell Corbin about the gutter.

Emeric went in at the ward on his way back up, because he had an errand there and wanted to be thrown out of it.

Abel Dunning was sitting up in the clean bed at the end, with the arm in its sleeve on the frame beside him, and at the end of the sleeve, under the gauze, there was a shape that was not a stump any more. It was a hand the way a bud is a flower: small, closed, pink, wrong-sized, with the creases of knuckles in it like lines drawn in wax. Abel was looking at it with a kind of terrified tenderness and holding a bowl of liver in his other hand with no tenderness at all.

"Don't," said Imani from the next bed, where she had Zale.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say it was coming on well. It is. It's not your hand. Go away from it." She didn't look round. She had Zale's head back against the pillow with one hand under his jaw and her thumb holding his left eyelid up, and she was looking into the eye with her sight open and her face as still as a pond. "The window, Zale. Not me."

"You're more interesting than the window."

"I'm busy. Window."

Zale looked at the window. The bruising on his forearm had gone yellow at the edges and brown in the middle. He had a letter on his chest with the Emberfell school's mark on it in green.

"There," said Imani, and let the eyelid go. "Clean. The little vessels at the back have closed. Once more in a week and then I'll be sick of you." She wrote on her sheet. "You can walk. You can't run, and you can't go in any water that isn't a bath."

"There's no water that isn't a bath on a ship."

"Then stay off your ship." She stood and saw Emeric and gave him the look she kept for men who came into her ward without being asked. "You're not sterile. And you smell of Ward."

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"Then everybody's right." But she came round the bed and put her hand flat on his chest over the stone, briefly, and took it away, which was not clinical either, and they both knew it. "Table at one. Ferra's moved it. Waylon wants something."

"I know what he wants."

"Of course you do," said Imani, and went to Abel, who had put down the liver.

Zale held up the letter as Emeric passed.

"My mother," he said. "She says the school's got its new roof and three hundred children under it, and she wants to know whether I'm eating, and whether that's my ship or the crown's, and why she had to read in the Kaldo News that I was near drowned past the edge of the world." He folded it. "I wasn't near drowned. I was near eaten. It's quite different."

"Write and tell her."

"I'm going to." He put the letter back on his chest. "She'll like eaten less."



The long table was laid for business at one, which meant the cloth was off and Ferra's ledger was at the head of it, open, with the agenda clipped to the left-hand page and a pencil on the right, and a pen beside the pencil that she would not pick up until something was decided.

The six were there, and Emeric. Ward had changed his shirt. Bram had not changed anything and had a drawing under his elbow that he was trying not to look at in case somebody asked him what it was. Saban sat in his usual chair with his back to the wall and his hands folded on the table. Armand had his letter out again. Down at the far end, where the table ran toward the windows, sat the others who sat when they were wanted: Keller with his leather case; Vantic in a linen coat the colour of milky tea, with the House's month folded in front of her and a glass of something cold; Waylon nearest the door, with Alina's letter delivered and his question not yet asked; and on the bench under the windows, because the Crestless never took a chair if there was a bench, Arthur, Mira and Bren, with Rylan behind them leaning on the sill.

Imani came in last, from the ward, washed to the elbows, and sat down next to Emeric. In the course of the next quarter of an hour, without anybody at the table seeing it, her shoes came off under the table one after the other, and her feet came up across his knees and settled there. She had her sheet in front of her and was writing on it. She didn't look at him once.

Ferra, at the head, saw. She saw everything that happened at that table, above it or under it. She wrote something in the margin of the agenda that Emeric couldn't read from where he was, and went on.

"Item one," she said. "Milford."

It was a single sheet. She read it the way she read the coal returns, without lifting her voice.

"The barony of Milford advertised nine clerk's posts in March: the barony court, the grain register, the water and the roads. Posted in the News and on the market cross at Milford, Wayford and the river landing, open to anyone over sixteen who could get to Milford hall on the twelfth of May with a pen. Sixty-one sat." She turned the sheet. "Ottoline set the papers. Sums. A letter to a farmer whose water's been cut off by his neighbour. A page of the grain register with six mistakes in it to be found. The charter's article on hearings put into plain words. Each of them wrote a number at the top instead of a name, and Ottoline marked by number. She didn't know whose was whose until Master Aubin opened the envelope of names in front of the Milford court on the twentieth."

"Who's got them?" said Ward.

Ferra read the nine names. They were not names anybody at the table knew. A miller's daughter from the ford. A widower who had kept the Milford grange's accounts for twenty years without ever being called a clerk. A girl of seventeen from the night school whose father couldn't read. Two of the old steward's men, who had been clerks already and sat the paper with everybody else and passed it. A boy from the stock pens. A woman from Grayrock who had walked in the night before. The steward's old clerk for the roads, eighth.

"That's eight," said Bram.

"The ninth was a tie. Two at the same mark. The charter says ties go by public lot, so Prudence Fairley drew it out of a hat in front of the court."

"Whose hat?" said Waylon.

"Prudence's. She was very firm that it be her own." Ferra didn't smile. "The other one can sit again in December. So can anybody who failed. Fifty-two failed."

"And among the fifty-two," said Saban, in the reasonable voice, "is the old steward's younger son, who was promised the grain register by his father in a letter I've seen, and has been telling the Milford taproom for a month that the paper was rigged to keep the old families out."

"He wrote four lines to the farmer," said Ferra. "The first one was spelt wrong."

"I'm not saying he should have it. I'm saying he's angry and he's got friends." Saban unfolded his hands and folded them the other way. "And I'll say the other thing, since nobody else will. You printed the papers in the News. All four. Anybody in this kingdom who wants a man of his own on a Kaldo register now knows exactly what to teach him."

"Then he'll have taught a man to find six mistakes in a grain register and write a civil letter to a farmer," said Ferra, "and I'll have a good clerk with a bad master, and I'll watch the master." She wrote. "Noted. Saban: the papers printed, a risk. Saban: the steward's son."

"Thank you."

"The appeal comes to my court," said Keller from the far end, "under the Hearing Rule. If the son wants to say the paper was rigged, he can come and say it to me, with the paper. Nobody has." He looked mildly regretful. "I should rather like somebody to."

"Next year the other three baronies," said Ferra. "Then the crown's own." She looked down the table. "Waylon."

Waylon had his mouth open already. He shut it.

"Alina wrote to me in May and asked for the papers for the north," said Ferra. "I sent them in June. They'll be at the duchy before you're home. Ottoline's written you a page on how to mark by number without anybody being able to cheat it, which she says is harder than it sounds, and she wants a copy of your results, because she's curious."

Waylon looked at her for a while.

"I came down on a train," he said, "with a man snoring in the third carriage, to ask you that."

"I know. It was a nice thought." Ferra picked up the pen and made a small mark in ink, and that was Milford. "Item two."

Emeric hadn't said anything. He noticed that at the end of item one, and noticed that nobody had asked him to. Imani's foot moved on his knee, once, as if she'd noticed too.

Item two was the Isle's quarter: the condominium's accounts from Duvrenne's side and theirs, which didn't agree by forty-one, and which Ferra had already found the forty-one in, a bale of cloth counted twice at the Isle landing, and was only bringing to the table so that the minutes would show she had found it. Item three was Bram's drawing, which turned out to be the housing for a thing Tess wanted and nobody had built, with a dish on top like a soup plate stood on its edge. Bram would not say what it was for until Tess had a thing to put in it. He only wanted the table to know that the housing would be ready first, as usual.

Item four was the post. A letter from Dagny at Wayford: the last of the mains was in on the river side, six of her team were holdings-born now and two of them were better than her, and she wanted that written down somewhere so it could be held against her later. A letter from Sabina, four streets away, which came every Thursday and was nominally about the weather; this week it was about the weather and the price of thread on the river market, which Sabina considered a scandal. Ferra read the scandal out. Then a letter with the capital's post-mark, in a hand so large and slanted that Emeric could read it upside down: You both promised.

"Maeve," said Ferra.

"What did we promise?" said Imani, writing.

"To write. She says that since the wedding she's had two letters from us, and one of them was a list."

"That was you."

"It was a very good list. It had everything she'd asked about on it, in order." Ferra read on, and her mouth went a little crooked at one corner. "She says if she doesn't have a proper letter, with weather and feelings in it, by Lantern Night, she'll come down on the people's train and live with us until she gets one, and she's not joking, and Harrold's to be told she's not joking." She folded it. "Imani writes the next one."

"Why me?"

"Feelings," said Ferra, and put it on the pile.

The last was Vantic's.

She didn't read it out. She put two fingers on the House's month and spoke from memory, which she preferred, in the flat quick merchant's voice that never sounded as if anything was very important and was most careful when it was.

"Three things from the factors. One. The Core's court sheet for May printed Waylon's betrothal. Two lines, under the Church's notices, between a bishop's death and a tariff on salt." She looked down the table at Waylon. "You were spelt correctly. Alina wasn't. They've given her an extra n."

"She'll be livid," said Waylon.

"Two. The Core's banks have stopped Duvrenne's credit. Not called it; stopped it. No new paper. Duvrenne's merchants found out at the spring fair when they went to draw." Vantic turned her glass a quarter on the table. "They've stopped ours as well. We never drew on theirs, so it costs us nothing, and they know it costs us nothing, and they did it anyway. So that people would see."

"And three," said Ferra.

"Three's the one I don't like." Vantic took her fingers off the paper. "There's a second Church man at the Emperor's table now. My factor doesn't know his name. He sits on the left, where the first one sits on the right. He came in April with no notice in any sheet, and the factor only knows he's there because the chamberlain has started ordering for two."

Nobody said anything. Out of the windows behind Rylan the harbour was bright and busy, and a sodium van went along the quay with its little whine, and down the hill the school bell rang for the afternoon.

"They know," said Vantic. "That's what all three say. Two crowns on their edge sitting at one table now, and about to sit at one wedding. And the king of one of them sent their gold back twice." She picked up her glass. "In the Core they think of us as one family. They're right. It's the first thing they've been right about in a while, and I'd rather they weren't."

"Noted," said Ferra, and wrote all three in ink.



He went back up to the dome after the table, by himself.

The bench was empty. His mother and father had gone down to the river market with Harrold's card and no purse. Her own body lay where it lay, and the tree hummed over it.

Emeric sat down on the gravel with his back against the braided trunk, where he had sat on the night of his stone and a great many nights since, and put his left hand flat on the nearest root, the big one that went down under the mattress's frame and away into the dark under the floor, and shut his eyes, and opened his room.

It was what it always was. The long low workshop of a place, the benches, the drawings on the walls that he had never put there, the window at the end that did not look out on anything in this world. He stood in it and let the stone's warmth settle.

And the floor. By the far wall, where the boards weren't boards, the head of the stair Armand had shown him on the night of his stone, going down. It went down into black water. The water was very still, and nobody had ever found a wall to it, and on it, far off, were the lights of other rooms, the way a town looks from a hill at night. Bram's, like a forge with the door shut. Imani's. Ferra's. Ward's, a lamp behind a wall. Armand's, close. His mother's, closest of all, warm, here.

And cold, coming up the stair, the way cold comes up out of a cellar when somebody's left the door open at the bottom.

He knew the one note in it. Far off, where it had sat since the winter of the war, in a dead man's house, wearing a dead man's face; and only three people in the world knew whose. It hadn't moved in months. It hadn't needed to. It had a second man at the Emperor's table instead.

And under that, much further down, much larger, the thing Armand had looked at once from the bottom of his own stair and come back up from, very glad of Imani's face in a crooked mask. Old, and cold the way the deep sea is cold, with something moving in it. Emeric had never looked at it. He didn't now. He stood at the top of his stair with his hands at his sides and felt its cold on his face, and the root bead warm on his wrist, a latch on his side of the door.

He didn't go down.

He thought about the Core instead, because it was a thing he could think about. The Church had sat beside the throne there for four hundred years, and between them they had made the kingdoms on the Core's edge what they were: thin, careful, poor, frightened of their own groves, ruled by men who were paid to stay small. Now two of those kingdoms had stopped being small. And the Core had answered by spelling Alina's name wrong and stopping a credit nobody drew on. That was what a frightened house did. It closed doors.

They had closed one on him, once. The long hall, the laughing, the steps, the dark. A chest of dead stones and a box of toy soldiers and the road south to a ruined town at the end of the coast, where nobody would ever have to look at him again.

Every door they closed had opened another. He had never put it into words before, and he didn't say it aloud now. He thought of the white root under Bram's plank, going sideways as if there were somewhere it particularly wanted to be.

He shut the floor and came up out of the room into the dome's cool air, with the tree humming against his back, and somebody was hammering on the outer door.



It was Wren.

She had come up the hill road on her motorcycle; he could hear it ticking as it cooled. She knew better than to come past the dome's outer door and didn't try. When Mabry opened it she was standing on the step with her goggles pushed up into her hair and a slip of Tess's paper held out at arm's length, as if it might go off.

"The island," she said. "Tess says read it now. She's sent the same to the base and the long room, and Ferra's already got hers, because Ferra was in the Motion Shop arguing about the soup plate."

He took it. It was in Tess's hand, the letters squared off, copied from the pad as it came: Fletcher's operator on South Sentinel, keying slowly and with care.

3.40. Lights in the water off the lee. Many. Under the surface, moving, keeping off the reef. Not fish. Guest on the slab, standing, will not come in. Fletcher asks for orders. Fletcher asks for Zale.

And under it, in Tess's own pencil: Sent again 3.52, same. Nothing since. T.

He read it twice.

"How fast can you get to the ward?"

"On that?" Wren looked offended on the machine's behalf. "Before you've finished asking."

"Tell Imani. Tell Zale he's coming and he's not to argue about it, and Imani's to say whether he can, and then tell him again."

She was gone. The motorcycle coughed and caught and went off down the hill road with a sound like a saw going through a plank.



The long room, when he got to it, had already decided most of it without him.

Ferra was at the head of the table with the ledger open to a clean page and her own copy of Tess's slip pinned to the top of it, writing a list, and Saban was standing at her shoulder reading it as it came and saying nothing, in the way that meant he had a great deal to say and was waiting to be asked. Ward was at the window with Rylan. Down on the base road Emeric could see Declan's company already moving.

"The knights," said Ferra, without looking up. "Ward's choosing. The Crestless. The four sentries. You. Me. Imani, if she'll come, which she will. Zale, for the language. Saban."

"Saban," said Saban, "would like it noted that he's going because somebody has to count how many of them there are and remember their faces, since they're about to see the one piece of ground in the world nobody was ever supposed to see."

"Noted," said Ferra.

"Written. Not noted."

"It's written." She turned the book round. Saban: the island seen. A cost. He read it and nodded and didn't look any less grim.

"It may only be her people," said Ward from the window.

"It's certainly only her people," said Saban. "That's my point. Her people are people. People talk."

Bram had his drawing rolled under his arm and was standing by the door with the face of a man who had been told he wasn't going, and had already had the argument in his head and lost it there. "Somebody has to keep the city," he said, to nobody. "I know. I know somebody has to."

"You and Armand," said Ferra. "And Tess on the pad. And Harrold and Waylon, when they're back from the market. And Keller, if anybody needs a hearing." She looked up at Emeric for the first time. "Your mother's canister's out at four. Somebody should be in the dome when she comes back."

"Armand," said Emeric.

"Armand," Armand agreed, from his chair. He was looking at Emeric's face, and then at his wrist, and then at his face again, and seemed to find what he was looking for, because he sat back. "I'll sit with her. Go and see what's in the water."

Vantic was on her feet at the end of the table with the House's month under her arm. She wasn't going. On her way out she stopped at Emeric's elbow, close enough that her sleeve touched his, and said low, in the flat voice, "Bring me back a merchant, if they've got merchants," and then, "or don't," and went.

Ferra closed the ledger and put it under her arm.

"The hull's on the castle mooring," she said. "Ward says twenty minutes."

"Ward said fifteen," said Ward.

"Ward says twenty," said Ferra, "because Imani has to come up the hill with her bag, and she'll want to put her shoes back on."



They lifted at twenty past four.

The hull came up off the castle mooring with her stones humming and the town going small and bright under the keel: the trams like toys on the hill road, the river market a spill of awnings by the water where, somewhere, a gold-eyed man with no purse was buying something with his card for a woman in a sand-coloured dress. Emeric didn't look for them. He looked at the sea.

The deck was full and quiet. Rylan's knights and Declan's and Osric's along the rail in plate, helms in their hands; Arthur and Mira and Bren together near the bow in bare plate, no crests, looking at the same piece of horizon; the four sentries in the hold, upright in their frames, seven feet each, their slot helms dark. Ward stood by the wheel and didn't touch it.

Zale sat on a coil of line by the mainmast with his legs out in front of him and a stick across his knees, because Imani had said he could come if he sat, and he was sitting with the air of a man who would stand up the moment there was anything to stand up for and had told her so. His mother's letter was in his coat. He was looking south.

Imani came and stood by Emeric at the rail with her bag at her feet and her shoes back on. Ferra came and stood on his other side with the ledger.

"Many," said Imani. "What's many, to Fletcher's man?"

"More than he wanted to count," said Ferra.

"That's what I was afraid of."

The coast fell away behind them, the cliffs and then the long beaches and then nothing, and the sun went down the sky at their backs and laid the hull's shadow out ahead of them on the water, long and thin. Ferra opened the ledger on the rail, held it flat against the wind with her forearm, and wrote the time, and under it: Out to South Sentinel. Lights in the lee. She looked at that. Then she wrote under it, smaller, in pencil, because nothing was done yet: Guests?

"You've given them a question mark," said Emeric.

"I don't know what they are yet." She closed the book. "When I know, they'll get ink."

Ahead, a long way off, very low where the sea met the evening, there was the first dark smudge of the island.
