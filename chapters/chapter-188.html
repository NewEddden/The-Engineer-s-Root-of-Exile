Chapter 188 - The Manifest

Saban's man in the capital wrote on the tenth of December, by the crown's post, which meant he wrote nothing he minded the crown reading; and what he wrote was that there was a story going about.

Several stories. That was the trouble with them, Saban said, reading the letter aloud at the long table on the fifteenth. One story could be answered. Five could not, because answering any of them meant admitting there was something to answer. In one, the first queen's niece had run away from her own wedding with a groom, and her father was too ashamed to say so. In another, Lord Sallow had been visited in the night by men in black who had come down out of the clouds on wings, and had taken his grandfather's sword off the wall without touching it, and laid it at his feet, which every priest in the north said was a thing only devils could do. In a third the men had been Kaldo's, and in a fourth they had been the duke in the north's, and in a fifth there had been no men at all and Lord Sallow had dreamed it, drunk, and woken to find his daughter gone with a knight and his household too frightened of him to say they had let her go.

"The first queen has heard the first one," said Saban. "And the third. She's said so at dinner. Not the details, because she hasn't any. Only that the prince who refused her niece in February seems to have found another use for her in November, and that she hopes the girl is happy in a ditch." He turned his hat once on his knee. "The king didn't laugh this time either. My man says he went very still."

"And Corbin?"

"Corbin's heard the second," said Saban. "The one with the wings. He's asked three men at court whether it's true that his little brother can fly." He put the letter down. "None of them knew. All of them have decided it was Kaldo. Nobody at court can say what happened, or how, or prove a word of it." He picked up his hat. "And everybody at court has decided we did it, and is afraid of us for it, a little, for the first time. Which is a price, and I'll put it in the book. And a kind of coin, and I'll put that in the book too."

"Put both," said Ferra, writing. "In two columns."



Ferra settled the manifest on the twentieth.

She settled it the way she settled the quarter, on her own, at her desk, with the counting room's door shut and a notice on the outside of it in Hale's hand that said the chancellor was not to be disturbed unless something was on fire, and then only if it was a large fire. It took her two days. When she opened the door on the morning of the twenty-second, she had a sheet of the mill's best paper, ruled in her own hand, with every soul and every pound that would go up off Kaldo's slip at midwinter morning on it, and the K Discovery's lift at the foot in a double-ruled line, and the margin between the two, which was not large.

She pinned it to the shed door herself.

Twelve souls. Emeric. Ward. Fletcher at the helms, both of them, because he would not let anybody else land her and would not let anybody else take her up. Orrin and Vesk, for the stones, who had never been higher than the keep's leads and had been talking about it in the charging room for a month. Saban, for Rowena's harbor dues and her clerks and everything else at Emberfell that would have a price on it. Vantic, at her own asking, as Livia. Maren, for the galley. Cade and Tobe, for the deck and the dials. Imani, with her bag. And Ferra, for the book.

Under the souls, the cabins. Four of them, felt-lined, two berths each, and the forward berths behind their curtains; and against each cabin, in Ferra's hand, the names.

The holder and the captain of the guard, cabin one.

Orrin and Vesk, cabin three.

Vantic, cabin four. Alone. And beside it, in the margin, smaller: The House pays for its own berth. At the coast rate.

Saban, forward, with the crew. Beside that, in the margin: He'll complain. It will be good for him.

And cabin two. Emeric read it twice, standing at the shed door in the cold with the river going by behind him, because it was the shortest line on the sheet and he wanted to be sure he had read it right.

Imani with me. Space is weight.

That was all. Four words and three, and no margin note at all. As if it were the most obvious line on the manifest, which perhaps it was, since two women in one cabin weighed exactly what two women in two cabins weighed and took up half the felt. He looked at it for a long time. Then he went into the shed, and did not say anything about it to anybody, and found that Fletcher, at the bench, was looking at him with an expression of great innocence and had very plainly read the sheet already.

"Space is weight," said Fletcher. "The chancellor's right. I've been saying so for two years."

"You've been saying it about the galley."

"Same thing," said Fletcher, and went back to his bench, whistling Cade's tune, which he had caught off Cade on the second run north and could not now get rid of.



The suits went into crates on the twenty-first, under Ferra's seals.

Two of them were going. His own, the thin black one with no levers, and Ward's, in its black plate with its lions. And the third, which was a gift, and went into a crate of its own, longer than the others because of the leg, and lined with felt, and sealed at both ends with the barony's seal and a card in Ferra's hand tied to the lid, addressed to Her Majesty Queen Rowena of Emberfell, with the Holder of Kaldo's duty.

"She'll open it on the quay," said Ward, watching Fletcher's men nail the lid down. "In front of her knights."

"Probably."

"Then she'll put it on in front of her knights," said Ward. "And try to fly it in front of them, before you've shown her how. And fall in her own harbor." He was quiet. "I'd like to see that."

"She won't fall."

"No," said Ward, after a moment. "She won't. That's what I'd like to see."

The ship's last fittings went in the same week, from Fletcher's list.

The list had been longer before the two runs north. They had been the longest flights she had ever made, the better part of a night out and the same back, low, in November, and Fletcher had come home from both of them with his hands blue and a page of notes in his pocket that he had written in the dark at the bow helm with a pencil, by feel. Cold at the rails, on deck, where a man had to stand to work the ladder, and his hands went dead in ten minutes even in gloves. Cold in the bow cockpit, from the panes, which were Ivo's best glass and let the night straight through. Not enough heat in the saloon floor once she was flat out over the sea, where the air off the water went through her hull's oak like a blade. And the ladder, Bram's ladder, which had been a better ladder than the knights' rope and was still not good enough, because a man in plate on a ladder in the dark swinging over a road could not see his own feet.

So the heater stones under the saloon floor went from four to six, and two more under the bow cockpit's seats, in iron boxes, with their exhaust under the floorboards. The rails got sleeves of oiled leather over felt, Abloh's, so that a gloved hand on them did not freeze to them. Bram made the ladder again, for the third time, with every tread painted white on its upper face so that a man could see it in the dark, and a guide line down the middle of it with a running ring, so that a knight in plate could clip his belt to it and not fall off if his hands went. Ambrose came down from the tower with a coil of copper strap and a box of rivets and spent two days in the ship's hull, bonding every metal fitting in her, every hinge and bolt and bracket and the iron boxes of the heater stones, to the lightning staff at her stern, with a strap; so that if anything ever came down out of the sky at her while she was up in it, it would go round her and out of her and into the water or the air, and not through anybody on the way.

"Nothing's ever hit her," said Fletcher, watching Ambrose crawl along a frame with his strap.

"Nothing's ever hit a lot of things," said Ambrose, from the frame, cheerfully. "Right up until the once."

The battery lamps went in, Tess's, lead plates in acid in a sealed box under the saloon floor with wires to every cabin, so that there would be light aboard her in a dead zone, where no stone would light anything. Breath candles went into the sealed locker beside the battery: a stick of chlorate and iron powder in a tin can with a match in its top, that would burn for a quarter of an hour and give out, as it burned, air a man could breathe, if she was ever sealed and her air was ever bad. Ivo had made two hundred of them in the dyehouse and burned twelve in a sealed barrel to prove them, and the twelfth had nearly taken his eyebrows. A chute for every soul aboard, packed by Abloh, hung on a hook by every berth, with a card on it in Tess's hand that said, as all her cards did: DON'T OPEN IT INDOORS.

And Imani's packing.

She brought it down to the shed herself on the twenty-second, in a box, and would not let anybody else carry it. Linen packing for the nose, rolled tight, in waxed paper, two hundred rolls, because Armand had told her what the men on the climbs had told him about the bleeding sickness, and she believed it; and twelve cork jackets, Abloh's again, one for every soul, in case they came down on the water; and her bag, with the ether in its padded case and the needles and the salve. She went up the ladder with the box under one arm and put it in the sick bay herself, and stowed it, and came down again, and stood on the slip and looked up at the ship.

"I've never been on a voyage," she said. "Of any kind."

"You've been north twice."

"That wasn't a voyage. That was a night's work." She looked at the dome. "This is the sky." She was quiet. "Ferra's put me in her cabin."

"I saw."

"Space is weight," said Imani. Something happened at the corner of her mouth. "She told me that before she wrote it. In my kitchen. As if I might argue." She folded her arms. "I didn't argue."



The forge made the last two things the ship needed on the nights of the eighteenth and the nineteenth, and neither of them was big, and both of them were the most delicate things it had ever made.

The first was a box the size of a pocket watch, of thin corrugated metal, sealed, with the air drawn out of it. That was all it was. A little flat drum with ridges round its face like the ridges on a scallop shell, so thin that a thumb could dent it; and when the air outside it grew thinner, as it did the higher anybody went, the drum swelled, the width of a hair, and when the air grew thicker it shrank, the width of a hair. An aneroid. The forge stamped its two faces out of a strip of the converter's best spring brass, and sealed them, and drew the air out through a pin hole and sealed the pin hole, all in one run, which no metalworker in Randillia could have done by hand in a year. Bram mounted it in a case with a train of levers and a hair spring and a pointer, so that the width of a hair the drum swelled became the width of a hand on a dial. And Linus took it.

He took it up the keep stair to the leads, and down to the river, and up the north tower, and to the top of the church tower, which he climbed in the frost on the twentieth with the dial in a box under his coat; and at every height he read it, and read his own barometer beside it, the mercury one he had kept the weather with for four years, and wrote both down. Then he put it in a cradle under a bell jar in the tower and pumped the air out of the jar a little at a time with Tess's mercury pump, to pretend it was going up a mile, then two, then three; and read it at every stroke; and drew a table. When the table was done he painted a new face for the dial himself, in his small exact hand, in feet: a thousand, two thousand, five, ten, twenty thousand at the top, where nobody had ever been.

"An altimeter," said Emeric, when it was done. "That's what it's called."

"It's a barometer that's been told what it's for," said Linus. He looked at the dial. "It'll be wrong. A little. Every day the weather's different, and it'll read the weather as height. I've put a screw on the back to set it each morning against the barometer on the ground." He gave it to Fletcher. "It's the best I can do without a mountain."

The second thing was smaller. It was a hair spring for a watch, coiled flat, of a steel the forge drew finer than any wire it had ever drawn, finer than the thread of the bulbs; and two jewels.

The jewels had taken a fortnight. They were red. They had begun as a jar of alum from the dyehouse, the stuff Abloh used to fix his dyes, white powder, and a pinch of the dyehouse's chrome yellow; and the forge had burned the alum to a finer white powder, and dropped the powder a grain at a time through a flame hotter than any furnace, onto a seed, so that it melted as it fell and froze where it landed, layer on layer, for six nights, until there stood on the forge's bed a pear of clear red stone the size of a man's thumb. A ruby. Not a ruby out of the ground; a ruby out of a dyehouse jar. Then the forge cut two tiny discs off it, each the width of a lentil, and drilled each one through the middle with a hole a hair could have gone through, and polished them.

Ottoline Fesk made the clock.

Fletcher had asked for it. He had said, at the long table, in October, that a ship's master who could not tell how far east or west he had come by looking at the sun at noon was a ship's master who would one day sail into the side of a hill; and that to look at the sun at noon and know how far east or west he had come, he needed to know, on his own ship, exactly what the time was at Kaldo, to the second, after a fortnight at sea; and that there was no clock in Randillia that would keep time to the second for a fortnight on a ship that rolled. Ottoline had made adding machines for the counting room and the tabulator for the census and the exchange's selectors; and she had looked at him across the table with her magnifying glass on its cord and said that she had wanted for twenty years to make the clock he was describing and had never had a spring fine enough or a bearing hard enough to make it with.

She made it in three weeks in her shop under the clock tower in the square. A marine chronometer. A brass case in a box of oak, slung in a pair of rings so that whatever the ship did, the clock stayed level; a balance wheel of two metals that would not run fast in the cold or slow in the heat; the forge's hair spring; and the balance staff turning on the forge's two red jewels, so that it would run for a month and not wear the width of a hair.

She was never told about the ship. She was told it was for Fletcher's boats, for the coast run. She did not ask what boat Fletcher had that needed a clock good to the second for a fortnight. She brought it up the keep stair in its box on the nineteenth, herself, and wound it, and set it against the counting room's clock, and handed it to Fletcher.

"It'll lose two seconds a day," she said. "Perhaps three. Every day the same. Write it down, and take it off, and you'll know the hour in Kaldo to the breath, wherever you are." She looked at him over her glass. "Wherever you are," she said again, as if it were a question, and when Fletcher did not answer it, she smiled, and went back down the stair.



Armand would not fly. He had said so in October, at the long table, and every week since, and nobody had argued with him, because he was eighty-two and his heart was what it was and the K Discovery was going up into air that had made younger men bleed from the nose. He said it again on the twenty-second, in his room, with the fire up and his blanket over his knees, when Emeric came in the evening to sit with him.

"I'll not go," said Armand. "You'll want to ask me again. Don't."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You've had the face on since the stair." Armand looked at the fire. "Sit down. I'll tell you about my master. Once more, and then you'll have it, and I'll not tell it again."

He had told it once, in the spring, over the pretty jar. The climbs. Two mages, one on the other's back, with a sack of stones, going up and up into the cold, two miles, three, until the air thinned and their heads went light and the mana grew so thick round them that a man could feel it on his skin like fog; and the stones that would not fill, and the coming down, every time, half frozen and empty-handed. And the letter from the Church that closed it. He told it again now, slower, by the fire, and some of it was different, because he was remembering it better or worse; and at the end of it he said something he had not said before.

"My master took a lamp stone up on his last climb," said Armand. "On a cord. Hanging off his belt. A spent one, grey, from the charging room's bucket, that would light if there was anything in the air to light it with and not otherwise." He was quiet. "He said it brightened. All the way up. Brighter and brighter, as the mana thickened. And then, at the top, where they stopped because they couldn't go on, he said it brightened faster." He looked at Emeric. "Not steadily. Faster. As if there were something above them that they couldn't reach, that was thicker still. As if they'd come to the bottom of something." He turned back to the fire. "The tower laughed at him. A spent stone on a cord. A boy's trick. They said the cold had got into his eyes." He was quiet for a long time. "He told me so at the foot of the tower stair, the week before the letter came. I was nobody. I was carrying his books." He moved his hands on the blanket. "I've thought for fifty years that I should have asked him what he meant by the bottom of something."

Emeric sat by the fire.

"The probe," he said.

"Your probe. On its cord, outside the hull." Armand did not look at him. "Pack it. Whatever else you leave behind. Watch it on the way up. And if it brightens faster at the top, you'll know my master wasn't cold in the eyes." He shut his own. "And then you'll come home and tell me what he meant, and I'll have waited fifty years for it, and I'd like to hear it before my heart decides I've waited long enough."



Bram gave him the brooch on the twenty-third, in the forge, at the end of the shift, without ceremony, out of his apron pocket.

It was a star. Silver, eight points, plain, the size of a child's palm, with no engraving on it at all; and in the middle of the star, set in a silver collar that Bram had raised by hand round it, a grey stone. Amara's. The second of the two Harrold had given Ward to bring home, two years ago, at court. The first had gone into his band, the first bead, the one that sat against his wrist inside every gauntlet and had flown his suit. The second had a chip out of one face, an old break, from before Harrold had ever had it; and the forge would not take a chipped stone for a bead, because a bead had to be whole, and so it had lain in its box in the cellar for two years doing nothing, and Bram had looked at it there, and asked, in November, if he might.

"For the coat," said Bram. "Your dining coat. For the queen." He put it in Emeric's hand. "She knew your mother. You said."

"She did."

"Then she'll know whose stone it is." Bram took his apron off and hung it on its hook. "That chip. You can feel it with your thumb, on the back. I set it so the chip's at the back, against the coat. So it doesn't show." He looked at the brooch in Emeric's palm. "A thing can be broken and still be worth wearing. You only have to set it right."

The dining coat came from Abloh the same evening, in a box: charcoal wool, cut close, with silver at the cuffs and the collar and a lining of grey silk, because Ferra had said, in November, that the prince could not go to dinner with a queen in a suit of black plate or in the coat he had worn to the king's banquet when he was a drunk, and that he would go in charcoal and silver, which were his colours whether he knew it or not. He put it on in his room, and pinned the brooch to it, on the left, over the heart, the way Bram had shown him, with the chip against the wool. The grey stone sat in its silver collar against the charcoal and did not light, and did not do anything at all.

He stood at his window for a while in the coat, looking at the river.

The letter to Rowena had gone on the eighteenth, by the coaster, south. It said that the Holder of Kaldo would call at Emberfell at midwinter, with a small party, by sea, for a few days, if Her Majesty would receive him, and that he hoped she was well. The coaster would put in at Emberfell a week after midwinter. The letter would reach her after he did. That had been on purpose, and Ferra had argued against it, and he had overruled her, and she had written it down. He thought, standing at the window, that he would very likely be wrong about that one too.



On the last night he went into the room and checked every stone.

Every stone in the ship. Every stone in the two crated suits and the third. Every case and every cell and every heater and the probe on its cord and the stones in the matrix that held her up, every one he had cut himself, and only he could change; against Tess's list. Tess had made the list in October, in her book, a page for every group, every stone with its number and its job and what its note should feel like when it was right. He sat on the floor of the room with the list open in front of him and went down it, line by line, stone by stone, with the whole ship laid out round him in the room's grey light like a map of a body, and felt each one, and ticked it.

It took him until the fourth hour. Then he went down it again. Twice. Because Tess's list had a line at the top of the first page, in her careful hand, that said: CHECK IT TWICE. AND THEN DON'T TOUCH ANYTHING.

All of them were right.

And two notes he had not had on the list, because they were not stones, and he had not written them down for anybody. Cade's. Tobe's. Two yard hands, picked for the voyage out of Fletcher's men and the foundry's because they were steady on a ladder and good with a dial; and because, though nobody had asked him and he had told nobody, they had each of them worked for four years beside charged stone, in the yard and the casting shed, all day, every day, the way nobody else in Kaldo but the still crew had. He did not know what that would mean in the Band. He did not know if it would mean anything. He had chosen them partly so that he might find out, and he had not told them, and he sat in the room at the fourth hour with their two notes in his sense, steady and ordinary, and wondered whether that made him a careful man or a bad one.

He did not decide. He left the room.

Outside the keep it had begun to snow. Not much. A thin dry snow, out of a dark sky, coming down past his window into the yard in the light of the arc lamps on the wall, and lying on the yard's cobbles in a white skin, and on the shed roof down by the river, and on the river wall; the first snow of the winter, on the eve of midwinter, on the night before the K Discovery would go up off her slip into the sky in daylight for the first time with twelve souls aboard her and climb until she found whatever was there to find.

He stood at the window and watched it come down. Two floors below him, along the passage under her name, a lamp was still lit under Ferra's door, as it always was. And in his sense, close together in her room, two notes. One small and hard and close. One wide and warm. Not asleep. Talking, he thought; the long low way of two women who had a great deal still to say and no hurry about saying it. Probably about the manifest.

He shut his sense, which he had not done at night for a year, and let them have it to themselves; and lay down in his clothes, and did not sleep, and was not sorry.
