Chapter 188 - Hull Four

There were two boxes, and Bram had made them both himself, out of elm boards from the yard's offcut stack, because he did not want anybody else's joints in the experiment.

They were the size of a small cask laid on its side, open at the top, with a rope bridle through a hole at each end. Into the first he had shovelled the mortar the masons laid on the flat every day of the season: slaked lime from the kilns and sharp sand off the quarry screens, one to three, worked wet. Into the second he had put the same lime and no sand at all. Where the sand should have been there was a grey powder, fine as flour, that he had made by running six sacks of Emberfell pumice through the ore stamp at the foundry and then through the finest sieve in the pattern store, twice. It had taken him two evenings. The pumice had come out of Saban's shed on the quay, from the last lot off the Marsh Hen, which had been going on to a builder in Wayford the week after the frost and had not gone, because Ferra had written to the builder to say it was no longer for sale.

He had filled both boxes on the second slip at the bottom of the ebb, and walked them down into the water on their bridles with Fletcher on the other rope, and let them go off the end of the slip where the lower water stood a fathom deep at the bottom of the tide. He had tied a cork to each bridle so they could be found. He had not told anybody what was in either box except Emeric, who had asked for the second one, and Fletcher, who had held the rope.

Ten days later he brought them up.

It was a raw morning, the fourth week of the winter, with a wind straight off the sea and a grey chop on the lower water that slapped against the end of the slip and threw itself back. Fletcher's shear legs stood over the slip's end on their splayed feet, and Fletcher's second, Cade, took the first bridle onto the tackle and heaved, and the box came up out of the water streaming, with weed already starting on the boards, and swung in over the slip and came down on the stones.

Emeric stood back from it with his hands in his coat. Ward stood a pace behind him and a pace to the side, where he always stood, looking at the yard wall and not at the box.

The wall was new enough that the stone still had its quarry colour. It had gone up through the summer on the rest days of two of the earth mages, course by course on the footing they had cut to the rock in the spring, and been closed at the end of the season with a gate of oak and iron on the side toward the town. It ran from the rock of the ridge down to the water on both sides of the flat Bram had levelled, and it was higher than a man on another man's shoulders, and nothing inside it could be seen from the flat or the lower water unless you stood on the ridge itself and looked straight down. The canvas over hull two's berth was behind it now. So was the dock's outline, a hand deep in the grey rock, with the frost's rime in it this morning like chalk.

Bram knocked the boards off the first box with a hammer. They came away wet and heavy, and what was inside stood on the slip in the shape of the box, a grey block that looked like a block of mortar and nothing else.

He took a mason's chisel out of his apron and set it on the top face and hit it once, not hard.

The chisel went in to the width of two fingers. He drew it out, and a curl of grey came out with it on the blade, soft and wet, and he held it up so they could see it, and then he rubbed it between his thumb and finger and it smeared.

"Cheese," he said. "Same as the prince said. It'll be cheese at the spring and cheese at midsummer. It'll set when it sees the air and not before." He wiped his thumb on his apron. "That's the dock, if we build it with what we build the wall with. Every joint in it like that, under the gate. The first time you pump her dry, the sea walks back in through the walls."

Fletcher looked at the curl on the chisel and said nothing.

Cade brought up the second box. Bram knocked the boards off it, and the block inside looked exactly the same as the first, grey and wet and square, and Emeric found he was holding his breath and let it go.

Bram set the chisel on it and hit it once, not hard.

The chisel did not go in. It knocked, a dull short sound like a chisel on soft stone, and jumped a little off the face and left a pale scratch the length of a fingernail. Bram looked at the scratch. Then he set the chisel again and hit it properly, the way he would have hit it to split a stone, and it bit in to the thickness of a coin and no further, and a chip the size of a thumbnail came away from the corner and skittered across the slip into the water.

Bram picked at the broken corner with his thumbnail. It did not smear. He looked at it for longer than he had looked at the whole of the first block.

"Ten days," he said. "In the salt. In the cold." He put his palm flat on the block's top, the way Armand put his palm on stones. "It's not done. It's nowhere near done. But it's going the right way, under water, in the cold, and the other one isn't going anywhere. Anything that's got this far in ten days has got a year of going harder in it yet." He took his hand off. "I sank a third one. It's still down. I'll bring it up at the thaw and hit that one too."

"The old people knew this," Emeric said. "Somewhere. With the ash off a mountain like Rowena's. They built harbours with it that stood a thousand years in the sea."

Bram did not ask what old people, or where. He had stopped asking that kind of thing a long time ago, and Emeric had stopped noticing that he had stopped. Bram only said, "Then it wants a great deal of ash," and began to count sacks in his head, and Emeric could see him doing it.

Fletcher had gone round the second block while they talked. He stood on its far side now with his hands behind his back, looking down at it, and then up across the levelled flat at the outline in the rock, and then at Emeric.

"So you can build your dock," he said.

"Yes."

"Bram drew it. I drew the wall round it. Ferra wrote it in her book in pencil. Two years it's been a line." Fletcher looked back at the outline. "I've never asked you what's going in it. I built you a boat that turned out to be something else, and I've built three more of the something else since, and I never asked you what any of them was for before you told me. I'm asking now. Before anybody cuts that rock."

"Tonight," Emeric said. "At your table. I've brought the drawings."

Fletcher looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded at Cade, and Cade put the tackle on the second block and swung it up and set it at the head of the slip against the wall, where the broken corner caught the light. Nobody told him to put it there. He did it the way a man sets a thing where he means to go on looking at it.

Fletcher's cottage had not changed since the first night Emeric had sat in it. The table was still too big for the room. The wall behind it was still covered with drawings, pinned edge to edge and over one another where the pins had run out: hulls, all of them, in pencil and in ink, some finished to the last rivet and some only lines. Luggers and cogs and a long narrow galley-built thing with oars, and a three-master with a flush deck that had been drawn and redrawn so many times the paper had gone soft at the folds. None of them had ever been built. Emeric had asked about them once, the first year, and Fletcher had said that a man with a yard builds what he is paid to build, and draws what he would have built, and does not confuse the two.

There were five of them round the table. Fletcher at the end nearest the fire, because it was his house. Bram on the bench along the wall, with his back to the drawings and his elbows on the wood. Ferra across from him, with the book. Emeric at the head. Ward had taken the stool by the door without being asked, as he took the chair by the door in the long room, and had his hands on his knees and looked at the fire.

Ferra opened the book before anybody had said anything, and turned back through it, a long way back, two winters, to a page for the lower water, and laid it flat in the middle of the table where they could all see it.

The line was there in pencil, as she had written it, small, at the foot of the page, because nothing in it had had a price. The ridge foot beside the yard. Walked, chained, drawn. A wall round the yard. A dock cut into the rock, gated to the lower water, roofed over. Not costed. Not begun. And under it, in ink, in the hand she used for things that had happened, a later line: Wall closed. And the date.

"That's where it stands," Ferra said. "In the book. The wall's costed and paid. The dock is still in pencil." She put her pen beside the page. "When it goes into ink it goes in as a dock. That's all anybody who reads the book will ever read. A dock is a dock."

Emeric unrolled the first sheet on top of the book.

He had drawn it in the pattern store over most of a month, at night, and then drawn it again in the delve, where a wrong line cost nothing, and then put it through the printer so that it would come out at a scale he could lay a rule on. The printer had given him four sheets, and he had pasted them edge to edge on a backing of linen, and the whole thing was taller than he was when he held it up. He did not hold it up. He weighted its corners with Fletcher's candlestick and Bram's tally book and Ferra's inkpot and his own hand.

She was black. He had drawn her black because she would be.

Three hundred feet from her stem to her stern, on the master foot. She was long and sharp in the bow and full in the middle and she ended square, and she had high sides, higher than any hull Fletcher had built him, so that from the waterline to her rail she stood as tall as a house of two floors. Between the high sides, over most of her middle, her deck was not a deck at all but a well: a working deck sunk down between the walls of her, open to the sky, long enough to lay out a market in, with hatches in its floor and lifting gear at its edges. Aft of the well she rose again, not in one wall but in tiers, one set back above another like the steps up to a church, each with its own rail, and out of the highest tier stood a tower, square and narrow, with windows all the way round its top. Round the tiers, at the level of the first, a narrow track ran right round the superstructure and back, a walkway with a rail on its outer edge. Forward of the well and aft of the tower her deck ran out flat and bare to her ends, two broad aprons of plate with nothing standing on them at all.

And on top of the tower, over the place where the wheel would be, he had drawn a dome. Not a hand's width. A dome a man could stand up in, all glass, in a cage of iron, so that whoever stood at her wheel would stand in the middle of the sky.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Bram leaned forward over the drawing on his elbows, and put a thick finger on the aft apron, and then on the forward one.

"Nothing on these," he said.

"They're for men who fly," Emeric said. "A place to go off from and a place to come down on. Clear. Nothing to catch a cape on."

Bram nodded slowly and did not take his finger off the apron. Ward, by the door, had turned his head from the fire.

Fletcher had not leaned forward. He sat where he was, at the end of the table, with his hands flat on the wood either side of the drawing's foot, and looked at her the way he had looked at the dish of nails in the trough on the first day, a long time, not moving. Emeric waited for the first question. He knew what it would be. It would be where the lift sat against her weight. It had been Fletcher's first question about the first ship, and he had asked it again about every hull since, and Emeric had drawn the answer into this sheet before he drew anything else, the stone seats low and the reserve set high on the deck beams, so that when the question came he could put his finger on it.

Fletcher looked up from the drawing.

"How many?" he said.

Emeric did not answer at once, because it was not the question.

"How many of her," Fletcher said. "Before you tell me anything else. Before I look at where you've put her stones or how you've stepped her tiers. I want to know whether I'm building one of her or more than one."

"Why first?"

"Because it changes how I build the first." Fletcher took his hands off the table. "I'll tell you what I've built you. I built you a hundred-foot iron steamer that turned out to fly, and every frame in her was cut to her and no other. Then I made over a lighter that was old when you found her, and there's not a plate in the Hod now that fits anything but the Hod. Then hull two. A barn with a keel. Then hull three, long and thin, for reach. Four hulls. Four different animals. Every one of them I've built from the keel up with my hands and my eye, and every one I've had to learn from nothing, because the last one taught me nothing about this one except that I can do it." He looked at the wall of drawings behind Bram's head, and away from it. "That's how a yard stays a yard. Builds one of everything, and never gets fast, and never gets cheap, and the men in it are always new at their work, however old they are. I've done it forty years. I'm tired of it." He looked back at Emeric. "So. How many?"

Emeric looked at the drawing under his hand. He had drawn her as one ship. He had not drawn her as one ship. He had drawn every frame of her on the same spacing, and every plate to one of four sizes, and the stone seats at the stations and nowhere else, and he had told himself it was because it was easier to draw. It had not been easier to draw.

"We build these," he said. "She's the pattern. The first of her teaches, and the rest copy."

Fletcher did not move.

"The same stations," Emeric said. "The same frames at the same places, the way I drew your first hull in sections, so she could be taken to pieces. Every plate in her one of four sizes. Every frame bent to a template you strike off the first one, and every frame after it bent to the template and not to your eye." He put his finger on a frame in the drawing and ran it along to the next. "When we made the screw for the first boat, we cast it rough and ground it rough, and then every blade was filed to one iron profile, struck from the wooden master. Three blades, each one as good as a man could make it by hand and none of them the same, would have torn the shaft out of her. Three blades filed to one template ran true. The template was the invention. Not the polish." He took his finger off. "Her whole hull is the screw. You build the first one slowly, and you make a template for every piece of her while you do it, and a jig for every join. The second one's built off the templates. It goes up in half the time, and it costs half, and your men aren't new at it, because they built the first. A plate out of one fits the next. A holed section comes off at its flanges and a new one goes on, off the same template, and it fits, because it's the same section."

"And a crew that knows one," said Ward, from the door, "knows all of them."

Everybody looked at him. He had turned back to the fire.

Fletcher sat for a moment longer. Then he got up, and went round behind Bram's bench, and began taking drawings off his wall.

He did it carefully, a pin at a time, and put the pins in a saucer on the shelf, and laid the drawings face down on the shelf one on top of another. The luggers. The long galley. A cog. He did not look at any of them as they came down. When he came to the three-master with the soft folds he stopped with his hand on her, and then took her down too, and laid her on the pile with the rest. There was a patch of wall the size of a door where the plaster was a different colour, cleaner, and a great many small holes in it.

"She'll want to go up there," Fletcher said, with his back to them. "Where I can see her every day. I'm going to be building her for a long time, and then building her again."

He came back and sat down, and pulled the drawing toward him across the table, and this time he leaned over it.

"Now," he said. "Where have you put her stones?"

Emeric showed him. The seats low along her keel, at the stations, and the reserve set high on the deck beams under the tiers. Fletcher followed each one with his finger and grunted at each, and at the last one said that was the only thing he would have argued with, and it was not there, which he had said about the first ship and seemed to enjoy saying again. Then he put his finger on the dome.

"And that."

"Last," said Emeric. "It goes on last, if it goes on at all. The glass for it doesn't exist."

"I put a dome over the first one's wheel." Fletcher tapped the tower. "The glassworks' glass. It's never cracked."

"It stands a hand's width. You said so. The wind can have that much." Emeric looked at the dome he had drawn. "This is higher than a man. The panes in it are bigger than anything the glassworks has ever blown flat. It stands on top of a tower on top of a ship three hundred feet long that bends in a sea and bends more in the air, and it goes up three miles into the cold with men standing under it. Our glass would crack the first night. I don't know how to make glass that won't." He said it plainly, because it was true, and because he had learned at this table, years ago, that Fletcher trusted him most when he said he did not know something. "So she's built to take it and she flies without it. There's an iron cap drawn under it on the next sheet. The cap goes on first. If the glass ever comes, the cap comes off."

Fletcher looked at the dome a while longer. "You drew it anyway."

"I drew it anyway."

"Aye," said Fletcher, the way he said it when he agreed with something he would have done himself, and moved his finger aft.

The second sheet was Bram's, and Emeric passed it down the table to him without unrolling it, and Bram unrolled it himself on top of the first.

It was an engine. Bram knew it was an engine before he had it flat. He had looked at nothing but engines, in one way or another, for years. Then he had it flat and stopped, and Emeric watched him stop, and watched him go over it from one end to the other, and back, and saw the moment he saw what was not in it.

"There's no boiler," Bram said.

"No."

One cylinder, upright, heavier in the wall than any cylinder Bram had ever bored, standing on a crankcase with a flywheel as tall as a man. On top of the cylinder, where a steam engine would have had its valve chest, a round iron bulb the size of a child's head, hollow, joined to the cylinder's top by a narrow throat. Beside the cylinder a small pump, drawn larger in a corner of the sheet, with a plunger no thicker than a pencil and a little spring-loaded valve at its outlet. A tank. A pipe from the tank to the pump and from the pump to the bulb. And a clutch on the shaft, drawn in the same hand as the clutch Bram had put on the rolls' wheel.

"It burns its fuel inside itself," Emeric said. "Not under a boiler. In the cylinder. You heat the bulb red with a lamp, before you start her, the way you'd heat a soldering iron. It takes a few minutes. Then you turn the wheel over by hand, or with air from a bottle, and the piston comes up and squeezes the air in the cylinder hot, into the bulb, and the pump squirts a little oil into the bulb at the top of the stroke, onto the hot iron, and it goes. It burns and it pushes the piston down. Once she's running the burning keeps the bulb hot and you take the lamp away."

Bram went back over the sheet.

"Why not steam?" he said. "I know steam. Every man at the works knows it."

"Three reasons." Emeric counted them on the table. "She starts in the time it takes to heat the bulb. A cold boiler takes an hour to raise steam, and a ship that has to get up and go, in a hurry, at sea or in the air, hasn't got an hour. She's lighter, by a great deal, for what she gives you, because she doesn't carry a boiler and a boiler's water with her, and every pound that isn't in her is a pound of stone she doesn't need. And..."

He did not say the third.

Bram was looking at the place on the sheet where the boiler would have been, and was not. He did not look up. He looked at it for some time, with his big hands flat on the paper either side of it.

"Aye," he said.

That was all. Ferra had stopped writing. Ward, by the door, was looking at the fire and his face had not changed, and Emeric thought of a wooden marker in the churchyard by the east wall with a bent bar at its foot, and did not say anything either.

"She gets a watcher," Bram said, after a while. He said it to the sheet. "There's no gauge on her and no valve to scream, but she gets one. A man who doesn't drive her, stood where he can see her, with the right to stop her. Answering to nobody." He looked up then. "Every engine. Not every boiler. Every engine. I'll write it under the other."

"Yes," said Emeric.

Bram looked back at the pump, and put his finger on the small valve at its outlet. "This is the hard part."

"It's the only hard part. It has to open at the same moment every stroke and shut clean, and not leak between, and it doesn't have to be fine. Not very fine. Your lathe can make it. Jorrin can make it. A man at sea with a file and a spare spring can mend it, and she carries the spare spring." Emeric hesitated, and decided to say the rest, because Bram would find it anyway. "There's a better engine than this one. It squeezes the air so hard it lights the oil on its own, with no bulb and no lamp, and it gets far more out of every cask. I could draw it. But it squirts the oil in against that squeeze, and the part that does it has to be made finer than a hair, and the only thing in Kaldo that could make it that fine is the forge." He shook his head. "An engine I can only mend at the forge isn't an engine for a ship. When she's three hundred miles out with a dead pump, the man who has to fix her is somebody like Cade, with his hands, and whatever he's got in the box."

Bram grunted, which was agreement, and began following the pipes with his finger from the tank to the pump to the bulb.

"She drives the screw on the arm, in the water," Emeric said. "Like the first ship's. And through the clutch, the airscrew, when she's up. Not to fly on. The stones and the air fly her. To cruise on, when there's a long way to go and the stones have better things to do, and to get her home if the stones have nothing left to do it with."

Bram's finger had got to the tank. It stopped there.

"What does she burn?" he said.

"Oil. The heavy part. What the still leaves behind when it's taken the lamp oil off the top." Emeric put his own finger on the tank beside Bram's. "Or the oil before it's been through the still at all. She's not particular. That was always the best thing about this kind. She'll run on nearly anything that will burn and pour."

Bram took his finger off the tank.

He did not say anything for a moment. Then he said, "The four casks."

"Yes."

"That's three hundred yards of road." Bram looked at the sheet. "You stood at the end of it and thought about this. I watched you think it. I didn't ask." He was quiet. "There's none left, is there. Not a pot. There's half a pot of the grease on the shelf in the pattern store, and I'm keeping that for the bearings."

"None."

"It's still Alina's," Bram said.

"Then we buy it," said Emeric.

Bram looked at him. So did Ferra. It was not what either of them had expected him to say, and Emeric knew it, and knew why.

The last oil Kaldo had had from the Duvrenne shore had come home in the forepeak of a ship that had come down on open water in the dark, far from any port, and gone inland without a banner and bought from men who never asked who was buying and would never know, and come back out to her the same night. It had been done so quietly that nobody on that shore had ever written it down. He had been pleased with it at the time. He was still pleased with it. And he was not going to do it again, not for this, not for casks by the dozen every season for as long as there was a ship in the dock, because a thing done once in the dark was an errand, and a thing done every season in the dark was a trade route that somebody would find.

"Rowena's merchants buy it already," he said. "From Duvrenne's factors, at Emberfell, for their lamps and their boats' seams and their physicians. It comes the long way round, and it's dear, and it's lawful. Saban puts an order down the sulphur lane with the next coaster. Emberfell merchants, Emberfell's paper, Kaldo's coin. Lamp oil, in casks." He looked at Bram. "It is lamp oil. That's what they'll sell us. What we do with it after is ours."

"That's dear lamp oil," said Ferra.

"It's the only lamp oil there is."

"I know." She wrote something. "I'm saying it will be dear, so that when Saban brings the figure nobody at this table looks at me as if I'd made it up."

They were an hour on the sheets after that. Fletcher took the hull, frame by frame, and asked about her plating and her bulkheads and her legs, which were the first ship's legs grown to her size, and her rams and her pumps, and wrote his own small figures in the margins in a hand nobody else at the table could read. Bram took the engine and did the same. Ward asked about the aprons, how far a man in a suit would have to drop off the aft one before he was clear of the tower's wash, and Emeric did not know, and said so, and Ward nodded and said he would find out the first time, which was what he always said.

Ferra had turned to another page in her book.

Emeric knew which page it was without leaning to see it, because she had turned to it at every table where a hull was talked about for three years. The gravity page. Stone raised at Grayrock, spring and autumn, by the pound. Stone sold under the covenant, the Crown's first purchase taken on every pound and paid. And the third line, Kept, in the barony, with the figure beside it that Oswin Fenner had read aloud across a tally-house table two springs ago in the chancery's voice, to the pound. He, or his clerk Lusk in his place, had stood at every raising since. One of them would stand at every raising after this one, at the beam, with a black book, watching the stone weighed, and write down the raised figure and the sold figure, and subtract, and never ask where. Nobody at the chancery needed to ask where. They only needed to go on subtracting.

She had the pencil in her hand. She had not written anything. She was looking at the kept figure, and then at the drawing on the table under Fletcher's elbows, the long black hull with its seats drawn along its keel, and Emeric watched her do the sum that he had done himself a month ago in the pattern store and put away.

Hull two had taken a long cartload of stone, set in pairs on her keel on a night in the autumn with nobody on the lower water awake. Hull three had taken less for her length, because she was built to stay up and not to lift, but she was half again as long. This one was three hundred feet. Every stone in her would be raised at Grayrock at a raising the Crown stood at, and weighed on Ilsa's beam, and not sold, and go onto the third line. And then the next one, off the same templates, in half the time, would want the same again.

The yard could be made fast. Fletcher had just been promised it. Nothing anybody at this table could do would make the stone come out of the hill faster than the hill gave it, or make the third line on the page grow without a man from the chancery at the beam watching it grow.

Ferra wrote, in pencil, small, under the kept figure: How many?

She looked at it. She did not write anything after it.

Fletcher saw her write it. He could not have read it upside down across the table, not in her hand, not in that size, but he saw her write two words and stop, and saw her face, and something in his own face changed a little. He had asked it about his yard. He had not thought, until that moment, that there was another way to ask it.

"She won't be the yard," he said. "Will she. What sets it."

"No," said Ferra. "She'll be the hill."

Nobody said anything about that. There was nothing to say about it that anybody at the table did not already know.

It was Ward who asked about the crew, near the end, when Fletcher had got up to put wood on the fire.

"How many men does she take?"

"To work her in the air, on a long run, with the watches kept and the engine watched and the suits on the aprons," Emeric said, "thirty. Forty with the suits. More if the well's full of something that needs minding."

"And every one of them knows."

"Every one of them has to. You can't take a man up three miles and tell him she's a coaster."

"Thirty men who know," said Ward, "is thirty men who go ashore." He did not look away from the fire. "It never takes thirty. It takes one. One who drinks, or one somebody's got a hand on. I've watched it go both ways, and the man it went through was always the one I'd have sworn to." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying count."

So Emeric counted.

He did it on the table with one finger, without meaning to, the way he had counted things on tables since he was a boy with nobody to count to. The people who knew now. Fletcher. Cade. Ned Tallis, who put his hand on the cold stone before every flight. Wes. Tobe. The hands on hull two, the ones who had flown her full of powder to the ford. Hull three's few. The four elites and the men they had chosen to trust. The six at the long-room table. He moved his finger a little along the grain of the wood for each of them, and each time he moved it he thought of a face, and whether it drank, and what it had to lose, and who it went home to.

He got to something under thirty, and the next man he would have had to count was a man he had never met, and he stopped with his finger on the table.

He took his finger off the wood.

"Not yet," he said. "She's two years off. I've got two years to find them."

"Find them where?" said Ferra. She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way she said a sum that did not close.

"I don't know," Emeric said.

She wrote that down too. Just that. Crew: not known.

Fletcher walked them out to the gate at the end of the night, with a lantern, though the moon was up and nobody needed one.

The yard lay quiet and silver inside its new wall. Hull two's canvas stood up in its long hump on the far berth. Hull three lay beside her, dark, with the frost on her plates. The Hod sat at her mooring by the slip with her rail picked out white. At the head of the slip, against the wall, the grey block stood where Cade had set it, with the moon on its broken corner. And beyond them all, across the levelled flat, the outline in the rock ran away toward the ridge, a hand deep and three hundred and more feet long and full of moonlight like a trench full of water.

"Eighteen months," Fletcher said, looking at it. "Two years. From her keel. Not from tonight." He held the lantern a little higher, as if it helped. "And her keel waits on that, and that's a line in the rock with frost in it. The mages won't be back on the flat till the thaw, and they'll want paying by the day out of my line again, and cutting that's not a wall's footing. Then the gate, and the pumps, and the walls laid in Bram's grey stuff, a year for the stuff to go hard. Then I'll want to float something small in it before I trust it with her."

"The Hod," said Bram.

"The Hod. If the gate goes, I lose forty feet of old lighter, not three hundred of this." Fletcher lowered the lantern. "So she's not this year's. She might not be next year's. Whatever you want a ship for between now and then, you'll do it in the ones you've got."

"I know," said Emeric.

He did know. He had known it in the pattern store, drawing her, and he had drawn her anyway, the way he had drawn the dome. Whatever the next two years asked of Kaldo in the air, the K Discovery and hull two and hull three and the Hod would have to answer it, and she would be a black shape on a cottage wall until they had.

"I've got oak in the stack," Fletcher said. "Good oak. It was for a lugger I never got paid for. I'll have it sawn to her deck plank this winter and stacked under cover, and her joinery out of the ash, so it's seasoned by the time there's a hull to lay it on. Wood's the one thing on her I can start tonight." He looked at the outline in the rock again. "It'll be the first thing on her that's ready, and the last that goes in."

He opened the gate for them, and they went out onto the road toward the town, and heard him drop the bar behind them.

Bram did not go home. He walked with them as far as the fork where the road went up to the flat and the lane went down to the works, and there he stopped, and said he was going to the pattern store.

"It's past midnight," said Ferra.

"The bulb," Bram said. "The throat into the cylinder. How wide you've drawn it's wrong, or it's right and I don't see why, and I want to know which before I sleep." He looked at Emeric. "I'll draw the cylinder. The first one. Wall and bore. You can tell me in the morning what's wrong with it."

He went off down the lane in the moonlight with the rolled sheet under his arm, walking the way he walked when he had a thing in his head, a little faster than usual and not looking where he put his feet.

Ferra watched him go. Then she looked at Emeric, and at Ward a pace behind him, and pulled her coat closer, and they went on up toward the flat together, the three of them, not talking much. At the top of the rise she said, not looking at him, that she had written the dock in pencil for two years because she had not wanted to write it in ink until she knew what was going into it, and that now she knew, and she still did not want to.

"Because of the hill," Emeric said.

"Because of the hill. And the man at the beam. And thirty men who go ashore." She walked a few steps. "And because it's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen drawn, and I can't cost it, and I don't like the feeling."

He did not have anything to say to that. He did not try to find something.

Saban came to the long room in the morning with his coat still on and snow on its shoulders, and read the order standing up.

It was a short order. Lamp oil, in casks, Duvrenne's, the heavy grade as well as the light if the merchants had it, as much as the next Marsh Hen run could carry back on top of her sulphur, bespoke through Emberfell's merchants before the spring fair, at their price, on Emberfell's paper, paid in Rands.

"The heavy grade," Saban said. "Nobody buys the heavy grade for lamps."

"Boats' seams. Roofs. Physicians."

"Physicians buy it by the jar." Saban read the order again. "You'll want it by the cask. They'll ask."

"Will they?"

Saban thought about it. "No," he said. "Not Rowena's. Rowena's merchants will ask each other, over their wine, what the prince wants with it, and one of them will guess seams and they'll all agree, because it's a better answer than none." He folded the order into his coat. "It comes the long way round. By the time a cask of Duvrenne's has gone across Duvrenne to the coast and down it and round to Emberfell on somebody else's boat, it's dear. Five Silver the cask at Emberfell quay last spring, for the light, and they'll want six this year because they'll have heard we asked. The heavy's less. Not much less." He looked at Ferra. "A cask a day, is it? When she runs?"

"Nothing runs," Ferra said, "for two years."

"Then it's cheap," said Saban, "for two years," and went out again into the snow to find the Marsh Hen's master before the tide.

Ferra wrote the order into the book in ink. Under it she wrote the price Saban had said, and then, because it would be wrong by the spring and she knew it, she drew a ring round it, the way she had drawn a ring round the Crown's odd figure in the spring, and left the ring open at the top.

Emeric went down to the pattern store. Bram was asleep at his bench with his head on his arm, and under his arm, on a clean sheet, was a cylinder, drawn in section, wall and bore, with the figures down the side in his blunt hand and a great many of them crossed out. At the top of the sheet, where Emeric would see it first, Bram had drawn the throat into the bulb, and beside it, in the same blunt hand, one word.

Why?

Emeric sat down on the stool across the bench from him and pulled the sheet gently out from under his arm, and took out his pencil, and began to write the answer underneath.
