Chapter 297 - Younger

He woke on the ninth of February at ten past four in the morning, and the first thing he asked for was tea.

Emeric was not there. Imani was, on her stool in the box, and she wrote it down afterward as it happened: on her four o'clock hour she had turned the lamp to his face and found his eyes open, and he had said, hoarse and quite clear, good morning, Imani, and then, is there tea, and then, why is everybody's handwriting so large.

Under that she had written: Pulse fifty-eight, in step. Breath ten. Heat good. No lines in the hour. Asked for tea. Told him no. He said that seemed fair. I.

And under that, smaller, in a hand that was not quite as straight: He is not old.



Paskin came on the twelfth, because nobody had thought to tell him.

Nobody had thought to tell him. Ferra had sent slips to the six and to Bettany and to Ned, and had forgotten the oldest of Armand's students in Kaldo, two miles north at the falls with his hands in a cofferdam, who heard nothing that did not come up through rock. She wrote it down afterward as her fault.

So Paskin came down on the morning train with his boots grey to the knee, to sit with the old man for his Thursday hour, and the door at the end of the east range was opened to him by a stranger.

Emeric was in the room behind. He saw it happen. Paskin stood in the passage with his cap in his hand and his mouth already open on the start of good morning, Master, and looked at the man in the door, and did not finish it.

He did not look at the face first. He looked at the hem.

It was Armand's own grey robe, the good one; and it stopped a hand above the ankle bone, so that a length of bare shin showed above the slipper, pale and new like the inside of a peeled stick. Paskin looked at that the way he would have looked at a footing that had moved in the night. Then his eyes went up the robe, tight at the shoulders and short in the sleeve, to the face at the top of it.

A long face. A horse's face, the town had always said, kindly. The long nose, the hollow temples, all of it there, and none of it with a line in it. The grey eyes looked out of it the way a man looks out of a new window in an old house. His hair stood up white where Bettany had cropped it on the tenth, and at the roots, all round, a finger's width of it was the brown of a wet oak stake. The beard the same, trimmed close. He had decided to keep it.

He was taller than Paskin by a head. He had not been taller than Paskin for twenty years.

"Paskin," said the man in the door. "Come in. You'll let the cold in, and I'm told I'm to be kept warm, though I've never been warmer in my life."

Paskin did not move.

"You're younger," he said.

He said it flat, a quarryman's son telling you where the rock was, and he said it before anybody else in Kaldo had said it aloud, though the whole castle had been thinking it for three days.

"Yes," said Armand. "I gather I am."

"How much?"

"I've not been told. Imani won't guess and Ferra won't guess without a figure, and I haven't found a glass in this room I'm willing to look in for long." He stood aside from the door. "Your robe's too short as well, by the look of it. Come and tell me about the dam."

Paskin came in like a man crossing ice he did not trust, and sat on the stool by the fire without being asked, and looked into it for a while.

"Thirty," he said at last. "Thirty-two. Not more." He took the pipe out of his pocket and looked at it and did not light it. "I'd have said it of a man I met on the road and never thought about it again."



The arguing about why had started on the tenth and did not stop for a week.

Two men had eaten a stone from the same tree in the same dome at the same density. One had come out with his knee mended and nothing else changed, and the other had come out fifty years younger. Ferra wrote the question at the top of a clean page and underlined it, and everybody had an answer.

"The beard," said Bram, on the eleventh, in the doorway, with his coat still on. "He's had his beard. You never grew one in your week. It's the beard."

"It isn't the beard."

"You don't know it isn't the beard."

"It was a week," said Imani. "Nobody grows a beard in a week. He was under seven."

"Then it's the weeks," said Bram, satisfied, and went to look at the jug.

Tess's came up the wire from the line room on the twelfth: IT DID HIM BECAUSE HE NEEDED IT. YOU WERE ALREADY YOUNG. T. And at three: OR BECAUSE HE OPENED THE VALVE. And at five, which Ferra read out without expression: OR HE IS LUCKY AND YOU ARE NOT. THIS IS ALSO A RESULT.

Ward had said, on the morning of the tenth, standing at the foot of the bed, "You'll want different boots," and gone to see about it; and that was as far as Ward went into the matter.

Armand listened to all of it from his chair by the window, kicking off the rug Imani kept putting over his knees, and let them guess at him the way he had let a room of first-years guess at a stone, with polite interest and no help whatever; until on the evening of the thirteenth Ferra sat down opposite him with her page and said, "You know."

"I think I know."

"Then you'll say."

"I've been enjoying it." He put the cup down. "Bram's beard was very good. I'd have given him a mark for the beard." He looked at Emeric, in the other chair. "You didn't bond yours."

"Nobody knew it mattered."

"Nobody did. That's why I bonded mine. It was the one thing I did that you hadn't, so that if I came out different there'd be one thing to point at." He spread his hands, long and brown and still, which he kept looking at when he thought nobody saw. "So I point at it. I showed the stone everything I was. Yours came into you a stranger. It's very likely the difference. I'd put very likely on a page."

"And mine," said Emeric. "If I'd bonded it."

"Might have done nothing to look at either." It was the old look across the tower table, and very strange out of that face. "You were thirty-three. Look at you. A man doesn't get younger than his best, or I'd be in swaddling. You may simply have been there already, and it found nothing to do but the knee." He picked up his cup. "Take it as a compliment. It never found that about me."

Ferra wrote for a while.

"Very likely," she said. "Bonded. And he may already be at his best." She looked at the line. "That's two guesses on one line."

"Then rule another under it," said Armand, "and write that I made them. You like that."

She did not smile, quite. She looked at him over the page instead, at the face, and something went across her own and was gone; and Emeric saw her put her pen down and leave it there a moment, which she did not do.

"You built me a house of windows," said Armand, gently, to the window, where the great dome stood over the colonnade with all but its last six panes in. "I'd like it on record that I was very moved, and also that I'm not in it."

"It was bought," said Ferra. "I'll not be told about the glass."



He asked for Imani's record on the fourteenth, at breakfast, because he had seen her write in it.

She had kept it from the first hour, apart from the hospital's sheet: a grey quarto book with every hour of his seven weeks in it. She had never offered it. He had known it existed since the tenth, because Armand had never in his life been in a room with a book he was not allowed to read.

"It's medical," she said.

"It's about me."

"That's what medical means."

"Then it's mine as much as yours. More. I supplied it." He held out his hand. "I'd like to read my own case. I've read everybody else's."

She looked at him for a long time across the table in the long room. Then she went out and came back with the book, and with something else in her coat pocket that made the coat swing, and put the book down by his plate.

He read it close, with his face near the page, though he did not need to now and kept sitting back, surprised, when the page went sharp at the right distance. He read the aspirin, and the thirtieth twice, and said nothing about either. At the last page he stopped.

"There's an entry here," he said, "for the thirty-first of January. Specimen. Kept. See jar."

Imani took the jar out of her pocket and put it on the table.

It sat by the butter, squat and green, sealed with wax; and between its two squares of glass, flat and clear, lay the back of an old man's left hand, the brown spots scattered on it like spilt tea.

A. Back of left hand. 31 Jan, yr 13. The old skin.

Armand read the label. Then he looked at his own left hand on the table beside it, smooth and brown, and back at the jar.

"That's mine," he said.

"It was."

"It is. It's my hand. I'll have it back."

"You can't." Imani took the jar off the table and put it back in her pocket. "It's a record. It's in my cabinet. It's going to be the first thing in my cabinet anybody sees before they eat one of those, so they know what's going to come off them and don't scream when it does."

"I had those spots for forty years."

"And now I've got them." She sat down and took a piece of his toast. "You've a new hand. Use that one."

"Imani, it's a matter of principle. A man's body is his own."

"Not the bits that come off it in my ward," said Imani, with her mouth full, in her ward voice, and that was the end of it; and Ferra, at the table's foot, wrote one line in her book and did not show it to anybody, and Bram laughed so hard at the far end that Maren came up from the kitchen to see who was choking.



The checks began that afternoon.

They had agreed on them the night he woke, the six, at Ferra's table: nobody else went near a stone until they were sure. Not Tess, bonded and waiting and saying so on the wire four times since the tenth. Not anybody. Before a second person lay down on those boards they would know, as near as two people could, that what had come out of the dome was well, and was Armand, and would stay both.

"Two people," Ward had said.

"Two ways," said Ferra. "His, and mine. They'll not agree on what they're looking at. That's the use of it."

They had weighed a third, an outside look from somebody who had known the old heart before, and dropped it inside a quarter of an hour. Armand had told nobody about his heart for twenty years. Anybody who had known of it was in the tower or the close, and nobody they could trust.

Ferra's way was paper.

She brought every letter and minute he had signed in Kaldo since the second year, which was a great many, and laid them along his table by date like a hand of cards, ending with his pencil line in her stone book on Long Table night. Then she put a clean sheet in front of him.

"Write your name," she said. "And under it the first line of the Almanac's preface. The one you wrote."

He wrote it with the pen held a little too hard, a man using an old tool in a new hand; small and square, the tower's hand, the g with its tail turned in. It did not shake on one stroke.

And it did not shake. Not on one stroke.

Ferra laid it at the end of the row. Down the table the signature went smaller year on year, and the downstrokes began to waver in the fifth year, and by the eleventh went down like a line drawn in a moving cart. Then this one, at the end. The hand from the start of the row. Steady as a rule.

"It's the same hand," said Ferra. "Every letter. I'd know it in the dark." She put her finger under the new line. "And it's the hand from year two. Before the shake."

"Then I'm who I say I am," said Armand. "That's a relief. I was beginning to wonder."

"Now the stair."

The east stair had twenty-two steps; he had climbed them on Long Table night with his stick and rested on the landing for a count of a hundred. Ferra stood at the bottom with her watch.

"Walk it," she said. "At your own pace. I'll time it."

He looked at the stair, and at her watch, and the corner of his mouth went where it went when he had won an argument.

He took it three at a time.

Three at a time, deliberately, the long legs in the short robe going up like a man up a ladder he had built, hand nowhere near the rail; and at the top he turned in her doorway, not out of breath, hands folded.

"Well?" he said.

"Six," said Ferra, looking at the watch. "Six seconds." She closed it. "It was a hundred and forty on Long Table night. With the landing."

"I wasn't timed on Long Table night."

"You wrote that you rested for a count of a hundred. I added it." She put the watch away. "You did that to make a point."

"I did it because I could," said Armand, from the top of the stair, "which is the same thing, at my age." And then, after a moment, in a different voice, looking down the twenty-two steps as if he had never seen them before: "I could. Ferra. I could."

Emeric's way was the body.

He did it that night in the dome's outer room. Armand sat on the crate by the collar with his shirt off, and Imani listened first with her silver cup, a full minute; then gave it to Emeric.

It was not the same heart. Two winters ago it had stumbled like a man on a stair with a bad knee. This went like a mill: slow, deep, every beat the weight of the last.

Then he put the cup down and his hand flat on the old man's chest, and went in.

He put his hand flat on the old man's chest and went in slowly, at the other man's rate, down into the heart's wall, low on the left, where a knot of dead flesh the size of his thumb's end had sat for forty years.

There was nothing. Not a scar; not a seam where one had been.

He went on through all of him. The stone was not anywhere. It had been a knot under the ribs on the twenty-sixth and a map spread over him by the end of January, and now it was only the grain of him, the way a stone's grain is not a thing in the stone but the stone. And under it the door, open, deeper than his own.

He came up.

"Again," said Imani, writing. "In a week. And again after that. I want three."

"Two," said Ferra, from the box door, where she had stood the whole time with her book. "We said twice."

"Then I'm adding a third, and I'll not count it, and you can't stop me."

They did it all again on the twenty-first, and it came out the same, except that he took the stair four at a time and Ferra did not time it on principle. Imani did a third on the twenty-eighth, and wrote it in her grey book under a heading that said only: For Tess.



It was on the fourteenth, the first night, that Imani began examining Emeric.

It started properly enough. She had sent Armand to bed in his short robe, and she sat on the crate in the outer room with the silver cup round her neck and looked at Emeric in the lamplight.

"Shirt," she said.

"Mine?"

"I've got his. I want yours beside it. You ate one too. I want to know what yours didn't do." She held out her hand for it. "For comparison."

Ferra, in the box door, opened her book to a clean page and uncapped her pen.

He took it off. She listened low on the left, and on the right, and then higher, which was not where a heart was in anybody, with her eyes on his face instead of the gauge.

"Seventy," she said.

"It was sixty this morning."

"I took it this morning." She moved round to his back, and he laughed because her fingers were cold. "Don't laugh, it's a record." Her other hand went flat on his ribs and stayed. "Breathe in."

He breathed in.

"Out." She took the cup away and put two fingers inside his wrist, and then, when she had his pulse, did not let go of the wrist. "Seventy-four. You see. Rising. That's what his doesn't do now. His sits at fifty-eight whatever I do to it."

"What are you doing to it?"

"Nothing. Comparison." Her free hand went flat on his chest. "Skin," she said to Ferra, without looking round. "His is new all over, not a mole. Yours has the knife at the capital, and the forge in the second year, and a burn on the right shoulder I don't know about."

"The steam pipe," said Emeric. "Year four."

"You never told me."

"You never asked."

"I'm asking now," said Imani, and put her mouth on it.

Ferra wrote something down.

"That's not a medical observation," said Emeric, after a while.

"It's a record of a scar." Imani's mouth went to his collarbone. "Old. Healed flat. Doesn't hurt?"

"No."

"There. A finding." She was smiling against his skin; he could feel it. Her hand had not left his wrist and her thumb was moving on the inside of it, which was not how anybody took a pulse. "Eighty," she said. "Ferra. Eighty."

"I've got it," said Ferra.

"Are you writing all of this?"

"It's a medical record. You said." Ferra's pen went on, steadily. "Patient's pulse eighty. Physician's hand, left, on patient's chest. Physician's mouth, on patient's neck. Physician has stopped counting." She turned the page. "Physician's mouth, on patient's mouth."

It was. Imani was standing between his knees with her hands on his face, kissing him the way she did when she had decided she was done with the ward, slow and not in the least clinical. The cup swung cold between them and she took it off over her head and dropped it on the crate.

"Physician has removed instrument," said Ferra.

"Stop it," said Imani, without stopping.

"You wanted a comparison."

"I've got one." She lifted her head, hot-cheeked, curls loose at her temples, brows drawn as if he were a case that had come out wrong. "His is a better heart. It doesn't go up when I put my hand on it."

"His isn't yours."

"No. That's true. Write that, Ferra. Patient's heart responds to physician." She kissed him again, and the pen in the doorway stopped; and after a moment Ferra put the book on the shelf under the gauge and came and sat on the crate beside him, and put her hand on the back of his neck, and said quite seriously, "My turn to check the patient," and did.

The needle stood on its line. Nobody came to the door, because Ward had gone to bed; and some time after midnight Emeric noticed that all three of them were on the paving with Imani's coat under them, and that Imani still had his wrist, and had long since lost the count.

In the morning Ferra's page was in the record. Imani put it there herself. It said, under the heading Comparison, in Ferra's square hand, everything, down to Physician has stopped counting; and Imani had ruled a line under it and written, in her own: Not a medical record. Kept anyway. I.



Ward proposed the last check on the twentieth, at supper, in passing, the way he proposed everything he had been thinking about for a fortnight.

"He's well," said Ward. "I've watched him on the stair. He's sound." He cut his meat. "You've seen what's in him. You haven't seen what comes out."

Armand, at the hearth end, put his glass down.

"A spar. At the base, in a yard with a wall round it. He was a High mage. Before anybody else lies on those boards I'd like to know what the stone did to the rest of him, and not find out at three in the morning when he sneezes."

"I've not cast since November," said Armand.

"Then it'll be interesting."

They went out on the twenty-second before light. Ward had sent the class to the range. The yard lay in grey frost, the gravity hall behind it and the trough at the end under its ice. Ward; Ferra with her watch; Imani; Rylan on the mounting block; Paskin by the armoury door, sent for by Armand himself; Emeric. And the man in the short robe in the middle of the yard, looking at his hands.

Emeric let his sense out first. That was the agreement: before anything, he would read him.

He had known Armand's note for twelve years: the deep slow note of a High mage, a great still lake so wide you could not see the far shore, worn down to the bed in places the way a lake goes down in a dry summer until you see the drowned wall.

It was the same note. He would have known it across a river.

And nothing was worn off it. The lake was full and over, and denser than water, so that his sense did not go in but leaned on it and stopped, as a hand stops on a wall. He had felt that once before, not in a man: at the Band's foot.

He drew his sense back.

"Well?" said Ward.

"It's him." Emeric heard his own voice and did not quite know it. "It's like standing beside the Band."

By the armoury door, Paskin took the pipe out of his mouth.



They began small, because Ward said so.

Armand lifted his right hand palm up, as on the wall for the paper planes, and a pale glyph came up off it: air. The frost got up off the cobbles in a sheet and went across the yard like a cloth pulled off a table, and lay down in a drift against the trough's foot.

He looked at it. Then at his hand.

"Oh," he said, quite quietly.

"Again," said Ward.

Then the left hand too, a blue glyph, and the trough's ice lifted off in one plate and the water rose after it in a rope as thick as an arm and wound round it, and the wind stood both on edge in the middle of the yard, like a pane with a river round it.

Imani said something under her breath.

Then a third glyph between the two, small and blue-white: lightning. It went down the rope with a crack like a whip, and the plate fell in halves, and the water splashed down steaming.

Three at once was High mage work. It was not the three that made Emeric's neck go cold. It was that the old man did not seem to notice he was doing it.

"Wind and water are mine," said Armand, to his hands. "The lightning's borrowed. I did my year in every school, we all had to, and by fifty I'd stopped bothering with any but mine. It ought to cost me three times. It didn't cost me anything I could feel."

"Fire," said Ward.

A small red glyph, a ring and a core and a stroke, and a bolt the size of a plum went from his hand to the drill frame at the far wall and took the top rung off it in a puff of black.

"Earth."

Amber. The cobbles at his feet came up in a ring round him to the height of his knee, every stone in its place and the frost still on them, and stood; and settled back, and were a yard again. Paskin, at the door, did not move at all.

"Gravity," said Ward.

Armand looked at him.

"I scratched at it," he said. "Everybody does, and everybody stops. It's the one that won't be told." The glyph came up violet and wobbled, as nothing else had wobbled that morning, and he went up off the frost a foot and hung there with his toes pointed down like a boy on a swing, and came down. "That. And no more. I could lift myself at twenty and I can lift myself now." He sounded pleased. "There's something left I can't do. I was beginning to worry."

"Then you'll spar," said Ward, to Emeric.



Emeric had told himself on the cart that he would hold back, and he found in the first exchange that the question did not arise.

They stood at twenty paces. Ward's rule: no gravity on Emeric's side, nothing the base's walls didn't already know. Fire was his; earth and air at three times, a price the stone had made payable.

He opened with fire, because fire was what he had. A bolt, quick, small, sunwise, at the old man's chest, the kind Orrin threw at canvas.

Armand looked at it.

That was all. Emeric felt for the dip a reversal takes, the half breath where a man counts the turn and the amount and the road, and there was no dip. A red glyph came up off the old man's hand as he looked at it, the same size, turning the other way, and a yard in front of the grey robe the two met in nothing: a small warm draught across the frost.

On the mounting block, Rylan unfolded his arms.

"Again," said Armand.

Emeric threw two, high and low, the high one turning against the low, so that a man who trusted his eye on the second would give himself the mirror. He had spent two winters learning to throw it. Armand reversed both at once, both clean, neither the mirror.

"You didn't count," said Emeric.

"I didn't have to. I saw it." Armand was looking at his own hands again. "I saw which way it turned the way you see which way a door opens. It's never once been like that."

Ward, by the trough, had gone very still. Emeric felt his note do it.

So he stopped throwing things at the old man to see what he would do, and came at him.

He took the Rail, fire in his hand, and the yard under his front foot went to water a hand deep, so that he came down skating and the fire went into the sky. He threw up a slab of cobbles; a wind came round it low from both sides and took his legs.

He was up at once, faster than anybody in the yard but Ward, and went round the slab behind a sheet of flame along the ground. Armand did not step back. The whole trough came up and fell on the flame like a dropped bucket, and out of the steam came five glyphs in a ring round Emeric's chest.

He went into his room for the half breath it took. He had the first and turned it out. He had the second. The third was water and he took it for the mirror and it was not, and his own glyph flashed white beside his face, and the water hit him in the chest like a shove from Bram; and the fourth and fifth were wind, and set him down on his back in the frost drift against the trough, quite gently.

He lay there.

Above him the sky was white. He could hear Imani saying something to Ferra and Ferra saying, "Two minutes and ten," and Rylan laughing on the mounting block, not unkindly, in great gasps.

A long face came into the white, upside down, looking down at him.

"You read the third as the mirror," said Armand, upside down above him. "Your room told you right and you didn't listen, because you'd been right twice and wanted to be right faster. I did it at thirty. I'm told I'm thirty-two." He held out a hand, and pulled Emeric up as if he weighed nothing.

Emeric took the hand. It pulled him up as if he weighed nothing.

Ward was looking at the place where the first bolt had met its reverse, as if it were a word read in a glass.

"He didn't count," Ward said.

"No."

"You count. You go somewhere to count. I've watched you for two winters. You go somewhere and come back." Ward lifted his eyes to Armand. "You looked at it."

"I looked at it," said Armand.

Paskin came across the yard from the armoury door.

Paskin came across the yard from the door, past the slab and the halves of ice, and stood in front of Armand and took the pipe out of his mouth.

"When I was yours in the tower," he said, "there was the elders' table, where you sat a while, and one man at it you'd stand up for when he came in. Only the one. He made the last climb. Came down with his eyes like lamps. He's been dead thirty years."

"Forty," said Armand.

"Forty." Paskin put the pipe away. "There's nobody at that table now I'd stand up for. There was one man above you, Master, and he's dead. There's nobody alive above you now. Not in the tower, not in the close, not anywhere I ever heard of. I'd put my name to it."

Nobody said anything.

Ferra wrote. Emeric watched her do it: one line, slowly, in the large clear hand she kept for things meant to be read by somebody who was not her.

Armand stood in the frost and looked at Paskin, and Emeric saw him not know what to do with it. He had been a High mage once, he had said in year three, the way a man says he had once been tall. He had been tall a fortnight.

Then he turned round, quite quickly, and walked to the yard wall, and put both hands on the top of it, and was sick over it into the nettles on the other side.



Imani got there first. She had her hand on his back before he had finished and two fingers on his throat as soon as he had.

"Ninety," she said. "Ninety-four. In step." She did not take her hand away. "You're white. You're wet through. You're breathing like a carter at the top of the hill."

"Yes," said Armand, into the nettles. "Isn't it wonderful."

"It is not wonderful."

"It is. I've been out of breath twenty years from my chest. This is my legs." He straightened, white and grinning like a boy sent off the field. "I sat at a desk seventy years, and now I've a body that's never run a step and it's furious with me. I'll run it in the mornings. Ward will lend me a yard."

"Ward will not," said Ward.

"At six," said Armand, "with the class. In the cold water." He was laughing again. "I'm going to be so stiff tomorrow."

He was stiff. Ward pulled him out of the cold trough by his collar at six on the twenty-third in front of sixty-two men, and the class talked about nothing else for a week.



He came to Emeric about the gravity on the twenty-fourth, at night, in the study, and asked it like a student.

"You walk on walls," he said.

Emeric put his pen down.

"Imani's store room. Bram's boot marks on the water tower." Armand sat down. "Of gravity I've up, and down, and nothing. I'd like to walk up a wall. I've wanted to since I was nine."

"It's a hybrid's trick. It needs the body as well as the cast."

"I've never had so much body. Your knights' trick, pushing it inward: I can do that now. I tried it on the stair this morning and went up like a goat and hit my head on the lintel." He touched the mark. "Teach me."

So on the twenty-fifth, after midnight, with Emeric's sense out on the sleeping town, they went up the keep tower.

Ferra came with her watch. Imani came because he was still her patient, and stood at the parapet with her arms folded. The keep tower stood up at the leads' north corner, brick faced over rubble on its upper half, twenty yards to the cap, and the cap's parapet jutted out over the drop on stone brackets like the brim of a hat.

"Three things," said Emeric. "Lighten. Plant. Balance."

"Lighten I have."

"Just to nothing. Not up, or you've nothing to plant with." He showed it, the violet at his soles, his coat hanging on him like a coat on a peg. "Then you push it down through the soles into the stone, the knight's way, inward to the skin and through. Whatever you've planted in is down."

"That's not how down works."

"It is when you're anchored. Your foot's the aim." He set his foot on the tower, and the tower became the floor, and he stood out level from its face in the dark with his coat and locs hanging toward the leads. "Too little and you peel off. Too much and you shove yourself away, or go into the brick."

Armand looked at him for a long time, standing sideways on the keep.

"Your coat's wrong," he said.

"It's right. It knows where the ground is. I don't."

Spider-Man, Emeric thought, without warning, at the old man's face, a scholar presented with a cat on a ceiling, and nearly lost the plant. He did not say it. Only the two women at the parapet would have known the word, and one of them was already trying not to laugh at him.

Armand lightened. The violet came up at his soles, a little ragged, wobbling the way it had wobbled in the yard. He put his foot on the tower.

And pushed.

He had never in his life pushed with half of anything. The brick under his heel went off its face with a crack and a flake the size of a hand spun away over the ward, and Armand went straight back off the tower, flat, arms out, like a man kicked off a jetty, and came down on his back at Imani's feet.

Imani looked down at him.

"Ninety," she said, without kneeling.

"You haven't counted."

"I don't need to." She stepped over him to the tower and looked at the hole in the brick. "You owe Bram a brick."

"Less," said Emeric. "A great deal less."

"I gave it almost nothing."

"Give it less than almost nothing."

He set his foot again, very carefully, and the brick held; and his face looked frightened and delighted at once and hid neither.

"It's down," he said. "Emeric. It's down."

"Now the other foot."

The other foot. Too little: he slid a body's length with both arms going like a windmill and caught it a yard from the bottom, level, his robe hanging toward the leads past his knees.

"Don't look," said Armand, to the women.

"I'm the physician," said Imani. "I've seen everything you've got. I've got some of it in a jar."

Ferra had her watch out. "From the first step," she said. "Go."

He walked up the keep.

Then he walked up the keep, a step and a stop at first, arms out, then not; and at the brackets he did not stop, but went out onto the underside of the stone brim, over the twenty-yard drop, upside down, white hair hanging toward the ward and the robe falling round his ears.

He stood there.

Emeric came out beside him, both of them hanging off the bottom of the world, the ward and the violet dome and the river lights below their heads like a lamp-lit ceiling.

"Four minutes and twenty," said Ferra, from the leads, far below. "From the first step."

"Come down," said Imani.

"No," said Armand.

"Armand."

"It's entirely unseemly," said Armand, to the town, pushing the robe off his face. "A Secretary of the Arcane, on the underside of a duke's parapet in his nightclothes with his shins out. I've never been so happy in my life. I'm not coming down."

"You'll be sick."

"I've been sick. I was sick on Thursday. It was wonderful." He looked at Emeric beside him, both of them hanging off the bottom of the world. "How long did it take you? The first time."

"I fell off a wall into a courtyard."

"You found it at your own window, dripping. I found it the first night of asking, with a teacher." The robe fell over his face again. From under it he said, contentedly, "Write that down, Ferra."

"I've written it," said Ferra.

He stayed twenty minutes, Imani counting them aloud in her ward voice, and came down, and sat very suddenly on the leads with his head between his knees.

"Ninety-six," said Imani, with her fingers on his neck. "I told you."

"You did," said Armand, into his knees. "Tomorrow night. Same time."



The kitchen girl was called Dilys, and she had come up from the dock quarter at Christmas, and she had never seen Armand old.

She was on the keep stair on the twenty-sixth with a tray of the ward's dinners, and Armand was coming down four at a time, as he took every stair since the tower; he stopped above her and stood aside with the tower's bow.

Dilys looked up at him.

Emeric, behind her at the stair's foot with Imani, saw her look up, and up, and go pink from the collar.

"You're the new mage," she said.

"I'm told I'm new," said Armand. "I'm not sure I'm a mage. I fell off a tower last night."

"You never." She shifted the tray onto her hip. "Off the keep?"

"Most of the way."

"You don't look broke." She looked him over, the dock quarter's way. "Maren says the new mage did the wind at the base. You could come and do it in the kitchen when the bread's out. It's an oven by two."

"I'd be delighted," said Armand gravely, "to cool your bread."

"I'm Dilys."

"Armand."

"Like the old master," said Dilys. "The one that was poorly in the garden." And then, kindly, as to a man who might be sensitive about it: "That's a good name. He was a nice old man. He used to give the boys caramels."

Armand opened his mouth, and shut it.

"Dilys," said Imani.

Imani had come up past Emeric in her ward coat, and stopped a step below the girl, and it was the ward voice, the one that told a man with a broken leg he would not stand on it.

"That tray is going cold," said Imani. "Those are my patients' dinners. Take them up."

Dilys went up the stair very fast.

Imani looked at Armand.

"She's sixteen," she said.

"I'm aware."

"You're eighty-seven."

"I'm aware of that too. I've not been flirted with on a stair since the old king died. I'll think about it in a wholly theoretical way, as a man thinks about the weather in a country he'll never visit." He looked after her. "She thinks the old master was a nice old man."

"He was," said Imani. "He's going to go on being one. On the stair." She went up past him. "And you'll not go in Maren's kitchen to do the wind. You'll get the bread in your beard."

Armand went down the stair three at a time, and banged his head on the lintel at the bottom, and said a word Emeric had not known he knew.



He told Emeric about the stair on the night of the twenty-seventh, in the dome, by the tree, because he said he wanted to tell it where he had gone down.

They went in without masks; the room could not touch the old man now. He sat down on the roots with his back to the braided trunk where his head had lain, and Emeric sat beside him, the blue going along the bark between their shoulders.

"I'll tell it once," said Armand. "Plainly. I'd like you to listen, and not ask me things until I've done, because I'll lose it if you do. I've been putting it in order for a fortnight."

"All right."

"I walked my shelves that night and didn't come back. The books went older, then weren't books. Where there's always been a wall there was a stair, stone, worn hollow by a great many feet, going down. I went down it. It isn't a place that has long."

The tree hummed.

"At the bottom there's a floor, dark like deep water, with no wall I ever found. And I knew what it was in a minute, because I've wondered twelve years how a delver feels another across a room. It's that floor. Every room stands on it. Like a wood: you'd say the trees were apart. Dig, and the roots are one thing."

"The roots," said Emeric.

"I'm calling it the Root, in my head. And there are lights on it, far off, the way a town looks from a hill at night. Each one's a room seen from underneath. Yours, near, very bright. Bram's, like a forge with the door shut. Ferra's. Imani's. Ward's, a wall with a lamp behind it. The girl's, small and quick. And one more, near the top, bright, that I didn't know. Not one of ours. Not a stranger's; a stranger's would be cold. Warm. Close. As if it were in this town." He glanced up at the tree, once, and away. "I'm not going to guess at it."

Emeric did not say anything. He had his hand flat on the root too. He found he could not take it off.

"And one very far. Large, larger than a room should be. Old, and cold the way the deep sea's cold, with something moving in it. I looked at it the way you'd look at a ship's light far out. And it turned toward me."

The blue went along the bark between them, and down into the roots, and out.

"I went back up. I remember Imani's face at half past five in a crooked mask, and being very glad of it. That's all I'll be able to tell you, however you ask."

"Did it follow you?"

"I don't know. I think it saw a light come on where there hadn't been one, and turned to look, the way a man looks at a window lit late." He looked at Emeric. "You've never swallowed yours. Don't go down there. Not yet. I'd like us both to know a great deal more before you stand on that floor with a light like yours. I'm asking as your teacher."

"You've always been."

"Then do as you're told," said Armand, and got up off the roots and pulled Emeric up after him.



He worked the rebuild out aloud to Ferra that same night, in her room, because she asked him what Armand had said and he could not tell her that yet, and gave her the other thing instead.

She was on the warm floor with her book, Imani at the ward until two with a woman in labour, and he lay on his back on the oak beside her.

"It's a survey," he said.

Ferra turned a page.

"Mine didn't know me. It came into a body it had no drawing of, so it touched nothing but what it tore, and made the room bigger, and mended the knee because the tear went through it." He looked at the beam. "On my world there were men whose trade was taking buildings down with charges, in a street, without dropping them on the street. None of them would set a charge in a building he hadn't walked. Every wall that carried and every one that didn't. Blow the wrong wall and it comes down on you."

"And Armand gave it the drawing."

"He walked it through every shelf for an hour. So when the room tore him open, it knew what each wall was for, and built them back the way they were meant to be, not the way they were. The height age took. The scar. The skin."

Ferra had stopped writing.

"Then the other side," he said, and heard his own voice go flat, and let it. "Do you remember, in my week. The last night. I asked you what happened if somebody ate one of mine."

"I remember. You asked me where every stone with your mark on it was. I told you."

"That's the answer. A stone bonded to me has my drawing. Eaten by anybody else, it'd pull down every wall he's got and try to put up mine. I don't think anybody lives through that."

Ferra put her book face down on the quilt.

"So nobody gains by stealing one," she said slowly. "Of yours. Or Armand's. Or Tess's. They'd not get you. They'd get a grave."

"Nobody gains."

"Good," said Ferra, and wrote that down and ruled under it, and he watched a weight he had not known she carried go off her into the page. Then, without looking up: "That's not what you've been lying there thinking about."

He had known she would. It was Ferra.

"Vey's people," he said. "The figures under the tree with their hands at their mouths. They did this. And in the cave I asked how people like us beat people who built that, with our bits of stone and our lamps. I've had it backwards. Armand came out taller than he went in, the strongest mage alive, and he's a scholar who's never run a step." He was quiet. "What if the ones who won the old war weren't people like us at all."

Ferra did not write that down.

Ferra did not write that down. She looked at him a long time with a pen through her hair; then put the book shut on the floor out of reach and lay down with her head on his shoulder and her hand flat on his chest.

"Not tonight," she said. "Tonight you can think about Armand on the underside of the parapet in his nightclothes."

He laughed. It surprised him.

"Four minutes and twenty," said Ferra, into his shoulder. "From the first step. I want it on record that I timed it."



Tess's log came up from South Sentinel on the K Endeavor on the last day of February with a note for Bram, and Ferra read it at breakfast because it came through her office first.

Most of it was the threshold bars since January: nothing, nothing, then at a third dust by binder volume, the bar warm under the hand. She turned to the new page.

"There's a line here I don't understand. In the back, where she puts the things that aren't for anybody yet." She read it out. "Twentieth February. Wren knocked the full liquid flask over on the threshold bench, over the plain fire cubes set in quartz grit, no keystones. Soaked the tray. Logged the loss. Twenty-seventh. Went to throw the tray out. Cube four is warm. Like a cup set down an hour ago. No keystone. None of the others. Checked it with Wren's hand in case it was mine. Still warm." She looked up. "And in capitals: I DON'T KNOW WHY. DON'T THROW CUBE FOUR OUT."

"Then I don't know about it," said Emeric.

"You do now."

"I don't know about it," said Emeric, "until she tells me." He reached for the bread. "Put the log back in the box for Bram the way it came."

Ferra closed the log and put it back in the box with Tess's note to Bram on top; and at the hearth end Armand, chin on his hand and his long new legs stretched under the table, said nothing at all, and was very interested in his tea.
