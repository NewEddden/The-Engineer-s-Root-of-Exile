Chapter 25 - Under One Thatch

Emeric had been awake for an hour when Ward came in, which meant Ward had let him have the hour.

"Rylan's back off the Wayford road," Ward said. "It's not a rumour any more. Grayrock's called its levy and Wayford's calling its household men, and there's a hundred out of Milford drawn up on the plain doing nothing at all, which I can't read yet and don't like."

"How long."

"Nine days if Renner's in a hurry. Fourteen if he wants everybody in one place first, which he will, because Renner's the one who has to be seen at the front of it." Ward put his hat on the table. "Call it eleven and don't build anything on the number."

Emeric had spent most of the night doing the arithmetic he could do, which was walls and men and how far forty of either would stretch, and he had it ready, and he started saying it.

Ward let him get four sentences in.

"Cut the grain," he said.

"I'm putting men on the joint and the ford, and I want the graded approach cleared to a hundred and twenty paces, and the western stretch is going to have to hold with three."

"Cut the grain first."

"Ward, there is an army coming."

"Aye. And it's coming to a town with two thousand acres of bread standing in a field outside the wall." Ward did not raise his voice, which was how Emeric knew he had already lost. "You've never been besieged. I have, at nineteen, on the wrong side of it, and I'll tell you what it is. It isn't men on ladders. Men on ladders is a morning. Being besieged is standing on a wall you're holding perfectly well and watching another man's horses eat your winter."

Emeric stopped.

"They'll burn it," he said.

"They'll eat what they can and burn the rest, and either way it's gone, and either way you're inside a wall with four hundred new mouths in the town and nothing under any roof anywhere. Then you don't lose the wall. You lose in the third week without anybody shooting at you." Ward picked the hat back up. "Every hand you've got goes to that field, including mine, including the forty. Get it in and then tell me where to stand."

"The wall's unmanned for eleven days."

"The wall's unmanned for eleven days and then it's manned by men with something behind it." Ward went to the door. "I'd rather fight hungry than starve comfortably, and I'd rather you heard that from me now than worked it out on your own in the third week."

Emeric sat with it for about ninety seconds, which was as long as he needed and longer than he was proud of.

Then he got Ferra out of bed and did the other arithmetic.

"Cutting, four hundred hands, nine divisions," she said, at the table, in her nightdress with a coat over it and entirely unembarrassed. "Six days if nothing breaks. Carrying overlaps it. Call it eight to have it all off the ground and under cover somewhere."

"And threshing."

Ferra found the line and read it out flat, because she had written it in the spring and had been waiting.

"Three hundred and ten man-days by flail, all of it inside eleven days after the cut, which does not fit in eleven days at any number of hands I have ever been able to put in this column." She looked up. "Or four men on a belt and a stone, running through the dark in shifts, which does."

Emeric looked at the two numbers next to each other for a while.

He had built the thresher because three hundred and ten man-days was the fattest line in a ledger and because there had not been enough hands in one fortnight of summer, and that had been true and had been the entire reason, and it had also been, he understood now, a farming reason. This was not a farming reason. Without the machine in that barn the harvest of the barony of Kaldo was standing in a field in the sun on the day Renner came over the rise, and there was no version of the last four months in which anybody would have flailed it out in time.

"Write that down as well," he said. "Not the number. The other thing. That I built it for the wrong reason and it turns out to be the only reason it matters, and I want it in the book in your hand so that I don't get to remember it as foresight later."

Ferra wrote it down. She had, he noticed, started keeping a separate page for that particular category some weeks ago, and had not mentioned it, and it was no longer short.

Bram brought the reading stone up at first light on the second day of cutting, four days early, wrapped in a rag.

"You said a fortnight."

"I said a fortnight for a man working days." Bram's eyes were the colour of a man who had not been working days. "You've never once asked me to hurry a thing. You asked me to hurry the bellows and I gave you rubbish and you never asked again, and I've thought about that more than you'd credit." He put it in Emeric's hand. "It's good work. Slow work, done fast, which is the only honest kind. Get it under your shed."

It went under the shed by mid-morning and it did exactly what it was supposed to do, which after the summer Emeric had had was almost unnerving. The stone read the grain and the shed stopped, and a man no longer had to stand in it with his hand in the pile deciding, and Tarrow's eldest was released from a job he had been performing conscientiously since the day a cart went over on the mill road.

Emeric let himself have that for about a quarter of an hour, standing in the shed doorway in the heat with the whole field working behind him, and then he did the throughput arithmetic and stopped having it.

The shed dried a load in a day and a half. That was the number. It had always been the number, and in a normal season it was a good number, because in a normal season the cutting smeared over three weeks and the loads arrived at the shed in a queue the shed could eat.

The cutting was not smearing. Four hundred hands were bringing in nine divisions in six days because an army was coming, and the shed did not care about the army, and the shed dried a load in a day and a half.

He stood there and worked out how many loads there were and how many day-and-a-halves there were between now and eleven, and got a number that was short, and then did it again more carefully with Ferra and got a number that was still short.

"Two loads," she said.

"Two loads that go in wetter than I'd like or two loads that stay in the field."

"They can't stay in the field."

"No."

He walked the queue twice. What he was looking for was a way to make the shed faster, and there wasn't one, because a stone that moves warm air through a slatted floor moves warm air through a slatted floor, and building a second shed took eleven days he was spending on something else. He had, he noted, arrived at the same shape of answer for the fourth time that year: the clever part was fine and the plain part was the constraint, and the plain part was a floor and a roof and a fortnight he did not have.

So he chose which two loads.

He did it standing at the shed with Dunnel and Halloway and Ferra, in daylight, out loud, because he had promised himself he would stop making decisions like this at night on a piece of paper.

"The venture's grain goes through dry, all of it, because nineteen households put that in on a promise and it goes to Saban and it has to survive a road." He counted it off. "The field's harvest goes through dry, because that's thirty-seven houses' winter and the parts get cut out of it. The barony's four parts go through dry, because they're mine and I don't get to be the exception in my own favour."

"That's everything," Dunnel said.

"That's everything except the seed."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Halloway looked at the shed and then at the field and then at the ground.

"Say the whole of it," Dunnel said.

"The seed store goes into the bin at the edge of store-dry instead of properly dry. Not wet. Inside what any man in this barony would have called dry in any year before this one, because before this one nobody had a stone that could tell them the difference, and the number I'm working to is a number I invented in June." Emeric put his hand on the doorframe. "It'll be the last thing loaded and the least dry thing in Kaldo, and it's going into the one building I've got that holds a thing exactly as it went in."

"Then don't put it in there."

"Where. It's the only store that isn't the granary, and the granary is one thatch forty paces from a mill and I've had two men on it at night since the rise, off the western wall, which Ward has told me twice is a stretch he can't hold." He heard himself and slowed down. "Say the objection properly and I'll answer it properly. You wrote the clause. This is your ground."

Dunnel took his time.

"My father spent the last nine years of his life buying seed off Grayrock's road at whatever they named," he said. "I made you write that clause twice in two sets of words because I've seen what a barony is when it hasn't got seed, and it isn't poor, it's finished, it's just a field and a man standing in it." He looked at the shed. "And I've no better answer than yours. That's what I'm angry about. If it goes in wet it's a risk, and if it stays in the field it's Renner's horses, and there isn't a third thing, and you've picked the one I'd have picked."

"I'd rather you'd found a third thing."

"So would I."

"Then have it written the way you write things," Emeric said. "Not as my decision. As the reason for my decision, with the shed's number in it and the eleven days in it, so that whoever reads that page in ten years knows it wasn't laziness and knows exactly what I traded."

Ferra wrote it. Dunnel read it and corrected two words and initialled it, and then went and stood in the field for a while by himself, which Emeric decided was not his business.

They got it in on the seventh day.

All of it. Nine divisions cut and carried and threshed in two stages by a stone that read what it was hitting, dried by a shed that knew when to stop, and stacked under the old granary's thatch beside the mill: the field's harvest, the venture's grain, the barony's four parts, four hundred new hands' worth of winter and thirty-seven households' worth of a year's work, all of it in one building, exactly as he had described it to Ward on the rise six weeks earlier while choosing a water wheel instead.

The seed went into the bin last, at dusk, at the edge of dry, and Emeric carried two of the sacks himself, which accomplished nothing whatsoever and which he did anyway.

Imani was at the bin when he got there with the second one, having come up the hill for no reason she offered, and she watched the sacks go in and did not ask her question again.

That was worse. He would have preferred the question.

"Say it," he said.

"I asked you in the summer what was in with the grain and you told me nothing was, in about half a second, and I took it because you've never been wrong at me about water." Imani watched the last sack go through the hatch. "I'm not going to ask you tonight. You've had four hundred people in a field for seven days and there's an army on the road and this is not the night."

"Ask me in the spring."

"I'll ask you in the spring." She turned to go down the hill. "Write down that I said I would."

He got the list out that night, in a castle where nobody was sleeping, and left the space empty at the top, and wrote underneath it:

In on the seventh day. Ward was right and I argued for four sentences before I heard him, and the thing he knew that I did not is that a siege is a man on a wall watching horses eat his winter. I have never been hungry. I keep forgetting that this is a gap and not a neutrality.

The thresher was built because three hundred and ten man-days would not fit in a fortnight of summer. That was the reason and it was a farming reason and it was wrong, or rather it was right and small. Without that machine every stalk of this harvest is standing in the field on the day they come over the rise. I did not foresee that. Ferra has it in her hand on the page she keeps for this, and the page is getting long.

Two loads short. The shed dries a load in a day and a half and always did, and I costed the stone and never once costed the shed, and the constraint was a floor and a roof and a fortnight I hadn't got.

The seed is in the bin at the edge of dry, on my decision, out loud, in daylight, with Dunnel's words on the page and not mine. Everything else in this barony is under one thatch forty paces from the mill with two men on it who are not on the western wall.

Imani will ask me in the spring. She has told me she will, which is a kindness I have not earned and would not have offered.
