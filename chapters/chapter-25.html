Chapter 25 - A Real Ward

There were two dark market halls in Kaldo, and the barony owned both. Hale could tell him so without looking anything up, and could tell him the year each had last taken a stall rent, which was the same year for both of them, and was the first year of the plague.

He walked them the next morning with Rylan, because it was one of Ward's yard mornings and Ward had left him Rylan and a list of streets.

The bigger of the two was too big. Its roof had been patched so many times that it had stopped being a roof and become an argument, and the whole lower ward could have stood inside it and heard its own echo. The smaller one stood two streets above Cooper's Row, on the cart street: a stone front, a timber back, and a board over the door that had been painted out so long ago that only the nails still held anything up.

Rylan stood in the doorway of it and looked at the earth floor.

"You're giving her this?"

"I'm offering her this."

"Does she know that's a different thing?"

"She'll tell me."

On the way down Rylan worked it out on his fingers. "Ferra had terms. Sela had terms. The doctor's going to have terms." He considered his hand. "Has anybody in Kaldo ever asked to hear yours?"

"I have terms."

"Name one."

Emeric thought about it for the length of the lane. Rylan had grown up in Kaldo and knew exactly how long that lane was, and he enjoyed every step of it.



Imani was on her step with a basin, wringing something out of a cloth that he did not ask about. She saw him come up the street, and before he had said anything she dried her hands, went inside, and came back out with a folded paper.

It was soft at the folds. The ink had gone brown. Whatever it was, it had not been written that week, or that year.

"My terms," she said. "For a ward. You'll want them before you offer me anything, so you know what you're offering."

She held the paper out, and he took it, and she kept her end of it.

She read them standing on the step, and he stood in the street below her and listened.

"It's mine. My register at the door, and every name goes in it, whoever they are. My rules, posted where people can read them, and they hold for anybody who walks in, you included. I say who works in it and I say who goes. The laundry is boiled, not rinsed. The ones who are catching get a room to themselves with their own door, so nobody walks through the sick to reach them." Her eyes stayed on the paper. "And you don't set foot in it without my yes. Not once. Not because it's yours, not because you're passing, not because you think you know better than..."

"Yes."

She stopped.

Her end of the paper dropped. It was his for about as long as it took her to reach down and take it back, and she knocked his thumb with her knuckles doing it.

She looked at him over the top of the paper. Then she went back to the head of the page and read the whole of it again, slower, with her finger moving down the lines, looking for the one he must have meant to say no to.

He waited. There was no catch, and he could not think of a single way to tell her so that would not sound exactly like a man with a catch.

"All of it," she said at last.

"All of it."

"In writing."

"In Hale's book. Today." He had written Sela's line on the back of a price, and it had done for Sela. This needed to outlast the price, and him. "Not a loose sheet."

"No," Imani said. "I've sent letters through that castle before."

He did not say anything to that.

She folded the paper along its old folds and put it inside her apron. "Where?"

He told her.

"Show me."



Hale wrote it into the barony's book that afternoon, in Imani's words and not his own, with the old market hall above Cooper's Row named in full and the healer's keeping of it set down plainly, and read it back to both of them aloud. Imani listened to every word with her arms folded. When he had finished she asked him to read the last line again, and he did.

"If the healer wants a hand at the register," Hale said, blotting it, "the keep has a laundress who can write."

"No," Imani said.

Hale inclined his head a careful inch and wrote nothing further. Emeric, who had spent the summer keeping the castle's people thirty feet from a door under his own keep, found that he had no argument to offer her on that one, and did not offer any.

She walked the hall the next morning with her bag on her shoulder and Emeric a step behind. At the door she stopped and looked back at him.

"It's not mine yet," she said. "Come in."

She did not say one good thing about it.

The floor was earth, and you could not wash earth, you could only move it about. The north end of the roof let the sky in, and she showed him where, by the stain that ran down the wall to the floor. The windows were too small and too high, and she could not see the color of a man's face by them, and the color of a man's face was half of what she needed to see. There was one door and it opened onto the cart street, so that every barrow in the lower ward would go past the beds at the height of a sick man's head. She found the drain under the back wall with her foot and asked where it went, and when he did not know, she said that was another thing. She found rat droppings along the whole of the west wall and did not trouble to remark on them. It was cold. It was October, and the hall was colder inside than the street was outside.

"And there's no water," she said. "You can't run a ward on buckets carried up from a well. I've done it. I'm not doing it again."

At the back of the hall there was a room he had taken for a store. It had been the weigh-room, when there had been things in Kaldo worth weighing, and it had its own door out to the lane behind. Imani went in and stood in the middle of it and turned slowly round once.

Nothing good about that either. She took a stub of chalk out of her bag and wrote on the inside of its door, in letters as tall as her hand: CATCHING. An isolation room, then, with its own door to the lane.

"The first plague winter I had them all in one room," she said, to the door. "Because one room was what I had. A man came in with a cut hand and went into the rows with the rest of them." She put the chalk away. "Not again."

"Not like this, either," she said, when she came back out to him, and meant the hall.

"Then like what?"

She told him. Flags on the floor, so it could be washed. Lime on every wall. The north end of the roof made good. A second door on the lane side, so the beds were off the cart street. The drain moved, once somebody could tell her where it went. And water, in the hall, not up the hill.

It would take weeks, and she knew it. She gave him the list as though weeks were the least of what she had waited through, and then she went back to the dock quarter, where she had a woman three days from her time.



He could have run it himself. Every ward he had read about was there in his head, and the ones he had sat in, in the other life, and he could have laid the beds and written the rules and hung the doors in a week and been right about most of it. He did not. It was hers, and he had a barony to run, and he had agreed to both those things on her step.

So he did the part that was his.

The flags came out of the yard of a house on Cooper's Row that the barony had held since the plague, for a tithe there was nobody left to pay. Two men off Ferra's count had laid stone before the war and had spent the summer digging Doran's graves. They took the flags up in a day and laid them in the hall in four, and the older of the two said nothing in all five days except to ask for more sand.

On the second of those mornings four of the garrison's men came and leaned on the wall of the house opposite and watched the flags go down. Nobody asked them to lift one, and they did not offer. Ward stood at the corner of the cart street and watched them watch, and when they had drifted off toward the market he came back to Emeric's shoulder.

"They are not the baron's any more," Ward said.

"No."

"They are not yours either."

"After winter."

Ward looked at the place on the wall where the four of them had been leaning, and went back to his corner, and said nothing else about it that day, which was not the same as having nothing else to say.

Limewash from the kiln went on every wall, two coats, and the hall smelled of it for a week. Imani said the smell was the first thing about the building she could stand.

She took on her two women in the second week of October, at the side door, while the lime was still wet. Emeric was in the lane with the carpenter and heard most of it.

"What do you do with a sheet off a fever?"

"Boil it."

"How long?"

The older woman thought about it. "Till I'm sick of the sight of it. Then again."

"Mornings," Imani said. "From the day there's water."

The younger one she asked something too low to carry, and the girl answered it just as low, and was taken on as well. Neither of them looked toward the lane. They had been told whose ward it was, and by whom.

The carpenter came up from the exchange and did the north end of the roof, and swore at it less than he had at the exchange, because there was less of it. He cut the side door on the lane, and Bram made the hinges, and the first barrow that went down the cart street after the beds were planned went past a blank wall.

The drain was harder. It ran under the back wall and down the slope toward Cooper's Row, the way every drain on that slope ran, and nobody could tell Imani where it ought to go instead. So they plugged it, and she wrote it at the bottom of her list, under the water, as a thing owed.

The rats left when the floor went down. The cold stayed.

Ferra priced all of it against the shop. It came out of the month she had read him the list for at the exchange, and a good piece of the month after. She made no remark about that. She had already told him, item by item, what that month had been for.

Abloh sent up a crate of plain bars without being asked, with a slate on top that said WARD in his square capitals, and charged the shop for them at cost, which Ferra entered, and then queried, and then let stand.

Imani wrote the laundry rota herself, on a board by the side door, before there was a single bed in the place: her two women, mornings, every sheet boiled and nothing rinsed, and the kettles scalded out at the end of the day along with the sheets. Bram made the kettles. He made three, and said so, and said that when Emeric came asking for a fourth next month the answer would be the same as it was now, which was that he had made three.

Emeric did not ask for a fourth. He had wanted to, and Bram had seen him want to, which was apparently enough.



By the third week of October the hall had a floor that could be washed, walls that smelled of lime, a roof that kept the north end dry, two doors, and an isolation room with CATCHING chalked on the inside of its door. It had nothing to drink.

Sela had a school morning that day, and he had gone down to stand across the lane from the exchange, as he had taken to doing. When the children came out she was on the step with the empty baskets, and she looked up, and he stepped back round the corner of the lane before her eyes got as far as him. He had been doing it since September. He did it now without deciding to, the way a hand comes off a hot pan.

He came past the hall on his way back up and stopped at the side door.

Imani was inside on her knees on the new flags with a stub of chalk, marking out where the beds would go. The marks ran in two lines down the long room, each one a short stroke and a cross, and she was moving the fourth one a hand's width to the left because she did not like where the third had put it.

He did not go in.

She saw him at the door and sat back on her heels. She neither got up nor asked him in. She looked past him, at the empty corner beside the door, where the flags ran right up to the wall and there was nothing.

"Water," she said, and pointed at it with the chalk. "In that corner. Then it opens."

She went back to her marks.
