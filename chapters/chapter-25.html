Chapter 25 - The Dead Zone

He went back to the notebook because he had run out of things to try.

He had read the last page of it a year ago, in a carriage, because the last page was about his mother's stone and therefore about him. He had never read the theory. Theory is the part you have to sit down with, and he had stopped sitting down with anything at the age of seven, and there had not been a week since he arrived in Kaldo when sitting down with a book would not have been the second most useful thing available.

Winter gave him the sitting down.

Armand wrote the way he had taught, which was slowly and without excitement and with the important sentence placed in the middle of a paragraph where it would not draw attention to itself. It took Emeric four evenings to get to the page.

A crystal pushed past what it can hold does not stop. It fails. It lets go of everything it has and everything it is at the same moment, and it lets go of its element along with it.

And then the part Armand had gone to some trouble to write plainly.

The failure does not only destroy the stone. It pushes the ambient mana out of the area around it, the way a hand pushes water out of a basin, and what is left behind is a patch of the world in which magic does not work.

Not weakened. Absent.

It fills back in from the edges, slowly, and how slowly depends on how large the stone was and how thick the mana had been there beforehand. Armand had put a range on it and the range was wide enough to be honest.

Underneath, in a smaller hand, drier than the rest of the page:

This is known. The Circle understands it. The Circle does not use it, because our own work is so far embedded in everything we run that a dead zone costs the man who makes it what it costs the man he aims it at, and it takes out every person standing inside it rather than the one it was meant for. It is not a secret. It is simply of no use to anyone who has anything to lose.

Emeric read that three times.

His edge was not knowledge. He had spent eight months quietly enjoying the idea that it was. The high mages knew this. They had known it longer than he had been alive and had written it down and filed it.

His edge was that he had less to lose and was willing.

He noticed that, and did not care for it, and did the test anyway four days later, which was the whole of the problem in one sentence.

He did it properly. Outdoors, on the frozen mud below the east wall where nothing was, at a distance, with the thing set on a stump and Emeric behind a cart at thirty-odd feet and Ward further back than that.

A chip of water crystal. Not one of the good pieces. Something off the riverbank the size of a fingernail, pushed steadily past its saturation with the patience of a man filling a bucket that has a bottom he cannot see.

It was not an explosion.

It was a wet crack, and a burst of water going outward in every direction at once, and a sound he did not hear so much as feel in the roots of his teeth. The ice of it went through his boots inside a second and he was still standing there noting that the range was smaller than Armand's middle figure when Ward said his name.

Emeric had known Ward for fifteen years in one life and eight months in the other and had never heard that voice out of him.

Ward had not lost his bracer's charge. The charge was fine. He checked it twice while he was standing there and it was fine.

What he had lost was the enhancement.

It went out of him the way heat goes out of a room with the door open. Not instantly. Not all at once. It slid, and it kept sliding, and the nearer the middle of the patch the further it went, and Ward took two steps toward the stump to find the edge of it and the two steps took more out of him than two steps have any business taking.

For about thirty seconds the best swordsman Emeric had ever met was a large man in his forties standing in a frozen field in a coat that was suddenly heavy.

He did not panic. He did something worse, which was that he stood there and took inventory, methodically, the way he took inventory of a treeline, and Emeric watched him find out one item at a time what he had been carrying and had stopped carrying.

It came back as the zone filled in from the edges. It took a good deal longer than thirty seconds if you were the man inside it.

Ward walked out, checked his bracer a third time, and said that the ground was bad and they should move the cart.

Emeric said something about it that evening. He did it badly, and he heard himself doing it badly, and Ward waited until he had finished and then asked whether the ore carts were going out on the Friday.

They never discussed it again.

The doctrine came out of that week and it was written cold. There was nothing in him that wanted to make a speech about it.

He had been building redundancy into things since the pump-house drawings out of what he had assumed was temperament. A habit from another life, where the good systems were the ones that failed politely, and where a man who put all his weight on a single supplier deserved what he got.

It was not a habit. It was the only thing standing between his barony and a stranger with one crystal and a grudge.

Anything in Kaldo that depended on magic could be switched off by any person willing to burn a stone in the right yard. The foundry. The pump. The granary, whenever there was one. A wall, if he ever built one worth the name.

And four men he had brought south with him, whose harness was mediocre steel over very good crystals, would become four ordinary men wearing armor worse than the garrison's.

He went and looked at the harness that night, with a lamp, in the armory, while the four of them were asleep two floors up.

The crystals in it were the finest stone in the barony by a distance that was not close. High-grade, properly cut, capital work, and they were the only high-grade stone he had or was likely to have for years.

He could have had all four sets out and into a workshop inside an hour. There was nobody in the building who would have stopped him and nobody in the kingdom who would have called it theft.

He did not take them. Not one, not a spare, not the cracked one out of Osric's left pauldron that was doing about half of nothing.

He wrote it as a standing rule instead, in his own hand, in the notebook, and he wrote it that night rather than in the morning because he knew exactly what kind of reasoning was going to come for him in a hard season and he wanted his own signature already on the page arguing against it.

The elites' stone is not a reserve. It is not available in an emergency. It is not available to me. When I come back to this page with a good reason, I will have a good reason, and the reason will be wrong, and this sentence was written by a man who was not desperate.

He read it over once and thought that it was an embarrassing thing to have had to write, and left it exactly as it was.

Then he went back through the foundry drawings and found three places where he had designed a bomb.

The worst of them was the one he had been proudest of. An industrial fire stone bedded under the hearth, held near its ceiling for hours at a stretch to keep the melt at pouring temperature, which was the entire point of having a fire stone in a foundry rather than a hotter fire.

A stone held near its ceiling for hours is a stone living at the edge of the page Armand had written. He had sketched that in the autumn, in a good mood, with Bram looking over his shoulder.

And then the thing that stopped him where he sat.

He had already sketched the gauge. Months ago. A splinter of crystal in the hearth wall, straining against the heat, and a reading taken off how hard it had to work, because that was the only way in the world to know the temperature of a melt without putting a man's face over it.

The number that told him how hot the iron was also told him how close the stone was to letting go. It was the same number. It had always been the same number. The instrument he had built to run the furnace was the instrument that would keep the furnace from killing the men standing in front of it.

He had not designed it that way. He had got there by accident, out of a want for a thermometer.

He said so out loud, to a room with nobody in it, and did not feel clever about it in any respect.

The mine took him one evening and he put it down.

There is no powder in this kingdom. The diggings stop where the seam turns down, because the rock is hard and the tools are iron and the method is a man with a hammer. He now knew a way to break rock that did not involve any of that.

Then he priced it honestly, which he made himself do on paper rather than in his head. A crystal per shot, and crystals cost more than the ore they would free. A fire stone letting go underground, in a shaft, with men in it. And a dead zone at the face afterward, which means no light, no lift, no moving air and nothing magical working at all in the one place on earth where a collapse is most likely and most immediate.

He put it down. He did not put it down permanently, and he was clear with himself about that in writing, and that was the uncomfortable part rather than the calculation.

Ferra's register came back with an answer to the rune question in the last week of the month, and the answer was one.

One man in the entire barony, out of one thousand eight hundred and forty-seven people, who could put a mark in a stone and have anything happen. A hedge carver out past the west farms, a half day short of the mining hamlet, named Nevin.

Unaffiliated. No guild, no lineage, no master and no catalogue. Self-taught off dead men's work, which in practice meant off broken pieces and grave goods and whatever came out of a ruined house, and alive at fifty because he had been lucky rather than because he had been careful.

Half of what he cut did nothing at all. The other half did something close enough to what had been asked for that nobody in forty years had looked at it hard.

The guilds point at men like Nevin every time anyone questions the monopoly, and Emeric had assumed that was propaganda until he read the register's column for cause of death among the unaffiliated, and it was not propaganda. The guilds were not lying about the casualty rate. They were simply not mentioning why a man might take the risk.

He rode out on a grey morning with Rylan and found a low house with a shed on the side of it and a great many small stones laid out on a plank in an order that made sense to exactly one person.

Nevin could not read.

He said it without embarrassment, in the middle of a sentence about something else, the way a man mentions that he cannot swim. He worked from tracings. He had a roll of them, oiled cloth and paper both, some of them copied off pieces he no longer had and one of them copied off a thing he had seen once in a church in Wayford and had reproduced from memory in the yard outside.

He could copy a mark perfectly. Emeric watched him do it, twice, and the hand was better than his own hand by an order of magnitude and would still be better in ten years.

And he had no idea what any of it meant.

He knew which tracing made a lamp stone hold longer and which one made a hearth mark run warm, the way a man knows which of two keys opens a door, without any theory whatsoever about locks.

Which is exactly as useful and exactly as dangerous as it sounds. Emeric came back down the west road with a carver he could not trust with anything load-bearing and had no intention whatsoever of throwing away.

He counted the state of the art that night, in the notebook, in one line, because he wanted to see it written rather than carry it around as a mood.

A prince who cuts badly. A hedge carver who cannot read his own work. And a method that spends the man and teaches nobody.

He looked at the sentence for a while. It was the clearest statement of his actual position that he ever managed to write down.

The last thing that happened that winter happened late, with the list in front of him, and he caught himself in the middle of it.

He was building a tech tree.

He had spent six months of a bad year on Earth playing games about exactly this. Food, then tools, then infrastructure, then trade, then the thing after trade, the whole ladder laid out in a neat panel with arrows on it, and he had been good at them. He had built beautiful cities. He had optimized granaries.

And he had lost the same way every single time, and he had lost that way because the correct move was boring. He built beautifully and he did not build a military, and somewhere around the middle of the game a man with worse cities and more soldiers arrived and took his.

He looked at the list. A foundry. A farm. A pump-house. A port. A school with no date on it. Three pages of it.

No defense of any kind, anywhere on it.

In a barony whose last holder had been executed for treason four months ago. Whose garrison was currently carrying brick for a copper and a half a day. Whose neighbor upstream had all the ore, all the stone, and every crystal vein in the region worth putting a tool to.

He added one line at the bottom and underlined it twice, which he had done to nothing else in that book.

He did not think about the games again.

The trouble with the line was that he already knew what it required and it was not soldiers.

Better crystals were out. He had written that rule himself, four nights earlier, specifically so that a version of himself in a bad month could not argue with it, and the ink was not dry.

So the answer had to be steel. Steel worth something on its own, so that a man standing in a dead patch of field with nothing working was still a man in good plate rather than a man in a costume. Armor that was armor before anybody put a stone in it.

He had no steel worth the name. Kaldo had never made steel worth the name.

He had one blacksmith, two apprentices who did not exist yet, a brick shell with three furnaces framed into it and nothing burning in any of them, and diggings that ran out in a few years by the foreman's own arithmetic.

Every road he walked that night ended at the foundry, and the foundry ended at ore, and the ore ended a day upriver in a barony whose water came down and whose goods never went back up.

He noticed that, and looked at it for rather a long time, and closed the book.
