Chapter 13 - The Ash Trade

Imani raised it as a complaint, not a request, which is how it got into his head and stayed there for three days.

Boiling fixed drinking water. It did nothing about hands. It did nothing about cloth. It did nothing about the fact that she had gone from a burial to a birth that morning with the same rag in her bag, because it was the rag she had, and because the alternative was a rag she did not have.

"You've made the water clean," she said. "Now do the rest of it."

He checked before he invented, because he had been wrong about this world twice already and both times had been embarrassing.

Soap existed in Randillia.

It came up from the south coast in small hard cakes, scented, sold by weight, and it cost about what a lower ward family spent on food in a week. He had washed with it every day of his life in the capital and had never once thought about where it came from, which was itself a piece of information about him that he wrote down.

There was also the grey slippery stuff the cloth trade used for scouring, which nobody in Kaldo thought of as soap at all, because it had a cloth-trade name and came in a bucket and was for wool.

So the thing he was about to do was not invent soap.

It was make it cheap, make it consistent, and put it in the hands of people who had never in their lives been able to buy any. He was clear with himself about the distinction on the first evening and he stayed clear about it afterward, because it was the difference between a miracle and a business, and he had no intention of being caught holding the wrong one.

Abloh was eleven days downriver and three streets from Fletcher.

Rylan went with him and Ward came whether anyone wanted him or not. It was the first time Emeric had left the island since he arrived on it, and he spent most of the boat working out what he was going to say and none of it was any use.

He arrived ready to explain, and Abloh, who had scoured cloth his whole working life, already knew most of it.

Ash. Water. Fat. Heat.

He had been making the soft version for the trade since he was fourteen. He could do it in his sleep and had, more than once, and he described it the way a man describes a thing he stopped finding interesting twenty years ago.

What he had never had was a reason to make it well, a way to make it hard, or anybody to sell it to.

Emeric brought two things and both of them were small.

Lime into the ash liquor, which made it bite far harder than ash alone ever had. And salt, stirred into the finished batch, which threw the soap up out of the lye as a solid and left a hard bar behind instead of a bucket of grease.

Abloh did the second one in front of him, on the bench, with a handful of salt and a stick.

He watched the curd rise, and stopped stirring, and sat down.

He did not say anything for some time. Then he asked what Kaldo was paying.

Every input was local except two.

Tallow from the butcher, and from the fishing boats, which had been throwing it into the water for as long as anyone had been fishing. Ash from every hearth on the island, which was the first time in living memory that anybody in Kaldo had paid a poor household for something it already threw away, and which by itself changed how four hundred doors opened to him.

Lime out of a kiln that had not been lit in a decade and took nine days to bring back.

Two of those days were spent finding a man who remembered how it had been run, and the man they found was seventy and had been a boy at it, and had to be walked up to the thing and left standing in front of it for a while before any of it came back to him.

It came back. He put his hand flat on the stone and said which course had been relaid and which had not, and was right, and then would not accept a wage for the first week on grounds Emeric did not fully follow and did not argue with.

Emeric lit it for soap. He did not think about what else a lime kiln is for, because there was no reason that month to think about it.

And salt, which had to come from the coast at the bottom of the barony, where the branches rejoined and ran down to the sea.

There was no road worth the name and the port was dead, so he paid a boatman to make the run and paid him badly, and the boatman went, and came back, and went again the following month.

Fletcher heard about it inside the week. Emeric did not hear that he had heard about it, and did not go down and look at his own coastline, and it did not occur to him even once that he had just given Kaldo a reason to want a harbor again.

Somebody had to count bushels of ash coming in from four hundred hearths, and Bram sent his daughter.

She was in her twenties, she kept the forge's books, and she did not introduce herself. She handed Emeric the week's figure on a folded sheet in a hand considerably better than his own, and she made one remark.

"The price you've set works in summer. It won't survive a winter. In winter a house burns more and has less to spare, so you've fixed a price against a supply that's about to move, and you've told four hundred people what it is."

She was correct.

She went.

He did not get her name and it did not occur to him to ask for it.

The third batch was too strong, because nobody had standardized the lye.

A woman in the lower ward burned her hands badly enough that Imani was involved, and Imani had opinions about it that she delivered in the room with the woman present, which was worse than if she had waited.

He stood in that room and owned it and did not distribute any part of it elsewhere.

The fix was not chemistry. There was no way to make it chemistry with what existed in Kaldo.

So the fix was process. Float an egg in the liquor. If the patch showing above the surface is wider than a copper, cut it with water until it is not.

Crude, repeatable, and teachable to anybody with an egg and a coin.

He paid the woman for the month she could not work. He paid her before anybody asked him to, which Bram noticed, and said nothing about, and put into the ledger Bram keeps in his head and has never once shown to anyone.

The first two hundred bars went out free.

On Imani's rounds, to the households already in her register, with no conditions attached and nothing said about it.

Then a fisherman offered to buy six.

Then the inn.

Then a tanner from outside the walls wanted forty.

Then a Wayford carter saw the stack in the yard on a Tuesday and asked what Kaldo was charging by the hundredweight, and that was the moment the thing stopped being sanitation.

The accusation came nine days later, in the lower market, in public, from a man who was neither stupid nor wrong to ask.

The prince says the sickness comes from filth. The prince sells the cure for filth. The prince has been giving it away until the town got used to it. Ask yourself what it costs next year.

A crowd came together the way crowds do in a market, which is instantly and from nowhere.

And this was Emeric at the very top of his form, because the answer had been prepared for a week and it looked like generosity from every angle it could be viewed from.

He gave away the method.

Out loud, standing on nothing, in enough detail to be used. Ash and lime and water. The egg and the coin. Fat, rendered clean. Salt at the end to throw the curd. He said it slowly enough for people to repeat it to each other, and then he said it a second time because somebody at the back asked him to.

Anybody in Kaldo may make it. Anybody in Kaldo may sell it. He would not stop them and would not want to, and he would buy their ash at the same price either way.

Then the hook, delivered cheerfully, with his hands open.

Anybody who fancied spending a day rendering tallow in their own kitchen was welcome to it. Anybody who would rather pay two coppers and not do that knew where the shop was going to be.

He had converted a conflict of interest into a product demonstration in about four minutes, and had made the accuser look small without once insulting him, and had done it so smoothly that the accuser bought two bars on his way out and went on buying them for years.

Imani punctured it an hour later.

"You've just taught four hundred people to handle caustic liquor."

He opened his mouth.

"In their kitchens. With children in them. What are you going to do about the next pair of hands?"

He did not have an answer.

He had one by the end of the month, and it was Abloh, teaching anyone who wanted to learn it, in the yard, on a fixed morning, for a wage Emeric paid out of the first money the shop took.

It became the first thing in Kaldo that was taught rather than told.

Two weeks after the Wayford order there was coin coming in, and he did the thing that defined him afterward, which is that he did not keep any of it.

It went into the kiln. Then into wages. Then into the first two wells, cut above the rows, under the order he had spent eleven days winning and could not fund.

Men who had not had paid work in eleven years were digging.

He took the signed sheet out of the drawer and looked at it for a while.

Nothing on the page had changed. Every one of Wexler's objections had been true on the day and was still true. What had changed was that there was money, and the money had not come from being right about anything.

He made himself do the rest of that arithmetic, and it was uncomfortable and he wrote it down anyway.

The thing that had finally moved Kaldo was not the map. It was not the trials. It was not the truth, which he had been handing out free for three months to people who nodded and closed the door.

It was employment.

Wexler came down to the yard on a cold afternoon and was pleasant about all of it.

He asked good questions. He asked how the ash price held against winter, which was the one thing Emeric had no answer for and which somebody else had already told him. He complimented the operation and meant about half of it, and the half he meant was the half about the rota.

He stayed twenty minutes and left having understood something nobody in Kaldo had said out loud.

Six weeks ago the crown had sent him a fool who would be gone by winter. Now there was coin moving through a yard that was not his, men on a payroll that was not his, a kiln relit, a blacksmith and a healer and a priest who all took the boy's word in public, and a prince who had learned in one season how to make a dying town profitable.

Waiting had stopped being a strategy.

Late, in the cold room, Emeric added to the list.

Wells, begun. Kiln, lit. Ash, priced wrong for winter. Rows, unresolved. Fletcher, unresolved. Petram, unresolved. Garrin, unresolved. Letters, unanswered.

And at the bottom, underlined once, the thing he still could not look at directly.

Two of my father's men were not my father's men.

Two floors below him a man who had run this barony for thirty years finished a list of his own, and it was much shorter, and every item on it could be counted.
