Chapter 13 - Two Thousand Acres

The instrument was two sticks, a length of twine, a lead weight, and a shallow pan of water, and Emeric was so pleased with it that he had trouble getting anyone to take him seriously about the rest of the morning.

"It's a level," he said.

Bram looked at the sticks. "It's a stick."

"It's two sticks. That's the entire innovation." He crouched, sighted along the water's edge, and waved Rylan another six paces down the floodplain with the marked pole. "Water sits flat. Always. It doesn't have an opinion about it. So if I know where flat is, I know how much this ground falls between here and there, and if I know that I know whether a channel runs by itself or needs something pushing it. Which matters, because the something pushing it would be men, and men are the one thing this barony has none of."

"You made a stick tell you that."

"I made two sticks and a bowl tell me that." He was aware he sounded delighted. He was delighted. It was the first morning in six weeks that he'd woken up with something in front of him that was purely a problem, with no one's feelings sitting on top of it, and he'd been out on the floodplain since the light was grey. "Rylan. Read it."

"Four and a bit above the mark, Your Highness."

"Four and a bit." Emeric wrote it down. "Bram, that's a nineteenth. Nineteen paces along, one pace down. Do you know what that means?"

"No, and I've a feeling I'm about to."

"It means the whole floodplain is a very slightly tilted table and the river's at the high end of it, which means if I open a channel up there the water walks down here on its own and I never pay a single man to lift a bucket again as long as this barony stands." He stood up and looked at the two of them, and they were both watching him with the same expression, and he recognized it because Ward had been giving him that exact expression since he was nine years old. "You're doing the face."

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're both doing the face. It's fine. I've been doing engineering at people who didn't ask for it my whole life. Both of my whole lives." He picked up the pan. "Move down. I want the fall on the east side before the light changes."

They surveyed until midday and then again for three days after that, and by the end of it Emeric had something he had wanted since the first week he'd stood in this town smelling its water: numbers on the whole thing at once.

He put them on two boards in the castle's long room, because there was no table big enough and because the long room had been sitting empty for eleven years doing nothing but proving how few people lived here now.

Ferra came up to look. She stood in front of the first board for a while without saying anything, which from her was a review.

"It's a loop," she said.

"It's a loop."

"I thought you'd draw it like a tree. A big channel down the middle and little ones off it."

"So did I, for about two days." He tapped the intake, up at the top where the western arm ran steadiest, above the burial ground and above the latrines, where the water was clean. "A tree's easier to dig and it's wrong. A tree has a trunk, and everything downstream of a break in the trunk gets nothing. Whereas this." He ran his finger the whole way round the ring, up one side of the fields, across the top, down the far side, back along the bottom to where the second feeder came in. "Water can reach any square from two directions. Break it anywhere, close two gates, the ring still fills from the other way round. It costs me maybe a fifth more digging and it means no single spade-length of this thing can starve the farm."

"That's the wells again."

"That's the wells again," he agreed. "One of everything and nothing behind it. That's how this barony fails at literally everything, and I'm done building things that fail that way."

He said the next part carefully, because he'd been trying to work out how to say it to somebody for weeks and she was the one who would hold him to it.

"Rule for everything we put on this plain. Whatever the clever version of a thing is, the stupid version has to be underneath it and the stupid version has to be able to carry the whole load on its own. This ring is clay-lined ditch and gravity. That's all. No stone, no crystal, no rune, nothing that needs me alive and awake to work. Water goes downhill because water goes downhill, and there is no morning in any world where it stops. Later I'll put things on top of it, and the things on top will be better, and if every one of them goes dead in the same hour the fields still get watered by a ditch dug by a man with a spade."

Ferra considered that. "You expect them to go dead."

"I expect everything to go dead eventually. Doesn't matter what I expect." He set the chalk down. "A farm that only works when the magic works is a farm that starves the first year the magic doesn't. So the magic never gets to be the floor. It only ever gets to be the ceiling."

"And the pipe you keep drawing in the margin?"

"Clay." He almost laughed. "Bram has enough ore for plows and hand tools this season and nothing past that, and I'm not spending iron on a thing I can dig out of the riverbank and fire. Clay pipe, brass at the joints when there's brass, and until there's brass it's ditch. Which works."

"How much land does the ring reach?"

"That's the good part." He moved to the second board, and here he slowed down, because this was the part he'd checked four times. "Currently worked: two thousand acres, give or take sixty. What this plain could carry, with water on it and the soil fed instead of guessed at, and rotation instead of the same thing in the same ground until it quits: four thousand six hundred. I'll defend four thousand four. I'd bet on four six."

Ferra looked at the two numbers for a while.

"Say the third number," she said.

"Which third number?"

"The one you haven't written on the board, which is the one you've been standing in front of for four days looking like a man who's been kicked."

He had not known it showed. He filed that with the other things about himself he'd been finding out lately from other people.

"Hands," he said.

He wrote it up plainly, because there was no version of it that got better for being dressed.

Two thousand acres, worked by roughly two hundred and forty working hands across the tenant farms and the smallholdings. Call it eight acres to a hand, which was about right for what they had, which was oxen where there were oxen and people where there weren't.

Four thousand six hundred acres wanted five hundred and seventy.

"Three hundred and thirty short," Ferra said, before he'd finished the subtraction, because of course she had.

"Three hundred and thirty."

And there it was, and he had known it was coming since the second day of the survey and had still needed to see it on the board.

"The levy took four hundred men," he said.

The room went quiet in a way the long room was well shaped for.

He'd had the number from Bram in the forge on the day he came home, thrown out as an argument, and he'd taken it the way he took everything, as an input. It had sat in the list for six weeks as a fact about the town's mood. It had not once occurred to him to put it next to the acreage, because the acreage was an engineering column and the levy was a political one, and he had been keeping those in separate books the same way Ferra had been keeping the iron.

Three hundred and thirty missing hands. Four hundred men who had walked out of that gate under a crown banner and not walked back in.

"It's not the same men," Ferra said. She'd worked out where he'd gone. "Some of the four hundred were smiths and boatmen and coopers. And the plague took more than the levy did, and it took women and children and old men, and none of that goes in a column called hands."

"No. It doesn't." He looked at the board. "It's not the same men. It's the same size of hole, and the hole has a cause, and my father signed it."

"You didn't."

"I've spent four months being told I'm the crown, by people who were being perfectly accurate." He put the chalk down. "I'm not going to stand here and be dramatic about it. It's a subtraction. But I'd been treating this plain as though it were empty because nobody had gotten around to filling it, and it isn't empty. It's been emptied. Those are different problems and only one of them is mine to be surprised by."

Ferra let that sit for exactly as long as it needed and then, because she was who she was, went back to the board.

"Then the ring," she said. "How many hands to dig it?"

He'd been dreading her getting there and had also been counting on it.

"Thirteen miles, with the loop. Call it forty men, working a season, if the ground behaves."

"Forty men from where?"

"From the two hundred and forty."

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. He said it himself, out loud, because saying it was the only way to be sure he'd actually understood it instead of just having a bad feeling about it.

"To open twenty-six hundred new acres I have to stop working three hundred and twenty of the acres I've got. For a year, minimum. In a town that eats what it grows." He rubbed the back of his neck. "And that's the easy version, because those forty men don't come out of one pocket. They come out of nine. Halloway lends me four and loses four men's work off his own ground, and the water arrives two years later and reaches Corbell's corner first. So Halloway pays and Corbell drinks. Nine men who each have to bet their own harvest that the other eight won't cheat them." He stopped. "That's not a labor problem. That's an accounting problem with a labor problem sitting on it."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning the arithmetic doesn't close while the plain is nine farms." He heard himself say it and felt the floor of it go solid. "It closes if it's one."

What he could do, he did, and he did it well, and for one afternoon it was the best week he'd had since he arrived.

He got the tenant farmers into the long room. There were nine of them who mattered and four who came anyway, and they stood at the back with their hats in their hands looking at the boards like men waiting to be told what the fine was.

He opened by explaining rotation.

It went badly. Not disastrously. Badly. He got about four minutes into why you don't put the same crop in the same ground every year before he registered that the man nearest him, a broad quiet tenant named Halloway who worked the biggest holding on the east side, had gone from polite to actively offended, and that he'd been putting the same crop in the same ground for thirty years because his father had, and that a twenty-two-year-old prince from the capital had just spent four minutes describing him as the reason his own yields were falling.

Emeric stopped mid-sentence.

"I'm doing this wrong," he said.

Halloway blinked at him.

"I came in here to tell you a thing I know and you've got no reason to take my word for it, and worse than that, I said it in a way that made you the problem. You're not the problem. You've had no water, no metal, no help, and half your hands are dead, and you've kept a hundred and forty acres in crop through all of it, which I could not have done." He put the chalk down. "Let me start over and do it the way that actually works. Can I have your yields? Not this year. Every year you can remember. And anyone else's."

It took them the rest of the morning and most of the afternoon, and none of it was written down anywhere, and all of it was in their heads, because a man remembers what his own ground gave him. Emeric wrote as fast as he could and Ferra wrote faster and cleaner, and by the time the light was going they had eleven holdings and between nine and thirty years each.

He put them on the board the way he'd put the deaths on the ledger map. Not as an argument. As dots.

"That's yours," he said to Halloway, and traced it. "Thirty years. It's a slide. Not a bad year, not weather, a slide. Every year a little less out of the same ground." He moved along. "That's Corbell's. Same slide. That's the Ashe holding, and that one isn't sliding, and I want to know why, and I think you all already know why and have never once said it out loud in the same room."

"Ashe floods," somebody said from the back.

"Ashe floods," Emeric agreed. "Every spring the river comes over that corner and leaves half an inch of silt on it and goes away again, and Ashe curses it, and Ashe's yields haven't moved in nineteen years. That's the whole of what I was trying to tell you before I made a hash of it. The ground gets tired and something has to feed it, and the river's been feeding one holding out of eleven by accident, and I can build you something that does it on purpose."

Nobody cheered. It was not that sort of room and he had stopped expecting it to be. But Halloway came up to the board afterward and stood in front of his own thirty years for a long time, and then asked a question about clover so specific it took Emeric ten minutes to answer, and eight of the nine put their marks to the rotation and the water schedule.

The ninth was Old Pell's nephew, which Emeric decided was a family trait and left alone.

Then Halloway wandered over to the first board, the one with the ring on it, the way a man looks at a picture when the talking is done. He looked at it for a long moment. He counted along the bottom with one finger.

"Nine squares," he said.

"Nine divisions. Three sections of three. Two working while one rests, and the resting one takes clover, so it comes back fed instead of just idle."

"Aye." Halloway's finger stayed on the board. "Which one's mine?"

Emeric opened his mouth.

He had drawn that grid to the fall of the land. He had drawn it four times, and each time he had moved a line to make a division sit square to the water, and at no point in four days of drawing had it occurred to him to lay the boundaries over the top of it, because the boundaries were not in the survey. The survey had slope and soil and distance in it. It did not have men in it.

There was no square on that board that was Halloway's. There was no square on that board that was anybody's.

"None of them," he said.

He watched the room change. It did not take long.

"I'm going to say the whole of it, because you'll work it out on the walk home and I'd rather you heard it from me standing here." He turned the board so they could all see it. "The ring waters four thousand six hundred acres if it runs as one field with nine divisions. It waters considerably less than that if it has to bend around eleven sets of hedges, because half the fall gets spent going sideways. And forty men have to dig it, and forty men from nine separate holdings is nine men each betting their own harvest on the other eight. I can't make that work. I've tried for four days. The only shape that works is one field."

"One field," said Halloway.

"One field, worked together, with everyone who works it holding a written share of what comes off it. Not tenancy. Ownership, on paper, in parts, and the parts are yours to keep and to leave to your children."

"And the hedges come out."

"The hedges come out."

A tenant near the door, a heavy-shouldered man named Dunnel who had not said a word all day, laughed once, without any humor in it at all.

"There it is," Dunnel said. "Four years back the crown sent a paper and took four hundred men off this plain. Last month the crown sent a paper and took the baron off that wall. Now the crown's got another paper and it wants the ground." He put his hat back on, which in that room was a sentence by itself. "My grandfather's hedge. I know every stone in it. You want me to hand it to you and take a piece of paper back."

"I want you to hand it to the farm and take a piece of paper back that says a part of the farm is yours."

"And who keeps the farm?"

"I do."

"Aye," Dunnel said. "So you keep the ground and I keep the paper. That's the trade my father took from Wexler, only Wexler had the decency to call it rent."

Emeric had four answers to that and every one of them was a number, and he understood, standing there with the chalk in his hand, that a number was going to be worth precisely nothing in this room in the next ten seconds.

So he didn't use one.

"That's fair," he said. "I don't have anything with me that would make you believe otherwise. I'd have to write it in a way you can check without me in the room, and I haven't written it yet, and if I stood here and swore at you it would be the same swearing every man in a castle has done to every man in a field since the invention of the field." He looked at Halloway, then at Dunnel. "Come back in eight days and I'll have terms on paper. Read them. Bring anyone who can count. If they're bad, say so out loud and I'll take the hedges out of it."

They filed out. Halloway went last, and stopped at the door.

"You're not wrong about the ground being tired," he said. "You're just asking me to stop being a man with land and start being a man with a promise. Those aren't the same size."

"No," Emeric said. "They're not."

Ward found him still in the long room after dark, sitting on the edge of the table with the boards turned to the wall.

"I heard how it went," Ward said.

"You always do."

"Rylan's wife had it off the tanner before Rylan had it off you."

"That's fine. That's actually the only part of the day that's working properly." He rubbed his eyes. "Say the thing you came to say."

Ward pulled a chair around and sat, which he did perhaps twice a season.

"You're about to put the whole plain in one set of hands," he said. "And then you're going to write down that a hundred men own a piece of it, and let them choose a man to speak for the lot of them, with paper to prove the claim." He let that sit. "That's a body. Bodies get used. In ten years, if a lord does something they don't like, that's not a mob you can ignore. That's a farm that can stop."

Emeric was quiet for a moment. It was, he thought, the most purely political sentence he had ever heard come out of Ward's mouth, and Ward did not appear to have noticed saying it.

"Yes," he said.

"You've thought of that."

"I thought of it on the second day."

"And?"

"And a field nobody owns is a field nobody guards." He looked up. "If it can stop, it can also refuse to be stolen. I'd rather build the thing that can hurt me than the thing that can only be taken from me. Ask me again in ten years and I may have changed my mind, and by then it'll be far too late, and I'd like you to remember that I knew."

Ward grunted. It was not agreement. It was the sound of a disagreement being filed where Emeric would find it later if he was wrong.

He walked down through the town two nights later with Rylan, who had a stated errand and an unstated one, and the unstated one turned out to be that Nessa had roasted something and it was not going to keep.

The house was the same small clean house. Finn did not hide this time, which Emeric noticed and had the sense not to comment on. Wren looked up at him from the floor, where she was doing something violent to a wooden horse, considered him for a while, and delivered her verdict.

"You're the one who made everyone boil the water."

"I am."

"It takes ages."

"It does."

"It's stupid."

"Wren," said Nessa.

"It is a bit stupid," Emeric admitted. "It's stupid and it works, which is the worst combination, because you can't even enjoy complaining about it." Wren narrowed her eyes at him, suspecting a trick, found none, and went back to the horse having decided he was at minimum not lying to her.

He ate at their table and let the conversation go wherever it went, which was to a cousin's wedding and the price of salt and whether Finn was too small for the work he wanted to be doing, and at no point in two hours did anyone ask him about the baron, or about the hedges, or about Dunnel. He was fairly sure that was Nessa, and that it had been decided in advance, and that he was not supposed to know.

He thanked her for the meal on the way out and she said the thing she'd clearly been holding all evening.

"My brother's in Wayford," she said. "Two children. Works another man's field and keeps a fifth of what comes off it, and he'll never hold anything of his own there as long as he lives." She looked at him steadily. "If you had ground going spare, and a roof on it, I'd write to him."

"I'd take him tomorrow."

"I know. That's not the difficult part." She wiped her hands on the cloth. "The difficult part is what I'd have to put in the letter. Come to Kaldo. It's got no baron because the crown hanged the last one. Half the town's not talking to the prince who lives in the castle. The water was poison until this spring." A small dry pause, entirely Rylan's wife. "Bring your own metal."

Emeric started to answer, and stopped, and stood in her doorway with his hand on the frame for long enough that Rylan shifted behind him.

"Say the first part again," he said.

"Which first part?"

"Your brother. What he keeps."

"A fifth," Nessa said. "Same as anyone."

He walked back up the hill without saying much, and Rylan, who had been reading him for four months and was better at it than most, didn't ask.

Imani caught him in the long room the next morning, ostensibly to argue about the boiling schedule for the new intake and actually to look at the boards, and asked what the pile of paper on the end was.

He told her. All of it, the eight days and the hedges and Dunnel's grandfather and the letter Nessa couldn't write.

She read the top sheet, which was a costing for a settlement bounty, and put it back down.

"That'll move eleven families," she said. "And then you're insolvent, and they'll come for the coin and leave when it stops."

"I know. I did that one to prove to myself it was stupid."

"Then know the rest of it too." She picked up her bag. "Nobody moves their children to a strange barony for a fixed sum each quarter. They move because a woman they trust wrote and told them the water's clean now and her own boy drank it. That isn't a number, and it's the whole thing, and it will not be you who writes it."

"No," he said. "I've worked that out."

He found Bram at the forge in the afternoon with two plowshares cooling by the door, the first two out of the barony's own iron in eleven years, and stood watching them go from orange to grey for a while before he asked.

"How do men here hold a thing that no one man can afford?"

Bram didn't look up. "Boat."

"A boat."

"A river boat's forty crowns and no man on this bank has forty crowns." He turned the shear with the tongs. "So it goes in sixteen parts. I hold two of the Marrow, have done nine years. Old Ashe holds four. Widow Corbell holds one and a half, which is a nuisance to write and she'd not sell it to God." He set the tongs down. "Parts pass to your children. Parts sell, if the rest of us will have the buyer. And the man who takes her downriver is Teague, who holds no part at all, because he's the best boatman on this water and none of us can steer."

Emeric stood very still.

"Say that last part again."

"Teague holds none of her and captains her, and we hold all of her and do what he says on the water, because on the water he's right and we're cargo." Bram finally looked up, and something in Emeric's face made him narrow his eyes. "What."

"Nothing." Emeric was already leaving. "Bram, who wrote the parts down?"

"Chandler's book. Third page. It's been the same page since before I was born, and if you're about to tell me you've never read it, I'd say that's the most princely thing you've done all year."

He got the list out that night, under the empty space, and wrote two lines.

The thing this barony needs most, I am the worst person here to get.

Then, because it was true, and because it was the eighth day in five and he had that long to write terms that a man who knew every stone in his grandfather's hedge would sign:

Chandler's book, third page. Stop inventing. Copy.
