Chapter 13 - The Name

The boy on Petram's door had shaved badly again.

He straightened when Emeric came along the east corridor, the way he always did, and Emeric nodded to him, the way he always did, and went in. The boy had been on that door over a fortnight. He was about nineteen and had a nick on his jaw most mornings, and Emeric had never once asked his name. He'd assumed, without ever putting it into words, that the boy was Wexler's.

Most of the house was Wexler's. That was the problem, and it had come about so gradually that Emeric had agreed to every step of it.

In the third week he'd taken Declan off the door, because Declan could read a level and somebody had to run the ground for the wells before Wexler would hear a word about them. That had been Emeric's idea. In the sixth week Imani had asked for Rylan on the low streets, because the women there had known his mother, and Emeric had said yes. In the eighth he'd put Osric at the soap yard to sit beside the coin bag at the ash scales, because a bag of coppers in the lower ward wanted a man with a sword near it. His idea again. And in the tenth, Ward had taken himself off the rota altogether and onto Emeric, and hadn't asked anybody.

Each time, Hale had filled the gap from the castle's own men, quietly and well, and Emeric had thanked him.

Declan still looked in most evenings on his way back from the levels and brought the reports. Petram was eating. Petram was sleeping. That was what Emeric had built, and he hadn't looked at the whole of it once.



Since the day with Corliss he'd been coming every few days simply to sit with Petram.

It was mostly dull. Petram didn't talk much, and when he did it was about the food, or the weather through the one high window, or once, for most of an hour, about horses, which it turned out he knew a great deal about. Emeric left the door open each time. An open door was a record. Rylan came with him when he could, and leaned on the wall, and said even less than Petram did.

Today Petram was sitting on the edge of the bed with his forearms on his knees, and he didn't look up when they came in.

"There'll be a magistrate," he said. "Sooner or later."

"Probably."

"And I'll hang."

Emeric didn't answer that. He sat on the stool by the door.

"I never met him," Petram said. "The one who paid. I told you that, and it's true." He looked at his hands. "I met the man he sent. If I give you his name, I want to stand up in front of whoever it is with my neck in one piece and say so myself. That's all. I want to get there."

"I can't promise you a magistrate."

"Then promise me you'll try."

Emeric thought about it longer than Petram seemed to expect. Rylan shifted against the wall.

"I'll try," Emeric said.

Petram nodded slowly, as if he'd been handed a coin he wasn't sure was good and had decided to take it anyway.

"Cobb," he said. "A courier. Rides the south road with letters for whoever pays him. He's the one brought the purse to the stable yard."

Outside the open door, the boy on watch went still.

It wasn't much. He'd been shifting his weight from one foot to the other, as young men on a door do, and he stopped. He didn't startle. He stopped the way a man stops when he hears his own name from the next room. Then he went back to shifting his weight.

Emeric saw it. He took it in and set it to one side and did nothing about it, and went on looking at Petram.

Petram had seen it too. His eyes had gone to the door and come back, and now he was looking at Emeric steadily, waiting to see what the prince would do.

The prince asked what Cobb looked like. Petram told him. Middling height, middling age, a horseman's legs, a face you'd forget. Nothing more. After a while Emeric thanked him and went out past the boy, who straightened, and nodded to him, and he nodded back.



They found the boy before dinner.

A carter coming in off the river road found him on the mill road, where it ran along the bank below the east bridge past the wheels. He was lying on his face in the dust at the side of the road, his purse cut and gone, his boots gone too. Robbed, the carter said, to anyone who'd listen. Robbed and done for, on a summer evening, not a mile from the gate.

By the time Emeric got there Imani was already kneeling in the dust, and somebody had turned the boy onto his back, and Emeric saw the nick on the jaw and stopped where he was.

Imani took her time. When she looked up, it was at Emeric, and she said it only to him, low.

"Once. From behind." She touched the back of her own neck, one place, exactly. "Somebody who's done it before."

"Robbed?"

She looked at the bare feet in the dust. "If you like."

He waited. She stood up, wiped her hands, and said nothing else.

Ward was beside him. He hadn't heard Ward arrive. Ward looked at the body for about as long as Imani had, and then up the road toward the keep, and then at Emeric.

That night Ward slept across Petram's door himself, on the floor, with his sword beside him. Nobody had asked him to.



Emeric found Hale in the steward's room late, with one candle, doing the next day's accounts.

"The boy on the door," Emeric said. "What was his name?"

He'd had to ask. He heard it as he said it.

Hale put down his pen.

"Tam," he said. "Sela's boy. The middle one." He looked at the candle rather than at Emeric. "He took the post in the spring. Half a copper a day more than the docks paid. His mother wanted him off the water." A pause. "He was on the gate the day you came in, Your Highness. It was him let you through."

Emeric remembered the gate. A boy with a bad shave who'd looked at his eyes and gone red to the ears.

Twelve weeks. He'd walked past that face a dozen times a day for over a fortnight and never put it with the gate.

Sela had three sons. He knew that. He'd known the first part of it since his third day in Kaldo, standing over her buckets in the mud: the eldest, taken by the crown for its war and never accounted for. He'd known the last part since the fourth week, standing outside a house in the dark while Imani kept the youngest alive. The middle one had been standing at Petram's door, and Emeric had nodded to him every morning.

"Thank you," he said, and went out, and stood in the corridor for some time before he went to bed.



He told her himself, in the morning, at her counter.

There were people in the shop. Three women and an old man with a basket. He could have waited until they left, or sent Imani, or gone to Sela's house, and he'd thought about all three on the way down the hill and decided against every one of them. It had happened in front of the town. He wasn't going to tell her in a back room.

Sela was setting out bars in their rows when he came in. She saw his face, and stopped.

He told her. Plainly, in a voice the room could hear, because she'd want to know that it was true and nobody had softened it.

She listened without moving. When he'd finished she put down the bar she was holding, square with the others.

"Where."

"On the mill road. Below the bridge."

"Was it fast."

He thought of Imani's hand at the back of her own neck.

"Yes."

Sela looked at him, at his face, and at his eyes, the gold ones, the crown's.

"Get out of my shop," she said.

He went.

She opened it the next morning, at the usual hour. Somebody told him. She kept the north well's fire that evening too, and the evening after, and went on charging two coppers a bar, counting change into people's palms the way she always had. Imani went down to her house each night that week and sat with her, and when Emeric asked, Imani looked at him and said nothing at all.

The shop had its worst week since it opened. People came in, looked at Sela, couldn't think what to say, bought nothing, and left. The week after was the best it ever had. Half the lower ward came through her door to buy a bar they didn't need, and put the coppers in her hand, and some of them held on to her hand a moment longer than the coppers needed.

When Emeric next came in for soap, she served the man behind him first. Then she took his two coppers without looking at him, and turned away to the shelf before he'd picked up the bar.



Rylan kept quiet for three days.

He did his work. He walked the low streets with Imani, and came up to the keep at night, and stood his share of watches, and he and Emeric passed each other in the corridors and the yard and didn't speak about the mill road once.

On the third evening he stopped in Emeric's doorway on his way to bed.

"In the keep," he said. "Should I stop saying Nessa's name."

It took Emeric a moment to understand what Rylan was asking, and what he'd been thinking about for three days. Then he thought of Petram's fire on the fifth night, and Rylan telling a stranger his wife's name, and his children's ages, and his street.

"Yes," he said.

Rylan nodded, as if it was what he'd expected, and went to bed. He never said her name inside the keep again.



"Wexler," Ward said.

It was late on the second night, and they were in Emeric's room with the door shut, and Ward said it at the volume of the whisper code, which only reached as far as the bed.

"No."

"He's the man with reach in this house."

"He is." Emeric was sitting at the desk with the notebook shut in front of him. "Which is exactly why I won't say it."

Ward waited.

"Everything about it points one way," Emeric said. "The house is his. The men are his. The boy was on a door Hale filled. So the name's sitting there, ready, before I've done any thinking at all." He turned the shut notebook a quarter round on the desk, and back. "A conclusion that arrives already assembled is a conclusion somebody else built."

Ward studied him. Then he said, "All right," which from Ward was a great deal.

There were three ways the name could have left that room in the four hours between Petram saying it and the carter finding the boy. Somebody had been listening that Emeric hadn't seen. Or the boy had told somebody. Or the boy had been killed by someone who didn't care what he'd heard, only what Emeric would do once he was dead.

Every one of the three needed somebody with reach inside the castle. None of them told him who.

He didn't write any of it down. He sat with the notebook shut and turned the three over, and somewhere in the turning he realized he'd started keeping a second set of books, in his head, where nobody could read them. One for what he knew. One for what he wasn't going to say until he did.

His father's rule, again.

What he couldn't put in either book was himself.

He'd taken four men off a door, one at a time, a fortnight apart, each move sensible, two of them his own idea, and put the castle's men in their places, and thanked the steward for it. He'd sat in that room and coaxed a name out of a frightened man with the door wide open to the corridor, because an open door was a record. He had never once asked the name of the boy standing in it.

He had seen a man's fall three steps before it happened, once. He had not seen the car.

When he looked down he found he'd closed his hand, and that his nails had gone into the palm deep enough to break it in four small crescents. He hadn't felt it. He opened the hand and looked at the marks and then went and washed it, in water that had been boiled, because there was no reason not to.



They had rebuilt it the morning after the mill road, before he walked down to Sela's counter.

Petram went down to the cell under the keep, a stone room with one door and one stair and nothing above it but the keep's own foundations. Ward set the rotation himself. Emeric's four men came back to it: Declan off the levels, Osric out of the soap yard, Rylan off the low streets, and Ward over all of them. Nobody from the castle came within thirty feet of the stair, and Hale, told this, said, "Of course, Your Highness," and arranged the kitchen so that Petram's food came down by Rylan's hand and nobody else's.

It cost. The well dig stopped for two weeks without Declan to run the levels. The soap yard lost the man who sat beside the coin bag, and Abloh had to count the coppers himself, and was slow at it, and said so.

At the end of that week Emeric went down to the forge and told Bram what he'd done, all of it, because Bram would hear it from somebody, and he would rather it was from him.

Bram listened without stopping work. He drew a bar from the fire, looked at the colour, and put it back.

"You should have done that in the spring," he said.
