Chapter 169 - Rack Dry

There were nineteen of them in the long room, and the prince had shut the door himself, and Ned Tallis stood at the back by the wall because there were not enough chairs and he had never in his life sat down in a room with a prince in it and did not mean to start.

Tobe sat at the end of the table with both hands on it palm up, wrapped in linen, as the physician had told him to keep them. Cade sat beside him with his the same. The rest were the ship's people: the rack hands and the yard men from the lower water and the two from the etching floor who went up with them on the long nights, and the knight Osric at the end of the table with his arms folded, and Ferra with her book shut in front of her. Everybody in Kaldo who had ever stood on the deck of the thing that went up in the dark, except the prince's other knights, and the prince himself, who was standing at the head of the table with one hand on the back of the chair he had not sat in.

"I'm going to tell you what's happened to you," the prince said. "And then I'm going to tell you what I did."

He did not start straight away. He looked at Tobe's hands for a while first. Ned watched him do it, and thought about the night, because he had thought about nothing else for six days.

He had been a lighterman for twenty years on the estuary below the lower water, and his father before him, and he had known what a lighter was all his life: a flat ugly boat for carrying what the ships could not get to, ore and coal and grain and stone, across water too shallow for anything decent. The Hod was a lighter. Everybody on the lower water said so. She was forty feet and blunt at both ends and had one hatch and no rail to speak of, and Fletcher had built her in five weeks in the winter before last with six yard men who had been told she was for the lake, and she was the ugliest thing Ned had ever steered. He had loved her from the first night.

The first night he had gone up in her he had stood at the levers with his knees locked and his hands shaking and watched the lake go small under him in the dark, and the fires on the island, and the lamps on the Grayrock road, and then nothing at all but black and stars and a cold that went into the teeth. Two miles, the prince said. Ned had never been two miles from anything straight up. At the top of it, in what they called the band, the air went thin and quiet and still, the way the estuary went still at slack water before the turn, and the racks in the hold began to hum. He had no gift. He had never had one. He could not feel a charged stone from a cold one in the dark with his hand on it. But he could hear the racks up there. Everybody could. It was a sound like a finger on the rim of a glass, very low, felt in the deck through the soles of the boots, and it went on all night while the racks filled, and when it stopped they were full, and you came down.

Two nights in seven, all through the dark of the year, for a year and a half. He had done it a hundred and some times. It was a job that had not existed when he was forty. He was forty-six now, and bald, and he had a job nobody on the estuary would believe if he told them, and he had never told them, and he never would.

His crew changed in the spring. Garrick and the boy Wes went to hull two on the slip to learn her racks before she flew, because the prince wanted men on her who had been up, and Saban gave Ned two from the big ship's people in their place. Tobe, who had been the prince's man in the foundry for as long as there had been a foundry, and who knew the stones in the racks by name, or seemed to, and had tended the big ship's racks for years on the long nights. And Cade, who was Fletcher's second on the lower water, who could steer anything that floated and had stood the helm-watch on the other thing, the one that did not need to float, for three summers now, the long nights when she hung in the band on her levers and somebody had to be awake beside them. Both of them had been up more times than Ned had. Both of them had been part of the four who kept the big ship in the band through all of the Wayford summer, turn and turn about, a month at a time, while the prince was in the north. Neither of them ever talked about it.

It was their third run together. Late spring, the nights getting short. They had filled the racks in four hours and started down with an hour of dark left and the wind in the band pushing them east all night, so that when they started down they were over the coast and not over the lake, and Ned had the levers and was bringing her round in a long slow slant to come in over the marsh.

He heard it go.

It was not loud. It was a crack like a stick breaking under a boot, down in the hold, and then Tobe's voice, not loud either, saying, "Ned."

And the deck went light under his feet.

He knew what it was before Tobe told him. He had heard the prince say it at the table, the one time the prince had said it, a year and a half ago, with Ferra writing it down: a dead patch in the band, or the racks running dry at height, and she falls. He had thought about it every night since, going up. Every lighterman thinks about the one way his boat can kill him.

The racks had not run dry. The racks were full. They were humming still, down in the hold. But the rail that carried what was in them to the lift bank under the deck was a rod of stone as thick as a man's wrist, running the length of the hold, and it had cracked clean across in the middle, and what was in the racks was not going anywhere.

The lift bank was running on what it held. It held a little. It always held a little, the prince had said, like a pump holds a little in its pipe. It was not built to hold her up. It was built to take what the rail gave it and pass it on.

"How long?" Ned said.

Tobe was on his knees at the crack with the lamp, and he looked up. "Not long."

She was not falling yet. She was sinking, the way a boat sinks when you step into her, faster than she should have been and getting faster, and Ned felt the air start to move past his face, which it never did in the band, and his stomach come up under his ribs. Two miles. Most of two miles still under them. He had worked it out once, lying awake: how long a thing takes to fall two miles. Not long.

He opened the flasks.

There were four of them at the helm in the iron rack, the step after the reserve, the prince called them, for a dead patch: quart flasks of iron with the lids pitched shut, filled at the top of the band. You opened one by the lift bank and for a count of ninety or so the air round the bank was thick again, the way the band was thick, and the bank could drink from it. He opened all four, one after another, and put them on the deck by the lift bank's hatch, and felt nothing, and heard the humming under the deck change note.

She slowed. He felt her slow. The air stopped moving so fast past his face.

Then the flasks were empty, and she started to go again.

"Ned," Cade said.

"I know."

"The racks," Cade said. "They're no good to us. Get them off her."

He was right. Ned saw it the moment he said it. The racks were the heaviest thing aboard. Two tons of charged stone, full to the top, and every pound of it being held up by nothing. He left the levers, which were no use now, and he and Cade went down the hatch into the hold and knocked out the pins on the rack frames with the mallet, one frame and the next and the next, and ran them to the side on their rollers, and tipped them over the low rail into the dark, a season of the barony's charge, a year's worth of Ferra's column, and did not hear them hit anything. Eight frames. Ned's arms were shaking when the last one went. She came up under his feet as it went, like a boat coming up off the mud when the tide lifts her, and slowed again.

Not enough. Not nearly enough.

When he came up the hatch Tobe was on his knees at the lift rail.

The lift rail was the other end of the cracked rod: the short piece of it that ran from the crack to the lift bank, as long as a man's arm, with the bank on the end of it. Tobe had both hands on it. Flat on the stone, the way the prince had shown every farmhand in Kaldo, years ago, how to put his hands on a rail and push what he had into it. Anybody could do it. That was the point of it. It needed no gift, no skill, no knowing how; you put your hands on the stone and you leaned into it with something that was not your arms, and a farmhand could run a pump stone for a few breaths that way before he was spent and had to sit down.

A few breaths.

"Cade," Tobe said. His voice was very quiet.

Cade went down on his knees beside him and put his hands on the rail beside Tobe's.

Ned stood at the hatch and watched them.

He could not feel anything. He had no gift. But he could hear the lift bank under the deck, and he heard it change. He heard the humming come up out of the deck like a kettle coming to the boil, low and steady and then not so low, and he felt her come up under his feet, and stop, and hang. The wind stopped going past his face. The stars stopped moving.

She was not falling. She was coming down. Slowly. The way she came down on a good night off a full rack, in a long slant. A little faster than that, but not much.

He went back to the levers because there was nothing else to do, and he held her on the slant and brought her round toward the sea, which he could see now, grey, a long way under, with a line of paler grey at the edge of it where the dawn was going to be.

He counted.

He had worked it out on the lower field once, with Ferra's clerk Hobb, who had a watch: how long a farmhand could pour into a pump rail before he was spent. The longest was a count of forty. A big man, strong, who had worked the rails for years. Forty, and then he had sat down in the mud and been sick.

Ned got to forty. He got to a hundred. He got to three hundred and stopped counting because he had lost his place and did not want to know.

They held her. Tobe and Cade, on their knees on the deck at the lift rail with their heads down and their hands flat on the stone, not moving, not speaking. They held her all the way down the sky. It was a long way. Ned would not have believed it was so long. The sea came up grey and then dark green and then close enough to see the lines of the swell, and he brought her in on the long slant into the wind, the way he would have brought a lighter onto a mudbank, and at the last he said, "Now," and they took their hands off.

She hit the sea hard. She hit it like a dropped cart, flat, and the spray went up over the bow and came down on them, and the whole deck shuddered, and something in the hold went with a noise like a tree coming down. And then she came up again out of the trough and rolled and wallowed and sat on the swell, low and ugly and wet, and floated.

Because Fletcher had built her a boat first. The prince had wanted a barge. Fletcher had built a lighter, with a keel and ribs and a caulked bottom, because Fletcher did not know how to build anything that would not float, and had said so, and Ned had thought it a waste of good oak at the time.

Tobe had fallen over sideways on the deck. Cade was sitting with his back against the hatch coaming and his hands open in his lap.

Ned went and knelt between them, and took Tobe's hand, and turned it over.

The palm was marked. Thin red lines across it, fine as thread, in a pattern like frost on a window, crossing each other, raised a little off the skin and hot to the touch. Cade's were the same. Ned had never seen those lines before. He had heard of them, on the lower water, the way men hear of things: what a stone does to a man's hands when he gives it more than he has.

"Can you stand?" Ned said.

"No," said Tobe, and started to laugh, and could not stop for a while.

In the long room, six days later, the prince told them what it was.

He did not dress it. Ned had heard him talk to the barony at the market cross, the day of the dead at the lower water, when he named the eleven and said he had drawn the ditch, and he talked the same way now. Plainly, in short sentences, as if he were telling a man the price of a thing.

A farmhand could pour into a rail for a few breaths, he said. A knight, one made in the chamber in the capital, with the tracery in his arms, could pour for a long time: minutes. That was what the chamber was for. That was what a knight was: a man who could hold a great deal and give it out and not run dry. Tobe and Cade had held a falling hull for the time it takes to come down two miles on a slant. That was not a farmhand. That was a knight.

Neither of them had ever been in the chamber.

"It's the stone," the prince said. "Years beside it. Years in the band, where the medium is thick, and years on a deck over racks of charged stone, and years in the foundry beside the forge. It changes a man. Slowly. It's been changing all of you." He looked round the table. "Master Armand has been measuring it for five years. He's measured every one of you, every winter, in the east room, and written it down. It's moved in every one of you. Some more than others. Nobody's got the tracery. Nobody's been hurt by it. And two of you held a hull in the sky with it."

Nobody said anything. Ned looked at the backs of the heads in front of him and could not see a single face.

"That's what's happened to you," the prince said. "Now what I did."

He took his hand off the chair.

"When I chose the men for the ship, and the racks, and the etching floor, I chose most of you because I thought this might happen. I didn't know that it would. I thought that it might. I wanted to know if it did. And I didn't tell you." He said it in the same voice as the rest. "I'm telling you now because two of you would be dead if it hadn't, and because you have the right to know what's in you, and what I did to put it there."

It was very quiet in the long room.

Ferra had her hand on her book. She did not open it.

Tobe lifted his wrapped hands off the table a little, and put them down again.

"Can it be stopped?" he said.

The prince looked at him.

"No," he said.

Tobe nodded, as if that was the answer he had expected, and did not ask anything else.

Hull two went up nine days later, a week before Fletcher had said she would, because the barony's charge for the season was at the bottom of the sea off the marsh and the works would stop in a fortnight without it.

She had two rail paths. The prince and the foundryman Bram had spent the nine days putting them in, with the old master Armand writing half the cells, sitting at a bench in the shed behind the outside foundry with his hand flat on the stones for an hour at a time and his face grey. Every rack in her hold was coupled to her lift bank by two rods, each on its own side of the keel, and either one would carry the load if the other went. And a third thing, which the prince called a watchdog, which Ned did not understand and did not ask about, except that if the lift bank ever began to run on what it held, a stone at the helm went cold under the pilot's hand, before the deck did.

Ned flew her. The prince asked him to. He said Ned was the only man in Kaldo besides himself and his three knights who had brought a hull down out of the band, and the only one who had brought one down without a rail, and he wanted Ned at her levers on the first night because of it.

The Hod was on a trestle on the lower water with her bottom stove in and a new keel rack half built beside her. Fletcher said she would fly again by the autumn with two paths and the prince's watchdog, and that he would give her a proper rail this time if it killed him.

Hull two was three times the Hod's length and half again her beam, and she went up off the far end of the lake in the dark like a building leaving the ground, and her racks filled in the band in three hours, and she came down with an hour's dark to spare and the stones in her hold humming. Her first flight was a supply run. Nobody said so. The prince stood on her deck at the top of the band with the stars all round them and looked at the dark to the east, where the sea was, and Ned heard him say something, very low, to nobody.

"There are other ways to fill a rack," the prince said. "I won't build them."

Ned did not ask what he meant.

He did not sleep much that summer. When he did, he was back on the Hod at dawn.

He was always at the same place. The swell had gone down and the light had come up grey and then pale gold behind a bank of cloud on the edge of the sea, and he was sitting on the hatch coaming with Tobe asleep across his knees and Cade asleep against the bow. The Hod sat low and wet and ugly in the water, with no racks in her and no mast and no sail and no oar, a mile off a coast he did not know, and she looked like exactly what she was: a flat-bottomed lighter, blunt at both ends, with nothing on her that belonged at sea.

And there was a boat. A fishing boat, small, lug-rigged, brown sail, coming out of the land on the morning breeze with her nets in, two men in her, the way fishing boats come out every morning on every coast in the world. He saw them see the Hod. He saw them stop what they were doing, and look, and turn toward her, and he saw the older of the two stand up in the stern with one hand on the sheet and stare at her across the water. A lighter, a mile out, at dawn, where no lighter had any business to be.

He knew what the man was seeing. He knew what the man would say at the quay that evening, and what his wife would say to her sister, and how far it would go before it stopped.

So he stood up, carefully, not to wake Tobe, and waved both arms over his head like a man who had been waiting all night to be found, and began to get it straight in his head while the boat came on. A lighter blown off the estuary in the night by a squall. Mast gone, sweeps gone, cargo gone over the side to keep her up. Three men on her, and two of them had burned their hands on the tackle, and they had been lucky. That was all it was. That was all anybody would need.

It was not true. Most of it was. The man in the stern would believe it, because it was the only thing a man in a fishing boat at dawn could believe about a lighter a mile out at sea, and it was nearly true enough.

The boat came on across the swell with the sun behind it, and Ned stood on the Hod's hatch with his arms up and his story ready.
