Chapter 169 - Last

Ward would not fly the new suit outside until he had flown it in the room.

He said so on the twentieth of December, at the long table, after the business, when Tess asked him when he would take it over the river. He said it the way he said everything, flatly, as a fact about the weather. He had flown it on the rope in the shed. He had flown Emeric's red line round the hull and down to the doors. He had not flown it in the open, in the dark, over water, with nothing under him and no rope behind him, and he would not, he said, until he had flown it once where it could not kill him.

"In the room," said Ferra.

"In the room," said Ward. "Alone."

The table was quiet. Ward was the last of the six. Ferra had gone in alone in the winter before last, and told Imani first; Imani in the spring; Bram, and Armand, who had been in more rooms than any of them; Saban in May, for the receiver, and come out with four hundred and thirty-one sales. Ward had been in with all of them, every month since the seating, and with Emeric more times than anybody could count, at night, in the first year, on the floor of a cold chamber in the keep with a hand on Emeric's shoulder. He had never once been in alone. Nobody had asked him why, and he had not said.

"Tomorrow," said Ward. "Night. Ferra's rules."



Ferra's rules were the oldest in Kaldo, older than the Standing Order, older than the school: never alone in the body, somebody awake in the house, not at night unless there was a reason, and past her count, she fetched Imani. Ward had a reason. So it was night, the twenty-first, the longest night of the year, in Emeric's study, with the shutters closed and the fire built up and Ward on the couch with his boots off and his band on his wrist and his eyes shut; and Ferra in the chair beside him with the sand glass and the book, keeping the count; and Imani in the next room with the door open; and Emeric on the floor by the fire with his back to the wall, which was where he always sat when somebody else was in.

Ward went in at nine. His note went quiet the way they all did going in, a slow even wall going down and down and away, like a bell's last sound, until Emeric could hardly feel it at all; and the room in the study was only a man asleep on a couch, and a fire, and a woman turning a glass.

He was in for two hours and a half.

When he came out he lay on the couch with his eyes open looking at the ceiling for some while, the way Saban had. Then he sat up and put his boots on.

"How long was it?" said Ferra. "Inside."

Ward thought about it. "A night," he said. "A long one." He stood. "I flew it. In there. It was in my room, on the shelf, because I'd seen it, every piece of it, every day for a month in the shed. I put it on." He picked up his coat. "And I flew it over a river that isn't there, in a dark that isn't there, for as long as I liked, and fell out of it on purpose four times, and the cape threw every time." He put the coat on. "Then I came out."

He went down the keep stair to the shed. Emeric went after him, because Ferra's rules said somebody went with anybody who had just come out, and because he wanted to. Ward did not say anything to him on the stair or on the hill or in the shed. He put the suit on, a plate at a time, in order, as Tess had taught them, and the helm, and walked out of the shed's river doors onto the frozen slip in the dark, and went up.

No rope. Two hearts, and the lever at his belt, and the stick in his left gauntlet. He went up off the slip a notch at a time, black against the black, and out over the river, and turned, slowly, on the twisted stick with its counter-burst, and went down the reach a little above the water toward the marsh with the ice at the river's edges catching the starlight under him; and Emeric lost him in the dark at the bend below the brickfield, and stood on the slip in the cold with his hands under his arms and his sense out after him, thin, as far as it would go, and felt him, a slow even wall moving over the water, a long way off and getting further. Then turning. Then coming back.

Ward came down onto the sand in the shed's doorway a quarter of an hour later and stood there, in the black plate, with the hearts humming down and his helm turned toward the river.

He took the helm off. He did not say anything at all. He walked past Emeric into the shed and began to take the suit off a plate at a time, in order, and hung each plate on its rack, and the helm last; and Emeric stood in the doorway and watched him do it, and did not say anything either, because there was nothing that needed saying, and Ward would not have thanked him for it.



He tried the door across the yard on the twenty-third.

Ward was on the wall walk on the north stretch, at the top of the keep yard wall, where he stood most nights at this hour anyway; and Emeric was at the foot of the keep stair, with the yard between them. Sixty paces. Further than the shed. Much further than the width of a hand in the yard in September with his fingers in the air an inch from Ward's shoulder.

He had his mother's stone in his coat. He let his sense out, thin, across the dark yard, past the mounting block and the cracked cobbles and up the face of the wall, and found Ward's note on the wall walk, slow and even, a wall on a wall. He leaned on it. Very gently. The way Paskin leaned on clay.

It gave. The width of a hair.

Ward, he said, through it.

On the wall walk, sixty paces off, a black shape against the lamps turned its head.

Yes, came back. Small, far, down the long wire of the hair. And then, after a breath, in exactly the flat voice Ward used for weather: Cold up here.

Emeric laughed aloud at the foot of the stair. He had not meant to.

Then he did the thing he had not done yet. He held Ward's door open, the hair's width, with Ward's note under it on the wall walk; and with the rest of his sense, a second thread, he went up the keep stair behind him, through the stone, two floors, to the counting room, where Ferra was at her desk with the lamp, as she was every night at this hour; and found her note, small and close and steady, the one he would have known in the dark in any town in the world. And leaned on that too.

It cost. He had known it would. A dual hold, Armand called anything that held two things at once, and a dual hold was hard, and three was High mage work, and he had felt it at once behind his eyes like a hand closing there. But he held them both. Two hairs' widths, open, at once, one up the wall and one up the stair. He felt the stillness go through Ferra's note, a breath before, the room going quiet.

Ferra, he said. Ward.

And both of them heard it. He felt it arrive in both. On the wall walk Ward's head turned again. In the counting room, two floors up, Ferra's pen stopped.

And then, down the hair from the counting room, small and far and quite steady, in the voice she used for suppliers:

I'm working.

And from the wall walk, at the same moment, over the top of it:

So am I.

He let both doors go. He had to; the hand behind his eyes had closed all the way. He sat down on the bottom step of the keep stair in the cold with his head in his hands, and stayed there until Ward came down off the wall and stood over him with a lamp.

"Both," said Ward.

"Both."

"It cost you."

"The rest of the evening." He took his hands from his face. "Two at once is a dual hold. I'll need a winter of it." He looked up the keep stair, at the light under the counting room door two floors up. "She said she was working."

"She was," said Ward. He held out his hand. Then, when Emeric was on his feet, he did not go back to the wall. He stood at the foot of the stair with the lamp and looked at Emeric, and Emeric saw, in the lamplight, that Ward had decided something, and was waiting to be asked.



So he asked.

He asked the question he had not asked in more than two years, since Ward came back from the palace in the capital with a box of faded blue velvet under his arm and two of his mother's stones in it, and stood by the table in the lodging with his hands behind his back and said that the king had given them gladly. He had not asked it then because he had been afraid of the answer, and he had not asked it since because every week that went by he had been a little more afraid of it.

"The stones," he said. "My mother's. In the capital." He looked at Ward. "You went to the palace alone. You asked the king for them. And he gave them to you. A knight from the south who'd been out of the capital for four years, with no appointment and no name at court, walked into the palace and asked the king for the late queen's stones and came out with them in a morning." He did not look away. "How did you reach him, Ward?"

Ward did not answer at once.

He stood at the foot of the keep stair with the lamp in his hand, in the cold, and looked at Emeric with his flat face, and for the first time in six years Emeric could not read anything in it at all; not because there was nothing there, but because there was too much, and it was all held down, the way Ward held everything down, very hard.

"Come in," said Ward. "To mine. I'll show you."



They went in on the floor of the guardroom, with the door barred, with Ward's back against the wall and Emeric's against the bench, and Ward's band on his wrist, and Ward's hand on Emeric's forearm. Contact. Consent. Ward had asked him in, which was the consent; Emeric had never once been in Ward's room, only Ward in his.

It was not like his own room. He had known it would not be. Ferra's was a counting house, ruled and shelved; Imani's was a ward, clean and plain, with beds; Bram's a forge. Ward's was a guardroom. Bare stone, a table, a bench, a rack of blades, and shelves; and the shelves were full. More full than any of the others'. Six years of a knight's life, everything Ward had seen and read and had to remember, which was everything, because a guard who forgets is a guard who loses his man.

Ward went to the shelf at the end. He took down a sheaf of papers, tied with tape, and untied it, and held it out.

They were letters. Copies, as Ward had seen them, in his own square careful hand, on cheap paper, each one dated at the top. And beside each, on a second sheet, the same words in a hand that was not a hand at all, rows of numbers and small marks, which Emeric had never seen and knew at once for what it was. A cipher. The king's.

"Every month," said Ward. "Since the road south. The first on the fifth night, after the poison, from the inn. To the king, by Kassim's line, which is a man in a bookseller's in Wayford and another in the capital who carries the king's reading to his desk. Nobody else has ever read them." He held the sheaf out a little further. "He asked me to. On the night before we left. In his office. To keep you alive, and to tell him how you did. I said I would."

Emeric took the sheaf.

He did not read it all. He could not have; there were seventy letters in it, more. He turned the pages. The poisoning, the burn on Ward's arm, the first summer. The wells. The war: the dead zone, the nine, Harl, Tess. The debt, and Vantic. The baronies. The bands. Each one plain and short and flat, in Ward's voice, the way Ward would have told it to a man who needed to know and had no time; and each one with its numbers beside it.

The forge was there. In a letter from the spring before last: that the prince had built a furnace below the keep that made fine steel and true parts, better than any smith's, and that Ward did not understand it and had not asked. Which was true, every word. And was, Emeric saw, perhaps a tenth of what Ward had seen in that cellar.

"You made it smaller," he said.

"I made it smaller." Ward did not look away. "I'm his man. I'm yours. When those pull different ways, I make a thing smaller and I don't lie. That's as far as I go." He paused. "So far it's been far enough."

Ward reached out and turned the sheaf back, through the letters, to one from the autumn of the third year, and laid his finger on the last two lines of it, and took the finger away.

The letter was about a morning. The dawn drill, in the keep yard, in the autumn of the third year: that the prince's guard had come up faster than his training, three times, and that Ward had put him on the ground the fourth time gently. That night, Emeric remembered, he had found Ward at the guard table writing two sheets, and covering them when he came in.

Under it, the last two lines.

You should be proud. I speculate your son is like you in more ways than you realize.

He read them twice. Then a third time.

Like you.

His father was a knight. Everybody in the kingdom knew that. And his father was something else. In more ways than you realize, Ward had written, to a man who would know exactly which ways those were, because they were his own. The guard that came up faster than training. The warmth on the downs. The four strides.

The king was a hybrid. And had been, all his life, under the lines, and had told nobody, not his court, not his queens, not his sons. Had made his third son a recluse at eight and a drunk at fifteen and sent him south at twenty-two, and had, all that time, perhaps, known. Or guessed. Or been afraid of exactly this line in exactly this hand.

"Did he answer?" said Emeric. His voice came out strange in the room.

"He never answers," said Ward. "Not once, in six years. Not a line." He was quiet. "But the stones came. When I asked. In a morning. Gladly, he said, twice."

Emeric stood in Ward's room with the sheaf in his hands, among the bare stone and the rack of blades and the shelves full of six years, and did not know what he felt. He went looking for it, the way he went looking for a wrong note in a stone, and found too many, all at once, all of them true. That the man who had stood beside him every dawn for six years and put him on the ground and pulled him up had been writing to his father the whole time. That he had done it because his father asked. That he had made the forge smaller. That his father had read every letter and never answered and sent the stones in a morning.

That he was not the only one.

"Do you write to him still?" he said.

"Every month. The next goes on the second."

"Will you tell him about this?"

Ward looked at him. "That's yours to say," he said. "Not mine. I'll write what you tell me to, about tonight, or I'll write nothing. Everything else I'll go on writing the way I have, and you can read every one before it goes, if you want. Or not." He held out his hand for the sheaf. "I'd rather you didn't. A man writes differently when he knows who's reading."

Emeric gave him the sheaf. Ward tied the tape round it, and put it back on the shelf at the end, in its place.

"Tell him," Emeric said.

Ward turned.

"Tell him I'm well," said Emeric. "Tell him the baronies are well. Tell him the bands work and the line runs to Milford and Ferra's book balances. Tell him whatever you'd have told him." He stopped. "And tell him I read his letter. The one from the third autumn. And that he was right."

Ward looked at him in the bare grey light of his own room for some while.

"I'll tell him," he said.

They came out on the guardroom floor, at the same moment, with the lamp burned low on the bench and Ward's hand still on his arm; and Ward took the hand away, and stood, and unbarred the door, and went up the stair to the north wall, because it was his watch. Emeric sat on the guardroom floor with his back against the bench and the cold coming up through the flags, and did not get up until the lamp went out; and when he did, he did not go to bed. He went up onto the wall walk, and walked the river stretch from the water stair to the north tower and back, alone, in the dark, until the sky went grey over the marsh, with his sense out thin over the sleeping town and one slow even note on the north wall at the edge of it, walking the same way.
