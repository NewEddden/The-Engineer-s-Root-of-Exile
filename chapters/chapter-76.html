Chapter 76 - Post Horses

Ward put him on the ground in the yard of the first posting house before it was light, which was no surprise to anyone, least of all the ostler.

The yard was frozen ruts and straw, with a lantern on a hook by the stable door and the post horses for the morning stage blowing steam in their stalls. Ward handed him a practice blade without a word and took the other, and they went at it in the cold with their breath going up between them, and Emeric went down on his back in the straw before the ostler had finished filling the first bucket. He got up. He went down again. The third time he stayed up long enough to see what Ward was going to do before Ward did it, and went down anyway, but later, and on his side instead of his back, and Ward stepped away and looked at him as a man looks at a horse that has started favoring the other leg.

"Better," Ward said.

It was the first time he had said it in eight months. Emeric lay in the straw and looked up at the lantern and decided not to ruin it by saying anything.

They did it every morning after that, at every posting house up the road, and the ostlers came out to watch, because a gentleman being put in the muck by his own man was worth a cold hand at any hour. Emeric lost every morning. He lost a little less each morning, the way a column of figures comes down a little each week when you are paying a debt, slowly enough that you would not notice it unless you were the one keeping the book. Ward was keeping the book. He did not say better again.



The north road out of Kaldo was not much of a road for the first day. It ran through the barony's own woods and then out across the plague country, where the farms had gone back to scrub and the villages stood with their roofs in, and the posting house at the end of the first stage was the only building for six miles with smoke coming out of it. On the second day the land filled up again, and there were carts, and other riders, and a man driving geese who would not get out of the way for anyone, and that was the north road proper, the one that went to Wayford and joined the south road there and became the crown's.

They rode side by side when the road was wide enough, and in file when it was not. Ward did not talk on a horse. Emeric had learned that on the first ride north, two summers ago, and had stopped expecting otherwise, and they went for most of the second morning with nothing between them but the sound of eight hooves on frozen mud.

"Why Keller?" Emeric said.

He had carried it out of Hale's office and up the tower stair and down to the gate, and over a day and a half of road, and it had kept, as he had told himself it would. Now it was the road and he took it out.

Ward rode on for a little.

"Because he read the paper," he said. "In the summer. With Wexler." He looked at the road ahead of them. "Most men read the name at the top of a page and decide. He read the page."

That was true. Emeric had sat in the room and watched Keller do it. It was also, he thought, the whole of an answer to a slightly different question, and Ward had given it in the voice he used for things that were true and not complete, the same voice in which he said the horses were fine.

He let it stand. He had let a good many things of Ward's stand over two years, and every one of them had been worth more standing than knocked down.



They came into Wayford on the third afternoon, under a low sky.

It was a junction town and looked it, a town built along two roads where they met, as if it had been poured into the angle between them and set. The south road came through the middle of it, broad and churned, with inns down both sides and the traffic of the whole lower kingdom grinding through: wool carts, salt wagons, a coach with a crest on the door that he did not look at closely, a string of led horses, a man selling hot chestnuts out of a pan who had positioned himself exactly where everyone had to slow down. The river was on the right, wide and grey. The posting house stood back from the road on the left with a yard big enough to turn a coach in, and they rode into it and gave their horses to a boy, and Ward went to see about the next pair.

Emeric stood in the yard with his gloves in his hand.

He did not look at the crossing house. He knew where it would be: on the town side of the ferry approach, Saban had said, stone, older than anything else in Wayford, with the crown's arms over the door. He looked instead at the posting house's long room through its windows, where a man in a good coat was eating alone at a table by the fire. It was Thursday. Saban had said Sethe dined here on a Thursday. Emeric did not know what Sethe looked like, and found he did not want to know, and looked away from the window too.

Then he looked at it.

It was exactly where Saban had put the blot. A square stone house at the head of the slope that went down to the ferry, older than the inns on either side of it and set a little back from them, as if it did not care to be seen in their company. The crown's arms were over the door, worn almost smooth. There was a cart at the front of it being unloaded by two men in the crown's grey, and the bags going in through the door were the same canvas bags with the same leather tags that went out from the post house on the north road at home, and he watched three of them go in. Round the back, a single chimney. The smoke coming out of it was thick and yellow and went sideways, and fell, and lay along the roof, the way smoke does from a stove that has never drawn properly in its life.

They complain about the stove, Saban had said. It smokes.

Ward came back with the fresh horses. He looked at Emeric, and at the crossing house, and at Emeric again.

"Do not," Ward said.

"I wasn't going to."

"Good." Ward handed him a bridle. "Then get on."



Past Wayford the road went into the forest.

It was old forest, crown land, oak and beech with holly under it, and the road ran through it for most of a day between banks the height of a man, cut down into the clay by four hundred years of wheels. On the ride north in August Emeric had watched the treeline for an afternoon and seen nothing. Going south, Ward had ridden on the forest side the whole day without saying why. Now it was December, and the trees were bare above and dark green below, and the light was the color of dishwater, and a fine sleet had started at noon and not stopped.

The tree was across the road at the bottom of a dip, where the banks were highest.

It was a beech, not a large one, felled from the left bank so that it lay across the whole width of the road with its crown in the ditch on the right. Fresh cut. The stump was pale, and there were chips in the mud. Behind it, in the road, three men were standing as if they had just happened to be there, and up on the left bank, half behind a holly, a fourth man had an arrow on a bow and the bow partly drawn.

Ward reined in. Emeric reined in beside him.

They were thin. That was the first thing. They were thin the way people had been thin in Kaldo's lower ward the first spring, when the plague had gone through and taken the men who worked and left the ones who could not, and their clothes had been made for broader men. One had a billhook. One had a cudgel, a length of something that had been a cart's tongue once. One had a sword, an actual sword, old and dark with rust along the edge, which he held as if it were heavy and he were not sure which end went first. The bow was a hunting bow, a decent one, the best weapon the four of them had, and the man holding it was the only one who looked as though he knew what to do with what he was holding.

"Horses," said the man with the billhook. "And the bags. And you can walk to the next stage. It's not far." He seemed to think this was a kindness, and perhaps it was.

Ward did not answer him. Ward turned his head and looked at Emeric.

"Yours," he said.

Emeric looked back at him.

"Without the trick," Ward said. He sat back in the saddle and put his hands together on the pommel, one over the other, nowhere near his sword, like a man settling in to watch a game he had money on. "Go on."

Emeric understood that Ward meant it: that Ward would sit there with his hands folded while an arrow came at him if that was what it took, and that this was the most Ward thing that had ever happened.

The fire was right there. It always was. A thumb of it in the bowman's face and the whole thing was over. The other thing was there too, under it, quieter, the thing Ward did not know about, which could have pulled the bow out of the man's hands from where Emeric sat. He left both of them where they were.

He got down off the horse on the right side, so that the horse was between him and the bank.

The bowman's eyes went with him, and then, because a man on a horse is a bigger target than a man behind one, went back to Ward. Ward sat with his hands folded. The bowman drew a little further and did not know what to do about a man who would not move.

Emeric went up the bank.

Not at the man. Along the bank and up, in the holly, where the bow could not swing without the bowman stepping out from his cover to do it. The bowman heard him and turned and stepped out, as he had to, and by then Emeric was close enough that the arrow was no use. A bow at that range is a stick with a string on it. Emeric took the stave in his left hand above the grip and pushed it up and away, and the arrow went off into the branches with a sound like a slap, and with his right hand he struck the inside of the man's arm a hand's width above the elbow, where the nerve lies close to the bone.

He knew where it lay. He had seen it drawn on a page, once, in another life, in a book of plates, a long pale cord running down the inside of the arm, labeled in a small neat hand. The hand had been right. The man's arm went loose from the shoulder down and the bow went with it, and Emeric took the bow, and put the flat of his foot against the man's hip and pushed, and the man sat down in the holly and stayed there, holding his arm and making a high thin noise.

That was one.

The three in the road had started climbing over the tree. The billhook came first, because the billhook was the one who had done the talking and had to go first now or never talk again. Emeric came down off the bank at him, and drew as he came, and the billhook swung, wide and flat and much too early. Emeric let it go past and stepped in behind it and took the man's wrist on the backswing, and turned it, out and down, the way the joint goes and then a little further than the joint goes, and the man let go of the billhook because his hand had stopped belonging to him. The hook's edge dragged across Emeric's forearm on its way to the mud.

He felt it as cold, not as pain. Later it would be pain.

He put the man down with his own weight, face first, onto the beech's trunk, and left him there across it like washing on a line.

That was two.

The third was the cudgel, and the cudgel was big. Not fat. There was no fat on any of them. But he had been a big man before the plague and his bones had not got the message, and the cart's tongue in his hands was as long as a man's arm and as thick as a wrist. He came round the end of the tree instead of over it, which was the first sensible thing anyone had done, and Emeric, who had been ready for him to come over it, was a half step wrong when he arrived.

Only a half step. It was enough. The tongue came round at the height of his head, and he got under it, but late, and felt the wind of it in his hair, and stumbled on the frozen rut and nearly went down, and the big man drew back for the second swing with all the time in the world.

He was slow, though. The second swing took him longer than the first, because he was hungry and the tongue was heavy and hungry men are slow. Emeric was inside it before it was halfway round. He did not try to stop it. He went past the big man's left hip and turned and kicked, low and hard, at the outside of the knee, just below it, where another of those long pale cords comes round the head of the bone close under the skin. The leg went. Not broken. Simply not there anymore, for a while. The big man folded down onto it with a look of enormous surprise and the tongue went out of his hands and the rest of him followed it into the ditch, into the beech's crown and a foot of sleet-water.

That was three.

The fourth man, the one with the rusted sword, was standing on the far side of the tree. He had not climbed it. He looked at the bowman in the holly, holding his arm, and at the billhook lying across the trunk, and at the big man sitting up in the ditch among the beech twigs with the water up to his waist. He looked at Emeric, who had a sword in one hand and a bow in the other and blood running down the inside of his wrist into his glove.

He put the rusted sword down on the trunk of the tree, carefully, as if it might go off, and stepped back from it.

Four.

The whole of it had taken about as long as it takes to say the alphabet, slowly. Emeric's heart was going as though it had taken an hour. He stood in the road with the sleet coming down on him and found he was very calm, and very cold, and that his hands wanted to shake and he was not going to let them in front of Ward.

Ward had not moved. His hands were still folded on the pommel.

"Four," Ward said.

"You counted."

"I count." Ward looked at the big man in the ditch. "You were slow on the third."

"He was big."

"He was hungry," Ward said. "Hungry men are slow. You were slow anyway." He looked at Emeric's arm. "And you let the hook touch you."

"It was on its way down."

"So was the man. It still touched you."

Emeric looked at his arm. The cut ran across the outside of the forearm for about the length of a finger, through the coat and the shirt, and was shallow, and bled the way shallow cuts on the forearm do, generously and without meaning much by it. Imani would have something to say. He found he could hear exactly what.

Ward got down off his horse at last. He walked to the tree and looked at the cut stump and the chips in the mud, and at the four men, and up the bank at the holly, as if he were pricing them.

"Not sent," he said. "Sent men would have cut a better tree."

The big man in the ditch began, unexpectedly, to laugh. It went on for some time and turned into coughing, and the billhook, still lying face down across the trunk, said something into the bark that might have been his name, or a prayer, or a complaint about the big man.

Emeric bent down and picked the billhook up out of the mud and looked at it. It was a good one. Somebody had kept the edge on it even when they had not kept anything else.

"Where are you from?" he said.

The bowman in the holly told him, through his teeth. A village a day east of the forest that had had forty houses before the plague and had nine now, and a lord who had died in it and an heir who had not come to see what was left, and no work in it from one end of the year to the other that would feed a man through the winter.

Emeric thought about Kaldo's lower ward the first spring. He thought about Hale's register, the census return, the difference between the tax roll and the people who were actually there, and every empty house on Ferra's list below the north well.

"There's a barony at the end of the north road," he said. "Kaldo. Back through Wayford and three days south. It's short of hands. It pays wages, every week, in coin, and it pays them on the day." He held the billhook out to its owner, handle first, and the man, after a while, took it. "Walk south. Ask at the keep for a man called Hale. Tell him you were sent. He'll find you something, and he'll write you down, and he'll pay you."

The billhook man looked at the handle of his own hook in his hand.

"Sent by who?"

"By a man who owed you for a tree," Emeric said. He laid the bow across the trunk beside the rusted sword, and went to get his horse.

They led the horses round the end of the beech, through the crown of it and the ditch, where the big man had to shift up to let them pass and did, with great politeness, still sitting in the water. Then they were on the road on the far side, and mounted, and riding, and the four men stood behind them in the sleet and watched them go, and Emeric did not look back, because Ward did not.

A mile on, Ward said, "You gave them the bow back."

"It was theirs."

"It was pointed at me."

"You didn't move."

"No," said Ward. After a while, in the voice he used for the horses being fine: "You went for the bow first."

"It was the only thing there that could reach you."

Ward did not answer that. They rode on through the forest in the sleet, and Emeric took his glove off and held the cut closed with his other hand, and let his hands shake now, a little, where nobody could see it but the horse.



The capital came up out of the plain on the seventh day the way it always had, the way it had come up behind him in the other direction two and a half years ago and he had not looked back at it: ring inside ring of wall, and roofs, and smoke, and the tower in the middle of everything with the gallery hanging off its crown in the grey air on nothing at all, and the sky-ships moving over the inner rings like flies over a table, each one a man somewhere inside it pouring himself out a breath at a time.

They went in at the south gate of the outer ring with the carts, and did not go any further in than that.

The inn Ward found was the kind of inn the outer ring was full of, built for drovers and carters and men who had come up the south road to sell something and wanted to be on it again in the morning. It had a yard and a long room and a woman at the hatch who did not look up when they gave their names, because they did not give the ones that would have made her look up. The room upstairs had two beds and a window onto the yard and a candle, and the walls were thin enough that Emeric could hear a man two rooms down explaining to someone the price of tallow.

He sat on the bed with the candle and a sheet of Kaldo paper and wrote a note to the judiciary.

He wrote it in his own hand. He did not write his title anywhere on it. He wrote that the barony of Kaldo had a matter it wished to lay before the magistrate Keller, touching the crown's post on the south road, and that the barony's representative could be found at this inn, in the outer ring, by the south gate, at any hour, until he was sent for. He signed it with his name and nothing in front of it, which was the first time in his life he had done that on a letter to anyone in this city, and looked at it for a while, and found that it was the most honest thing he had written in two years.

Ward took it down to the yard to find a boy to carry it.

Emeric unwound the strip of shirt he had tied round his forearm in the forest, and looked at the cut by the candle. It was closing. It was ugly, and red at the edges, and it had bled through twice on the road, and it was closing anyway, the way things on the body close when they are left alone and kept clean. He washed it in the basin, and tied a clean strip round it, and lay back on the hard bed with his boots still on and listened to the man two rooms down, who had moved on from tallow to the price of candles, and was very angry about it, and was not wrong.
