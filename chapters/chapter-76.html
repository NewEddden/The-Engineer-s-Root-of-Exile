Chapter 76 - The Room Above Kaldo

It took most of an hour to get six people through a door that one of them had used every night for eight years, and the failures were the useful part.

They did it in the long room in the early afternoon, a fortnight after the reading, with the shutters closed and the fire banked and every chair pulled in to the table. Ferra set a ceiling, because Ferra had set a ceiling on every session since the first one, and stood a marked candle at her end of the table, and Ward put Declan outside the door with orders to come in at dusk and shake the nearest shoulder and ask nobody anything. Declan had spent nine years not asking Ward anything and said that would be fine.

Emeric had his mother's stone in his left hand. The six had theirs, and he reached for them one at a time.

Bram was in before Emeric had finished reaching. He was simply there, the way he had been the first time, years ago, when he reached for Emeric's shoulder and found himself somewhere else, entirely unbothered, looking about him the way a man looks round a new workshop to find out where the light comes from.

Ferra held on the first time. She felt herself doing it and knew exactly what it was, because she had spent three attempts at her own table learning its shape. On the second she let go on purpose and was through, and stood there for a moment furious with herself at how much easier it was when you were not frightened.

Imani came in without any difficulty at all, looked about her for perhaps half a minute, and went back out again on her own. Nobody had told her she could do that. Emeric felt the place where she had been stay empty for a while, and then she came back, and stayed. She had been in this place once before, on the worst day of her working life, with a man bleeding on a table under her hands, and she had needed to arrive in it a second time on her own account. Nobody said anything about it.

Ward took four attempts. He did not say why and Emeric did not ask. On the fourth he came through all at once and stood very still for a moment, and then was entirely himself again.

Saban came through on the first try, which nobody had expected, Saban least of all. He looked about him with the expression of a man who has walked into the wrong house and found that he quite likes it.

Armand was last and took the longest and was visibly annoyed about both. Emeric could feel him at the far end of the reach, the way he had once felt Ferra, except that Armand was not holding on. He was measuring. He was watching his own mana go and noting the rate.

Ferra felt it too, or saw it on him, and went. He felt her go, a weight gone out of the room, and for the space of a few breaths there was nothing at the far end of the reach but Armand, counting.

Then Armand stopped counting, and came through, and stood with his hands behind his back and said nothing at all for ten minutes. Ferra came back a moment after him.

"What did you say to him?"

"That he couldn't keep the count and leave the room." She did not look at Armand. "He said he wasn't keeping one. I said he was, and that I had."

Somebody had once said the same thing to her, in her own kitchen, and she had not liked it either.

She had also, while she was up there, read the candle.

The afternoon inside was a long one. Ferra, who had timed every session since the first, wrote down both clocks.

What Emeric had built was a room.

It was long and high, with a table down the middle and chairs enough for everybody, and glass on three sides from the floor to the roof, and it stood on nothing, some way above Kaldo in the air where no building could have stood. Below it, filling the whole floor of the world out to where the glass met the sky, the barony lay in full relief.

The island first, because the eye went there. The wall round it with the nodes set into the stone. The keep, the church, the market squares, the river port. The Western Gate and the Left Bridge running out to the farmland. The two branches coming down white off the Mere and parting round the island and meeting again below it. The foundry across the water with its wheel turning. The near farms laid out in their divisions, the line of the main under them, the pump-house, and the Whispering Woods dark along the approach.

Five of them said something about it and all five said it differently.

Saban said "Oh," which, from Saban, was a speech. Imani said she had never seen the whole of the Mere at once. Ward said you could see every foot of the wall from up here, in the tone of a man admiring a woman across a room. Armand, who had apparently stopped measuring, said "Light's sake," the way old men swear. Ferra said it was beautiful, in precisely the voice she used for a column that balanced.

Bram said nothing. He walked the whole length of the glass, all the way round, looking down, and then he walked it all again.

Then Emeric told them it was wrong.

"I've never seen Kaldo from above. Nobody has." He put his hand flat on the glass. "That's a tithe map I read in my first month, and a survey I paid for in the second year, and a wall plan I drew myself, and nine years of walking. Everything I've walked is sharp. Everything I haven't is soft." He pointed down into the lower rows, where two whole lanes lay as a grey suggestion of roofs, like something described from memory by a man who had not been paying attention. "I've been down those twice."

He turned round.

"You've all lived here," he said. "Fill it in."

Four of them had lived in Kaldo, and they immediately disagreed about almost everything.

Nothing depended on it. That was the pleasure of it, and it was a real pleasure, and he had not known until that afternoon how much he had wanted to see it. Nobody was negotiating. Nobody was being paid. Four people stood at a pane of glass above their own town, each entirely certain they knew where the cooper's yard was.

Ferra and Bram were current, and between them they held most of the working town. Ferra's contribution was administrative, exhaustive, and very slightly inhuman. She filled in the north well's lanes house by house in the order they had been entered in the census, which was not the order they stood in, so that for a moment a whole lane was being built out of sequence, house nine and then house two and then house fourteen, like a column being filled from the bottom. Every roof she put in stood where the roll said it stood.

Bram corrected her twice.

The first was a house the roll counted as one dwelling that had been two since the third year, when the brothers who shared it stopped speaking and paid him to put a wall down the middle. "I did the lintel," he said, and the house split in two below them. The second was a building on the roll as a workshop that had been a man's bedroom for four years, because the man had declared it a workshop to keep it off a rate.

"The roll's what was declared," Bram said. "I carried iron through that door. There's a bed in it."

Ferra wrote it down. She had brought nothing to write on, and the room gave her a sheet anyway because she expected one to be there, and she wrote it down on that.

Imani's map was another map entirely.

She knew where the wells were, and which were sweet, and which had not been sweet since the plague. She knew where the ground stayed wet into May, and which lanes flooded when the Eastern Branch came up, and which houses she had been inside, and how many people had been sleeping in them when she was. She put in a whole layer that nobody else in the room could see, and it changed the shape of the lower rows. Two houses Ferra's roll held as empty had families in them. A lane Emeric had drawn dry ran with water for three weeks of every year.

She was also the only one of them who put in things that were not buildings. A step outside the bakehouse where old women sat in the afternoons, because it caught the sun and because it was where she could always find them. A drain that backed up at the thaw. A corner where the air went bad every August and had done since before she was born.

And Saban was wrong.

He was wrong about a great deal, confidently and in detail, and it was the most useful thing that happened all day.

He had left Kaldo in the war years and never come back to live, and since then he had come through it as a man with carts, which was not the same thing. He knew the gate and the bridge and the market squares and the river port as they had been that morning, because a carter looks at nothing else, and he put them in current and correct. Everywhere else, his Kaldo was the Kaldo of a young man who had walked it before the plague.

He put a warehouse on the lower quay with its doors open and its hoist out over the water.

"I pulled that down," Bram said. "Second year. Its timbers are in the foundry roof."

He put a chandler's on a corner off the market, with the name painted over the door.

"That's a house," said Imani. "Four children sleep in the front room."

He put in the cooperage on the long street with hoops stacked outside it and a boy rolling a barrel out through the gate, and Ferra said it had been the etching floor for five years, and Bram said Vesk had made him take the whole floor up and relay it on account of the draught, and Saban said he had bought barrels at that gate for ten years, and all three of them were right.

And the room showed it anyway.

That was the thing, and Emeric made himself look at it properly. The room had no opinion about whether a building was still standing. It contained what had been put into it. Saban's dead warehouse stood on the lower quay in full relief, solid, its doors open and its hoist out over water it had not overhung for seven years, because a man who was entirely certain had put it there. It was as real as the keep. From above there was nothing whatever to tell you which of the two was true.

"This room," Ferra said, "is exactly as true as the last person who corrected it."

She said it once, flatly, the way she would have said a column did not include the carry, and went back to the north well.

Nobody took the warehouse down for most of an hour.

Somewhere in the middle of it Saban put in one house three streets from the north well with more care than he had given anything else, down to the colour of the door, and did not say whose it had been. Nobody corrected it. Emeric, who had known for seven years which street Saban had grown up in, did not ask.

He was tiring inside twenty minutes and hiding it badly.

He could feel all six of them. Not their thoughts. Their weight. Every one of them doing something, putting a roof in or taking one out or walking the wall, and every roof and every step coming out of the same place, which was him. The space itself had always been cheap. He had sat in it alone for eight years at the price of a poor night's sleep. What cost was what you built in it and how many of you were building, and he had never once held it with six people in it putting up a town, and the drain was not slow and it was not theoretical.

The clock had gone strange as well, and Ferra had seen it first. Alone, or with one other, an hour in here had always been a minute or so outside, badly, and never the same way twice. She had read the candle when she went up for Armand, and she had caught Emeric's eye across the glass as soon as she was back, and he had known from her face what she had found. With six of them in it the two clocks were running far closer together than they had any business to. Neither of them had a reason. She had written it down and gone back to the north well.

Armand had spent his first ten minutes not looking at the town at all. He had been looking at the glass, and the table, and the place where the floor of the room met the air it stood on, and the joins in all of it, the way a man looks at a bridge before he walks out onto it.

He came down the room to where Emeric stood.

"Who holds this up?"

"I do."

"Alone."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Eight years. Not like this. Not with six."

Armand looked at him the way he had once looked at a buried pipe collar in a wet field. "Then why," he said, "is nobody helping you carry it?"

Emeric did not have an answer.

"Orrin and Vesk held a furnace for most of a day," Armand said. "Six hours each, turn and turn about, pouring into the fire stones while the glass went. Two men feeding a working neither of them had cut. If a man can pour into a stone that somebody else is using, I can see no reason in principle why he cannot pour into this." He paused. "I have no idea whether it will work. I would like that understood before I try it."

Then he tried it.

It worked. Emeric felt it the way you feel a second man take the far end of a beam, which was what Bram had said about the root four winters ago and was the only description of it that was any use. The drain did not stop. It halved, near enough, and steadied, and the grey that had been creeping in at the edges of his attention drew back.

Armand could not hold it indefinitely, and said so after about the time it takes to boil a kettle. But by the end of the afternoon the two of them had worked out that the load could be passed between them, one pouring while the other rested, the way the furnace had been, which meant the room could stay open longer than either of them could have held it alone.

Nobody had known that was possible. Emeric had not known. He had held this space by himself for eight years and had never once had to ask whether anybody could help him hold it, because there had been nobody to ask. The answer had been available the entire time. He noticed that, standing at the glass with the weight halved, and it sat badly, and it went on sitting badly.

It made Armand load-bearing within an hour of walking in, a fortnight after he had been handed a title he took for decoration. Nobody said that either.

The limit was plain by evening, and Ferra wrote it down so that nobody could remember it differently later. The room was not free. It cost by the head and by the work, and six people building a town in it was heavy. It needed at least one person holding it and was better with two. Nobody but Emeric could open it, and Emeric could not hold it long by himself, not like this, not with the town in it. It was a place to go together, for an afternoon, with a man on the door. It was not a place to live.

Ward saw his before anybody could show it to him.

He had gone to the glass at the north end and stayed there looking down at the wall, and after a while he went down into it, which Emeric had not known a guest could do on his own. He walked the Western Gate. He walked stretch nine behind the church, over the deep channel, where the stair went down through the wall. He stood men on the wall in his head and the room put them there, forty of them, grey and patient, and he took them off again and put them somewhere else.

He could run an assault against that wall as often as he liked, from any side, at any hour, with any number of men, and never tell a living soul he was doing it.

He came back up and stood at the glass for a long time without speaking.

Bram's came from Emeric, because Bram would never have asked for anything.

Emeric took him to the far end of the table, away from the argument, and showed him a bearing.

Not a drawing. The thing itself, in the air between them, the size of a cart hub and opened up like a cut fruit. An outer ring and an inner ring, and between them a row of hardened balls running in a groove, each turning on its own as the hub turned, so that nothing slid against anything else at all. He set it going. It ran. It ran for as long as they watched it and made no sound.

Bram watched it the way he had watched the furnace eight years ago, which was not with wonder.

He was doing arithmetic. Emeric could see him do it. Thirty-odd years of putting an axle through a hole and greasing it and accepting what came back. Every cart in the barony. Every wheel at the foundry. The water wheel itself. Every one of them paying a tax he had never been able to see, let alone name, grinding a little of itself away on every turn for thirty years, and here was the size of it, turning in front of him in silence.

The arithmetic finished and something came after it that was not far off grief.

"How long have you known?" Bram said.

Emeric did not answer. There was no answer he could give that would survive the next question, and Bram had been very nearly the first man on this island to take his word for anything, and was owed better than a wrong one. So the question stayed where it had landed, between them, next to the bearing, which went on turning.

Imani found hers by herself, in the lower rows.

She had been putting in the houses where she had sat with the dying, because those were the houses she knew best. Somewhere in the middle of it she stopped, and looked at her hands, and then at the table, and put a man on it.

Emeric saw her do it from the other end of the room and did not go over.

She opened him. She looked for a long time. She closed him, and opened him again, and closed him, and he was whole each time she began, and it cost her nothing but the effort of the afternoon. She had learned surgery on dying men in bad light with a queue outside the door, at whatever rate the queue supplied them, and she had never once in her life been able to do anything twice. In here she could do it forty times before she touched a living person.

The bodies she practised on were built out of bodies she had seen. She was going to spend the next several years rehearsing on the dead she had not been able to save.

Ward's second came late in the afternoon, and it came from Ward, almost reluctantly.

"Can a man train in here?"

It was a working soldier's question and he had thought it through before he asked it, which Emeric could hear. A great deal of what Ward did with a blade was not strength. It was a path his hands already knew, and a path is learned by going down it over and over, and most of the going is not in the arm. It would not build him. Nothing in here would put an ounce on him. But it might let a man of his years drill a movement three hundred times in an afternoon without tearing a shoulder he could not afford to tear.

"It'll agree with you," Emeric said. "That's the thing to know first. I've drilled in here for years."

Armand turned round at that. "So that is where you practise," he said, mostly to himself, and turned back again.

"If the path in your head is wrong," Emeric said, "it'll let you run the wrong one three hundred times, and it'll feel like getting better."

"Then I'll run the one I know is right," said Ward, and went down the far end of the room and did.

It half worked. The half that worked was the movement, which came out of it cleaner, and a week later in the yard everybody could see it. The half that failed was the interesting half. Ward came out of two hours of it more tired than Emeric had seen him in fifteen years, in a way that had nothing whatever to do with his body, and he did not sleep that night. He said so the next morning in four words and went and stood in the cold yard until it passed.

Armand's, Emeric showed him himself, and it was the thing he had been building longest and had shown nobody at all, because there had been nobody who would have understood what they were looking at.

It was a slate. That was the nearest word. A face of stone standing in the air, and on it a working written out the way a man writes one before he cuts it, and beside the working a way to run it without cutting it. You set a charge at the top and watched it go down through the lines. Where a branch held, the charge went the way the branch sent it. Where a shape repeated, it repeated, and you could count the turns. Where the working was wrong it went wrong in front of you, and you could see exactly where, and wipe the line, and write it again.

At present every test of a working cost a blank, and a day, and every so often on Vesk's floor a piece of somebody's hand. In here it cost the afternoon.

Armand's reaction was the most extreme of anybody's in the room, and it was almost entirely silent.

He stood in front of the slate for a long time. He wrote one working on it from memory, one Emeric recognised from the notebook the old man had pressed into his hands at a farewell banquet nine years before, and ran it, and watched where it went. Then he wrote it again with one line changed and ran that. Then he sat down, which he did not often do, and looked at it.

"It will tell me whether I have written what I meant," he said at last.

"Yes."

"It will not tell me whether what I meant is true."

"No," Emeric said. "That's the bench."

Armand nodded slowly, the way a man nods at a colleague who has got the one important thing right, and said nothing else for the better part of an hour.

Saban found his by walking off the edge.

He had given up on the lower rows once Imani started on them, and had gone to the glass at the south end, where the lower water ran out of the relief toward the coast. He looked at it for a while. Then he put in the road. The river road south as far as the bar, and then the coast road west, and a ford, and an inn at the ford with a bad roof, and a market at the inn's back door, and the stall at the end of the market that sold rope. The room took all of it. It ran out past the edge of anything Emeric had built and kept going for as long as Saban kept going, which was a long way.

Nobody else in the room had walked any of it. Saban had walked all of it, for thirty years, and the room held it as solidly as it held the keep.

He was the only one of them whose material could not be checked by anybody. Every roof Ferra put in, Bram could argue with. Every well Imani put in, Ferra had a line for. Nobody at that table had ever seen the inn at the ford, or was ever likely to, and whatever Saban said about it was what it was.

He looked up from the road at the same moment Emeric looked at him, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said a word.

Ferra's was last, and it was the one that turned the afternoon.

Emeric asked for it at the glass above the north well, with the town very nearly finished below them, and he asked for it plainly.

"Every person on the rolls," he said. "Kaldo first, then the other three. Family, and where the family is. Debts, and to whom. What they owe and what's owed them. Who isn't speaking to whom. Who has a sister in another barony, or a note with a lender, or drinks, or is frightened of something, and of what."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Ward has the soft places for everybody in service, and their families," Emeric said. "That list is good and it isn't enough. It was built after the stair, which is to say after somebody else had already built theirs. The men who took Finn knew the name of a boy and the name of his aunt a day west before this barony had written either of them down anywhere. They found the handle on Rylan before we knew he had one." He did not look at anybody. "I'm about to put men into posts across four baronies and a relay faster than Ward can learn their sisters' names. I want the handle known before the man is in the post. I'm not going to have another man made into a door."

Imani said it.

"Every man who ever built a file on a town," she said, "said it was to protect the town." She did not raise her voice. "I believe you mean it. Believing you isn't a safeguard." She looked down at the lower rows, at the houses she had just filled with sleeping children. "And the men on Ward's list took your pay and a post. They knew what they were signing. The woman with four children in what used to be a chandler's didn't sign anything."

Emeric did not argue with a word of it.

"You're right," he said. "All of it. I'm asking anyway." He made himself go on to the end. "The other way, the next handle gets found by somebody else first, and we learn what it was from the man he opens a door for. I would rather be the invasive one than the surprised one."

He heard how that sounded. He did not soften it.

Ferra's stay was a fortnight old. Everybody at the glass knew it existed, and two or three of them looked at her.

She did not use it. She conditioned it instead, in writing, on the spot, on the sheet the room had given her, in three clauses.

One. Who may read it. Herself and him, together, and nobody else, ever.

Two. If he dies it is burned, pages and key, on the day the word comes, by whoever at that table hears it first.

Three. The key is hers alone. The file is to be written in her cipher, which he will not be given, which means he cannot open it without her. And she reads it in her head. Nothing decoded ever goes onto paper, because anything she has read on paper sits on a shelf in this room whenever she is in it, and she was standing in it now, with five other people.

"The pages go where the soft places already are," she said, and looked at Ward, and Ward nodded once. "I told you four winters ago I wouldn't be a complete set. I'm not one. Ward will hold paper he can't read. I'll hold a key to nothing. You'll hold neither."

He agreed to all three at once.

It surprised her. He saw it land, and saw her decide not to wait for it to wear off, and she wrote the three clauses out in full before he could reconsider any of them and made him read them back to her. That night she wrote them again in ink, in the real room, and brought them to him to sign, and he signed without reading them, which she noticed and disapproved of.

She knew what she had just made herself. The most invasive document in the kingdom was going to exist, and the only thing standing between it and the man who wanted it was her judgment on whatever afternoon he next asked. She had looked for something better while she was writing, and there was nothing better to find.

Then, because he could not ask them to picture what it was for, he showed them.

He did not have the file. Nobody had the file. The only person on earth he had enough written about to run was himself, from the sheet he had given Ferra for the Order three weeks before, the one with a mark beside Thursday.

He put it in, and a small grey figure walked out of the keep below them.

It went down the steps into the yard at the hour he went down the steps. It crossed the market, and passed the church, and stopped at the Western Gate to say good morning to the morning man, the way Emeric always did, and went on.

"He's not there," Ward said. "He's been up the river road since the reading, learning the first post. You signed it."

He had. He had signed it at that table.

The figure went over the water to the foundry and in at the yard, and a little before noon it turned toward the room off the foundry office, where the bench was.

"You have not come to me before noon since you gave me Kaldo," Armand said. "You come at the end of the day. I asked you to."

Twice inside a minute, and both of them decisions of the last fortnight, and the figure went on walking a Tuesday that did not exist any more, at the hours the sheet gave it, past a man who was not at the gate, to a meeting at the wrong time. Everybody in that room had spent the afternoon watching Saban's warehouse stand on the quay. Nobody needed it explained.

"It won't go live," Emeric said. "I want to say that now, so nobody has to find it out. It can't. It's a picture of what somebody wrote down, and it's out of date the moment the ink is dry, and the older it gets the more confidently it's wrong." He watched the figure turn at the foundry door. "I'd like it to be more than that. I don't believe it ever will be."

He said it the way a man gives the load a beam will take, and he did not come back to it. The room was a memory. It had never been an eye, and nothing he could build into it was going to make it one.

Declan came in at dusk and shook the nearest shoulder, which was Saban's, and asked nobody anything.

They came out one at a time into the long room, with the shutters closed and the fire gone down to red, seven people round a table with nothing on it but their hands, and every one of them was exhausted. Not one of them could have explained to anybody outside that door why. They had sat in chairs for an afternoon. Ferra had the figures for how long it had been on the inside and would not discuss them yet. Saban said, to the table, that he had been tireder after crossing a mountain and could not remember being tireder after anything else. Bram got up and went down to the forge, which he did not work any more, and worked it until long after the work ran out.

Emeric sat on after the rest had gone.

It was not the map that stayed with him, or the file, or the bearing turning in the air with Bram's question beside it.

It was Armand. An old man had walked into a space Emeric had held alone for eight years, and looked at the walls of it for ten minutes, and asked why nobody was helping him carry it.

The answer had been there the entire time. There had been nobody to ask. And there had been nobody to ask because, eight years ago, alone in a room with a stone in his hand, he had decided that there was nobody to ask, and he had never once gone back to check the figure.
