Chapter 85 - Lead Rooms

Saban brought the toll in on the last Tuesday in April, with the receipt in his hand and the face of a man delivering a bill he had not run up.

"Wayford," he said, and put it down on Ferra's trestle in the back room of the exchange, between Ferra's book and Vantic's case, because those were the two things on the trestle that would know what to do with it.

It was a scrap of grey paper, cut square, with a stamp on it and a figure and a date and a clerk's initials, and it had been folded by a carter into his hatband and come south in it for five days, and smelled of the hatband. Ferra did not pick it up. She read it where it lay.

"A copper a cart," she said.

"Each way." Saban sat down on the bench by the door without being asked, which meant he meant to stay. "They've put a gate on our road. Not on the south road. On ours, where it comes into the south road, a mile short of Wayford's bridge. A little table under an awning, a clerk at the table, a bar across the road, and a man to lift the bar when you've paid." He spread his hands. "It's on Wayford's land. It's their road from that stone onward. They've every right."

"And the south road?"

"Free as it ever was. Everybody else's carts go up the south road and through Wayford and on north and never see the gate." Saban looked at the receipt. "Only ours come down that mile. So only ours pay."

"It's fair," said Emeric.

"It's perfectly fair," said Saban. "That's what I don't like about it. A copper a cart, every cart, charged to the letter, a receipt for every one. If he'd made it a silver I could have gone to the crown's receiver at Wayford and complained of it. A copper, nobody complains of. It's a copper." He looked at Ferra. "It isn't the copper."

"It's being stopped," Ferra said, writing.

"It's being stopped. Every cart, every time, at his table, with his clerk writing down what's in it."

The carter had told Saban one more thing, and Saban told it because he told everything, eventually, for nothing, once he had made you wait. The rate was painted up on a board beside the clerk's table. A big board, white, with the figure in black, a hand high, so that a carter could read it from the bend and have his copper ready before he got there and nobody could say afterward he had been charged more than the board said.

Emeric heard it and felt his face do something.

The soap yard had painted its ash price on a board at its gate in the first winter, big, in black, so that every woman who carried a bucket of ash in could read what she would be paid before she put it down, and nobody could be cheated, and everybody could see that nobody was. He had stood in the lower market and shouted it. Saban had stood beside him. Somebody from Wayford had been in the market that day, or in one since, and had read the board, and gone home, and thought about it.

Vantic held out her gloved hand across the trestle.

"The receipt," she said.

Saban gave it to her. She read it. Then she opened her book at a clean page, near the back, a long way past the weekly pages, and ruled a column down it, and wrote at the top in her small grey hand a heading he could not read upside down because it was too small, and under the heading the date, and the toll, a copper a cart, each way, Wayford, and folded the receipt once and laid it in the book against the page.

Emeric supposed the House did that with everything. A lender kept a note of whatever touched what it was owed. It seemed reasonable. It seemed, in fact, so reasonable that he did not think about it again for some time.

"Did your carter say anything else about the board?" Vantic said.

Saban told her. The size of it, the white of it, the clerk who sat under it and pointed at it when anybody argued, without saying a word. Vantic listened to all of it, and then asked him to say again the part about the clerk pointing, and he did.

She closed her book.

"They've paid you a compliment," she said to Emeric.

"By copying my board?"

"By charging you in your own hand."

And the corner of her mouth moved. Not much. Not anything that could have been called a smile by anybody who had not been watching her face across a trestle every week since September waiting to see whether it ever did anything, which he had been, without having noticed that he was. It moved, and it went back, and she picked up her pen.

He did not say anything about it. She did not either.



Ambrose Lark came in the first week of May, with two more of Armand's students on the same cart, and did not go up to Armand's room.

The other two did. They asked the gate crew for Master Armand and were taken up to the hall with their bags. Ambrose asked the gate crew where the machine was. He asked it as if there could be only one machine, and the gate crew, who had been asked a great many things by Armand's students since April and had decided to stop being surprised by any of them, pointed him down the hill to the water gate, and he went down it with his bag still on his back and his coat flapping, and nobody saw him again until supper.

Emeric found him at the powerhouse in the afternoon.

He was sitting on Harl's stool by the dynamo. He was a tall man, mid-forties, long in the arm and leg, with hair that went in several directions and eyebrows that were not quite there, as if they had been singed off once and had never entirely forgiven him. There was a burn healing on the back of his left hand, and an older one, healed, on his right. He had his bag on the floor between his feet and his hands on his knees, and he was looking at the dynamo going round.

He did not say anything. Tess, in her chair by the board with the bench book on her knees, said afterward that he had not said anything for an hour, and that she had begun to think he could not.

Emeric stood in the door. The belt slapped. The field hummed in the housing. The needle on the board stood where Tess's column said it ought to stand for the river this high, steady, and did not move.

"It keeps going," Ambrose said at last. He did not look round. He said it to the dynamo. "It just keeps going."

"While the river does."

"I've been trying to make it keep going for thirty years." Ambrose turned round on the stool then, and looked at Emeric, and his face was so naked with something that Emeric did not have a name for it. "Do you know what I am? I'm lightning. That's all I've got. I can put a bolt into a tree from forty paces and split it to the root. And then it's gone. It's always gone. A crack and a smell and it's gone. Every mage of my kind that ever lived has stood in a storm and thought, if I could only make it stay, if I could only make it run instead of burst." He pointed at the dynamo with his burnt hand. "And you've done it with a river."

"Bram did most of it."

"Who's Bram?"

Bram was in the door behind Emeric, as it happened, come down at the end of his day as he did every day now, to sit an hour on Harl's stool before he went home. He looked at the stranger sitting on it.

Tess said, from her chair, "That's Harl's stool."

Ambrose looked down at the stool. Then he looked at Bram's face, and whatever he saw in it, he got up at once, and picked up his bag, and stood holding it against his chest like a schoolboy.

"I beg your pardon," he said.

Bram looked at him a while. Then he went past him and sat down on the stool, and put his hands on his knees, exactly as Ambrose had, and looked at the dynamo.

"Who was Harl?" said Ambrose, quietly, to Tess.

Tess told him. She told him in about four sentences, the way she told everything that mattered, and he listened to all four with his bag against his chest. Then he put the bag down, and pulled the other stool over from the bench, the one with the short leg that nobody used, and sat on that instead.

After that he talked.

He talked for the rest of the afternoon and most of the evening, and it was nearly all about lightning. That the snap off a cat's back in winter and the bolt in a summer storm were the same thing, and he would stake his ring on it, and the tower had laughed at him for twenty years for saying so. That a bolt went to the tallest wet thing it could find, every time, a tree or a tower or a man on a hill, as if it were looking for a way down. That he flew kites in thunderstorms, on a wet string, to see where it went, and had the hands to show for it. He did not seem to need anyone to answer. Emeric, who could have told him in a sentence that he was right about the cat, did not, because it was plain that Ambrose had not come here to be told.

Tess did not tell him either. She sat in her chair by the board and let him talk, and wrote down the parts she thought were true, and when he picked up the copper cut-out off her bench to look at it she took it out of his hand and put it back, without a word, the way you take a shoe out of the mouth of a large dog that has got into the house.

At about the sixth hour he stopped talking and looked at her.

"Why does it stop when the river drops?" he said. "The needle. You've got it in your column. It comes down with the river. Why doesn't it just go slower?"

Tess looked at him.

Nobody had ever asked her a question about the dynamo as if she were the one who would know. They asked Bram. They asked Emeric. They asked Tess the river's height, or the lamp hours, because she kept the columns. Emeric watched her understand that this tall singed stranger with a burn on his hand had looked round the powerhouse and decided, in an afternoon, that the person who knew was the girl in the chair.

She put her pen down.

"It does go slower," she said. "That's the trouble. Come here, I'll show you."



The lead came out of Kaldo's own hill.

It had been there all along. On the day he had first gone up the hill to the old workings, two years ago, the old miner had shown him the grey cubes in the spoil, bright as new pewter and heavier than they looked, and called them lead, and said his grandfather had sold some to the church for its roof. Emeric had filled his pockets with the brown stuff that came out beside them and made zinc of it, and left the lead where it lay, because there had been nothing yet to want it for.

There was now. Oil of vitriol had been made in the dyehouse since the summer of the first cells, a jar at a time, from green crystals cooked in a clay retort, slow and stinking and never enough. The cells wanted it. The copper wanted it. Tess's jars wanted it. And there was a better way, an old way from his other world, older than anything else he had built here, and the better way wanted lead, a great deal of it, because lead was the one common metal that the acid would not eat.

So the lead came down from the hill by the sack, and Bram melted it down in an iron pot over the forge fire, which was the easiest thing he had ever melted and which he did not trust for that reason, and poured it in sheets on a bed of sand, flat and grey and soft enough to dent with a thumbnail. And inside the dyehouse, inside its long stone walls, between the old dye vats, they built rooms of it.

A timber frame, and the sheets hung on the frame, and the seams between the sheets joined. They could not be nailed. Nails would rust through in a week and the acid would find the hole. They could not be soldered, because solder was mostly lead and tin, and the tin would go. They had to be burned: the edges of two sheets laid together and melted into each other along the whole length of the seam with a thin, hot, steady flame, so that the two sheets became one sheet. It was slow, careful, exact work, and it wanted a flame that would stay where it was put for hours.

Orrin did it. He walked the seams of the first chamber with one hand held flat above the lead and a look of extreme concentration, talking the whole time about what lead burning would cost by the yard if he were doing it on the coast, which was a great deal more than Kaldo was paying him, and the seams came out behind him bright and smooth and whole, and he did not stop talking once.

Three rooms, by the middle of May, each as high as a man and as long as two, standing in a row down the middle of the dyehouse floor like grey tents. Into the first went smoke: the hill's spoil roasted in a little kiln at the end of the house, the brimstone in it burning off sharp and choking, with a pinch of saltpetre in the fire that Saban bought on the coast by the jar from the men who cured hams, and a jet of steam from a kettle. The smoke and the steam met in the lead room and did there, slowly, in the dark, what they would do nowhere else, and ran down the lead walls to the floor of the room as a thin oily liquor that ate the paper he dipped in it.

Chamber acid. He said the name to Bram, and Bram said it back, as if it were a disease he had heard of.

Vantic came down to see it, because the dyehouse was a running work and the lead rooms were inside it, and she stood in the doorway with her case under her arm and looked at the three grey tents for some time without coming in, because of the smell. Then she went back up the hill and wrote, and the half-sheet came down that evening, under the House's form and the date.

More acid from a works that already made acid is the works' output. Under that, a line, smaller.

The House will not rule it so twice.

Ferra read it, and read the second line again, and put it in her book in its place with what he could have sworn was satisfaction.

Tess kept the columns. There was no glass in Kaldo to make a float of, and so she weighed it, as he showed her: the same glazed measure filled to the same line, first with water and then with acid, on Harl's beam in the tally room, against Harl's brass weights. The heavier the measure, the stronger the acid. He called it the specific gravity, and she wrote it at the top of a page and then called it the weight from that day on. Every batch went into the column. The first week's acid weighed in the measure at very nearly half again what the water did, and she drew a line under it, and showed it to Ambrose, who had followed her chair down the path to the tally room without being asked, and he held the measure up to the window and looked at it through the window light in silence, which for Ambrose was the highest compliment there was.



The pipe was the potter's.

He had fired the insulators for the line, two summers ago, and complained about them, and then fired the roof tiles for the powerhouse and the dock quarter and complained about those, and now he was firing pipe and complaining about that. Straight lengths of clay as long as a man's arm, with a plain end and a wide end, a spigot and a socket, so that each length's plain end went into the next one's wide end and the joint was packed with tarred rope and pitch. They came out of his kiln red and ringing, and he lined them up along his yard wall in rows and tapped every one with a stick, listening, and threw out the ones that did not ring.

"Two in nine," he said. "I lose two in nine. If you want nine in nine you can go and find a potter who's a liar."

Bram stood in the potter's yard with his arms folded, looking at the rows of pipe.

"There's lead now," he said.

Emeric waited.

"Sheet lead. Rolled round a bar and the seam burned, the way the mage does the rooms." Bram nodded at the clay. "A man could run a hundred yards of lead pipe in the time it takes him to fire twenty of those. And it bends. You don't need a joint every arm's length."

He was right. He was right about every word of it, and on Emeric's other world men had been right about every word of it for two thousand years, and run lead pipe into every house in every city that could afford it, and drunk out of it, and gone slowly, quietly, wrong in the head, and their children with them, and never known why.

"No," he said. "Not for water. Never for water that anybody drinks."

Bram looked at him.

"Lead in water poisons," Emeric said. "Slowly. Not so that you'd see it in a week or a year. A little goes into the water off the inside of the pipe, every day, and a little goes into everyone who drinks it, and it stays in them. It builds. It goes for the head and the gut. Children worst, because they're small and it doesn't take as much."

"How slowly?" said Imani.

She had come down to the potter's yard to see the pipe for the ward's line, because she had walked the elm line with the mason in the first autumn, before a single length of it went into the ground, and she meant to walk this one too. She was standing by the yard gate with her arms folded.

"Years," Emeric said.

"And children first."

"Yes."

Imani looked at the lead in Bram's cart, and at the clay along the wall, and at Bram.

"Then no," she said, as if that settled it, and it did.

Tess, in her chair by the kiln, where she had come to watch the firing, wrote it down. He saw her do it. Lead pipe, never for water. And under it, after some thought, a question mark, and then she crossed the question mark out.

The clay went down the line of the old elm pipe through May, length by length, the potter complaining and the carpenter laying and the masons' men filling in behind, and where the old line ended at the second cistern at the top of the lower ward, the new one went on. Ferra had laid that before Vantic already decided, in the House's form, and Vantic had initialed it without reading it twice, because Ferra had made it impossible to read any other way. The elm line had stood on the day of signing. A clay line in the same ground, carrying the same water to the same place, was the elm line mended. Every yard beyond the old cistern was new.

So the line stopped at the cistern for now, and the extension waited on its own page in Ferra's book for the fifth, with a figure beside it.

"The House says a new pipe is a repair," Ferra said to him, at the trestle, the day it was initialed.

"I said that."

"You were about to say it. In August. I could see it coming." She ruled under the figure. "It's better coming from the House."



The April pair had gone out in the third week of the month, the quarter's account, written out twice in Hale's hand and checked in Ferra's, one copy weighed and slipped at the post house gate on the north road for two coppers, one copy west in Dunnel's bale. Eight days after, to the day, the coast house's line had come back by the carrier. It was in the left-hand box now, on top of the February slips. The right-hand box was empty. Ferra dusted it on Fridays.

He came back down to the exchange late in the month, after dark, for a sheet he had left there, and found the back room's lamp still lit.

Ferra was at one end of the trestle. Vantic was at the other. Between them lay Ferra's book and Vantic's book and a long sheet of the potter's drawings of the new clay line, and they were arguing.

He stopped in the passage. He did not go in.

The elm line, it emerged, had not run straight. Where it came down past the old tannery it had gone round a house that had stood there in the brewer's day, in a long bend, thirty yards out of its way. The house had come down in the plague. The new clay line did not go round it. It went straight through where the house had been, and saved twenty-two yards of pipe, and Ferra had entered the whole of it as repair.

"It doesn't follow the old line," Vantic said.

"It doesn't need to. It's the same water from the same cistern to the same cistern."

"Then the twenty-two yards it doesn't use are a saving to the barony, and the eight it uses where the old line never went are new."

"Eight yards in a line of four hundred that's shorter than the one it replaces."

"Eight new yards," said Vantic, "in a line that's shorter."

Ferra leaned back. Vantic did not move at all. The lamp stood between them. Neither of them was angry. Neither of them, he thought, watching from the dark of the passage, had enjoyed anything so much in weeks. Ferra had the face she wore when she had found a column that was out and knew already where; Vantic had the stillness that in anyone else would have been a person leaning forward.

"Define a line," said Ferra.

"Gladly," said Vantic, and uncapped her pen.

He went back up the hill without the sheet.
