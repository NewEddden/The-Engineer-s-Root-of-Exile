Chapter 85 - Steam on the Water

Saban raised it at the table two days before they left, and he raised it the way he raised everything, as a question about carriage.

"Two keys," he said. "The whole of the relay key lives in two heads, and both heads are getting on one boat."

Nobody pretended not to understand him. Emeric had said it at the table the day the relay was costed, and Ward had heard it and not changed his expression. The full key to every line in the south, in two hands. Emeric's and the Chancellor's. And the same wooden boat, with a fire in it, on the same river, on the same day.

"The ore contract is mine," Ferra said. "Grayrock's shallow veins are failing, and the rate we pay for what's left has to change before the deep ground opens, or we'll be paying shallow prices for deep ore for a decade. I've written every line of that contract since the first one. I'll be the one in the room when it changes."

"Nobody's saying you shouldn't."

"You're saying I should be on a different boat."

"I'm saying there isn't one," Saban said, reasonably. "Which is the problem."

They settled it in about the time it took to say. She went up with them. The day the contract was signed at Grayrock, she came straight back down on the boat's first return run, before any of the shaft work began, and Emeric stayed. For three days, the day up and the days of the contract and the day down, both keys would be on the water, and everybody at the table knew it, and it was entered in Ferra's book as a risk accepted, with the dates, in her hand. Armand held Kaldo. If the boat went to the bottom of the river with both of them in it, Armand would open a sealed letter in Ferra's strongbox, and the letter would tell him what to do about the relay, and nobody at the table asked what it said.

It was not the only argument about the boat.

Imani made the other one, and she made it before Ferra had finished reading the dates. A boiler was a fire. A wooden boat was a pile of fuel with a keel on it. A river was water in every direction but one, and the one direction was downward, and they would be a day from Kaldo and a day from help. She had seen what a lamp did to a hay loft. She did not see what was different about a boiler in a boat, except that it was hotter and the loft could not sink.

It was a reasonable argument. Several people at the table thought it was the whole argument.

"I did the arithmetic," Emeric said. "The bed's iron, and there's an air gap between the iron and the wood, and a pump off the river that floods the bed in a count of forty. Rudd's trained Sim to watch her, and Sim has the same sheet Rudd has, and the same right." He looked down the table. "Ward will be there. And the water's right there. If she burns, we step off her."

"Into a river."

"In spring, a yard from the bank, with Ward." He did not smile. "Ward can swim."

Ward, who had not been asked, said, "Badly," and the argument ended there because Imani laughed, once, and then looked annoyed with herself for doing it.

She did not come. She had a hole in the ground at the top of the long street and forty men standing round it.

They went on the second morning of the true spring, at first light, with the mist still lying on the Eastern Branch in a flat grey sheet and the island only half awake.

She had gone back into the water a week before the way she had come out of it, on the four stones and Armand's hands, and it had cost the old man the rest of that day as well, and he had noted the minutes against the first time and found them the same. Now she lay against the bank below the outside foundry with steam up. Sim stood by the water cocks with his sheet, looking as though he had not slept, which he had not. Bram was at the engine, where he would be the whole day. Two of the foundry men stood by with the coal. A river man from the lower water called Pask, who had steered barges on this river before the plague when barges still went up it, had the tiller, because nobody else aboard knew where the shoals were.

Ferra came down the bank with a satchel and a document case and nothing else, and stepped aboard without a hand. Ward came behind her, and did not step aboard until he had looked at every face on the bank.

Vantic came last, at exactly the hour she had said, with a small bag and her book.

She had asked to come. Not in those words: she had told the table, a week before, that a capital asset of this size was a line in her next report, and that she would rather see it before she read about it, and the table had understood that this was a request and that it could not be refused, and nobody had tried.

"Your Highness," she said, and stepped aboard, and looked once along the length of the deck at the brick bed and the boiler and the chimney, and went and stood at the rail in the stern where she could see everything and be in nobody's way.

Bram opened the steam.

The engine began to go, and the shaft turned, and under the stern the water began to move. There was a moment when nothing happened except noise and a great deal of churning white water, and the men on the bank laughed. Then she moved. She came off the bank slowly, as though she were being pulled away from it by a rope nobody could see, and Pask put the tiller over, and her bow swung out into the stream and then round, and pointed up the Eastern Branch towards the lake.

Against the current.

There was no sail. There were no oars. There were no horses on a towpath. There was a chimney breathing grey smoke into the mist and a sound like a long slow heartbeat coming out of the middle of her, and she went up the Eastern Branch against the river at the speed of a man walking fast, and the men on the bank stopped laughing and watched her go.

The lake was flat and pale in the early light, and she crossed it in an hour, leaving a long spreading V behind her that went all the way to both shores. Fishermen in skiffs stopped pulling their lines and stared. One of them stood up in his boat and nearly went over. Pask steered her into the mouth of the river above the lake, where the water narrowed and quickened and began to push back in earnest, and she slowed, and dug in, and went on.

For the first hour Emeric did not stop moving. He went from the engine to the stern to the bed to the bow and back, feeling the planking for heat, watching the shaft where it came through the stern for knocking, watching Sim at the cocks, watching the chimney. Nothing went wrong. The engine ran. The gland on the stern tube wept a little and Bram took it up a quarter turn and it stopped. The bed stayed cool on the wood side of the gap. By the second hour he found that he had nothing to do, and did not know what to do with that, and went and stood at the rail.

Vantic was already there. She had been there since they cast off, writing occasionally, watching the banks go by.

"Your Highness seems to be an ideaman," she said, without looking round. "Always building something that could revolutionise a kingdom."

It came out dry. It had the shape of a compliment and the content of an entry, and he had known her long enough to hear both halves.

"One kingdom at a time," he said.

"Mm." She made a small note. Then she closed the book on her quill and looked at him properly, and there was no dryness in her face at all. "You know we can't let anybody hear of this one."

"I know."

"I'd like you to know why I'm saying it." She looked down at the water churning away from the stern. "A thing that takes ore and grain up and down a river against the current, with no towpath and no horses and no men on a rope, at the cost of a few carts of coal. It's worth a great deal. It's worth a great deal more if nobody else has priced it yet. My house holds your paper. My house would rather the market learned about this late, and all at once, from the far side of a contract, than early and by rumour from some carter in Milford." She paused. "That's all it is. It's not a secret. It's a price."

That was the whole of her, he thought: timing. She lived on the line between a thing hidden and a thing not yet announced, which was the line between a lie and a quarterly report, and she had always kept to the right side of it.

"Keeping it quiet should be simple enough," he said. "The last eight things I built are sitting in sheds, every one of them, with nobody able to say what it's for. There's a wall at the works covered in guesses. The best one's wrong." He looked at the chimney. "This one's going to be difficult to put in a shed."

She considered that. Something happened at the corner of her mouth that was not quite a smile.

"You could try a very large shed," she said.

He laughed. It came out louder than he meant it to.

Behind them, somewhere near the bed, there was a small sound.

"Hmm."

It was Ferra. She was standing by the boiler with her document case under her arm, looking at nothing in particular, which was to say at the river bank going past on the port side, and the sound had come out of her the way a sound comes out of a person who has not decided to make it and has no intention of explaining it.

Emeric looked round. Ferra's mouth had set in a line he had seen on her perhaps twice. It was not a line she used on columns.

"Is the smoke bothering you?" he said.

"Yes," said Ferra.

He thought about moving her further forward, out of the drift from the chimney, and then saw that she was already standing exactly where the drift did not reach, and did not know what to do with that either, and let it go.

Vantic had watched all of it, and had said nothing, and went back to watching the bank.

They passed the first of the fords in the middle of the morning. The river widened there and shallowed and ran over gravel in long chattering sheets, and Pask took her across to the far side where the channel ran deep under a bank of alders.

"The far side," Vantic said, to nobody, watching. "Below the ford. It runs harder there than it looks."

Pask glanced at her. He opened his mouth to say something and then shut it again, because she was right, and he had been about to say so, and it was not a thing a man who had steered barges on this river for twenty years expected to hear from a woman with a book. The boat slowed as she came into the deep channel, and dug in, and the engine's heartbeat deepened, and she crept past the alders against a current that ran, indeed, harder than it looked.

Nobody asked. It was the kind of remark anybody might make, looking at water.

Emeric did not notice it. He was watching the shaft.

Ferra noticed it. He did not see her notice, and she gave no sign of having noticed anything, but somewhere in the next quarter of an hour she took her book out of the document case and wrote something small in the back of it, and put it away again.

It was not until the middle of the afternoon that Vantic asked about the pen.

They were in the long reach below Grayrock by then, where the river ran straight and quiet between flat fields, and there was nothing to watch but the banks and the chimney. Ferra had sat down on the coal bunker's lid with the document case on her knees and was going through the contract for the fourth time, making small marks in the margin. She was making them with the pen. She had taken it out of her coat at the first ford, and again at the second, and now she was using it, and Vantic, from the rail, had noticed it twice already, because Vantic noticed what people wrote with. She read what they wrote.

"May I ask what that is?"

Ferra looked up.

"A pen," she said.

"It doesn't dip."

"The ink's inside it." Ferra held it up, not offering it, and turned it so the light went along the barrel. "It's filled from a bottle, once in a while. Not from any well. It holds enough for a day's writing. The point is steel and it flexes, and there's a channel under it that feeds the ink down at the rate you write."

"Where did you get it?"

"He made it."

She said it in exactly the same voice as the rest. No weight on it, no ornament, no glance at him. Three words, as flat as the entry for a cart of coal.

"He made it," Vantic said.

"In his room, with the door barred. Last summer. It took him most of a month." Ferra looked down at the contract. "It's the only one."

There was a very small pause.

Emeric was standing by the engine, a few yards off, with Bram, and heard all of it, and found that he could not think of a single thing to say that would not make it worse, and so said nothing, and looked at the shaft.

What he did not see, because he was looking at the shaft, was Ferra's face in the moment after she had said it. It did not change much. It was not a face that changed much. But the line that had set in her mouth in the morning, by the boiler, had gone out of it, and did not come back for the rest of the day, and she went on marking the contract with the pen for another hour with something in the set of her shoulders that anybody who had known her for ten years would have called easy.

She did not appear to notice that anything had changed, and nobody told her.

Vantic, at the rail, took perhaps two seconds over it.

She looked at the pen, and at the woman holding it, and at the man by the engine very carefully looking at a shaft, and she put the whole thing together the way she put together a borrower's quarter, all at once, with the total already showing. Then she turned back to the water. She had no interest whatever in standing anywhere inside that sum. She was the best reader of other people's positions on the boat, possibly in the south, and she read this one and filed it and did not write anything down, which for Vantic was the same as a closed door.

"It's very fine work," she said, to the river.

"Yes," said Ferra, and went on writing.

They came up the last reach in the late afternoon, with the light going gold on the water and the engine still beating, and Pask brought her round the long bend below the hill.

Grayrock rose out of the fields ahead. The hill first, grey and bare on its upper slopes, and then the scar of the spoil down its side, and then, one by one, the head-frames of the workings coming up out of the distance on the hill's shoulder, black against the sky, the wheels at their tops standing still at the end of the day. The tally house. The long roof of the hall. The river landing below it, with its stone quay and a few barges drawn up that had not moved in years.

And on the landing, standing quite still, a woman in a dark coat with her hands folded in front of her.

Bryda had been on the landing for some time. She had heard it before she saw it, the way everyone at Grayrock had, a slow heartbeat coming up the river from the south, and then she had seen the smoke above the alders, and then the boat had come round the bend. A boat of seventy feet, with nobody at the oars, with no sail, with no horses on the towpath, which had not been walked since the plague. Coming up the river, against the whole weight of the spring current, steady as a man walking, with smoke pouring out of the middle of it.

She did not move. Ilsa came down the path behind her at a run and stopped beside her and did not say anything either.

Bryda watched the boat come in to her landing. When it was close enough to see faces she found Emeric's at the rail, and looked at it for a long moment, and then at the chimney, and then back at him, and her expression did not change at all. She had spent twenty years married to a man who did not let her do the real work. She was very good at not letting a face say anything it had not been given permission to say.

"Well," Bryda said, to her daughter, quietly, as the boat bumped against the stones. "We'll need a bigger landing."
