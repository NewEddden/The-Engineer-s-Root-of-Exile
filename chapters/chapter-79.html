Chapter 79 - Midwinter

They came in at the South Gate on the evening before the shortest day, with the lamps already lit along the wall and the river loud under the bridge, and the first person Emeric saw inside the gate was Hale.

Hale was standing in the gate yard in his coat with a lantern, as if he had been passing, which at that hour and in that cold he had not been. He looked at the two of them, and at their horses, and at the satchel under Emeric's coat, and at the strip of linen round Emeric's forearm where the sleeve had ridden up, and he said, "Your highness," and nodded to Ward, and then, because he was Hale and some things had to be said in their proper order, "Master Armand has held the barony for twenty-six days. He would like to give it back."

"Is he well?"

Hale considered the question, as he considered all questions, for the price.

"He has been very busy, your highness," he said.



Armand was in his chair by the fire with the blanket over his knees and a writing board across the blanket, and the room was full of paper.

It had been full of books before. The books were still there. The paper was on top of them now: on the second chair, on the bench, on the windowsill, in stacks on the floor along the wall, some of it written on and some of it waiting. Emeric had told Ferra, the night before he left, that Armand would want a great deal, and Ferra had said how much is a great deal, and he had said he did not know, and she had sent up a ream. There was more than a ream in this room. There was, he thought, looking along the wall, very nearly all the paper in Kaldo.

"Sit down," said Armand. "No. Not there. Those are the coast."

Emeric moved a stack off the end of the bed instead and sat. Armand watched him do it with the pleased, slightly guilty expression of a man who knows he has been caught and intends to enjoy the next part.

"I held your barony," Armand said. "Every Tuesday, the disputes. Every morning, the sheets for signing. Your garrison's orders kept standing, as instructed, by a garrison that would not have let me change them if I'd tried. Nobody died who wasn't going to." He breathed. "I made two decisions you'd have made differently."

"Only two?"

"Two that matter." Armand laid one long hand flat on the writing board. "The first is all this. I wrote to my students."

Emeric looked at the paper.

"How many?"

"Forty-one. The ones I could find. The ones who are alive." Armand did not look at the stacks; he did not need to. "Forty years of them. Most of them hedge-placed now, some in lords' houses, some in nothing at all. Good hands, a lot of them, wasted in stupid places. I've told every one of them that there's a barony at the bottom of the kingdom with more work than mages, and an old fool in it who would like to see their faces again before he stops being able to. I've told them the wage. I haven't told them anything else." He looked at Emeric then. "They went by Saban's carriers and the coast house. Not by the crown. Ferra told me what you'd found before you'd got to the end of the north road. Nothing I sent went anywhere near Wayford."

Emeric had thought about it himself. He had thought about it on the road north and on the road home, the industrial core he could not build because there were not enough hands, the yard, the kilns, the slip, every one of them a season's work by hand and a fortnight's with a mage who could shape earth. He had thought about it and put it away, because a tower's mages did not come south to a debt barony on the edge of the map, and because he had not wanted to ask Armand to ask them, and could not have said why not.

Armand had not waited to be asked.

"How many will come?"

"Fewer than forty-one." Armand smiled, a little. "Most of them will be cross with me for asking. Some of them will be cross with me for leaving. A few of them owe me something, and a few of them will hear something in my hand that I didn't put in it on purpose." He did not say what. "Ten. Fifteen, if the spring is kind. In ones and twos. Ferra has already priced it."

"Of course she has."

"She came up the day after the first ones went, with a sheet. The wage, the tithe houses at nothing, what they'd bring in recharging stones for the neighbors, and how long before the one paid the other." Armand's smile widened. "She told me I was right. Then she told me the first three months would be ugly, and how ugly, to the copper, and that she'd say so to you, and she will."

"And the second decision?"

The smile went away. Not far, but away.

"The second," Armand said, "you'll hear about from somebody else, and you should, because they'll tell it better than I would. I was wrong about it. Not very wrong. Wrong enough." He moved the writing board. "Go and see the ward."



Imani was in the ward with her sleeves rolled up, and she told it better than he would have.

She told it at the linen press, folding, with her back to him, in about nine sentences, and every one of them was the length it needed to be and not a word over. On the fourth day after he left, Tess had asked again to go down to the powerhouse in her chair. Imani had said no again. Tess had asked Armand, because Armand was holding the barony and the barony owned the powerhouse. Armand had said yes. He had sent four of the garrison to carry her back up the hill when she was done, and they had, and on the way up it had come on to rain, a cold rain off the river, and the four of them had stopped at the top of the lane to argue about the best way to hold a chair, with Tess in it, in the rain.

"She had a chill by night," Imani said. "A fever by the next. A week in bed. She's out of it now. She's well." A sheet went into the press, corners first. "She wanted to see the dynamo. She saw it. She says it was worth it." Another sheet. "It wasn't his to say yes to."

"It was the barony's powerhouse."

"It was my patient." Imani turned round at last. She looked tired, and very clear. "I've said nothing to him. I won't. He's old and he meant it kindly and it would only make him ill to be told. I'm telling you."

"Then I'm told." He meant it. "I'd have said no."

"I know you would." She looked at his forearm, where the linen had ridden up again. Her face changed, not much. "Who closed that?"

"Nobody. It closed."

"It closed crooked." She did not come any closer. She looked at it the way she looked at anything that had been done wrong to a body, as a personal affront, and then went back to the press. "Come and let me look at it tomorrow. In daylight. Not tonight."

"Tomorrow's midwinter."

"Then the day after."

By the window, Tess was sitting up in her chair, thinner again, with the bench book open on the board across the arms. She had heard every word and was pretending to have heard none of them. When Emeric went over she looked up.

"Was it worth it?" he said.

"It's beautiful," Tess said, without any hesitation at all. "With the river this high. You can hear the field in it. You can hear it from the door." She looked at her hands on the board. "Bram let me hold the lever. For a count of twenty." Then, lower, so that Imani would not hear, and Imani did: "I'd do it again."

"I know," said Emeric. "So would I. That's why I'd have said no."

Tess thought about that, and decided it was unfair, and wrote something in the margin of the bench book that he suspected was about him.

On his way out, at the door, he said to Imani, "Armand says you boil spoons."

"I do boil spoons," said Imani, to the linen.



He went back down to Armand's room late, when the keep was dark and the fire in the room off the hall had burned down to red, and Armand was still awake in the chair with the blanket and the board, because Armand did not seem to sleep any more so much as stop for a while.

Emeric sat down on the end of the bed among the paper. He did not say anything about the ward.

Armand did not look at him. He looked at the fire.

"Did he show you?" he said.

"Yes."

The fire shifted, and a log broke in the middle and went down into the red, and Armand's hand moved on the blanket, once, and was still. That was all. Neither of them said anything else. After a while Emeric got up and put two pieces of wood on the fire, the small ones, the way Armand liked it at night, and went up to bed.



Bram's kitchen at midwinter was the warmest room in Kaldo, and this year it was full.

Last year there had been three of them. The year's book on the table, and the jug on the year's book, and three cups off the shelf over the stove, and the mended chair. This year Bram had brought in two benches from the forge and a trestle from the yard and put the trestle against the end of the table so that the table went most of the way to the door, and there were still not enough seats, and nobody minded.

Ferra was at the end nearest the stove with the year's book, which she did not close, because it was not the last night of the year yet and the totals were not hers to draw until it was. Ward was on the bench by the forge wall, where it was warmest, with his back against the brick and a cup in his hand, looking as close to comfortable as Emeric had ever seen him look indoors. Bram was everywhere. Bram, it turned out, had views on midwinter, and the views involved a goose, and the goose had been negotiated for in October from a farmer on the west side in exchange for a set of hinges, and the farmer had tried to swap the goose for a smaller goose in December, and Bram had noticed, and the story of how Bram had noticed took most of the time it took the goose to cook.

Emeric sat in the mended chair. Nobody told him to. Nobody needed to.

Tess came at dusk.

She came on a door. It was an actual door, one of the tithe houses' doors that Bram had not yet found a use for, with a blanket on it and Tess on the blanket, sitting up, with her arms folded, and four of the garrison carrying it at the corners like men carrying a very important dish. Her chair came behind on a handcart, pushed by a fifth man who looked as if he had drawn the short straw and wanted everyone to know it. Imani walked beside the door with her hand not quite on it, all the way down the lane from the ward, and came in last, with snow-light behind her, and stood in the doorway looking at the room as if she had not entirely decided to come in.

"Her idea," Imani said, to nobody in particular, about the door.

"I'm not missing it," said Tess, from the door, as they set her down. "I missed the dynamo for three weeks lying in a bed. I'm not missing a goose."

"You didn't miss the dynamo," said Imani. "That was the problem."

The four of the garrison put the door down across the two benches, so that Tess sat at the level of the table, enthroned, and then stood about not knowing whether they had been asked to stay. Bram told them to sit down or go away, and they sat down, all four, on the end of a bench built for three. The fifth man, with the chair, was given a cup of something and went away happier than he had come.

It was a loud evening after that.

Not in any way that mattered. Nobody made a speech. Bram carved the goose with a knife he had made for the purpose and refused to say when, and it was the best knife Emeric had ever seen used on a bird, and Bram used it as though it were a cleaver. One of the garrison turned out to know a song, and sang it, and it was about a tinker's wife, and it was not a song Emeric would have sung in front of Imani, and Imani laughed at the end of it, out loud, so that the man who had sung it turned red to the hair. Tess drank cider and argued with Bram about the dynamo's bearings, and was right about one thing and wrong about two, and Bram told her which, and she wrote down all three. Ward said almost nothing and ate more than anyone, and once, when the garrison men were arguing about whose turn it had been to hold the chair in the rain, Emeric saw him smile into his cup.

Ferra wrote one line in the year's book in the middle of it all, without putting her cup down, and he did not see what.

He told the table about the forest past Wayford, because Ward was not going to and somebody had to account for his arm. He told it short, and made the third man bigger in the telling, and Ward said, "He was hungry," from the bench by the wall, and the garrison men laughed, and Imani looked at his arm across the table with a face that said the day after midwinter, in daylight, and that she had not forgotten.

He did not tell them about the room under the Circle. He did not tell them about the four hours. He told them about Keller, a little, the counter in the corridor and the crooked seal on the chancery's slip, and when he took the slip out of his coat and passed it down the table, Ferra held it for a long while, and looked at the seal, and the date, and the clerk's initials, and put it inside the year's book, at the back, under the cover, where she kept the things that mattered.

"Ward said you'd want it framed," he said.

"Ward's wrong," said Ferra. "I want a year of them."

It was late when they carried Tess back up.

The four garrison men took the door again at the corners, a little less steadily than they had brought it, and Tess sat on it with her arms folded and told them which of them was out of step, and the fifth man came back from wherever he had been with the handcart and the chair. Emeric went with them, because the ward was on the way to the keep. Imani walked beside the door. Ferra stood in the doorway of the kitchen with the lamp behind her and watched them go up the lane, and then the door closed, and the lane was dark except for the wall lamps away above them, and the river loud below.

Halfway up the hill it started to snow.

Not much. A few flakes first, out of a sky that had been clear when they set out, then more, coming down slow and large and dry into the light from the wall lamps, and settling on the blanket over Tess's knees, and on the shoulders of the four men carrying her, and on Imani's hair, which she had tied up for the evening and which was coming down, as it always did by the end of anything.

Tess put her face up into it, and opened her mouth, and caught one on her tongue, and nobody said anything about that.

Imani looked up at the snow for a while, walking. Then she looked across the door at Emeric.

"Two days," she said.

He did not understand at once. Then he did. A week before the end of November, in the ward, with a sheet half folded in her hands: will you be back before the snow.

"Two days," he said.

She nodded, as if a figure had come out where she had expected it to, and looked ahead up the lane, and walked on beside the door through the first snow with her hand not quite on it, all the way to the top of the hill.
