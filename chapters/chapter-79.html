Chapter 79 - The Honest Part

Nobody in Kaldo had ever seen him practise.

The two mages had remarked on it in their first winter, the way men remark on a thing they have decided is either a lie or a secret. Here was a prince who drilled the garrison on written schedules, who had the evening classes counting to twelve on the master foot, who timed the bucket line and posted the times on the church door, and in five years neither of them had once caught him at so much as a warm-up. Armand had said, in the room above Kaldo, so that is where you practise, and turned back to his slate. That had been half of it. The other half nobody had asked about, because nobody knew there was another half.

He had spent eight years learning to make things with his hands and his mana at the same time.

Not casting at a target. Anybody with the gift could learn to throw a blade of air at a man, given a season, and the garrison's enhanced men could do worse things than that before breakfast. What he had been doing in the delve, on the nights he did not sleep, was something he had never seen anybody do and had no word for, which was holding a small object in the air and working it into a shape. It was slow. It had no audience. It had produced, in eight years, nothing that anybody had ever seen.

In the first week of summer he sat down in his room with the door barred to make something that somebody would.

On the table in front of him there was a blank of steel no bigger than a fingernail, which Bram had forged out of the end of a knife bar and filed thin and handed over without asking what it was for. There was a short brass tube with a thread cut inside one end, which Jorrin had turned on the second lathe to a drawing with no explanation on it and a great many dimensions, and which Jorrin had then made again, unasked, because he did not like the first one. There was a stub of hard boxwood the length of a finger. There was a small stoppered bottle of ink he had made himself in a borrowed pot at the foundry, over three evenings, out of galls and iron and gum and rainwater, strained four times through linen until it ran thin and clean. And there was a lamp.

He barred the door because what he was about to do would look, to anybody who walked in, like a man sitting very still in a chair with a piece of metal floating in front of his face, and that was not a thing he could explain.

He took the blank's weight away.

It was the third of his three drills, the slab of stone raised up off the ground and let fall, which he had been taking smaller for years, a flagstone and then a brick and then a pebble, until the drill was not about lifting at all but about the exact amount of lift a thing needed to stop being heavy. He did not raise the blank. He simply stopped it falling. It rose an inch off the table as the last of its weight went, and hung there in the lamplight, turning very slowly, touched by nothing.

That solved the first problem, and it was a real problem. A thing that small could not be held hard enough to be worked without being marked by whatever held it. Every vice in Bram's shop left its jaws in the metal. Every pair of tongs left its teeth. The blank hanging in front of him had no jaw marks on it because nothing had ever gripped it, and nothing would.

Then he warmed it.

He did not warm all of it. Mages held a furnace at heat by pouring themselves into fire stones in shifts, and he had watched Orrin and Vesk do it for most of a day for a school full of glass. What he held now was a chip of fire stone the size of a grain of rice on the end of a copper wire, and what he poured into it was a thousandth of what Orrin had poured, and he brought the chip to the one spot on the blank where he wanted the steel to move, and nowhere else. The spot went a dull cherry. The rest of the blank stayed grey and hard.

And then he shaped it, with the second drill, the blade of air, taken down past anything he had used on the lathe, until it was less an edge than a pressure. He bent the soft spot a hair and let the heat go, and moved the chip, and bent the next spot.

None of it was new. Any mage in the kingdom could hold a stone off the floor, and heat a chip of fire stone, and cut with moving air. What nobody did, because nobody had any reason to, was do all three at once, at that size, to the same small thing, for hours, without dropping any of them.

It was exhausting. It was exhausting in the particular way that a thing is exhausting when three different parts of you have to keep hold of three different ropes, and the moment any one of them slips, the other two go with it. And it was slow. A single nib took him most of a night.

He noticed that, somewhere in the second hour, and found that it pleased him more than almost anything else about the work. It could never be production. There was no version of this that a factory could do, or even a second man. The thing he was making could only ever be made like this once.

He did not make it by imagining it.

He went down, holding all three ropes, into the delve. Not the room above Kaldo and not the office. A plain table under a plain light, and on it the finished pen.

He had built that pen in there over months. Every line of it had been worked out on a real bench first, with real steel and real ink and real failures, and then carried down and set in place, until the pen on the delve table was not a picture of what he wanted but a statement of what worked. The shape of the nib, curved so it would flex under a hand and spring back. The angle of the shoulders. The length of the slit. The narrow channel cut along the top of the boxwood feed, which had to carry ink down from the barrel to the nib at the exact rate a hand would use it, and not faster, and not slower.

So he worked by comparison. The true nib stood on the table in the delve. The real blank hung in the lamplight in his room. He held both at once, which was the part he had spent eight years learning, and he moved the real one towards the true one, a hair at a time, watching the difference close.

He was not carving from imagination. He was tracing something that already existed, in a place only he could stand in.

The time did the rest. An hour in his room was a great deal longer than an hour in there, and a patience that no man could have at the speed of the world was available to him at the speed of the delve. He could look at a single bend in the steel for what felt like a morning, and decide it was a hair too sharp, and let it out, and in his room the lamp would have burned down by the width of a straw. He had never told anybody that this was what the hours were for. He had never had anybody to tell.

He got three wrong before he got one right, and the three wrong ones were all wrong in the same place.

The nib was not the difficult part. The nib came to the true shape on the second night and stayed there. The difficult part was the channel in the feed, the groove along the top of the boxwood that sat under the nib and fed it.

The first one he cut too wide. He filled the barrel, screwed the nib and feed into place, set the point to a sheet of paper, and the ink ran straight out of it, down the nib and over his knuckles and along the inside of his wrist into his sleeve, in a thin black thread that did not stop until he turned the pen upside down. He sat and looked at his wrist for some time.

The second he cut too narrow. It held the ink so well against its own weight that nothing he did with his hand would coax any of it down. He dragged the point across good paper and it left a pale scratched groove with nothing in it, a line you could see only by tilting the sheet to the lamp.

The third was narrower than the first and wider than the second, and it wrote for the length of a sentence and then went dry, and started again when he shook it, and went dry again.

The working channel lived between those failures, in a gap narrower than anything on Bram's bench had been made to cut. He found it on the fourth feed, somewhere towards dawn, by moving a single wall of the groove by less than the thickness of a hair and trying it and moving it back by half of that. When he set the point to the paper the fourth time, a line came out of it. It was black and even and it went on as long as he drew it, and when he lifted the pen, the ink stopped, and when he set it down, the ink began.

He wrote his own name with it, and then a column of figures, and then, because there was nobody to see, a long looping line of nothing at all, for the pleasure of watching it not stop.

There was one thing he did not do with magic, and he had decided it before he started.

He did not cut the slit.

The slit was the fine split down the middle of the nib, from the point up to a small round hole, that let the two halves of the point spread under pressure and let the ink run down between them. It was the finest cut on the whole pen. He could have made it with the air blade in a heartbeat, far truer than any hand, and he did not, because a nib that needed the delve to exist was a nib nobody else could ever make. If the slit came from him, the pen came from him, and so would every pen after it, and he would have made the most useful small thing in the barony into something that stopped when he did.

What he did instead was smaller and stranger.

He held the true slit in the delve, a single straight line, and carried that line up with him into the room the way a man holds a plumb line in his eye after he has put the plumb away. He could see exactly where it ran across the real steel. Then he took an ordinary graver out of Bram's rack, the kind any engraver in the kingdom would have owned, and set the blank on a block of lead, and cut the slit by hand.

His hand had been good at fine lines once, before he was seven, and then for fifteen years he had made very sure that nobody ever saw it be good at anything. It was steady enough. The certainty was the magic. The cut was not.

When it was done he sat and looked at it for a long time.

It was a master. That was all it was, and he had known that from the start. Bram would strike dies off it, on the lathes, and from those dies an ordinary smith with an ordinary press would be able to punch out nibs as fast as he could feed strip into the press, and none of them would ever need a man sitting in a barred room at night with three ropes in his hands. That was the plan and it was a good plan. It had one hole in it, which was that there was no strip. Nobody in four baronies could roll steel that thin and that even. Until somebody could, this nib was not the first of anything. It was the only one.

It was the second time in a season that he had spent magic once, deliberately, so that he would never have to spend it on the same thing again. First the lathe. Now this. He noticed the pattern and found it satisfying, and then found it slightly alarming how satisfying he found it, and put the pen in his pocket.

She was at the long table in her room with three order-books going at once, one hand running down a column and her eyes two lines ahead of the hand, and she did not look up when he came in, because she never did until she had reached the bottom of something.

He had rehearsed several things to say on the stair. He had distrusted all of them. So he set the pen down on the table beside her open book, without a word, and that would have been fine, except that he then ruined it with a sentence that was nothing like what he had meant to lead with.

"Never fill it from the office well," he said, and put the little stoppered bottle beside it. "Only from this. What's in the well will eat the nib."

She wrote that down. She took a clean corner of the order-book and wrote bottle only, never the well, in her small square hand, with her quill, before she had so much as touched the pen.

Then she looked at it.

She looked at it for longer than he had expected, and in a way he recognised, because he had seen her look at sums like it. It was the look she gave a column that had come out too round, a total with no loose change in it, the kind of number that was either exactly right or was hiding something. She did not pick it up.

"What is it," she said.

"A pen."

"It's a very small pen."

"The ink's inside it."

She considered this. Then she picked it up, and turned it over, and looked at the nib, and at the barrel, and at the thread Jorrin had cut twice, and then, because she was Ferra and there was a column in front of her that she had not finished, she put its point to the page and went on.

She laid down a figure. Then a line under it. Then, without stopping, the running total of the page he had interrupted, and the next, and a note in the margin about a Milford carrier who was a week behind, all of it in one unbroken hand, without once lifting the pen to reach for the well. The pen kept pace with her. It kept pace with a mind that had never in its life been able to write down a number as fast as it could see one, and he watched her hand discover that, somewhere in the middle of the third line, and go faster, and then faster again, and find that the pen was still there.

She stopped at the bottom of the column.

"Oh," she said, quietly.

It was not a word she gave away often. He had heard it from her perhaps three times in nine years, and never about anything he had done.

"Everybody else," he said, "it saves a dip." It was the only smooth thing he managed that morning, and he had not rehearsed it. "You, it keeps up with. I couldn't have built one and given it to anybody else first."

She did not blush. He would not have known what to do if she had. She looked up from the column and held his eyes, which she did not often do either, for a moment longer than a pen needed, long enough that the moment went on past the point where one of them ought to have looked away, and it was not her.

Then she looked down at the column again as though nothing whatever had happened, and started the next one.

He went and stood at the window for a while, because it seemed to be the thing to do, and then he went out. She did not look up when he left. She did not put the pen down for the rest of the morning.

Bram came up with the foundry tallies an hour later, as he did every seventh day, and found his daughter at her table writing faster than he had ever seen her write, with something in her hand that was not a quill.

"What are you so pleased with?"

"Nothing," Ferra said, and kept writing.

Bram looked at the pen. Then he turned his head and looked at Emeric, who had come back in behind him with a question about the Milford carrier and now very much wished he had not. It was a flat, patient, unreadable look, the look of a father deciding with great care not to say the thing he was thinking, and it went on for some time.

Emeric decided, as he had once before in that man's workshop, that he had seen nothing.

He asked his question about the carrier. Ferra answered it without lifting her eyes. Bram put the tallies down on the corner of the table, square to its edge, the way she would have, and went back down the stairs.

It was the last easy thing he would make for a long time.
