Chapter 52 - The Bench

He came up the road in a hired cart in the third week of autumn with three crates in the back and a hired man sitting on them, and he stopped at the Western Gate and asked for the prince by name.

The gate did not know who he was. There was no reason it should have. The man on duty was twenty-two years old and had been eight when the plague came and had never in his life met anybody from the capital who was not either a crown courier or a problem, and what he saw was an old man in a travelling coat with a carter he had clearly not paid enough.

He asked what it was regarding.

"It is regarding nothing," Armand said. "Tell him the name and he will come down or he will not."

Emeric came down at something faster than a walk and did not entirely manage to stop himself doing it, which the gate noticed and told people about.

The first thing Armand said to him was not a greeting.

He got down off the cart stiffly, put a hand on the wheel to steady himself, straightened up, looked at the man he had taught for six years, and said:

"Why did you never write?"

There was a carter behind him and two gate men and a boy holding a horse. Armand did not appear to have noticed any of them and would not have cared.

"I wrote twice."

"I had one." He said it flatly. "The autumn after the trial. Eleven pages, with the thing about the stone in it, and a great deal about drainage which I read twice and did not understand and have thought about since. I answered it inside a fortnight."

"I never had an answer."

Armand looked at him for a moment. "No," he said. "Well."

Neither of them made anything of it. It was a road. Roads lose things. It went past both of them in under two seconds and neither of them thought about it again for three years.

"And then?" Armand said.

Emeric had the honest answer available and heard himself decide to give it, and it was not a decision he was proud of afterward, because what he was actually doing was reaching for the version that would end the conversation fastest.

"I wrote again in the winter. Shorter. Nothing came back from that either, and then I stopped, and I stopped on purpose." He made himself say the rest of it. "A man who writes three times is asking. And an old teacher who crosses a kingdom because his student kept asking arrives as a favour rather than as a decision. I knew what I was eventually going to have to ask you to do and I did not think any of it would survive being a favour."

It was the first time he had said it out loud. He had held it in his head for three years as a piece of reasoning, and out loud, in a gateway, in front of a carter, it did not sound like reasoning at all.

Armand did not shout at him. That was the part that stayed.

"I settled what needed settling in fourteen months," he said. "Two students through their course and a bench handed to a man who will ruin it. I spent the three years after that assuming I had been forgotten by a young man who had found something more interesting, which is an ordinary thing for an old man to assume and I was not especially wounded by it." He looked at the crates. "I have heard worse reasons than yours. I have given worse reasons than yours. Get somebody to help with the second crate, it is the heavy one and it is the one that matters."

And he turned round and started untying the ropes, and that was the end of it, and Emeric would have very much preferred to be shouted at.

The crates came up to the room off the foundry office because it was the only room in the barony with a stone floor, no traffic and a window facing north, and Armand chose it in about ninety seconds after being shown four.

What came out of them was not impressive and everybody said so.

A bench. A joiner's bench, essentially, but heavier, with the legs let into the floor rather than standing on it, because the whole apparatus was worthless if anything moved. A cradle on top of it with three adjustable arms. A frame of brass rods and a beam with a hair-fine pointer on one end that you read against a scale scratched onto a plate.

And a padded case with eleven reference stones in it, cut, and graded, and calibrated against the tower's own standards by a process Armand described in four sentences and refused to describe in five.

Colm looked at the bench for a while and said it was a decent piece of joinery and asked who had done the legs. Ward looked at it and said nothing. Bram asked what it was for and was told, and said that it looked like a lot of brass for a thing that did not do anything.

"It does not do anything," Armand agreed. "It tells you what is true. There is nothing south of Wayford that does that and there has not been one in this half of the kingdom since the founding."

He spent two days setting it up and refused all help except Colm's, and he levelled the bench four separate times.

Before he would measure anything he walked the barony, and that took three days, and it was the strangest three days Emeric had spent since the census.

He had expected the old man to be impressed. He was braced for it, in the slightly shameful way a man is braced for being admired by somebody whose opinion he has wanted for twenty years.

Armand was not impressed. Armand was disturbed, and it took Emeric most of the first day to work out the difference.

They went out along the fourth division first, because that was where the main ran, and Armand asked to see one of the sections, and they took a cover off, and he knelt down on wet ground in a good coat and looked at a shallow etching cut into the inside of a clay pipe collar.

"How many of these are there?"

"On this main? A hundred and six."

"Buried."

"They have to be. It's a water main."

"They are buried," Armand said, "in wet ground, in a field, under the frost line, and they have been there for how long?"

"Three years on the first forty."

He stayed kneeling a while longer and then let Emeric help him up.

At the foundry he stood in front of the gauge for a long time with his hands behind his back and asked the second question of the week, which was who read it.

"Farris's shift man. Then whoever's on."

"A furnace man."

"A furnace man."

"A furnace man reads a working and takes a decision off it and pulls a lever," Armand said, in the voice of a man reading a sentence out of a book to check that it says what he thinks it says.

"Twenty times a shift."

He met Vesk, and the two Vesk had taken on, and was told what they were doing, which was a standing problem on substrate failure that Kaldo paid them a wage to work on and had paid them a wage to work on for nineteen months without a result. He asked what happened if they never solved it and was told that they would be paid anyway and put on something else.

He did not say anything about that at all. He asked Vesk two technical questions, got two answers, and looked at him for slightly longer than was comfortable.

And on the third morning Emeric took him to the school, which was the thing he had been looking forward to, and stood at the back of Orrin's room while thirty-one children between seven and thirteen were taught the fundamentals of mana in a barony that had had no mage in it at all six years earlier.

Armand watched the whole lesson without moving.

Outside afterward, in the yard, with the noise coming through the wall behind them, he said the thing he had been assembling for three days.

"You have not built anything advanced. I want that understood, because you are going to take it the wrong way and it is not an insult." He was looking at the wall and not at Emeric. "There is not one working in this barony that a competent second-year could not produce. Nine ordinary things, cut shallow, put where they do work, and repeated. That is the whole of it."

"Then what's the matter?"

"The matter is that none of it should function." Armand turned round. "I wrote the standing theory of ambient interaction. Four decades ago. It is in the tower's curriculum and it is in the notebook I gave you and it says, in terms, that a shallow working in unattended contact with soil and water degrades to nothing inside a season, and that a working left unsupervised in a load-bearing role is a working that will eventually fail into whatever it is bearing. It says so because it is true. I tested it."

"Then how is the main still running?"

"That is the question I have been carrying around your fields for three days," Armand said, "and the answer, which I dislike very much, is that I tested it eleven times over nine years on eleven workings and you have four hundred of them in the ground and eleven hundred people walking past them every day, and the difference between eleven and four hundred is not a difference of degree."

He started walking back toward the gate.

"I have not been wrong for forty years in a way that cost anybody anything," he said. "I have been wrong for forty years in a way that stopped anybody trying. Those are the same thing and only one of them is visible."

The measuring started the next morning.

Armand asked him what a stone held.

Emeric gave him the constant. He gave it without hesitating, because he had derived it himself in a trove in his second winter, and had confirmed it, and had used it to specify every margin in the barony for four years, and it was as much a part of his thinking as the length of his own arm.

Armand wrote it down, took one of the eleven reference stones out of the padded case, set it in the cradle, and spent forty minutes doing something to it with the brass frame that Emeric could not follow at all.

Then he read the pointer and wrote a second number under the first.

He did it again with a different stone. Then a third time with a fourth stone, and a fourth time with the first one again, at a different hour, because the hour mattered.

At the end of it there were five numbers on the sheet and they agreed with each other to within the width of the pointer, and every one of them was below the figure Emeric had given him by about a sixth.

"That is not a rounding error," Armand said. "A rounding error is noise. It goes both ways and it goes away when you take more of them. This goes one way and it does not get smaller. Your constant is high."

Emeric stood at the end of the bench and did the arithmetic without being able to stop himself, because the arithmetic was the only part of it he was equipped to do at that moment.

If a stone held a sixth less than he had believed, then every design in Kaldo that loaded a stone was loading it closer to failure than its designer thought, and the direction of the error was the one that killed people.

The drying shed. The cutoff fired at a threshold set against the wrong number, which meant it fired later than intended, which meant the reader was carrying more than he had ever intended a reader to carry, on a working he had cut twice because the first one had not felt deep enough.

The furnace gauges. Twenty reads a shift, a lever pulled off each of them.

Eleven sealed sections on the main, under frost, in wet ground, specified against the same figure.

The four suits of harness, which had walked onto a field with men inside them.

And the threshold breakers, which Bram had fought him over and had built to fire at a level chosen on the strength of a number Emeric had told him was certain, and which existed for the specific purpose of preventing the exact event that a thin margin makes likelier.

"Every one of those," he said out loud, "has a margin in it that isn't there."

"Yes."

"They've been running for years."

"They have been running for years inside a margin you put there because you are cautious," Armand said. "You specified generously out of temperament. That is the only reason we are having this conversation in a room instead of at a funeral, and temperament is not a method."

Emeric worked out why he had been wrong while standing there, and did it out loud, in front of the old man, which was the worst available way to do it.

He had derived the constant from observation. Then he had confirmed it, and the confirmation was the part that had made him certain, and the confirmation had not been a second observation at all.

He could not say where he had confirmed it. He said that he had checked it against a model of his own, built out of his own understanding of the subject, and that the model had returned the same figure, and that the agreement had been exact, and that the exactness was what had convinced him.

Armand let him get to the end of it.

"Your model returned your own figure," he said.

"Yes."

"Because your model is built out of what you believe."

"Yes."

"Then you did not confirm anything. You asked a mirror whether you were correct and the mirror said yes, and you have been running a barony on that answer for four years." He said it without any relish at all, which made it considerably worse. "This is not a failure of intelligence. It is the specific failure that the bench exists to prevent, and it is why the tower has one, and it is the only thing the tower has ever done that was worth the tower. A man who reasons without measuring is doing philosophy. It is a respectable occupation. It does not hold a roof up."

Emeric had written that caution down twice in four years. Once in the second winter, in his own hand, in the notebook, in a paragraph he could have recited. Once again after the census, in different words, when Ferra's two figures had taught him what the difference between a count and a belief was worth.

He had written it down and it had not protected him, because the only thing that catches that error is an instrument, and until eleven that morning there had not been one.

Armand put the reference stone back in its case and closed the lid.

"What else are you confident about?" he said.

The answer to that took two hours and the second correction came out of it inside twenty minutes, and Declan was in the room for it, which had not been planned.

The dead zone. Emeric explained the falloff, which he had measured himself in a frozen field in February with Ward standing thirty feet away and a line of pegs, and which he had measured three times because he had known how much would be built on it.

He gave the radius. He said that on the day it had rolled further than that, over the lane, and had taken the crystal out of one of the four suits, and that he had never been able to account for it and had written it down as a fault in his own measurement without ever finding the fault.

Armand asked one question, which was what the ground had been like.

"Empty. A field in February. That was the point. Nothing on it to interfere."

Armand closed his eyes briefly.

"You measured how far a wave travels across a dry floor," he said, "and then you put it in a basin."

He explained it in about a minute. A failing crystal does not carve a hole of a fixed size. It pushes the ambient out of the area the way a hand pushes water, and how far it pushes depends on how much there is to push. A frozen empty field in February is the thinnest ambient in the calendar. A lane packed with men, four suits of charged harness, a working at either end and a mage on the far side of it is the thickest that ground would ever be in its life.

"Your radius was not wrong. It was measured under a condition that will never again occur on a day when it matters. Every number you have for that effect is a floor and you have been using it as a ceiling."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Declan said, from the corner, "So it wasn't the suit."

"No," Armand said.

"And it wasn't me having it in the wrong place."

"No."

"Right." Declan thought about it. "I'd like that written down somewhere that Rylan can read, because he has had an opinion about where I was standing for four years and I would like it settled."

He went out perfectly cheerfully. He had come out of that lane alive with a dead suit on him and had carried the question ever since, and it had taken a stranger with a brass frame sixty seconds to hand it back to him, and he took it the way a man takes a thing off his back.

Emeric took it a good deal worse, and had no right to, and knew it.

Armand asked to see what the school taught on the second day of measuring, and Emeric gave it to him without a moment's hesitation, because the school was the thing he was proudest of in the entire barony.

It took Armand a day and a half.

He came back with the curriculum and thirty-one pages of it marked, and he sorted what he had found into three piles on the table and went through them in order, because he had been a teacher for fifty years and could not do it any other way.

The first pile was simply wrong. Definitions that had drifted, a classification scheme two generations out of date, three explanations that were not so much incorrect as meaningless. Nobody would ever be hurt by any of it. It would merely have to be untaught.

The second pile was wrong in a way that would eventually put a child in Imani's ward. Four items. The worst of them was a rule of thumb about handling charged stock that was safe at the sizes children practised on and stopped being safe at about the size of a foundry blank, taught as a general rule, to a room of eleven-year-olds who were going to be working on that floor in five years.

The third pile was the one that mattered and Armand put it down last on purpose. Load-bearing. Things that were not themselves catastrophic but that Orrin had built other things on top of, so that the error did not sit in one lesson but ran up through six, and every one of the six had been taught, correctly and carefully and brilliantly, off a foundation that would not hold.

"How many children," Armand said.

"Through the school? Since it opened, about ninety."

"Then you have ninety of them," he said, "and a third of what they know is not true, and they learned it well, which is the difficulty. Nothing is harder to take out of a person than something he was taught properly."

Emeric sat with that one for a long time and got no comfort out of anywhere.

He had spent six years on a single idea, which was that a thing that lives in one man's head is worth almost nothing and a thing that can be written down, taught, and repeated by ordinary people is worth almost everything. He had built the bucket line that way, and the census, and the sieve, and the foundry, and Orrin's school most of all.

And he had never once, in six years, asked which direction the multiplication ran.

A method that replicates a true thing ninety times is the best instrument in the world. The same method replicates a false thing exactly as efficiently, and faster, because a false thing that has been taught well is defended by everyone who learned it.

He had built the machine for exactly this outcome. It had produced exactly this outcome. The machine had worked perfectly.

Orrin took it badly for about a day.

That was the whole of it, and Emeric had braced for a great deal more. He went down expecting to have to manage a proud man and found instead a man sitting on a wall behind the school, in the cold, with the marked curriculum on his knees, who had been sitting there since the middle of the morning.

Orrin had not lied about anything. That needs saying, because nobody in that barony ever said it out loud, and because Emeric checked, privately and thoroughly and with some shame, over the following fortnight, and found nothing at all. He had taught what he had been able to learn, out of three books and a correspondence and his own head, to children in a town that had no business having a school, and he had done it better than most men with a tower behind them do it, and about a third of it was not true.

He asked one question, that afternoon on the wall, and it was not about himself.

"The pile that could hurt somebody. How fast can we pull it?"

And by the following morning he was in the measuring room at seven with a list of every child who had been through his room in four years and which of them had had which lesson in which year, which he had assembled overnight from his own registers, and he put it in front of Armand and asked where to start.

That was worse than the day of it. Emeric found it considerably harder to watch than anger would have been, and never worked out a way to say so that would not have been about himself.

Vesk said nothing.

He had been correcting Orrin quietly for a year, apprentice to apprentice, never in front of anybody, never in writing, in the way of a man who has been owned by people and does not volunteer information in a room with a lord in it. Half the second pile he had already dealt with on his own floor without mentioning it to a living soul.

He did not raise any of that, then or afterward, and he did not appear to get one moment's pleasure out of having been right.

Ferra worked out what the bench was inside a day, and she worked it out from a direction nobody else in the barony would have approached it from.

She came up in the evening with four years of books and asked Armand, in front of Emeric, whether the instrument could measure the charge in a foundry blank at intake.

Armand said that it could.

"Then every blank we have bought since the second year was priced on somebody's estimate," she said. "Ours or theirs. And every one of those figures is in my book as a fact."

"They are not facts."

"No." She put the books down. "They are four years of numbers I have been treating as measured and which were opinions, and I have built the running averages off them, and the averages are what we set the contracts against."

Emeric had been braced for this one too, because it was the sort of thing that had made her furious before.

She was not furious. She was, in her own entirely undemonstrative register, delighted, and it took him a moment to recognise it because he had only seen it twice.

What followed went on for about two hours and he understood perhaps a third of it. She wanted to know the instrument's repeatability, and whether two readings by two people agreed, and what it would cost to take a reading, and how long, and whether it could be done at a gate by a clerk or needed a mage, and what the smallest difference was that it could honestly be said to detect. Armand answered every one of those and then started asking her questions back, about what a running average was for and why she kept two of them and what she did when a series broke.

At some point they stopped explaining things to each other and started arguing about sampling.

Emeric stood in the doorway for a while with a cup of something going cold in his hand.

He had nothing whatsoever to contribute. There was no version of that conversation in which he was the useful person in the room, and he found, standing there, that he was enjoying watching it considerably more than he would have enjoyed being in it, and that this was new, and that he did not entirely know what to do with it.

He did not show Armand the delve.

He intended to twice. The first time was on the second evening, after the constant, when Armand asked him where the model had come from and he said out of his own understanding and let the sentence stand there doing half the work. The old man had looked at him for a moment and had not pressed, and Emeric had had the whole thing ready and had not said it.

The second time was a week later, at the end of an evening that had gone well, with the corrections running and the room warm and the bench under a cloth. He got as far as putting his cup down.

It was not distrust. He tried that explanation on himself afterward and it did not fit anywhere. He would have given Armand the delve inside an hour of the man arriving if the conversation had started anywhere else.

The difficulty was that it could not start anywhere else. To explain where the model came from he would have to explain what the model was, and to explain what the model was he would have to explain that he had had it since his second winter, and that he had sat in it for four years checking his own arithmetic against himself, and that during all four of those years there had been one man alive who could have told him it was not a check, and that he had written to that man twice and then stopped for a reason he had said out loud in a gateway and could not say again.

There was no sentence that got him from here to there. He looked for one on three separate occasions and did not find it, and each time he put the cup down and talked about something else, and each time it got slightly harder.

He did not show him the pump-house either. That one he did not even reach for. Nor the board in the gatehouse, which was the same refusal with a different lamp on it.

So the arrangement settled into what it settled into, which was excellent. Armand had the bench, and the corrections, and the school, and every number in the barony to take apart, and he was busier and more interested than he had been in fifteen years, and he said so once. They worked together every day. They talked about capacity and falloff and substrate and sampling and the ninety children, for hours, with great pleasure, and about nothing else at all.

Emeric had spent his entire childhood on the receiving end of exactly that arrangement from a man in a larger building, and had understood it at seven as a verdict on himself, and had carried it south in a carriage, and thought about it most weeks.

He did not notice.

At the end of the autumn the room off the foundry office held a bench let into the floor, eleven reference stones in a padded case, and a stack of Kaldo's own figures four years deep with corrections written down the margins in a hand nobody in that barony could read at speed.

Nine of them were substantial. Two of them were dangerous. One of them touched everything.

Emeric sat in that room on the last evening of the month with the cloth off the bench and the lamp low and went through the whole four years in his head, slowly, the way you check a floor after a flood.

He had been right about a great many things. He had been right about the census and the burial rows and the sieve and the drainage and the foundry and the ledger and Wexler and the lane and the ore, and he had been right about them against the opinion of nearly everybody, and none of that was worth anything tonight.

He had been wrong about one number. He had been wrong about it in a room where the only witness had been himself, and he had built four hundred workings and a school and a set of margins and four suits of armour on top of it, and every one of them was thinner than the man who had specified it believed.

The corrections would take a year. Some of them would take longer. Two of them were going to have to be made to things currently buried under a field in November.

And the coalition had not gone anywhere, and Sethe was six days north, and every margin in the barony was a sixth smaller than it had been at breakfast.
