Chapter 52 - Current

It ran the right way round on the fourth night of January.

Tess was at the lever this time. Emeric had not asked her to be. He had come into the back room with the lamp and found her already standing there with her hand on it, looking at the two white arrows, the one he had painted on the yoke and the one she had painted on the post, and he had gone and stood by the jars instead.

He did it as he had done it before, because the part of it they had done before had been right. Springs up off the new drum, wedged. The jars across the field coils for a count of twenty, out loud. The nail against the face of the arm: it stuck, and came away grudging. Springs down.

Harl was on his stool at the foot of the stair, with a bucket on each side of him and his hands on his knees. He had not said a word to anybody since they came in, and did not now.

"Go on," Emeric said.

Tess pulled the lever. The belt slapped across. The ring jerked, and turned, and gathered, and the sound came up off it, and Tess did not look at the wire on its board at all. She watched the ring, and the arrow, with her lips moving.

The wire went red.

It went from grey to a dull deep red along its whole length, which it had not done for a breath in December, and the needle in its box swung hard over against its pin. And it stayed there. Three breaths, and then ten, and then a count of a hundred, which Emeric was saying under his breath without having meant to, and then the hundred again. The red did not go out of the wire. If anything it brightened a little, as the wheel outside settled to its night pace, and steadied, and held.

Tess let her hand fall off the lever.

There were sparks at the springs. Small ones, blue, a faint fizz of them where the copper strips rode on the turning drum. Emeric went down on his knees beside it with the lamp, and loosened the clamp that held the springs, and eased the pair of them round the drum by the width of a finger, with the turn, and then a little further. The sparks shrank as he moved them. At one place they nearly went out altogether, a thin blue fur along the edge of each spring and no more, and he tightened the clamp there.

He looked round. Harl was looking at the springs.

"Those are small," Harl said.

It was the first thing he had said in the back room since December. He did not say anything else. He went on sitting with his hands on his knees, between his two buckets, watching the fur of blue light at the springs, and the red wire, and the ring going round the right way under the arrow, and Tess, when it was plain that the wire was going to stay red, sat down on the floor with her back to the wall and her bench book open on her knees, and began writing down the hours.

They ran it until the sky over the east bank went grey. The wire was red at midnight, and at two, and at four, and at six. At seven Emeric pulled the lever back himself, and the ring ran down into its long falling hum, and stopped, and in the silence the wheel outside went on turning, and there was nothing else to hear.

Harl stood up, picked up his two buckets, and carried them back to the race, and emptied them into the river. Then he filled them again, and brought them back, and set them down on either side of his stool for the next night, and went up the stair to bed.



It was the wire that held them up after that, as it had been the wire since July.

Every lamp on the walls would need a coil to work it. Eight lamps. And the line wanted a second needle at the gate, and Ward wanted a key and a needle at the river tower, and the needles wanted fine wire, many turns, and fine wire still came through the small plate for one man in Kaldo, at night, behind sacking, and for nobody else.

In January the stones stood still by day. It was the dead weeks, Harl said, when the town had ground what it was going to grind and the mill waited for the spring. So Tess took the drum by day, in the back room, with the machine standing silent beside her, and tried again.

It broke at the fourth hole. It broke at the fourth hole again. Harl came down the stair at the second break, as he came down at every sound in his mill now, and stood in the doorway with flour on his sleeves from nothing in particular, and watched her feed the third length in, and watched the drum turn, and watched the wire snatch and part.

"What's it doing?" he said.

Tess told him, in her flat quiet voice, without looking at him, coiling the broken end round her finger: the river pushed, the drum snatched, the wire went, and slowing it had never helped.

Harl looked at the drum. Then he went out without a word.

He came back with an old belt, black and supple and worn shiny on its inside face, nothing like the carpenter's stiff new leather, that had been hanging on a peg in the stone room since before Tess was born.

"Sack hoist," he said.

The sack hoist, it turned out, ran off a slack belt. When the miller wanted a sack lifted, he pulled a cord, and a lever pressed the slack belt tight against its pulley, and the hoist took the weight. If the sack caught on the trap on its way up, the belt slipped, and the pulley went on turning under it, and nothing broke, and nobody lost a hand. The pull was as hard as the lever pressed it, and not one hair harder.

"You don't lift a sack with a fist," Harl said. "You lift it with a pull that gives."

He put the old belt on the drum himself, slack, and hung a stone on the end of a lever to press it against the pulley, and moved the stone along the lever a hand's width at a time while Tess fed a length through the first hole, and watched the belt, and moved the stone again. When the wire went through the plate easily, the belt gripped and the drum turned. When the wire caught, the belt slipped on the pulley, a little, hissing softly, and the drum slowed, and the wire was held, and then it came through, and the belt gripped again.

The wire came through the first hole. The second. The third. Tess did not breathe through the fourth. It came through. It came through the fifth, and the sixth, and wound itself onto the drum as fine as a hair and unbroken, and Tess stood looking at it on the drum with her two hands at her sides.

Emeric had come in at some point during the fourth hole. He stood in the doorway beside Harl, watching the old black belt slip and catch, slip and catch, on the pulley. It was doing with a stone on a stick what his own hand had done at night since July: feeling for the wire, and giving when it caught.

Tess drew the second length. It came through. She drew the third.

Harl went back up the stair. He did not stay to see the fourth. He said, on the third step, to nobody, "It's only a sack hoist," and went on up.



Tess brought the coil up the hill at dusk, nine lengths drawn in an afternoon, and laid it on the dyehouse bench in front of him with its measure on the tag in knots.

"Nobody's nose bled," she said.

That evening Emeric wrote on the dyehouse wall.

He wrote it in chalk, above the bench, at the height of his own eyes, where Tess would have to look at it every day and where he would. He wrote it in the plainest letters he had, the ones off the school's sheets.

Every job my magic does, something else has to learn to do.

Then, under it, he wrote down every job his magic did. It was a longer list than he had expected. The fine wire. The zinc, seven hours of a held fire for every handful. The brazing. The needle of flame for annealing between the holes of the plate. The fire on the rock face at the mines, which the crew still would not light without him. The flash of the jars into the field, which was not magic, and which he wrote down anyway, crossed out, and then wrote down again, because nobody but him knew how to do it yet, and that was the same thing.

Tess read it over his shoulder. Then she took the chalk out of his hand, and went to the first line, the fine wire, and drew a line through it, and wrote beside it: Harl's belt. And the date.

Bram came in from the lane while she was doing it, to see why the lamp was still lit, and read the wall from the doorway with his arms folded. He read the list twice.

"Half of that's mine," he said.

"Which half?"

Bram came in and put his thick finger under the zinc, and then under the brazing, and then under the annealing.

"A fire that holds still for seven hours is a fire that's got its draft right and its fuel right and a man who knows what he's doing at the damper," he said. "I've got a wheel in bay three that blows all day without getting tired. I can blow a pipe off it as fine as you like. And annealing's annealing. I've been doing it in charcoal since before you were born." He took his finger away. "You never asked."

"No."

"You keep not asking." Bram looked at the wall again, and at Tess's line through the first job, and the date beside it. "Leave the mines. Them I don't want."

He went out.

Bram was quicker than Emeric expected. In the second week of January he ran a pipe off the water bellows in bay three, a long iron pipe from the wind box down to a nozzle no wider than a straw, with a tap on it, and set a fat-wicked oil lamp in front of the nozzle, and opened the tap. The lamp flame went long and thin and blue, and hissed like a cat. It was a jeweler's blowpipe with the river behind it instead of a man's breath, and the river did not run out of breath.

He brazed a test seam on a scrap of copper strip with it, a hand's length, while Emeric stood by and did not offer to help, and then another. Then he cut the strip open across the seams with a cold chisel and looked at the brass inside.

"It's not as neat as yours," he said.

It was sound all the way along, though, and he had done it with his own hands and the river and nobody's glyph. Tess drew a line through the brazing on the dyehouse wall that evening, and wrote beside it: Bram's pipe.



The house for the machine went up beside the mill in the last three weeks of January, a day at a time, whenever the frost let it.

The mason had been making gravestones since the granary, and said so, and came anyway. The stone came from the old quarry on the Milford road, the sharp-sanded one, on the barony's cart, and the lime from the kiln Emeric had lit for soap in his first summer. Lime mortar would not set in a frost; the mason would not lay a course on a morning when the puddles in the mill yard had skins on them, and on two mornings in three they did, so the walls rose on the mild days only and were covered over every night with straw and old sacks like a sick animal. The carpenter roofed it in the last week of the month, and roofed it in tile. Harl had walked round the walls when they were shoulder high and said one word, and the word was thatch, and the way he said it settled the matter. So the potter's red tiles went up, fired in the same kiln as the knobs on the line, laid on battens over oak. By the time it had a roof on it, everybody had stopped calling it the house for the machine and started calling it the powerhouse, and nobody could say afterward who had begun it.

They moved the machine into it on the twenty-ninth, by night, on rollers, out of the back-room door and across the yard and in. The belt came through a slot in the wall from the wheel's shaft. The door in the house's other wall opened onto the river and nothing else, and had no way through to the mill at all. Harl had asked for that as well.

On the first night in its own house the machine ran from dusk until the sky went grey, and the wire glowed on its board all night, a steady red, and nobody needed to be there.

Harl came anyway. He came at about ten, across the frozen yard in his old coat, with his stool in one hand and a bucket in the other, and went back for the second bucket, and set the stool down inside the door of the new house, between the two buckets, and sat on it.

Emeric was there because he could not stay away either. He did not say anything about the stool. They sat on either side of the door, the two of them, with the machine humming between them and the blue fur at the springs and the red wire on its board, and the sound of the wheel coming through the slot in the wall.

It was Harl who spoke, in the end.

"What does it grind?"

Emeric thought about how to answer that. There were several true answers, and most of them would have taken half the night.

"Nothing," he said. "It turns the river into something that goes down a wire. In a month, if Tess and Bram and the potter and I get it right, that something will light the walls."

Harl considered this.

"That wheel's turned every night of my life," he said, "for nobody. My father's wheel, and his father's. I used to lie up there and listen to it, after." He did not say after what. "Going round in the dark. Pulling on nothing."

He looked at the machine, and at the red wire.

"It's a better noise than nothing," he said.
