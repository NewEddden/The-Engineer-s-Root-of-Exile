Chapter 256 - Sparks

The tram left the South Gate at eight in the morning on the twenty-fourth, a Saturday, with forty-one people aboard who had paid nothing for it and a great many more running alongside who had paid less.

It was green, which was Abloh's doing, and nobody had argued with him. A long wooden body on a steel frame from Grayrock's wagon shop, with Ivo's glass down both sides and the gold rose on each end, two benches facing each other down its length and a brass rail along the ceiling for the ones who stood. It ran on rails let into the road flush with the setts, so that a cart could cross them and a horse would not catch a shoe. On its roof a long pole leaned back, with a small grooved brass wheel at its tip pressed up by a spring against a copper wire; and the wire ran away ahead of it on iron posts, out under the gate's arch and over the moat bridge, along the river road and over the town bridge to the works.

Under the floor, between the wheels, were two of Tess's motors. Nobody aboard could see them. Everybody aboard knew.

The stop was a stone platform under the gate's arch, as high as the car's floor, so that you walked onto the tram as you walked through a door. Tess had asked for that in March, in writing, in a line under the drawing: The step is where the floor is. Bram had read it and signed it and said nothing, and the masons had built the platform a hand higher than the drawing because Bram had told them to, and then cut it back to the drawing because Tess had come down and looked at it. So she went aboard on the motor, wheels down, at a walking pace, across the gap without a bump, and turned the chair in the space Abloh had left clear behind the front bench, and set its brake, and looked up at the wire through the roof glass.

Osric got on after her and stood. He did not put a hand on her chair. He had not put a hand on her chair in public for a year, and he had stopped noticing that he didn't, which Emeric thought was the best thing about him.

Emeric sat across from her, on the bench by the window, with his knees almost touching the chair's footplate, and looked at the people.

They were the works shift, most of them: the eight o'clock men with their bait tins and caps, who would have walked it in half an hour and had come up at seven to be sure of a place. Two market women with baskets of eggs on their knees, pushed aboard by the crowd and sitting very straight. A boy of about nine who had got aboard without anybody seeing him do it and was standing on the bench with his face to the glass. And an old man in a black coat who had looked at the tram for a long time and said loudly that he would walk, thank you, and was now standing at its door, waiting for it to go so that he could.

The motorman was a powerhouse hand called up for the day, in a clean shirt, standing on the front platform with one hand on a brass drum the size of a hatbox. It had a lid, and a handle on the lid, and the handle went round in notches. Tess had built it. He had practised on it every evening that week, after the shift, up and down three hundred yards of rail by the works with nobody aboard, and he had the face of a man who had practised a great deal and still did not quite believe the thing would go when it mattered.

He looked back down the car at Tess.

Tess nodded.

He turned the handle one notch, and the tram moved.

There was no noise to it. That was what the people aboard noticed first, Emeric saw; more than the moving. Everything in Kaldo that moved made noise, and the engines at the works made more than anything ever had. The tram made a hum under the floor that you felt in your feet more than heard, and a soft ticking from the roof where the little brass wheel ran along the wire. And it went. Nothing pulled it and nothing pushed it. It went out of the arch into the light, with the old man in the black coat walking beside its door, and then walking faster, and then stopping, with his hands at his sides, as the car drew away from him at the pace of a trotting horse.

One of the egg women said, "Oh," in a small voice, and held her basket tighter.

The boy on the bench whooped.

On the moat bridge a carter coming in with slate had his horse by the head already, and the horse rolled its eye at the green thing gliding toward it with no horse of its own and backed two steps and stood. The carter said something to the tram's windows as it went by, in the voice a man uses to another who has cut in front of him on a road; and one of the works men leaned out of the door and told him to get a wire.

Then the river road under the lime trees, the children running behind on the cobbles and falling back one by one as the motorman found his second notch, and his third.



Tess had said nothing since the gate.

She sat in her chair with her hands on its arms and her eyes going up to the roof glass and the wire, and down to the floor where the hum came up through the boards, and back up, and her lips moved very slightly now and then, which was her counting something. Osric stood beside her with one hand on the ceiling rail and looked out of the window at nothing, and let her.

They were halfway along the river road when she spoke.

"What if the trains were electric?"

Emeric had been waiting for it since March. He had not known which way it would come, but he had known it would come, the way you know a kettle will.

"Go on."

"Not the engines. The line." She lifted her chin at the roof. "A wire like this one, all the way. Grayrock, Milford. The station makes it, the wire carries it, the train takes it off the wire as it goes. You'd not want coal on the train. You'd not want water every forty miles. You'd not want a stoker." She frowned. "Odo would want a stoker. Odo would want a stoker if you took the fire away."

"Copper," said Emeric. "Seventy miles of it."

"I know." She dismissed seventy miles of copper with a turn of her hand. "That's money. That's Ferra." And then, in the same flat voice, as if it followed, which for her it did: "And then you'd not want the wheels."

Osric looked down at her.

"You can push with a magnet," said Tess. "Every relay on the line pushes with a magnet. A magnet'll lift, if it's strong enough, I've lifted the scrap tray off the floor with the big coil in the crane. So lift the train. Hang it on magnets over the rail and push it along with more of them. Nothing rubs. Nothing wears." She looked at Emeric. "And you'd not want stones for it. The stones go, in a dead zone. A magnet doesn't care."

Emeric laughed.

He had not meant to. It came up out of him all at once, out loud, in the middle of the tram, so that the egg women looked round and the boy on the bench looked down from the glass; and he put his hand over his face and went on laughing into it, helplessly, with his shoulders going.

"What," said Tess.

"Nothing."

"It's not nothing. You're laughing."

"At me." He took his hand down. "Not at you."

Because he had given her that. Not the magnets; she had got to the magnets herself, in the time it took to go half the river road. The other thing. Nine years of it: the dynamo house, built because the stones could stop and the wire wouldn't; the battery lamps in the hulls; the chair she was sitting in, with its motor under its stones, because he would not have her stranded in her own chair on the day the air went dead. He had said it to her in a hundred ways, plainly, as a rule of building, the way he said it to everybody. And she had taken it the way she took everything he gave her, and turned it over, and kept it; and here it was coming back to him on the river road in exactly his own voice.

"You've caught it off me," he said.

"Caught what?"

"Never mind. Yes. A magnet doesn't care."

"So why's it funny?"

"It isn't. It's the best idea anybody's had on this tram." He looked round the car, at the works men, at the eggs. "Admittedly against light competition."



"What else would it do," said Tess.

She had her book out. It lived in a pocket Abloh had sewn into the side of her chair, and it was the same kind of book as Ferra's, a stiff board cover and Kaldo's grey paper, and it was nearly full; she turned to a clean page with one hand and had a pencil out of her hair with the other before Emeric had decided how to answer.

"The wire," she said. "Into a house. We've got the lamps in. What else."

So he told her.

He told her the way you empty a pocket onto a table, quickly, without sorting, because she would sort it herself. Light in every room, not only the ones on the lane switch. Heat on a dial, so that a room was as warm as you said and no warmer; and cold the same way, a box that drew the heat out of whatever you put in it, so that milk kept a week in August and Imani's medicines kept a season. A tub that washed the clothes by itself. An iron that was hot without a fire. Something to sweep a floor with that sucked the dust up instead of pushing it about.

"Sucked it," said Osric.

"Like the forge's draught. Backwards."

Osric thought about it, and nodded slowly, as a man nods at a reasonable thing he is going to have to see before he believes.

A box that cooked your dinner with lightning, in a minute, without a pan getting hot.

"How?" said Tess.

"Later."

"That means you don't know."

"It means later." Machines that counted, with switches, the way her board did, only more of them and faster, until you could ask one a sum and it answered before you had finished asking. A voice, sent down a wire, as the telephone sent it now, but further; and then without the wire. A picture sent the same way. A play, a hundred miles off, on a pane in your own wall. Music kept in a box and let out when you wanted it.

"You'd not need a guitar," said Osric, who had heard about the cellar.

"You'd always need a guitar."

Carts that went without fire. Arms in the works that lifted and turned and welded all day without tiring, like Abel's arm, only fixed to a floor. A lamp that could see inside a body.

Tess's pencil stopped at that one.

"Imani's frame," she said. "The tank."

"Something like it. Not with stones."

She wrote it down. She wrote all of it down, in her square hand, one line each, with a dash of the pencil at the start of every line and no comment beside any of them, the way she logged a pressure test; and when he stopped she looked at the page, and then up at him, with an expression he had seen on her face in bay three when a weld came out of the fixture clean and there was nothing to do but grind it.

"Where's all that out of," she said. "One of Armand's?"

"Some of it."

"The cooking one's not out of anything. You made that up."

"I may have."

"Hm." She did not cross it out. She drew a small ring beside it instead, which in Tess's book meant prove it, and turned the page.

He watched her do it, and thought, without meaning to, of a man in the other life, in the old books: a tall thin man who had lit a city and dreamed of lighting the whole world without wires, and had fed pigeons in a hotel room at the end, alone, with his notebooks. There had been a good deal of that man in Tess for years. Emeric looked at Osric, standing over her chair with his eyes on the river, who had not followed one line of the list and did not need to, who would carry her drafting board up four flights of stairs for the rest of his life and think it nothing; and thought that she was luckier than the tall thin man had been, and did not know it, and that it was not his to tell her.

"How do you push it," said Tess, "without wheels to turn?"

"Hm?"

"The floating train." She tapped the page. "A motor turns. A wheel turns. Mine are round. If there's no wheel, what does the motor push on?"

He held out his hand for the pencil, and she gave it to him, and the book.

He drew her a motor. Not one of hers, which he would have had to draw well for her to accept it; a plain one, a ring of coils round a drum, the way a child might draw a wheel. Then he drew a line across the ring at one side, and drew it again beside, opened out: the ring cut through at one place and laid out flat along the page, so that the coils that had stood round the drum in a circle now lay in a row, one after another, like the sleepers of a line.

"Cut it open," he said. "Along one side. Lay it out flat, along the track. The coils that went round the drum go along the rail. Fire them one after another, down the row, the way you fire them round a ring. And the drum..." He drew a small box sitting on the row. "Isn't a drum. It's the cart. It gets pulled along the row the way the drum gets pulled round the ring, and it never gets to the end of it, because the row goes on as far as you lay it."

Tess looked at it.

She looked at it for a long time, while the tram took the curve onto the town bridge and the river opened under the windows on both sides, wide and brown and slow with summer.

"That's a linear motor," said Emeric.

"That's a description."

"It's both."

She took the book back, and the pencil, and wrote LINEAR under the drawing, and then looked at the word as if it had done something to her personally.

"It's a motor that's been cut open," she said.

"Cut open and laid flat."

"Somebody should have done that years ago." She shut the book. "I'll have one by tonight."



On the bridge, everybody looked downriver.

The column had gone up that week on the far bank, a quarter mile below the works, outside the old fence, where the Siting Code put anything that might burn: an iron pipe as thick as an oak, bricked to half its height and scaffolded to the top, with smaller pipes coming off its side at three heights like the branches of a stripped tree. At its foot stood a brick furnace, built and cold, with a heap of Grayrock coal beside it under a sheet; and on a stool in front of it, in the sun, with his coat off, sat one of Armand's fire students with his hands held out flat toward the iron, perfectly still, and the air over his hands shaking as it shakes over a road at noon.

It was his fourth day of holding the heat by hand, eight hours on and eight off with the other, while the stokers learned the furnace beside him. The furnace was the recipe; the man on the stool was the first week, and at the end of it the coal would go in. And the first thing off the column would not be lamp oil or anything that could be sold, but a thick clear oil from the heavy end for the K Discovery's bearings, because tallow went hard as soap at four miles and her winch had already stuck on it once.

And then the works gates, open, and in the yard Wat, the new tank engine, off the floor that week, shunting coal trucks up to the boiler house with her name still raw in white paint on her side; and the platform at the works end, stone, level with the floor, and the motorman bringing his handle back notch by notch with his tongue between his teeth until the car stopped at the platform's edge without a jolt.

Nobody went in to work. Not for a while.

The egg women stayed in their seats.

"It goes back," said Tess, to them, from her chair. "In a quarter of an hour. It goes back and forth all day."

The older one looked at her, and at her chair, and at the platform where it met the floor, level, without a step.

"I'll wait, then," she said, and settled the basket on her knees.



She had one by four o'clock.

Bay three was Bram's by every paper in the counting room and Tess's by every other measure, and she had the end of it nearest the river doors, where the light came in off the water: three benches in a horseshoe, a rack of coils wound and unwound on the wall, the board from March with its switches and lamps, a slate with a truth table on it that nobody had been allowed to wipe since March, and, on a shelf above the slate, the relay with the scratch on its base.

On the middle bench, a plank of deal eight feet long with two strips of iron screwed down it for rails. Between the rails, eleven coils, each wound on a little iron spool from the crane's spares and set into the plank on its end, an inch apart, all the way down. A wire from each to a brass drum at the plank's head, so that when you turned the drum's crank its studs fired the coils one after another down the row. And sitting on the rails, on four brass wheels, a cart the size of a loaf, with a plate of soft iron under its belly a finger's width above the spools.

"Wheels," said Emeric.

"Wheels first." She did not look up. "Push first. Float after. One thing at a time."

She turned the crank.

The first coil caught the iron plate and pulled it. Then the second, as the drum came round, a little ahead; then the third. The cart went down the plank. Not smoothly: in a row of small hops, a pull and a coast and a pull, like a man being shoved down a corridor by a crowd; but it went, all the way down the eight feet with nobody touching it, and stopped against the block at the end.

Tess sat back.

Bram had come down from the other end of the bay to watch, wiping his hands, and he stood at her shoulder and looked at the cart against its block.

"It's lumpy," he said.

"Eleven coils. It wants a hundred. Smaller. Closer."

"It wants a hundred," said Bram, "and you'll wind them."

"Isa'd wind them."

"Isa's in the scanner shop till Monday." Bram looked at the cart a while longer. "It's lumpy," he said again, quite differently, and went back to his end of the bay, and Emeric heard him, a minute later, tell one of the apprentices that the girl at the river end had made a cart walk down a plank on its own by magnets and he wanted the floor swept round her bench before she made it do anything else.

Then the float.

She had been building it at the other bench all the while, because one thing at a time with Tess meant two. A frame like a gallows, a hand high, over a short length of iron rail. In the crossbar of the frame, pointing down, the big coil from the crane, the one that had lifted the scrap tray. Under it, on the rail, a bar of iron the length of her forearm with a hook in its middle.

"If it pulls the bar up," said Tess, "it'll float under it."

"It'll pull the bar up."

"And then?"

"Try it."

She gave him a look that said she had not been going to do anything else, and threw the switch.

The bar came off the rail and went straight up and hit the coil with a clang that turned every head in bay three, and stuck there, flat against the iron face of the coil, and stayed.

She switched it off. The bar fell back onto the rail.

She turned the current down, through the rheostat, a quarter. Switched on. Nothing; the bar sat on the rail. Up a little. Nothing. Up a hair. The bar rose an inch and hung there for half a breath, trembling, and then went up the rest of the way and clanged against the coil.

"Hm," said Tess.

She worked at it for an hour. Emeric sat on Bram's stool and let her. She tried a spring on the hook to hold the bar down against the pull, and the bar sat on its spring and buzzed. She tried a second coil underneath, pulling down against the first, and the bar shot to whichever was nearer, every time, however she set them. She tried it with the rheostat in her hand, turning it as she watched, and once she held it a finger's width under the coil for the count of three, with her tongue between her teeth and her whole face gone to the bar; and then it was gone, up, clang.

"You can't," said Emeric, when she put the rheostat down at last. "Not by itself. Not with any set of magnets you can lay out, however you lay them. Something's always nearer and it always wins."

"I held it."

"You held it. Your hand held it. You watched the gap and changed the pull every time it moved." He nodded at the bar. "That's what it wants. Something watching the gap, and changing the current every time the gap changes. Too far: more. Too near: less. Over and over. Not three times a breath. Thousands of times. Every second."

Tess looked at the relay on the shelf over the slate.

"Twenty," she said. "On a good day. A relay. Twenty clicks a second before the arm can't get back fast enough." She looked at the bar on the rail. "Thousands."

"Thousands."

"Then it wants a faster switch."

"It wants a much faster switch."

"There isn't one."

He didn't answer that.

She looked at his face. Then she rubbed out the bottom corner of the slate with the side of her hand, carefully, so as not to touch the truth table, and wrote FASTER there, and drew a line under it, and then another line under that, and sat back in her chair and looked at the word with her arms folded, the way Bram looked at a casting he had not decided about yet.

"I'll float the cart," she said, "when I've got one."

"You will."

"I'm not asking. I'm telling you." She unfolded her arms. "Go away. You're in my light."



Hull two sat on her cradle on the slip on Sunday morning with her stone cases open for checking, and Ned Tallis sat on an upturned bucket under her bow in the sun with a pipe he was not smoking, and looked at them.

"She came down whole," said Emeric.

"She came down whole." Ned took the pipe out of his mouth and looked into its bowl. "And then she sat. In a hayfield, with her leg folded under her, and Garrick and me sat on her while Cade and the boy walked to the wire. For the air to come back." He put the pipe back. "Hours, before Kaldo knew. Kaldo had us down in the book as over the wood, and we were in a field with a hole in the sky where the stones should've been, and there wasn't a thing I could do from where she lay to tell anybody."

"Two of you walked to the wire."

"In the end." Ned looked up at the hull's bow over his head. "If she'd come down in the sea, there'd have been no wire to walk to. If she'd come down at night over the Isle, with a patrol on the shore." He did not finish it. He had come down in the sea once already that summer, in a different ship, with two burned men, and watched a fishing boat turn toward him. "A ship that can't shout," said Ned, "is a ship that goes quiet. And then nobody knows where to look."

Emeric stood under the bow with his hands in his pockets.

He had known it for a fortnight. He had known it for years, if he was honest, the way you know a thing you have been putting off for good reasons: the line first, the telephone, the wire down every road, all of which worked and all of which stopped at the end of the wire. A ship was not at the end of anything.

"Give me a week," he said.

Ned looked at him from his bucket.

"Not me," said Emeric. "Give me a week of somebody else."



Ambrose Lark had been in and out of the works for seven years, up the lightning rods and along the K Discovery's frames with his strap, and he still sat on Harl's stool in the powerhouse now and then for no reason he would give; but he had never been to Tess's benches. Tess's benches were for things that moved, and Ambrose's whole life was things that didn't stay.

He came in behind Emeric at two in the afternoon with his hands behind his back, the way a large man walks into a room full of glass, and stopped.

The cart was on its plank. The bar was on its rail under the gallows frame. And on the board from March there was a new thing since the spring: a row of four lamps in porcelain cups, a pair of relays wired to hold each other under each, and a telegraph key on a block; and as Ambrose stood there Tess, without looking up, pressed the key, once, twice, three times, and the four lamps went lit, lit, dark, dark.

"Three," said Tess, to the board.

Ambrose made a noise.

"Four lamps," said Tess. "They count to fifteen. I'm not explaining it twice, so you'll have to stand there till you see it."

He stood there. He stood there while she pressed the key four more times, and the lamps went through their changes, and Emeric watched his face go through the same changes they did: the frown, and the frown deepening, and the moment at seven, lit, lit, lit, dark, when the thing came apart in his head and went back together, and his mouth opened and stayed open.

"They remember," said Ambrose.

"Since June."

"They remember. A switch that remembers. I've had switches thrown in my face by storms for thirty years and never one that..." He bent over the board without touching it, hands still behind his back, the way a man bends over a pool to look at a fish. "Who did this?"

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because a hand has to know what it was doing a moment ago," said Tess, "and so does a door, and so does a signal, and nothing on the line did." She looked at Emeric. "What's he here for?"

So Emeric told her: the slip, and Ned on his bucket, and hull two in a hayfield with no way on earth to say she was there. A ship that couldn't shout. Then he said the one thing he had decided on the way down, and no more.

"A spark talks to everything," he said. "Build something that listens."

Then he turned round and walked out of bay three.

He did it before he could say anything else, because the rest of it was standing in his mouth: the coil, the gap, the wire up a pole, the little glass tube, laid out the way they had been laid out in a book he had read at a kitchen table in another life, about an Italian and his boxes on a hill. One more word and she would have it from him and not from herself, and Ambrose would have it from neither of them.

He was at the river doors when she called after him.

"You had the face."

He stopped.

"The one you have," said Tess, from her chair at the board, not loudly, "when you know the end of it. You had it on the tram. You've got it now. You had it at the March board." She turned back to Ambrose. "Go on, then. Go. He's no use to us with that face."

He went.

The doors had not swung shut behind him before Ambrose started to talk.



He heard the rest of it from Osric that night, at the long table, and from Bram in the morning, and from Tess not at all, because Tess did not tell a thing until it worked.

Ambrose had talked for an hour, and Tess had let him, with a slate in her lap and the chalk moving now and then. He had talked about the cat's back, and the kite, and the bolt that had wanted the ground in the third year; and somewhere in the second half hour he had come to the line room. In a storm, any storm, for seven years, the sounders in the gate tower had twitched. Not when the lightning struck the line; the arrester took that. When it struck somewhere else, a mile off, three miles, a tree on the far bank, the bell tower at Low Bank. The sounder would give a little click all on its own, a breath before the thunder, with nothing touching the wire at all.

"The spark talks to the wire," Ambrose had said, "from three miles off. Through nothing. Through the air." And then, according to Osric, who had come in with the basket at the end of it and stood in the door with a loaf, he had stopped talking altogether, which was the most frightening thing Osric had ever seen him do.

Tess had said, into the quiet, that there was a joint on the Grayrock line.

A twisted joint at the fourth pole past the ford, never soldered because she had never got the pole climbed on a dry day; and it read bad, all week, sulky, the current dragging through it like a cart through mud. Then there would be lightning anywhere about, over the hills or down the river, and the joint would read good. Clean, like a soldered one. And it stayed good for days, until somebody kicked the pole or the wind shook it; and then it read bad again.

"Loose bits of metal," Tess had said, "touching, not touching. And when a spark goes by in the air somewhere, they cling."

Ambrose had looked at her.

"Then make it all joint," he had said.



By Monday noon there were two things on the river end bench that had not been there on Saturday, and Bram had moved his own lunch outside again.

The first was Ambrose's. It stood at one end of the bench on a slab of slate: a coil the size of a churn, wound in Abloh's waxed silk round a core of iron rods, with a little trembling hammer at one end like a door bell's that made and broke the battery's current so fast that it sang; and on top of the coil, on two brass pillars, two brass balls the size of plums with a gap between them the width of a fingernail. When Ambrose pressed the key, the hammer sang and the gap filled with fire, a stream of blue-white sparks so loud and bright that the apprentices at Bram's end put their hands over their ears. From one ball a wire ran up the outside of the river doors to the top of the pole Bram used for the works' noon flag. From the other a wire ran down the bank to a copper plate on the river bottom: Ambrose's own trick from the arrester, the ground given an easier road.

"A spark gap," said Ambrose to everybody, happily. "It's the arrester. It's my arrester. It's the thing I built to throw the lightning away, and now I'm throwing it on purpose. Shouting." He pressed the key again, and the gap roared. "In every direction at once. Everywhere. At everything. It can't help it."

The second thing was Tess's, and it was so small that Emeric, coming in at noon, almost did not see it.

A glass tube, Ivo's, no thicker than a pencil and no longer than a finger, lying in two clips on a block of wood. In each end a plug of silver, the plugs so close they nearly met; and in the hair's gap between them, a pinch of filings, grey and bright. Nickel and silver, she said, filed by hand off a scrap of the incubator's ingot and a broken spoon of Maren's, because the grinder's were too coarse and iron rusted. From one plug a wire went up a second pole by the boiler house at the far end of the works yard, a hundred yards off. From the other it went through a cell from the battery room and a relay, and on the relay's contacts, a bell.

"Press it," said Tess to Ambrose.

Ambrose pressed his key.

The gap roared at the river doors, and at the boiler house end of the yard the bell rang.

And went on ringing. Ambrose took his finger off the key. The gap went quiet. The bell rang on, steadily, cheerfully, at the far end of the yard, with nobody touching anything, and every man in the works looked round at it.

"It heard," said Tess, flatly, "and now it's deaf. They've clung, and they won't let go." She wheeled across the yard to it, wheels down, at the motor's best pace, with Ambrose loping beside her and Emeric following, and when she got there she reached up and tapped the glass tube on its block, once, with her fingernail.

The bell stopped.

"There," she said. "They want knocking loose. Every time." She looked at the bell. Then at its hammer, which was still twitching on its spring. Then at the tube, on its block, an inch from the hammer's reach.

It took her twenty minutes. She moved the tube on its block until it lay under the bell's hammer, and bent the hammer's arm with pliers so that on its back stroke it fell on the glass; so that every time the filings clung and the bell rang, the bell's own hammer knocked them loose, and the tube was ready for the next. When Ambrose pressed his key now, the bell gave a single ding and a tick, and stopped the moment he let go.

"It hears," said Ambrose, "and then it shakes its head."

"It's a coherer," said Emeric. "The filings cohere. Stick together."

Tess looked at him.

"That's a description."

"It's both."

"You keep saying that," said Tess, and wrote COHERER on the block with a pencil, under the tube, in capitals, as if pinning it down.



They tried the distance after that, the coherer and its bell carried down the works yard, then out along the river road on a handcart with its pole lashed upright, Tess beside it on the motor and a boy running messages back. It rang at the gate. It rang, faint and doubtful, at the lime trees; and stopped at the bridge's foot.

Emeric stood in the works yard with nothing to do, which he did badly, while the bell went back to the boiler house end for the night and the others argued about poles; and he looked at the tube on its block a hundred yards off, and at his own hand, and thought: a burst. A burst is never a current. Every sum he had made at the arc furnace in February had come to that; the furnace had done all day what his hand could not do for a breath. But a coherer did not want a current. It wanted a burst. It wanted exactly the one thing his hand could do.

He lifted his right hand, palm out, toward nothing, toward the middle of the empty yard; and found it in himself, the thing he did worst, and let it go.

A crack, like a stick broken over a knee. A white thread a yard long in the air in front of his fingers, there and gone. Every man in the yard flinched. A gull went up off the boiler house roof.

At the far end of the yard the bell went ding, and tick.

Ambrose took his finger off the key and stared at him.

Emeric looked at his fingers. They were tingling to the elbow and the back of his neck had gone wet.

"It heard me," he said, and could not keep the grin off his face. "Across the yard. By hand."

"It did," said Tess, from the chair, where she had come back up the yard and was sitting with her hands folded on her book. "Once. And you're sweating." She nodded down the bench at the coil on its slate, at Ambrose's brass key, at the balls on their pillars with their fingernail's width of gap. "His does it every time he presses that. Without him. He could be in bed."

"I could be in bed," Ambrose agreed, delighted. "I'd much rather be in bed. Highness, you throw a spark like a man throwing a shoe at a cat."

"Don't," said Tess.

"It's a compliment. I've thrown shoes at cats. Nobody ever hit one."

Emeric laughed, and wiped the back of his neck with his hand, and went over to look at the coil on its slate; at the hammer that sang, and the gap that roared every time a finger came down on the brass, all day and all night if anybody wanted it, without a mage in the county.

"Every time," he said.

"Every time," said Tess. "Now get out of my yard, the both of you. I want a taller pole."



The first message was to come from the ship. That had been Fletcher's idea, on Monday evening, standing in the K Discovery's shed with his arms folded while Ambrose crawled about inside her hull for the second time in his life with a coil of wire, undoing what he had done the first time.

"I strapped every bolt in her to that staff," Ambrose had said, from somewhere under the saloon floor, "so that if the sky ever came for her it would go round her and out. Seven years ago. Now I'm running a wire up the staff to let the sky in on purpose." His head came up through the hatch. "Through a porcelain bell. Insulated. Nobody touch it."

The second set, the ship's, went into the space beside the battery lamps under the saloon floor, where the cells were already: a smaller coil, smaller balls, a key on the saloon table and a second coherer in its clips with its own bell beside it. The staff at her stern was her pole. The whole of her bonded hull, every bolt and bracket of it, was her copper plate in the river.

"If you get the ship to say it," Fletcher had said to Tess, "from up there, from the Band, from four mile up and twenty mile out, then that's the thing done. Saying it across a yard's not a thing done. Anybody can shout across a yard."

So on Tuesday night, the twenty-seventh, at a quarter to ten, she sat at the river end of bay three with every lamp in the shop out but the one over her bench, and waited for the K Discovery to shout from the sky.

It was hot. The river was low and brown and smelling of itself, and the river doors stood open on the dark and the frogs, and the works was quiet but for the engine house going on across the yard. Ambrose sat at the coil with his finger over the key and his knee going up and down. The gate tower's night clerk, the fastest hand in Kaldo, sat on Bram's stool with a board on his knee and three sharpened pencils. Osric sat on an upturned crate by the doors with the basket on his knees, because it was past ten and Maren had sent it anyway.

And on the board beside the coherer, wired to the coherer's relay, Tess's counter.

It was her four lamps from June, with their pairs of relays that held each other and counted to fifteen; and she had put one more relay in front of them, a slow one, its core sleeved in a thick copper tube so that when its current stopped it let go late, the way a heavy door takes its time to swing shut. A letter came in clicks with short gaps, and the slow relay held through them. A word ended in a long gap, and the slow relay dropped, and pressed the counter's key once. So the lamps would count the words.

She had not told anybody what it was for. She had told Osric it was to keep the night clerk honest, which had made him laugh.

The ship had gone up at dusk from the slip, Fletcher at the bow, Old Edrin in the casting seat, and Emeric at the saloon table with the second key. They had watched her go up dark, without a lamp, a long black shape against the last green of the sky over the hills, smaller and smaller, until there was only the place where she had been; and then nothing, for two hours, but the stars, and the frogs, and Ambrose's knee.

The noon flag pole outside the doors now stood a good deal taller than the noon flag needed. Bram had added twenty feet to it on Monday night without being asked, with a splice and two iron bands, and had said, when Tess thanked him, that he wanted his flag where the gulls couldn't reach it.

At ten by the engine house bell, Ambrose pressed the key.

He sent it the way they had agreed, slowly, in the clerks' code, the letters as the gate tower sent them: KALDO TO K DISCOVERY. ANSWER. The gap roared and stopped and roared, and the blue light jumped and filled the bench end, and Tess sat with her hands in her lap and watched the dark outside the river doors as if she could see the sound go up into it.

Ambrose took his finger off the key.

The gap went quiet.

Nothing.

The frogs. The engine house. Osric, very slowly, taking the cloth off the basket, and stopping, and putting it back.

Nothing.

Tess counted. She did not mean to; she counted the way she breathed. At forty she began to think about the staff's insulator and whether Ambrose's porcelain bell had cracked in the cold up there. At sixty she began to think about the battery under the saloon floor. At seventy she had decided it was the staff, and was working out how long it would take to get the ship down and the porcelain off and a new one on, and whether Ivo had another.

At eighty-one the coherer's bell went ding, and tick.

Then again. Ding, tick. Ding-ding, tick. A long one; a short. The clerk's pencil was moving before she had looked round, the board on his knee, his lips moving with the letters; and the relays on her board under the four lamps were moving too, clacking, the slow one with its copper sleeve holding and holding through the short gaps and dropping at the long one; and the first lamp came on.

One.

Ambrose was standing up. She had not seen him stand. Osric had put the basket down on the floor.

The first lamp went out and the second came on. Two. Both: three. The bell went on ticking at the far end of its wire in the dark, and the clerk wrote, and wrote, faster than she could have read it off the sounder herself, and she did not look at what he wrote. She looked at the lamps.

Dark, lit, lit, dark. Six.

Then a long gap. A very long one. The slow relay had dropped and stayed dropped. The bell was quiet.

"Six," said Tess.

The clerk looked up from his board. He looked at her, and at the lamps, and back at his board, and moved his pencil down what he had written, word by word, with his lips.

"Six," he said.

He read it out. He read it in the gate tower's voice, the flat careful one they all used, the way he would have read out a timber price from Grayrock, which made it worse.

"Heard you. Four miles. Cold. E."

Nobody said anything.

Then Ambrose Lark walked out through the river doors into the dark of the works yard, in his good coat, and sat down on the ground at the foot of Bram's pole with his back against it and his legs out in front of him, and began to laugh. He laughed the way he had laughed in the mud of the mill yard seven years before, with his head back, until he had no breath left to do it with.

"He does that," said Tess, to Osric.

"I know." Osric was looking at the four lamps on the board. Dark, lit, lit, dark. He did not know how to read them; she had never taught him, and he had never asked, and he did not ask now. He looked at them for a while. Then he looked at her. "You knew it was six."

"The lamps knew."

"Before he'd finished writing."

"That's what they're for," said Tess. Her hands, in her lap, were not quite steady, and she put them flat on the arms of the chair until they were.



Ferra and Imani came down at half past ten.

They came in through the river doors past Ambrose, who was still sitting at the foot of the pole and lifted his hand to them as they passed, and they were dressed for the house and not the works, and Ferra had her book under her arm, and they were both of them looking extremely calm in a way Tess did not trust at all.

"We heard," said Ferra. "From the gate. Ambrose's boy ran it up the hill before your clerk was off his stool." She looked at the board. At the coil, and the balls, and the key. "That's the key."

"That's Ambrose's key."

"Ambrose is sitting in the yard."

Tess looked at her. Then at Imani, who was looking at the coherer in its clips with the expression she wore over a slide.

"You can't send," said Tess. "You don't know the code."

"We learned it," said Imani.

"When?"

"Today." Imani took her eyes off the tube. "The gate tower. Ferra had it by noon. It took me till four." She said it as if it were a small fault of hers she had decided to live with. "Ferra kept a column."

"It's a column," said Ferra. "Letters down one side, what they sound like down the other." She put her book down on the bench, open, but not at that page; Tess saw a page of letters in groups of five, in Ferra's square capitals, and a ruled line, and Imani's rounder hand underneath it. Ferra turned it face down before Tess could read any more. "May we?"

Tess looked at the clerk. The clerk looked at the ceiling.

"Don't break it," said Tess.

Ferra sat down at the key.

She sent the way she wrote, square and even and without hurry, every gap the same, and Tess, listening to the gap roar and stop, could follow the letters, and the letters made no sense. Five of them, and a gap. Five more. Five more. Not a word in it that anybody knew; the clerk was taking it down out of habit and frowning harder at every group. Tess had cleared the lamps with the reset; they counted the gaps between the fives as words and stepped up, solemnly, through four and five and six. At the end, after a long gap, two letters on their own.

D. F.

Ferra got up. Imani sat down.

Imani's sending was nothing like Ferra's. It was quick, and close, and the gaps were short, and once she sent a letter wrong and said a word under her breath that Tess had never heard her say outside the ward, and sent it again. Five, gap. Five, gap. Fewer of them than Ferra's. And at the end, after the long gap, two letters on their own.

D. I.

Imani took her hand off the key.

Ambrose had come back in from the yard and was standing in the river doors with grass on his coat, looking from one of them to the other with enormous interest. Osric was eating an apple and looking at nobody.

The bell went.

The ship was answering. It answered in fives too, quick and steady, more of them than either; and the clerk wrote it down letter by letter with his lips moving and his face getting longer, and the lamps stepped up, and up, and ran out of lamps at fifteen and went dark all together and began again; and at the end, after a long gap, one letter.

E.

The clerk put his pencil down.

"I can't read this," he said, to Tess, as if it were her fault.

"Nobody can read it," said Tess. "That's the point of it." She looked at Ferra. "That's a cipher."

"It is," said Ferra.

"Whose?"

Ferra picked her book up off the bench and closed it.

Tess turned in her chair to the clerk's board, and took it off his knee, and looked at what he had written: three blocks of nonsense in fives, each with its little signature at the bottom. She knew F. She knew I. Everybody in Kaldo knew E. She did not know D.

"What's D?"

Nobody said anything.

"There's nobody in Kaldo who signs D," said Tess. "Not on the line. Not on any book I've seen. Ottoline doesn't send. Declan doesn't write anything down." She looked at Imani. "D-F. D-I. It's the same D. It's both of you. What is it?"

"Not telling you," said Imani, pleasantly.

"It's a station," said Tess. "If it's a station I log it. I've got to log it as something."

"Log it as traffic," said Ferra.

And then, because Tess was still looking at her, and was going to go on looking at her for as long as it took, and both of them knew it, Ferra did something Tess had never once seen her do in nine years, at any table, about anything. She lost the word she had been going to say, and shut her book on it, and turned round, and walked out of the river doors into the dark with her book under her arm. Imani went after her, laughing before she reached the doors, and Tess heard the two of them go away up the works yard in the warm dark, their voices low and then one of them giving way altogether.

Ambrose watched them go.

"Do you know," he said thoughtfully, "a spark talks to everything."

"I know," said Tess.

"Everything. In every direction. That's what it does; it can't help it. If somebody had a tube of filings and a bell in Lisle tonight, a pole and a plate, he'd have heard all of that." He nodded at the clerk's board. "Every word. And not understood one of them."

Tess looked at the board. At the three blocks of fives. At the two women gone up the yard who had learned a code that morning and come down at half past ten with a cipher already made, as if they had known before anybody else in the shop what the thing would be for.

"No," she said slowly. "Well. They would."

She took the log book off its hook, and opened it at the day, and wrote in it under the six words from the ship, in her square hand: Ten forty. Traffic. Two stations, signed D.F. and D.I. Answer from the ship, signed E. Unreadable. She looked at it. Then, after a moment, she drew a small ring beside the D's, which in her book meant prove it.

Osric gave her the heel of the loaf.



Emeric came down to bay three at eight on the Wednesday morning with Bram's noon flag going up the new length of pole outside, very high, with the gulls watching it.

He had slept four hours. The ship had come down at two, dark, onto the slip, and Fletcher had shaken his hand on the deck without a word, and he had walked up through the sleeping town with his own sheet of fives folded in his coat, which he had read in the saloon at four miles with the key still warm under his hand and Old Edrin politely looking at the ceiling, and which he read again by a candle on the edge of his bed, because he wanted to.

Ferra's had said: Your side of the bed is down eleven inches of blanket. I have measured. Bring them back. D.F.

Imani's had said: Count your pulse when you land and bring me the number. If you round it down I'll know. D.I.

He had answered both in one, in Ferra's cipher, which was in his coat because Ferra had put it there on Tuesday at noon while she did up his collar. What he had answered was nobody's business, and it had taken him most of the way down to the Band's foot, because the cipher was very good and he was slow at it.

Tess was at the board. She had the log book open on her knee and she held it out to him without saying good morning.

He took it, and read her entry under the six words from the ship, and the small ring beside the D's, and gave it back.

"What's D?" said Tess.

Emeric became very interested in the coherer in its clips.

"Saban came down at seven," said Tess. "He'd heard from the gate tower clerk there was a station on the air at night that nobody could read. He wanted to know whose, and could he have one." She shut the log. "I said ask Ferra. He said he had. He said she told him to make his own."

"She would."

"What's D?"

"I'll tell you," said Emeric, "when you've floated the cart."

Tess looked at him for a long time over the log book with her mouth very straight.

"That's not fair," she said. "That's a very long time."

"It's an incentive."

"It's blackmail." But she put the log book back on its hook, and he saw her look up, past it, at the corner of the slate where FASTER sat under its two lines, as if the word had moved in the night.

He had a thing in his coat pocket. He had had it there since Saturday evening, since the tram, since the bar went up and clanged against the coil; he had gone up to the keep that night and taken it off the shelf in his room where it had been sitting since the winter, and he had not decided until now, on her stool, whether to give it to her this year or next.

He took it out and put it on the bench beside her hand.

A lamp bulb, one of Ivo's, burned out, its filament broken and sagging in its glass, the inside of the glass gone faintly grey with what the filament had thrown off over the hours it had lived. Except that between the two wires that came up through its foot to hold the filament there was a third: a bare wire with a little flat plate on its end, standing up inside the bulb beside the dead filament, touching nothing. He had had Ivo make it so in January, without saying what for, and had run it on his own desk until it died.

Tess picked it up. She turned it in the light from the river doors, and looked at the third wire, and at the plate on its end, and at the grey on the glass.

"It's dead," she said.

"Yes."

"It's got three wires."

"Three."

"What's the third one for?"

He got up off her stool and went to the river doors, and stopped in them, and looked back: at the board with its four lamps and its tube of filings with a pencilled word under it; at the cart on its plank and the bar under its gallows; at a woman of twenty-five in a chair with chalk on her sleeve turning a dead lamp over in her fingers in the morning light, and frowning at it, the way she had frowned at a tuyere once at fifteen as if she had never wondered where the air went in.

"That's the face," said Tess, without looking up.

"It is."

"Go away, then."

He went. Behind him, before the doors had swung shut, he heard her put the bulb down on the shelf over the slate, beside the relay with the scratch on its base, under the word FASTER; carefully, the way you put down a thing you are going to come back to.
