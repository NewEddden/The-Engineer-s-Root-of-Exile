Chapter 337 - Counted Home

They came down over the river on the sixteenth of February in the order they had gone up, a little after two in the afternoon, under a sky the colour of a pewter plate.

The escorts first, three and four, with the Isle's salt still grey on their plates; hull two with her starboard rail patched in raw steel nobody had had time to paint; the K Endeavor and hull four, heavy with their racks; the K Discovery, with the king's standard rolled on its staff because the king had asked for it rolled. And last, because she would not hurry for anybody, the K Amara, her shadow going ahead of her over the thistle field and the coal station's cold stacks.

Emeric was in her tower with his left arm out of its sling. Imani had taken the stitches out the evening before, twenty-two of them, into a saucer, and looked at the pink seam they left with the face she kept for work she could find no fault in and would not say so. The arm ached in the cold. He kept it on the rail, where she could see he was not using it for anything.

Behind the fleet, and a long way south of it, the rest of the war went the other way.

The four taken hulls, white, with their own stones boxed in iron in their holds so that nothing the pope had written could wake on the way: the great hull out of the stubble field, long as a church, and the three small ones behind her on steel cable, all four carried on Kaldo's lift frames lashed through their decks. Escort one went with them, and behind her on a tow, riding on the lift stones the anchor teams had bolted to her corners to raise her off the river bottom in a January snowstorm, with the mud dried grey along her plates and her own stern stations dark, escort two. Osric's people had done the raising, and Selby had stood on the bank and watched them do it and said nothing at all until she broke the surface, and then, "Well, she was always a mucky ship," and gone to find somebody to give him a drink.

The tow went down the marsh coast, low and away from every road, toward South Sentinel and the Cradle. Church hulls on the slips by the lake would have been the loudest thing in the kingdom by Sunday. On the Cradle, behind the mountains, they would be work.

He looked down at the town instead, and found that he had to hold the rail a little harder.



The quay had been full since half past eleven, when somebody on the tram saw the first speck come up over the Wayford road and told everybody on the tram. It stood behind the rope as it had stood in November, in its coats and shawls and joggers, with its breath going up white: wives and mothers, children on shoulders, men off the shift with the works' black still in the creases of their hands. Somebody had carried down a can of the coffee from under the South Gate's arch, which smelled like a burned fence across the whole yard, and people were drinking it out of tin cups anyway, pulling faces. Ivo had the motion camera on its legs at the quay's edge, cranking, two turns a second, and Merrin beside him had the sound camera's horn up on its pole.

And at the end of the quay by the crane's foot, in her grey coat with the collar turned up, with the manifests on a board under her arm and a pen through the knot of her hair and another in her hand, Ferra. He could see nothing of her from a thousand feet but the copper of her hair against all that grey, the size of a coin.

It took her until the light went.

The escorts' crews came off first, and the town found them before Ferra did; there was no keeping a mother behind a rope. So she let the rope go and stood at the crane's foot and read. Every name, in her flat clear voice, in order; and the name said "Here," or stepped past her, or was pointed at by three people at once, and she ticked it and read the next. When somebody shouted about a man not off yet she said, without looking up, "Hull four. The second launch," and went on.

Rylan's detachment came off escort four at about half past three, burned faces and new pink skin on the backs of hands, a garrison left behind on the wells; very quiet coming down. Escort four's sheet was the longest, and Ferra read it to the foot, and every name on it answered; and when she reached the two lines she had ruled through in December she did not read them, and did not stop. Emeric, coming down the K Amara's ladder to the launch by then, saw her thumb go along the board and rest there a breath, flat, the way she kept a place in a book, and lift.

Wilf Abery came down near the end of it, big and fair, without his scarf; and a woman at the rope who was certainly his mother looked at his bare neck before she looked at the rest of him.

"Where's your scarf?"

"At the bottom of the sea, Mam."

"I knitted that scarf."

"I know. I had Cady's after." Cady Lorne, small and dark behind him with her eyebrows only half grown back, took a careful step to one side. "It's black now," said Wilf.

His mother put both arms round him anyway; and the people near enough to hear laughed, the first laugh on the quay all afternoon, and Merrin, at the horn, grinned and did not move it an inch.

The K Endeavor's people came off by the long ramp, Bettany going from rack to rack with a list. Mira came down on her own feet with a face that dared anybody to remark on it, and Bren behind her, and Arthur; and Sela at the rope counted Arthur the way she counted her schoolroom, with her eyes, once, top to bottom, and nodded, and let Ferra have him.

"Brey, Matthias," read Ferra, off the bottom of the K Endeavor's sheet.

"That's me," said Brey, between two of Osric's people, with a slate under his arm and a bearded and delighted face. "Is there a cookshop? I was told there'd be..."

"Next," said Ferra.

Then the cradle.

Eight of the yard's men carried it down on their shoulders, its corner stones let down and dead, so that it went like any other hurt thing under its canvas. The town did not know what it was. It saw a long shape carried very carefully, and a tall woman in a faded blue dress walking beside it with one hand flat on the canvas's edge, and behind her a big young man with white curls in a good coat, with one hand inside the coat against his chest as if he were keeping something warm; and the rope went quiet as it passed, the way a street goes quiet for a coffin. It went up the yard steps toward the dome with Imani beside it, and she did not look round once.

At the rope's end by the yard steps Tess put her stick to the cobbles and got up out of her chair, and stood. She did not go to anybody. She stood. Osric, coming off the hospital ramp behind the cradle, saw her standing and stopped as if somebody had put a hand on his chest, and went to her.



The K Discovery's people came last but one, because the king had asked to come off after his son's crews and not before them.

Harrold came down the ladder in the plain brown coat, and the town knew him; he had walked its streets in a borrowed hat that fooled nobody. Behind him came Keller in his black, with one hand on his hat, who had been asked south by the king in writing and had answered in writing that he would come to see a peace drawn that could be read in his court, and had brought his own ink.

"His Majesty," read Ferra. "Harrold Randillia."

"Here," said the king, and stood with his hands behind his back while she ticked him, as if he had been counted onto quays all his life.

Then Corbin came down the ladder, and the quay found out who he was.

He came between two of Osric's people in a plain dark coat, holding the rail on his left side because of the rib; and behind him his mother, very straight, in grey, with her steward Mabry carrying her one box. The last of the yellow was still under his jaw. He stepped off and saw the faces along the rope, and Emeric watched him understand where he was: on a quay where two lines had just not been read, in front of people who had come down to count their sons; the Lieutenant who had named their town traitor.

Nobody shouted. Nobody threw anything. The quay simply stopped making any sound at all, from the crane to the yard steps, and looked at him; and it was a great deal worse than shouting, and Corbin stood in it with his face doing nothing, which was the one thing he had always known how to do in a room.

Ferra did not look up from her board.

"Corbin Randillia," she read. "Carried."

He turned his head. He had not imagined anybody in Kaldo would put him on anything, and did not know what a man said here when his name was read.

"Present," he said at last, the court's word, quite low. Ferra ticked him, and read, "Her Majesty, Sabina," and Sabina said, "Here," in exactly the voice she would have used to a chamberlain; and they went up the yard steps between Osric's two, and the quay let its breath out behind them.



He came to her last, because he had left it last, and she had known he would.

Ward and Saban had been read ahead of him, Saban touching his hat to her as if she were a toll-keeper; and then there was nobody on the quay between him and the crane but the gulls and the crowd beginning to go home in the dusk, and Ivo still cranking, and Ferra with the board against her chest and one name left on it.

She looked at the board. Then she looked up.

He had had the thing ready since the ladder on the night of the fire; since a quay in November, if he was honest. He had said it over all the way down the Wayford road, the way he said a figure over before he gave it to her. He stood in front of her on the cold stone and opened his mouth, and it would not come. Not because he did not mean it. Because he meant it so entirely that his voice would not carry it, and he was not going to say it to her in a voice that broke on the yard's quay in front of the town and Ivo's crank.

She saw all of that. She had asked him on the set for his face, and she looked at it now, steadily, at the whole of it, the way she read a column to the foot before she wrote anything; and he let her, and did not look away, and did not try again.

Something in her own face gave. Not much. Her mouth, at one corner.

"Counted," said Ferra, aloud, and ticked the last line, and the pen stopped.

Then, so low that the gulls had more of it than he did, with her eyes still on his face, she said it again.

"Counted."

"I had a thing," he said. "To say."

"I know. I've seen it. That's what I asked for." She put the pen back in her hair, which meant the day's book was shut, and took his healed arm above the wrist and looked at the seam through the open cuff, and let it go. "You can say it when it'll come out. I'm not going anywhere. I've a list." She turned the board over. "Go up. Your father's waiting for you to go up first, and he's too polite to say so, and he's cold."

At the yard steps he looked back. She was standing by the crane in the dusk with the board against her chest, not writing; and Ivo had stopped cranking at last and stood beside his camera with his hands in his pockets, like a man who had seen something he would have to decide about in the cutting room.



They named the dead in the long room at five, with the lamps lit and the shutters closed.

Nobody had arranged it. Rylan had said on the Isle that he would tell the rest at home, properly; so the long room filled without anybody asking it to, and the landing outside it. The divers, eleven, stood together. On the bench by the table, where somebody had put them, sat two women who did not know each other, in their good shawls: a heavy grey woman with a red neck like her son's, and a thin young one with her hands in her lap who was Robin Sennet's mother and could not have been more than forty.

Ferra had the death book open on the table. Imani came in from the dome with her sleeves still rolled and sat beside her, and laid her own book down, open, without saying what it was.

Rylan stood up, and found that everybody was looking at him.

It was not a speech. He gave it the way he would have given it to Ward on a roll: the jetty at the village, the divers going under at ten in the morning to cut Kesrath's boats loose; Sennet coming up at the wrong end, on the outside of the piles; Fenn going off the bottom of the ladder after him and getting him by the harness; the mage over the water. He gave the hour. He did not give what the mage did, and nobody asked.

"Every one of us wrote on the back of our roll card what to do with us," he said, "before we went aboard. That was Fenn's idea. He made us. Fenn's said where I go in." He looked at the heavy grey woman, and did not look away. "So that's where he is. On the dune over the beach, where you can see the water. Robin's beside him; Mistress Sennet said he could be." He stopped. "Declan cut the post. It says Kaldo on it."

Declan, against the wall by the door, looked at the floor and said nothing, and went on saying it.

Ferra read the names in plain words, as she had read them up the line in December so that anybody listening could hear. Ade Fenn, sergeant, the swimmers, the jetty at the village, the thirteenth of December. Robin Sennet, diver, the same. And then, because they were the war's too and had gone home first, Thaddeus Wyatt, ward three, the levy, the coal quay; Joby Dowd, Milford, the shore teams, nineteen; the east gate. Four names. She ruled her line under each.

Then Imani turned her own book a little toward the room, and Emeric saw what it was.

It was the register of the saved: every soul since the first of December who should have died and had not, in her small square hand, with the hour. Escort two's hundred off her rail onto hull four's deck, by name. Mira's catch, and Arthur's, and Bren's. The forty-one burned on the Claim, and the jars of Hester's broth. Danner's arm. Four pages, close-written, beside Ferra's one page with four names on it. She did not read it out. She left it open.

The two books lay side by side under the lamp. Ferra's hand was flat on the edge of hers and Imani's on the edge of hers, and Emeric's own hand lay on the table between them, not touching either; and none of the three of them moved.

He had stood on the quay in November and watched Fenn count his swimmers up the gangway by touching each of them on the top of the head, a barrel of a man with a voice like a gull, very pleased with himself and not saying so. He had watched Ward tell Robin Sennet twice to take his hands out of his pockets, and watched the boy take them out, and put them back in at the top where he thought nobody could see. He had not known either of them better than that. He found that two things were enough; that he did not know where to put his hands; and that nobody in the room was going to tell him it was all right, because it was not, and he did not want them to.

Nobody made a speech. After a while Fenn's mother went round the table to Rylan and took his face in her two hands, which he let her do, and said something nobody else heard. Then the room began slowly to empty.

Wilf Abery had stood at the back by the door the whole time, without his scarf. He went out last, and on the landing he stopped and put his hand up to his bare neck, as if he had only just noticed it was cold.



Corbin's terms were given at eight that evening in two rooms on the keep's second floor that Ferra had had aired for him.

Emeric said them. Ferra had the written copy. Ward stood by the door, because the door was Ward's.

Bram had brought the anklet up from the works himself, wrapped in a cloth. Emeric had drawn it in January and thought of it the whole time by a name from another world that he did not once say aloud. A hinged band, cut to look like a prince's jewellery and not a gaol's: Bram's steel inside a gold-washed band with a running pattern of leaves, no rivet showing. Against the skin, a flat drain stone of the kind the mage cuffs carried, to take whatever a man threw outward and leave him what was his inside. Under the leaves, a small sense stone with a note written into it that every wall sensor in Kaldo would know for his. The lock was a plain steel pin and a key. The key was on Ferra's inventory, under sundry.

Corbin sat by a fire that drew properly and looked at it.

"It's quite handsome," he said.

"It's meant to be," said Bram.

Corbin put his foot up on the stool without being asked, and Bram knelt and closed the band round his ankle, gently, and gave the key to Ferra.

"You'll feel it pull when you reach for anything," said Emeric. "Fire, earth, a stone in your sleeve. It goes out of you into the air before it reaches your hand. Everything else is yours. You can carry a sack of flour up the keep stair. You can't light a candle without a match. In a dead zone the drain stops for the day; the steel doesn't. And there'll be people watching you, inside the walls and out. You won't be told who. Not ever."

Corbin put his foot down. "Go on."

"You can go anywhere in Kaldo. You're not free. Both of those are true, and you'll carry both. Go in the coffee house under the gate, if you can stand the coffee." He did not soften it. "We'd like to know you. That takes time, and you set the pace by what you do, not by what you say, because you've been very good at saying things all your life. We care for you. We care for our own people more. That's the order, and it won't change. We do things differently here. Step out of line and we'll bind you tighter." He held his brother's eyes. "Do something bad enough, and we'll end you. And I'll be the one to do it."

Ward, at the door, did not move at all.

Corbin looked back at him a long time with their father's eyes. He did not look offended. He looked like a man who has been handed a bill and found it correctly added.

"Yes," he said. "I'd have said the same. I'd have put it worse." He looked at Ferra. "Is that all of it?"

"No." She turned the copy round and put her finger on its last line. "These terms to be read again, in writing, by both parties, at this table, on the sixteenth of February, year fifteen. A year from tonight." She took her finger off. "Nobody here writes a thing they mean to leave standing for ever without looking at it again. You won't be the first."

Corbin read the line twice, at the same pace both times. Emeric had seen their father read that way, and did not say so.

"In writing," said Ferra. "Sign there, and there. Your hand, not your ring."

Sabina had hers a quarter of an hour later, three doors down: the same watchers, the same freedom of the town, the same review a year out, and a lighter band with no leaves, because she had never cast anything in her life but a look. She checked the date of the review against the date at the head of the sheet, found them a year apart to the day, and signed, and thanked nobody.



Saban was in the passage when they came out, on the window seat with his hat on his knee. Corbin had come to his door behind them, one hand on the frame.

"Your man," he said to Emeric. "The one who keeps lists."

Saban got up.

"Twenty-eight years," said Corbin, "I sat in the anteroom outside his closed room in the palace, where he heard people. He liked me to be the one who saw who came out. I don't know what they said. I know who sat on the bench and waited, and how often, and whether he walked them to the door with his hand on their arm. I've been writing it down since the yard." He held out a folded sheet. "Nine. Council lords, four. Palace clerks, three. An under-secretary of the chancery. And a brother."

Saban took it without unfolding it. "Your father?"

"Never. Not once in twenty-eight years. He wouldn't let a priest's hand on him. I used to think it was pride." Corbin looked at the sheet. "The brother came twice in the spring, at night, and was let in ahead of a duke. A lay brother of the northern houses. I asked his name because he had clean hands and I thought it odd. Iven."

Saban's face did nothing, which on Saban was a great deal. Emeric felt his note go down, and knew without being told that Saban was back in the study in July, looking at an old priest's finger on a line in a lodgers' book: Brother Iven, of the northern houses, to the coast. The room over the kitchen.

"Thank you, Highness," said Saban, and put the sheet in his waistcoat, unread, where he had once put a silver token.

"Will it be any use?"

"I'll find out which," said Saban. "It'll take a while."



The Kaldo News came off Hobb's press at five the next morning, the first number, four pages, a copper; and Emeric had one with his breakfast at seven, still damp, because Maren's girl had brought it up off the stack in the works' yard and would not wait for it to dry.

Saban's office had put it together over the winter, quietly: notices, the trains, the Works Office's lists, the price of bread at three bakers, births. Saban said he liked to know what the town read.

The first page was the names. Sela had asked for it, in the voice she used for a boy who had not done his sums: every soul counted off the quay, in the order Ferra read them, so that a woman in Milford who could not get down to the yard could read her son's name in her own kitchen, and know. A column and a half in Hobb's smallest type; and at the foot of the page, in a plain ruled box by itself, four names.

He read the whole of it. Then he sat with the sheet flat by his plate.

The Church had never much minded what people thought. It minded every kitchen in a town knowing the same thing on the same morning. A thing said from a pulpit could be unsaid from it next week; a thing printed was in every kitchen by seven and could not be got out again. Any house that held a thing alone, and lived by holding it, was afraid of that. He had thought for years he knew why the Church feared the press. Looking at the box at the foot of the page, he thought he had not known until this morning what it was afraid the press would one day print.

He put the paper down, and finished his tea.



He brought the switch to Ferra at five that afternoon, an hour before the six were called, because he had promised himself in January that she would have it first.

She was in the long room alone, with three books open and the lamp lit early, and she looked at the paper stack under his arm and put her pen down before he had said anything.

A packet of the Church's secrets, never Kaldo's: that control stones existed; the Band, and that the Church had known and cut the page out; the white hulls and the rubbings of their stones; the bleeders and the drains; Amara, and who killed her, and on whose word. Never how to delve, never powder or ships or the Forge. In iron cases, each with one plain stone cut from inside and given a job, so the world could see a stone working with nobody's hand on it. Held three ways: keepers, Saban's cells, who did not know each other; caches, where only Kaldo could reach; and plates, at Hobb's works and with a printer in another kingdom.

"And it goes when?"

"When we stop." He laid it out with his finger as if it were a circuit. "Every month each keeper gets a word, out of a one-time book in your cipher, which the Church can't read or fake. It can come from me, or any of the six, or my father. Kaldo has to be silenced altogether for it to stop; not one man. A missed month starts a clock. Three months and nothing by any road, and the keepers post their packets to every crown and the tower and the emperor and every press that'll take them. And there's a second word. If one of us is taken and made to send, he sends that one instead, and it goes as surely as silence."

She had not written anything. She turned back in her book to the seventeenth of August, year ten: the stay in her larger hand, every letter separate, and the line under it about a heap of grey dust. She read it through, and looked up.

"If we're all taken in one night," she said, "and you with us. Does this hurt our own?"

"No. It hurts the Church. And the world's peace for a while, because every crown starts hunting what the Church hid, and some of what they find is ours." He made himself say the rest. "Somebody comes for the tree. That's the cost to us, and it's why it's believed. A threat that costs the man making it nothing sounds like a bluff."

"Not a ship in the air. Not the ward."

"Nobody's ship comes down. Nobody's tap stops. It's paper."

She was quiet a while. Then, because she would always find the one place he had not looked, she put her finger on the table where the three months had been.

"And if it fires because you're asleep?"

He looked at her.

"April," said Ferra. "The dome. You and five of the six and three knights, under, for weeks; Imani can't tell me how many. If we're under when the word's due, who sends it? Armand isn't sleeping. Your father isn't. Is that two hands, or two men in a dome with a lamp, and one of them a king who's used one cipher in his life, and that one Ward's?"

He had not thought of it. He had thought of every road a man in Ostrel might take, and not the plainest: all of them on their backs under glass, doing nothing at all.

"The book can send ahead," he said slowly. "Two months' words in one, if we know we'll be quiet. And nobody's under three months."

"You don't know that."

"No."

"Then Armand sends April's and May's, and your father learns the book from me in March and sends one in front of me so I've seen him do it." She wrote it at last, a line, small and upright. "And both halves of the keepers' list copied once more and sealed with Armand, in case Saban and I die on the same night and nobody's left who holds the whole of it. He'll outlive us all; he's said so often." She looked up. "What else."

"Nothing else."

"Then I want a warning shot. You've built a thing with two settings. Nothing, for years, and then every secret on one morning and the world on fire. You've told Bram so about every valve he ever drew. If a keeper goes missing, or a cache is touched, we let one part go. Only one. The bleeders and the drains. That's the part that turns a congregation, and it isn't the part that tells anybody where a stone comes from." She held his eyes. "So he knows it's real. And what the rest will cost, before he's paid it."

He found he was smiling, very slightly, the way he did when somebody else finished one of his drawings better than he would have.

"Yes," he said. "That."

She wrote it. Then her hands went flat on the table either side of the book, and she asked what he had known since a chart table in January that she would ask, quietly, for one person.

"Would you let it go out?"

"The keepers would. That's the point of them. It doesn't need me."

"That isn't what I asked."

He looked at the ink on her fingertips, and the one strand of copper come down against her cheek that she had not noticed.

"Yes," he said.

Ferra looked at him a long time. Then she dipped the pen and wrote a single word under the line about the warning shot, and blotted it, and turned the book round for him to read.

Yes. F.



The six came at six, and his father at a quarter past, with Hale behind him carrying a chair.

The head of the long table was Armand's, and Armand would not give it up to a king twice; so Hale put the chair, a heavy carved thing nobody had sat in since the old baron's day, at the foot, and the king of Randillia sat in it and looked down the table at the six and at his son and the lamp, like a man at somebody else's supper who has decided to eat what he is given.

"Don't stand," he said to Armand, who had not.

"I wasn't going to, Majesty. I was going to say that chair has woodworm, and I'd sit forward in it."

Harrold sat forward.



Emeric told the six what he had told Ferra, and Ferra read them what she had written under it.

"Who carries it?" said Ward.

It was the question he had asked from the door in the year ten, about a grey stone, in the same voice he used for a roll. Emeric had wanted it asked.

"Nobody carries it. Every month somebody here has to choose to send a word. Nobody has to do anything for it to go out but be gone."

Ward thought about it with his whole face, slowly.

"It fires when we're dead," he said. "Not when we're angry."

"Yes."

"Then that's different," said Ward, and sat back.

"The list in halves," said Saban, turning his hat, which meant he had already begun. "Each keeper knows one face, and that face doesn't know the next. Two months before the first packet leaves this room. And I'll not use the House's factors without asking the House."

"I want my pages in it," said Imani. "The boiling. The broth, and how to grow it, and how to tell when it's gone bad. The cowpox scratch. The blood register." She folded her arms. "If it ever goes out, the world might as well get something it can use in the same packet. A man in Kesrath with a child in a fever won't care who told him to boil the water."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"I wasn't asking."

"Iron cases," said Bram. "Sizes by Friday."

"I'd like to read it before it's sealed," said Armand. "Not to change a word. I read the stub of the climbers' cut page my whole life in the tower. I'd like to read the page."

His father had said nothing. He sat forward in the woodwormed chair with his hands between his knees, and Emeric could not read him at all.

"And I'm one of the hands," said Harrold at last. "That sends the word."

"If you'll be."

"I've signed worse things for less reason." He looked at Ferra. "You'll teach me the book."

"In March. You'll send one in front of me."

"Of course I will," said Harrold, and very nearly smiled. "And this letter. Every king writes to Ostrel, and the answers come back from a cardinal's clerk. How does yours reach his own hand?"

"By Augustine's road," said Emeric. "A road past every cardinal. I had it from him. At the end."

His father looked at him. So did Armand, who had felt something go down a line in a hospital hold in December and had not asked for the rest, and still did not. Ferra did not look up from her book, and Imani looked at nobody at all.

"At the end," said Harrold. He let it lie. "Then send it by his road. It's the only use I've ever heard of for him."

Nobody voted. Ferra wrote it as a thing the table knew, and under it the names of the six, and the king's.



The green plan took less time than anybody expected, because Ferra had read it to them over the set three times that winter: the short lines electrified off the dams, the steam engines kept in their sheds for dead zones; the pilot shaft back in fire when Dov had proved the line, and the full works when the river dam ran; the blast furnace banked, not pulled down; the coal crews' first claim on every new job at better pay, and the reserve kept for any man who would rather keep a cold station than learn a new one.

"Marrick and ten," said Ferra. "At a fireman's rate, for as long as there's a coal station." She turned a page. "They'll want it in writing that they're not being kept out of pity."

"They're not."

"I know. They'll want it in writing."

The leak rule she did not read out. She copied it into the plan from the works book, word for word in Dov's words and not tidied, Gas goes up and goes through down to Look for the air going wrong; signed under his name; and turned it for Bram.

"Again?"

The coal station. And under it: year twenty-two.

"The stone book," said Ferra.

She had been drawing up the sleep from it over the winter, and had found it there in her own hand, and gone back to the first page to be sure it was the only place.

"The six. Bonded, bonded, bonded, bonded, bonded, bonded." Her finger stopped. "Rylan. Declan. Osric. Year twelve band stones." She looked up. "Held."

"Who bonded them?"

"Nobody," said Imani.

She said it as she would have read a temperature. Then she put both hands flat on the table.

"Held isn't bonded. A held stone sits in your band and gives you room, and that's all. It doesn't know you. It's never been inside with you." She looked down the table at Emeric. "An unbonded stone swallowed is his week in the dome. All of it, the fever and the cuffs, and none of the rebuild at the end, because the stone has nothing to build to. Their tracery stays. Rylan's burn stays. They come out exactly as they went in, and sick for a month. So either somebody teaches three knights to go down inside their own heads in seven weeks, or I'll not let them near the dome in April. That's my rule. I'll write it if anybody wants it written."

"Seven weeks," said Bram. "Tess took six."

"Tess did it in Kaldo's air. The dome's thicker, and it's faster where it's thicker; you've all said so." Imani shrugged. "Four weeks if they go every night. I'd want five."

"Then it's three more," said Saban, pleasantly, and Emeric heard what was under it.

"Three more in this kingdom who can go down a stair inside themselves, and be taken, and read," said Saban. "I know every head that can do it now, and what each would say with a knife at it. Three more, and all three knights, who go where the fighting is and get taken there." He turned his hat. "I'm the one who keeps the secrets. I'd like fewer heads to keep them in. I'm against."

"They went down the cliff on the Isle," said Ward. "Rylan took the ferry's fire on his arm for the king. Declan fired the round." He did not raise his voice; he never did. "They're eating a stone in April whatever we say. We decide whether it does them any good. They've kept harder things than a stair. For."

"Three stones swallowed for nothing," said Ferra, "or three more names that can be read. I can't make those one column. I'd rather the names. A name can be guarded. A stone that's gone is gone. For."

Imani for. Bram for, because Osric was Tess's and he would not have the boy sick a month for want of a door. Armand for, with a small inclination of the head in the old tower form that he had not lost with the years.

They looked at Emeric.

"For," he said. "I'll take them in myself, the first time, one at a time. Not tonight; they've been home a day." He looked at Saban. "And I'll tell them what you said. All of it. So they know what they're carrying, and who was against."

"Good," said Saban. "I'd like them to know who."

Ferra wrote it, six to one, and Saban's reason under it in his own words, which she read back to him. His father had watched it with his chin on his fist, like a man seeing a council lose a vote and nobody afterward look at the floor.



"And Vantic," said Ferra, before anybody could get up.

It was short.

"The bank wants the cards, and the cards want the delve, or she can't know what she's lending against. She's known about the K Amara since the year eleven and never written a word of it. I'd offer her what this table knows, not the dome, and she decides what she takes. She'll take less than we offer. She always does."

"Against," said Saban. "She answers to a House with branches in every port the Church can reach, and a secret the House knows is a secret the House can be made to give up."

That was all he said. Nobody tried to talk him round; Emeric thought it would have been an insult to try.

"For," said Ward. "She's never once written it down."

"For," said Imani. "I'll not run a hospital on a bank that can't see its own book."

Armand for. Bram for. Emeric last, for, on Ferra's terms; and Ferra wrote it, and Saban's sentence under it as he had said it.

"He'll say he told us so," said Bram, to nobody, "when she's turned out right."

"I'll say I was against," said Saban. "I'll still be against. It isn't the same thing as being wrong."



He had carried Vantic's sheet in his coat since the tenth. He laid it in front of Ferra and said nothing, and she read it to the foot twice, the second time with her pen moving down the margin, not touching the paper.

"She held it at silver through the war, on purpose, and sold it to the Empire's houses and Ostrel." She put her finger on the figure under the ruled line. "Within a few hundred of the turbines."

"The enemy bought very fine cutlery," said Emeric. "We bought the turbines. That's hers, not mine."

"It would be. The widow in Fairhythe bought from the House's factor at the market there, at the House's price with his commission on top. A third. Give her back the House's difference and she's still a third short, and she'll tell all Fairhythe the honest House paid her short. Pay the difference on what she paid, on the bill or the factor's word. It costs the House more. It's the only way it works."

"She said you'd find something."

"Don't tell her she was right." Ferra wrote a line on the back of the sheet. Then she sat still with the pen lifted, and turned her own book over to the last pages: to the column she kept at the back of every book and never showed anybody. She ran her finger down to a line written small in the May of the year eleven, the evening she had looked at the first white bar on Vantic's desk; and he watched her read it, and read the sheet, and go very slightly pink at the ears.

She turned the book round and pushed it down the table to him.

The Polytechnic alloy, the column said. Falls to the price of tin. Year thirteen.

"It's fourteen," said Ferra, before he could open his mouth. "It hasn't fallen yet. It falls this year, when she says so, because she's held it up through a war with her own two hands. I had the dam right, and the pot room, and the bauxite. I didn't count her." Under the thirteen, without striking it, she wrote: off by one. Vantic. "She held it past what the metal deserved, to make the people who paid for the war pay for it. I'd never have done that."

"Off by one," said Imani, "in three years."

"Off by one," said Ferra, "is off."

His father had followed all of it with the face of a man told the price of a thing he had not known he owned. "The Church," he said slowly, "paid for your turbines. With altar plate."

"Bought altar plate, Majesty," said Armand. "We sold it them. It's a distinction the tower always found useful."

"And they'll bring it back. Ostrel. To a Kaldo bank. And open an account."

"Vantic says she'd like to see the face of the clerk who carries it," said Emeric.

Harrold looked at the lamp. Then he laughed, once, a short dry bark nobody at that table had ever heard out of him, and stopped as if it had surprised him, and looked at his hands.

The buy-back passed without a vote, because Ferra said it was the House's to do and they would only be voting on whether they liked it; and they did.



Keller came up the keep stair at nine with a board under his arm, and sat where Hale put him, on the king's right, and folded his hands, and waited to be asked a question.

Ferra asked it.

"The settlement of Harrow Bridge. Year nine, under the Lieutenant's seal in the king's name. The crown and this house bound to keep the king's peace with each other, and three hundred men under arms in time of peace." She had the page open; she had a page on everything. "In August you wrote that it bound nobody, because the Lieutenant had named this house traitor. In January the council found there was never any treason, and every oath binds as it bound. Is Harrow Bridge back? And the three hundred with it?"

It was the question the table had not wanted asked, with a fleet in the yard and a knights' base full in the woods.

Keller did not need his board.

"No. I wrote that a settlement does not outlive its parties calling each other that. The Lieutenant called this house a traitor to the crown, in open council, in the king's name, with the council standing. The council has since found he had no right to call it that. It has not found that he didn't say it. He said it. It's in the council's book." He folded his hands the other way. "I can unwind a verdict, Mistress. I can't unsay that it was said. It's dead, and it stays dead." He looked at the king. "The crown may make a new peace. That isn't a matter for my court."

Harrold sat forward in the woodwormed chair.

"Then the crown will. In my own hand, not the chancery's." He looked down the table. "Write it for me, Mistress Ferra, and I'll copy it. I've no gift for words, and you'll put them where they can't be read two ways."

Ferra took a clean sheet. Nobody spoke while she wrote; somewhere below in the inner ward Brey's voice came up faintly through the shutters, asking somebody whether there was any more of the soup.

She wrote it short. The king's peace between the crown and the house of Kaldo, both ways, each to keep it with the other and neither to raise force against the other on any cause. And where the old settlement had had its figure, nothing: no line on the house's levy at all, the house to keep what men under arms it saw fit and answer to the crown for their keeping as any duke answered, and no more.

She read it aloud once and turned it round. Keller read it upside down and nodded very slightly, which from Keller was a seal.

"No figure," said Harrold.

"No figure, Majesty."

"The council will want one."

"The council stood up to call my house a traitor in August," said Ferra. "It can want."

His father looked at her with something Emeric had not often seen on him. Then he took Keller's pen out of Keller's hand without asking and copied the peace out fair under her draft, in the quick tall upright hand that wasted no stroke, and signed it, Harrold R.

Not much. A word that said more than it meant; a clause that told the man something he did not need telling.

He wrote it under the rest on the same top sheet: In the same packets is how to grow a stone in a shed, out of gravel and sweepings, with no mine and no blessing. Every crown gets it on the same morning.

He had had it since a word in the dark of a linen store. He sat with the pen over the top sheet anyway, deciding whether to say it as plainly as it was in him; and while he decided, his hand did what it had always done when he was deciding something, on both sides of everything, since a schoolroom in another world. Move on Kaldo and I'll annihilate you both.

It came up the stair at Ostrel in the third week of March, when the snow was going off the passes, in a courier's satchel among a dozen others that were not grey.

The chamberlain took it at the stair's foot, as he took every letter under plain grey, without looking at the man who carried it, and went up. It was a long stair, narrow and very old, every step worn hollow, and he was not young. He went up with his hand on the rope in its rings, as he had twice a day for forty-one years; and at the top the lamp was burning in its niche beside the small door, and he looked at it as he passed, as he always did, to see whether it wanted trimming. It did not.

He did not read letters. That was not his office. He looked at them.

His room was beside the door: a table, a stool, a book in which he wrote the day and hour of every letter that came up the stair and nothing else, and a small lamp with a tin hood that he used for one purpose only. He wrote the day and the hour. He broke the grey wax, which somebody had pressed with the side of a thumb, rather well; set the folded page inside it aside unread; and laid the letter flat under the hooded lamp, and turned the lamp very low, and drew it slowly across the sheet from the side, low and raking, so that its light went along the paper and not onto it, as he had been taught as a boy to look for what a man had pressed into a page that he had not meant anybody to see.

The ink lay flat and black and told him nothing. He moved the lamp.

And in the margin beside the last line, where nothing was written at all, something rose out of the paper in the raking light, the way a shape rises out of snow when the sun is low: a dent in the grain, very faint, that no pen on this page had made. Other ghosts came up with it, crossed words and faint lines under the clean ones; but those were only words. What he looked at was a small ring, with one stroke through it that stopped short of the far side.

He knocked at the door at the top of the stair, twice, as he always knocked, and went in.

The holy father was at the desk under the high window: since the winter, the same tall pale man who had come up the stair for twenty years with his right hand closed at his side. The chamberlain did not look at the face. He looked, as he always had, at the gold Eye on its chain at the holy father's breast; he had not looked at the face above it, whichever it was, in forty-one years. He laid the letter on the desk with the hooded lamp beside it, and stood back.

"There is a mark, Holy Father. In the margin. Pressed, not written. I thought it might be a message."

The holy father did not answer. He drew the hooded lamp across the sheet himself with his left hand, low, from the side, slowly; and stopped it at the margin, where the small ring rose out of the paper with its one short stroke; and the lamp did not move again for so long that the chamberlain, who did not look at faces, found that he was looking at the hand that held it.

It was quite steady.

Then the holy father said one word.

He did not say it to the chamberlain. He did not say it to anybody the chamberlain could see; the word went past him toward the dark window as if somebody were standing there. It was short. It was not in the Church's tongue, or the old tongue its books were copied in, or the Empire's, or any of the half dozen he had heard come up that stair in forty-one years. It did not sound like a word that belonged in a mouth at all. It had been said gently.

The chamberlain went out and shut the door, and sat at his table, and opened his book at the day's page.

He wrote down everything that came up that stair. He had written down everything that came up it for forty-one years.

He did not write the word down.

He never saw the letter again, and did not ask where it had gone. It was not his to ask.

The pope kept the letter.
