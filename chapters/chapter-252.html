Chapter 252 - What Ward Knew

Ward found him on the bridge at first light, halfway across, where the water ran black under the arches between the island and the new city, and stood beside him at the rail without saying anything, and looked at the water too.

They had come down on the lake an hour before dawn. Coming down out of the cloud, the king had stood at the cabin port beside him and seen the new city below them on the snow, every street of it drawn in small white lamps, a wheel with a dark hub, and had looked at it without a word until the lake came up. The king had been taken up to the palace in a closed cart by Imani, who had been waiting on the shore with a lamp and a blanket and had looked at the old man getting down off the ship's ladder in his fur and said nothing at all, not one word, and taken his arm. Emeric had watched them go up the road toward the north gate. Then he had walked, because he could not sit, and his feet had brought him here, to the bridge, where Ward had delved for the first time alone, the winter before last, and come up out of it gasping with his hand clamped round his wrist.

Ward's wrist was bare now. The band was in the cradle in his breastplate, and the breastplate was on its stand in the gatehouse, and his arm without it looked like an arm that had forgotten something.

"It wasn't Aldane," Ward said.

He said it to the water.

"I told you it was, on the ship. Not in the words. I told you I believed the evidence and didn't believe him. I told you about the passage and the floor, and I let you carry the rest of it home." He was quiet for a while. "Twenty years I've watched that man across every hall I was ever in with him. Every sitting. Every road. I watched him at the elevation look at me and look away. I thought it was because he knew what I knew. I thought he was the one who'd been up that slope." He put both hands on the rail. "He was drunk in a passage once and put his hands on a woman he didn't know was a queen. And she put him on his face on the floor, and I stood over him with a sword, and he's been afraid of me ever since. That's all. That's all he ever was. A man who was afraid of me for twenty years, for a thing he did in a passage, and I took it for guilt, because I wanted it to be him."

Emeric did not say anything.

"It was the churchman," Ward said. "I felt him open in the carriage. I had my band in and my room open and he was a dozen paces off, and he opened, and it was the same hand. The same edge. The same weight. It's been on my shelf twenty years, from up the slope in the channel, beside the passage, and I've looked at it every night since the bridge, and I couldn't read it. I read it last night." He turned his head, at last, and looked at Emeric. "I don't need anybody to tell me I'm right. I've got the shelf. I went back in when we landed, on the ship, before I came down the ladder, and laid the two of them side by side. The road and the channel. They're the one hand." He looked back at the water. "He was up the slope that day, in the hooded men, and he opened, and I felt it in my teeth. And she had her hands up with the light coming off them. And then I had you, and I ran."

The water went on under the bridge.

Emeric stood at the rail with his hands on the cold stone and thought about how to begin, and found that there was no way to begin it that was not simply to say it, in order, the way Ward said a road.

So he said it.

The Deep Channel. The boat, and the hold, and his hands tied, and the stone in his pocket he could not reach. The first time the sense had gone out of him on its own, through the planks, into the dark, and found seven men on the deck, each with his own grain. And the one among them who had a beat in him. Slow and even, underneath everything, like two strings tuned a hair apart and struck together. He had walked it to the hatch in his head and back. He had heard the voice that went with it, level, putting its weight on no word: along the coaming, all of it; and the same voice, off a ledge behind the church, the night of Stretch Nine, telling Rylan to carry his lord down to the water, gently. He had filed them together. He had carried them for years. He had laid the beat once against Marrow on the floor of the Staple's strongroom and found it was not him, and Marrow had looked at the ash.

"Last night," Emeric said. "In the carriage. Before you said anything. I felt it. The beat. The same one." He stopped. He took his left hand off the rail and turned it over, so that the cuff fell back and the bead showed on its cord at his wrist, round and grey. "And this answered it."

Ward looked at the bead.

"One of the two strings," Emeric said. "Not both. The bead knew one of them. It was hers. In the comb case. It was the root, before I took it into the forge." He did not know how to say the next part, so he said it as it was. "It knew the one that was hers. In his hand. He had his hand closed on something the whole time, in his lap, the right hand, with the left over it. It never opened. And the bead sang to it, across the carriage, the way a glass sings on a shelf when the right note is struck. Two strings a hair apart. One of them's him." He put his cuff back down over the bead. "The other one isn't."

Ward did not move for a long time.

He stood at the rail in the grey light with the water going under the arches, and looked at Emeric's wrist where the cuff had gone back over it, and his face did what Ward's face did, which was nothing; except that Emeric, who had known that face since before he could remember anything, saw it do something else underneath the nothing, very slowly, the way the ice on a winter pond gives a long way down before it cracks.

"Then he's had her," Ward said, "the whole time."

It was the only thing he said about it. Then or ever.

Imani was waiting for him at the palace door when he came up from the bridge.

She had the lamp still, though it was light now, and she had not slept, and she looked at him the way she looked at a man coming in off a road to see where he was hurt before he said anything. Then she said, "He's awake."

Emeric stood still on the step.

"Last night. A little after dark." She said it the way she said a pulse. "I was on the stool. He opened his eyes and looked at the tree for a long while, and then he looked at me, and asked me what the date was, and I told him, and he said, Six weeks, as though I'd given him a reading and he didn't think much of it. At midnight he was sitting up. At the second bell he told me to go down to the shore, because you'd want me more than he did, and when I said I wasn't leaving him alone under glass he said he'd been alone a good deal further down than that and it hadn't hurt him." She shifted the lamp to her other hand. "I had Bram bring him out before I went. He's in the end room of the east range. He's asleep. Properly asleep. I checked him when I came up."

"Marks?"

"None." She looked at him. "His heart's the heart of a man twenty years younger. I don't know what that means, and neither do you, and I'd be obliged if nobody said so at the table until one of us does."

He went along the east range and opened the end door very quietly and did not go in.

The old man was asleep on his back in the narrow bed with the blanket to his chin and his hands outside it, folded, as Imani had folded them under the glass. His colour was good. His mouth was a little open, and he was snoring, faintly and without apology, like a man who has earned it.

As Emeric began to close the door the snoring stopped, and one eye opened.

"My page," Armand said.

"Ferra has it. It's in the box."

The eye considered this. "Then it can stay there," Armand said, and shut again; and a moment later the snoring began again where it had left off.

He had said nothing about where he had been. Emeric did not ask. He closed the door.

The king was under the dome.

Imani had put him to bed in the east range, in the room next to Emeric's, and he had slept three hours and got up and dressed himself and gone out into the gallery without asking anybody, and a servant had found him standing at the pass-box door looking through its glass, and had not known what to do, and had gone for Emeric.

Emeric went through the pass-box, because that was the only way in, and the old man came after him without being asked, stooping under the inner door, and they stood together on the flags under the glass.

On the flags on the north side of the middle bed there was a paler oblong where a pallet had lain for six weeks and been taken up in the night. His father did not ask what it was. He may not have seen it.

It was warm under the dome. It was always warm. The winter light came down through the glass onto the beds, white and even, and the air was close and damp and smelled of earth, and in the middle bed, in the round where the table had stood once, there was a tree a forearm high: a thin grey stem with the bark just beginning to crack, and eleven leaves, and a twelfth unfolding.

His father looked at it.

There was nobody else under the dome. There was nobody in the pass-box behind them, and nobody in the gallery beyond it, and no door anywhere in the glass with a gap under it. Emeric had built it that way, because what grew under it could not be let out and nothing from outside could be let in. It was the most private room in the kingdom.

His father spoke first.

He did it under the breath. Not a whisper; a whisper hisses, and hissing carries. The other thing, the thing between two men standing close together, at the edge of the ear, that Emeric had learned in the mews at eight years old with a hand on the back of his neck, and had not heard since the elevation, when a king had set a coronet on his head the way a man puts a lid on a pot and said, in a dead woman's voice, you kneel like her; too quick.

There was no door. There was no one to hear. His father whispered anyway. It was the only language they had.

"That's hers," the king said.

"Yes."

"The one with the chip out of it. From the comb case." His father did not take his eyes off the tree. "I had it in a drawer twenty years. In the treasury. I used to take the case out, some nights. I didn't know what it was for. I only knew she'd kept it." He was quiet a moment. "Ward asked for her things, years ago, by his road. He didn't say why, and I didn't ask. I sent them down with the carrier. I thought you should have something of hers that wasn't a grave."

Emeric could not answer that.

"You planted it."

"It was a seed. It was always a seed. So are the others. I didn't know that for years." Emeric kept his voice at the same place, under the breath, and found that it was easy; it had been in him all his life, waiting. "It's the only one I could plant. The others are working."

His father looked at the twelfth leaf unfolding for a long time.

"You weren't sent for," he said.

"No."

"I said you would not come up that road again unless I sent for you. In the day room. Loud." The ghost of something went over the old face, dry, the thing that had been in his voice when he set the coronet down. "I meant every word of it. For the door."

"I know."

"Somebody sent for you."

Emeric did not answer. His father did not ask again. He stood with his hands behind his back, looking at the tree his wife's stone had become, and after a while, very slowly, he put out his right hand, without looking, and put it on the back of his son's neck, and left it there.

"Good," he said. "Again."

They stood like that for some time, under the glass, in the warm, with the winter light coming down on them and on the tree, and neither of them said anything else, and there was nothing else that needed saying at that volume.

They were all at the table that night, because they were all at the table every night, and the king was not, because Imani had said he would not be and nobody argued with Imani about a body.

Ward told it. He told it standing by the door, the way he told a road, in order, without anything in it that was not a fact: the carriage, the man opening, the hand on his shelf. He did not tell the bridge. He did not tell what Emeric had told him. He only said, at the end, flat, the word that had only ever been said at this table about people who sat at it.

"He delves," Ward said. "The cardinal. He opened in the carriage, a dozen paces off. He's a delver."

Nobody said anything.

Armand was in his chair. It was the first night in six weeks he had been in it; Imani had said he could sit for an hour and not a minute past, and had put herself where she could see him. He had said almost nothing since he sat down, and he said nothing now. He sat with his hands folded on the table, and looked at Ward, and then down at his hands, and did not look up again for a long time. Emeric watched him not look up. He had watched the old man hear a great many things at this table, and argue with most of them, and he had never once seen him hear a thing and have nothing whatever to say to it.

The Church had a delver. The Church, that kept the chamber and the rite and the fourteen hours and the eight priests at the eight places, and held that a man's capacity was given and not made, and had forty years ago made Armand swear to it; the man at the head of the office that kept all of it could open a room of his own, and had done so on a road in the snow, and nobody in the whole of the Circle had ever known. Emeric could see Armand see it. He could see him go back through forty years, through every rite he had stood at and every roll of the dead he had read, and find each of them different now, and not be able to say so.

"Saban," Emeric said. "The coin."

Saban looked up.

He had carried it for years, in the purse inside his coat, next to his own money, where he had put it on the deck of a burning boat and done up the string: a thin silver thing a little larger than a Silver Rand and a good deal thinner, with no king's head on it. He had shown it in every market in four baronies and on the coast and in the capital, to every money-changer and every factor and every man who might have seen its like, and none of them had. He had kept it anyway. He had never shown it to Armand, because Armand was not a market, and it had never once occurred to anybody at this table that it should be shown to anyone but a market.

He took the purse out, and undid the string, and put the coin on the table in front of the old man.

Armand looked at it.

He did not pick it up. He did not need to. He looked at the face with the tower on it, rubbed nearly smooth, and the thing over the tower that might have been a crown or might have been a flame; and then, with one finger, he turned it over, and looked at the other face, at the ring of letters round the worn mark, and Emeric saw him read it. It took him no time at all. It took him the time it takes a man to read his own name.

"That is the office," Armand said.

His voice was quite steady. So was the finger on the edge of the coin. Emeric saw Imani see it, from across the table, and look at nothing, and say nothing.

"The words of the office. In the old tongue. They go round the edge of the cardinal's seal, on every roll of the dead of the chamber at every feast. I have read them there for forty years, in red wax, at the foot of every list of names, before the list was read." He touched the edge of the coin with his fingertip, the way a man touches the edge of a thing he is not sure is real. "And that is not a tower. It's the grand church at Ostrel. With the Light over it. It's on the lintel of every house of the brothers from here to the sea. It's struck for the lay brothers of the Ostrel houses. For their journeys. It isn't money." He looked up, at last, across the table at Saban. "It was never money."

"No," said Saban. "It wasn't."

He said it flatly, entering it. He had asked every market in four baronies, and it had never been in any of them, because it had never been in a market in its life. He looked at the coin on the table for a while. Then he looked at his own hands.

"I asked the wrong people," he said. "For four years. I asked everybody who handles money. It was on a dead man's neck on the deck of the Gannet, and I asked everybody who handles money." He was quiet a moment. "I'd have found it the first week if I'd asked a priest."

Nobody said anything to that either. Ferra, at the end of the table, had her book open. She did not write in it. She looked at the coin, and at Armand, and at Ward by the door, and at Emeric, and closed the book, and put her hand flat on the cover, and left it there.

He wrote to Maeve that night, after they had gone.

He sat at the table in the empty room with the lamp and a clean sheet, and squared it, and found, for the first time in six weeks, that he knew what it would be safe to put on it. It would be safe because it was going into one pair of hands, by a man in grey who would not read it, to a woman who had put her name to a thing for him and never asked for anything before in her life. And because she had asked him, once, years ago, in four lines, for exactly this, and said she had no opinion she was entitled to until she had it.

He wrote four lines. It seemed the right number.

You asked me once what I found in that cellar: eleven people on benches in grey smocks, whom five lords had drawn on for years, in chairs, the way you'd draw on a stone, and a man of thirty-one who looked eighty and died in the eighth week.

I was angrier in that room than I have ever been, and I let it out of me into the whole building so that the men upstairs would feel it, and I am not sorry.

Father is here, and well, and has seen the stars from above the cloud, and has stood with me in a room with no door.

You asked me for something. It was the first time. Thank you. E.

He read it twice. He did not change anything. He sealed it, and put it in his coat for Wald, who was asleep again on the bench in the gatehouse and would go north on the first train, and would not read it.

He went to Doran before the strongroom.

The chapel was dark, and the two rooms at the end of the ring street were not. Doran was at his table with the lamp and his stick across his knees, not reading, and he looked up when Emeric came in out of the cold and did not seem surprised; old priests are not surprised by a knock after midnight, because it is nearly always a death.

"The parish book," Emeric said. "The old one. The first you kept, from the year I came. The first winter. The week of the hard frost, when the payroll board froze. The night I went down the water stair."

Doran did not ask why. He got up, which took a while, and went to the shelf where the full books stood, and took down the first of them, and brought it to the table, and turned the leaves under the lamp. He did not turn to the burials. He turned, instead, to the pages he kept at the back of every book, which were not for souls at all but for the parish roof: who had slept under it, and when, and going where. A priest writes them down because a traveller who dies on the road should have somebody who knows where he last slept. Doran had always written them down. It was in the old form.

He found the week. He put his finger on a line.

Brother Iven, of the northern houses, to the coast.

"I remember him," Doran said. He sounded faintly surprised to find that he did. "A travelling brother. He came in on the carrier's cart, three days before, and asked for a bed as they do, and had the room over the kitchen, and ate at my table, and asked nothing of anybody. I mentioned him to you at the gate, I think. And then one morning he was gone when I woke. I thought nothing of it. Travellers go early; they like the road before it's busy." He looked at the line for a while. "He was old. That's all I remember of him. He was old and he didn't look it. Very clean hands. I remember thinking the northern houses must feed their brothers better than I'd heard."

The room over the kitchen. Emeric knew the parish house. He knew the kitchen's back window, and the lane behind it, and the wall that ran blind for sixty yards behind the church to the water stair, and the ledge above the stair where a level voice had come up out of the dark and told Rylan to carry his lord down to the water.

It proved nothing. It would not stand up in any court in the realm. A travelling brother with a name that was very likely not his own, on a line in a country priest's book, sleeping sixty yards from a stair on the night a prince was carried down it. Keller would have looked at it for exactly as long as it took to read, and said it was a pattern.

It was everything.

Doran was looking at him.

"My lord," he said. "I asked you once, in the winter, whether I did wrong by keeping the book well." He put his hand flat on the page. "I'll ask again. I don't know what this line is to you. I can see that it's something." He waited. "Did I do wrong?"

In the winter Emeric had had no answer he could give a priest. He had one now.

"No, Father," he said. "Every other honest book in this kingdom has been used against us this winter. Yours is the only one that has him in it." He looked at the line under the old man's hand. "You kept it well. It's the one true thing on paper anywhere that he didn't know was there."

Doran looked down at the line for a long time.

Then he closed the book, gently, and put it back on the shelf with the others, in its place, and stood with his hand on its spine.

"I'll pray for him," he said. "Whoever he is. It's what the book's for, in the end."

Then he went down to the strongroom.

He went alone. He did not tell anyone he was going. He crossed the bridge in the dark, where Ward had stood at the rail that morning, and went through the old gate into the keep, and down the stair under the east tower, with a lamp, past the guardroom where the night man sat and got up when he saw who it was and sat down again when he was told to. The strongroom was at the bottom. It was dry, and it had a window high up with bars in it, and a table and a bed and a stool and a grille in the door. Rylan came down once a week and looked through the grille and did not speak. Saban came down when he had a thing to try. Emeric had not come down since the winter of the duke's file.

Marrow was sitting at the table. He had a book open in front of him, which he had never done before, that Emeric knew of; he had never asked for books. He looked up when the key went into the door, and saw who it was, and closed the book, and laid his hands flat on it, and waited.

He had got thinner in the years. The hair was grey now where it had been dark. The eyes were the same: pale, watchful, patient, the eyes of a man who had spent a long time in a small room keeping himself alive by saying one true thing at a time and never two.

Emeric came in and stood by the table. He did not sit. He did not put the sense out; he did not need to. He looked at the man with his eyes, the way he had looked at him the day of the coin.

He said one word.

"Augustine."

Marrow looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time. Longer than he had looked at the coin, the day his face moved and was put back. Emeric stood by the table and let him look. He did not ask anything. He had said the only word he had come down to say, and he waited, the way Saban waited in his counting house for a slow clerk to bring a roll up from the cellar.

Marrow's hands lay flat on the closed book. Something went across his face, around the mouth, very slightly, and this time he did not put it back.

"He never lets it out of his hand," Marrow said.

His voice was hoarse, and low, with the flat northern vowels in it. It was one sentence. It was, as all of them had been, true.

Then he turned his head and looked at the wall.

Emeric stood there a moment longer. Then he went out, and locked the door, and went up the stair past the night man, and out through the old gate, and back across the bridge in the dark, with the water black under the arches and the dome on the palace's crown faintly lit from inside, and his mother's other stone round his wrist on its cord, quiet.

He did not tell anyone that night. He went up to his rooms in the east range, past the door of the room where his father was sleeping, and sat on the edge of his bed with his hands between his knees, and looked at the bead on his wrist until the lamp went out.
