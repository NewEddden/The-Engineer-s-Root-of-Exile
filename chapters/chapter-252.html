Chapter 252 - Rack Dry

The people's train left the platform below the works at seven on the Monday morning, the seventh of June, with twelve people in it who had paid and forty-three who had not.

Eight coaches. Grayrock's wagon shop had built them over the spring, the first thing that shop had ever made that was not a wheel for an ore wagon, and Bryda's foreman had ridden them down on the Friday, walked the string twice touching every door handle, and gone home without a word, which Ferra said was the most satisfied she had ever seen a Grayrock man. Oak bodies on Kaldo's steel frames, dark green, Ivo's glass in the windows and the gold rose small on every door. Ash benches facing across an aisle. A dumb heat stone in a pierced iron box under every second bench, and a small black coal stove at the end of each coach with its pipe through the roof: the stones for the winter, the stove for the winter the stones went out. A guard on the back step of the last coach with his hand on the brake wheel and a whistle round his neck, which he had blown once already, at a quarter to seven, by mistake.

The booking window was a hatch in the platform shed with a clerk behind it and a dating press at his elbow the size of a bread bin. You gave him a Copper Rand; he put a stiff buff card with a number on it into the press and pulled the handle, and the press bit the date into it with a sound like a small dog snapping at a fly. Ottoline's tabulator would count the cards at night. Ferra had asked for that before she had asked for anything else.

The woman at the head of the line had been there since six, with a cake in a cloth for a sister in Grayrock who had had a baby in April. She took her card and turned it over and asked the clerk whether it would bring her back.

"Out and back is two," said the clerk.

She looked at him, and at the card, and at the train; and said, in the voice of a woman who had walked to Grayrock twice in her life and slept in a hedge both times, "And I'll be back for supper?"

"Half past six."

She put a second coin through the hatch without another word and got on the train with the cake held out in front of her in both hands like something that might go off.

Emeric watched from the end of the platform by the water crane, where he had come to stand so that nobody would make anything of it. Saban stood beside him turning his hat. Ferra stood on his other side with the rate book open on her arm, making a stroke for every paying passenger as they passed the window, which Ottoline would also do at night, and which Ferra was doing anyway because she wanted to see it with her own eyes before she saw it on a sheet.

It was the first thing in a fortnight the town had come out to look at that was not a burial, and it came: children along the fence above the cutting, and the mothers who had come to fetch them back and stayed, and the old men from the benches by the north well; and the forty-three on passes, Ilsa's clerks going back up to teach in Bryda's counting house and a dozen of Bram's apprentices bound for the pithead, standing on the platform in their good coats with the passes in their hats as if they had been asked to a wedding and did not know the family.

"Twelve Copper Rand," said Saban pleasantly, to the train. "And Wick to Grayrock and back twice a day, with eight coaches behind her, and a guard, and a clerk, and a press, and the coal."

"Nine Silver and a half a day," said Ferra, without looking up. "I gave you the sheet on Thursday. The ore train behind the same engine on the same rails makes forty. I've put the people's train on the ore train's page. The freight carries it. It always would have. I've only written it where you can see it."

"It's not the nine Silver."

"I know it's not."

"A fare anybody can pay," said Saban, "is a door anybody can walk through. Milford's half a day from Aldane's border by the drove road. And now any man with a Copper Rand and a good shawl can be in Kaldo by noon with a card in his hat that says he's entitled." He turned the hat once more. "I've spent five years making it hard to get into this town without my knowing."

"Every card's numbered," said Ferra. "Ottoline counts them at night, and by morning I'll know which number got off where. There's an arrivals book at the Grayrock landing and another at Milford, kept by Ilsa's clerks, who were taught to write by me." She made a stroke. "You'll have more names off that hatch in a week than you've had off the roads in a year. That's your door. Count it."

Saban looked at her with his face perfectly pleasant. Then he put his hat on and wrote something on his cuff.

"I'd ride it," said Emeric. "If anybody wants to know it's safe. I've a Copper Rand."

"The press does the date," said Ferra. "It doesn't do dukes."

"A little crown on the card. For a Silver."

"And every old woman in the coach," said Saban, "would want to know where you'd left the big one. Rylan's girl has told the whole lower ward you left it in the carriage. I've had it repeated to me in three taverns and once by a priest."

The guard blew his whistle, on purpose this time, and Wick took the strain at the front, and the couplings went tight down the string one after another like a man coming down a stair in boots, and the train moved: a walk, then faster than a walk, then faster than a horse, along the cutting under the children on the fence, who ran with it until the fence ran out. The woman's face went by in the third window, wide-eyed, with the cake in her lap. Then it was gone round the long bend toward Grayrock with its smoke going up in the still morning, and the children came back along the fence walking backward, watching where it had been.

Ferra closed the rate book on her pencil.

"A weekly ticket," she said. "Three days' fares for a week's riding, for anybody who works in one place and sleeps in the other. On the window by the first of July." She looked at Saban. "That's another two hundred cards a day. You'll want a bigger cuff."



The charge book was the Hod's: a long narrow book bound in grey with a brass corner on every page, like a ledger for a man who would carry it in the rain. Ferra kept it on its own shelf in the counting room and nobody else wrote in it. Ned brought the figures up the hill on a slate after every run and she copied them in: height by the house gauge; the hour the hatch opened and the hour it shut; which way the westerly had taken her and how hard Ned had leaned on the air against it; the moon; and, tier by tier, both sides, how long each tier of the racks had taken to fill. She had kept it for ten months. It was the dullest book in Kaldo. She said so herself.

On the Tuesday evening, with the counting room shuttered and the first day's cards in their rubber band waiting for Ottoline, she put it open in front of him and put her finger on a column.

"There."

She had ruled it in pencil at the right-hand edge of every page since April, which meant she had not yet decided it was a column. Against every run, one letter. T, for a night Ned had held her over the town. W, for a night he had leaned her twenty miles west into the westerly, out over the hill country beyond the Milford road, and let it carry her home, because Ferra had priced the long run against a night's holding over the town and the racks paid for the air. And beside the letters, the top tier's filling time.

He ran his eye down it, and then again, slower.

The W nights were faster. Not every one; the moon moved things, and the wind. But taken all together, every tier, both sides, and she had taken them all together at the foot of the page in ink: the west a fifth quicker.

"Same height," said Ferra. "Same hour; I took out every run that went up after midnight. Same moon, near enough. Same stones. Same crew." She took her finger off the page. "A fifth. Twenty miles."

He went through it as he would a fault in a machine. The wind was the same wind both ways. The hills were higher by a few hundred feet, and the Band was four miles up. The town drinking from the sky over it? From four miles up Kaldo was a faint warmth on the ground that the Band minded about as much as the sea minds a boy fishing off a pier. He came out the far end of all of it with nothing in his hands.

"I don't know," he said.

He took his notebook out and wrote, under three lines about the bridge piers, in pencil, without a figure anywhere in it:

The Band isn't even.

He underlined it once and shut the book.

"You've written a sentence with no number in it," said Ferra.

"I haven't got a number."

"You've got mine."

"A fifth over twenty miles. I don't know what it is over forty." He put the notebook away. "I'll want Ned to go further."

"You'll want to pay for it," said Ferra, and wrote a figure on her slate, and he did not ask what it was.

Bram had been at the end of the table the whole time, up about the coaches' brake blocks, which were wearing faster than he liked on the bank down to the Grayrock landing; and he had been reading the charge book across the table upside down, the way he read every drawing and ledger left open within six feet of him, with his spectacles on the end of his nose.

"Not that column," said Bram. "That one."

He put a thick finger on the bottom tier's filling time and drew it across to the hour the hatch shut, and back, run by run, down the page, the way he would run a finger along a bar to see if it was straight.

"Bottom tier's full at an hour and a half," he said. "Every stone in her. And she sits up there another hour after it with the hatch open. Every night. What's she doing that hour?"

"Filling the house stones off the drawers," said Ferra. "The head tank off the cold trap. The drawers going down the trap into the racks."

"And when they're all full? Racks, drawers, tank, house. And she's still sitting up there an hour because it's cheaper to sit than come down and go up again." He tapped the page. "What's coming into her?"

"As much as at the start," said Emeric. "More, on the west nights."

"And where's it going?"

"Past her."

Bram took his spectacles off and held them in his fist.

"Like the river went past this town four hundred years before anybody put a wheel in it." He looked at the page. "What's it for?"

"It isn't for anything. It's the sky."

"The river was the river," said Bram, "till you put a wheel in it. Then it was the mill." He put the spectacles back on. "I don't like a thing going to waste over my head every night. Find it something."

He took his note about the brake blocks and went out, and they heard him go down the stair one step at a time, the way he took every stair now, and they waited until the street door shut below.

Ferra drew her finger down the bottom-tier column herself, and turned to a clean page at the back of the book, and wrote at the top of it, SURPLUS, and nothing under it.

"He's always right," said Emeric. "It's very tiring."

"That's Rylan's," said Ferra. "About Ward." Her mouth did something at one corner. "My father said it on the platform on Friday. About you. And told me I wasn't to tell you." She shut the charge book. "So I've told you."

She opened the trade book at the paper page. The presses had eaten every sheet the mill made since the spring, and Ferra had stopped selling outside in March without saying so; the price in the capital had doubled since midwinter, and Rowena's steward had written, which was worse than a dealer writing twice.

"Milford grows straw," said Ferra. "Every steading has a rick of it nobody wants by March. You can make paper of straw. Hollis says his grandmother wrapped butter in it. And Grayrock has a river with a fall below the landing, a town full of rags, and a wood." She wrote one line under the page's last figure, Milford, a straw mill; Grayrock, a rag and wood mill; ordered, and the date, and pushed the book across with the pencil on it for his initial.

He initialled it.

"You didn't argue," said Ferra.

"There was nothing wrong with it."

She looked at him across the lamp with her chin on her hand for longer than the paper needed. She had sat beside him through all of it: the loft, the three carried down out of it, the fourth in Imani's end bed with the lines on his arm that would not close.

The door opened and Imani came in with her bag on her shoulder and her sleeves still rolled.

"It's half past nine," she said.

"Work first," said Ferra.

"I've had a cart wheel over a carter's foot and a baby feet first, and a man down from Milford on that train with eleven teeth that want out, who came for a Copper Rand because it was cheaper than the drove road and says he'll ride it back on Thursday for the rest just for the ride." She took Emeric's pulse at the wrist without asking, with her eyes on the clock. "Sixty-six. So that's working. I thought you'd want to know."

"Fun later," said Ferra, and blew out the lamp with all three of them still at the table, so that the only light in the room was the line under the shutter from the ward below. "It's later."



The Hod went up at ten on the Wednesday from the reeds, on the western run, with three aboard.

Cade had the racks. They were his the way the yard's ropes were his, and nobody went down the trap on a flying night without his say. Since the winter Fletcher had built them to come out whole: eight oak frames, twenty-six or twenty-seven stones a frame in straw, pinned at the top under the hatch coaming and at the foot to a bearer; and in her flanks either side of the hold, above the waterline, an iron door the height of a frame, so that at the works' wharf a frame could run out on its skids onto the quay in a minute instead of two hundred stones being handed up a ladder. Cade stood at the top of the plank in the last of the light with his chute on his back and counted them in through the side doors and drove the pins home with his maul; and the stones lay in their straw grey and asleep, two hundred and twelve of them, like eggs.

Bren Tallis was on the reed bank. He was not supposed to be. He had come down after the evening drill in Ward's yard and carried straw for the frames that nobody had asked him to carry, and he stood at the foot of the plank with his hands in his pockets looking up at Ned in the house door, as he had on half the flying nights since September.

"It's not your night," said Ned, from the door, without looking at him.

"It's nobody's night. It's three of you."

"Two's the book. Three's two and one more." Ned looked at him then. "Go home. Your mother'll have my ears."

"She's my mother. She can have my ears."

"She'll have mine. She always has." Ned stood aside from the door for Tobe. "Go home, Bren."

Bren went home. He went slowly, along the reed bank toward the lamps on the lake path, and stopped twice and looked back, and the second time Cade lifted a hand to him from the plank and he lifted one back, and went on.

Inside, the house was the house: Ned at the helm post under the cupola with the steady humming at his feet; the drawers in their case under the crown, the top one working; the bypass's cap locked at the third shade, and Imani's key to it on the far side of the lake; the probe grey on the wall; the masks on their hooks; and in the after corner, by the window cut two fingers low, the chair. Its strap was buckled across the seat on flying nights now, since the spring, so that nobody would put a coat on it. Cade thought Tobe had begun that.

Nobody looked at the chair. Everybody always did.

"Seal," said Ned, at a mile, in the cloud, and Tobe went round the dogs on the door with his hand, and the pump began under the floor, and they went up.



She came through the top of the cloud at a mile and a quarter into a sky with no moon in it yet and the stars very thick, and turned west, and climbed.

Cade liked the climb, and had never told anybody. He liked watching Ned fly, all small movements and none wasted, like a man rowing a boat he had rowed all his life. And since the mark there was the other thing, which he told nobody either: that somewhere past two miles his back stopped. Not stopped hurting; stopped being there, as if somebody had taken it off him and hung it on a hook, and it stayed on the hook until they came down through the cloud again, and he sat on the bench and felt twenty.

At four miles the probe lit.

Ned said "Hm," and eased the air a notch as the stones outside felt it and leaned. Cade took his mask off its hook and got into his fur. "Masks," said Ned, and they put them on, and Tobe knocked the dogs off the door, and the cold came in like a slap, and they went out on their lines along the rail to the hatch, and Cade put his shoulder to the winch, and the hatch cover ran back on its rollers, rumbling, and the Band came down into the racks.

He could not feel it come. He was not a mage. But he could see it: two hundred and twelve stones below the open hatch going from grey to lit a tier at a time from the top down, like a street at dusk when the lamplighter has been along it. Then he went down the ladder with the shuttered lamp, as he always did, and began moving the bottom tier's stones up to the top by feel.

It was a good night. The westerly came at her bow hard and steady and Ned leaned the air into it the way a lighterman holds a barge against the ebb, and the racks filled; quicker than on the town nights, Cade thought, coming up the ladder at the hour, and did not know why he thought so, and did not say. He took the long brass-jointed pole with the shrimper's hoop forward along the rail to the bow, and stood in the dark with it out over the side, waiting for the starlight to come off something small going by.

He got two. They came along at the height of the deck a few yards apart, drifting on the westerly slower than the wind, as if the air were thicker for them than for him; he missed the first and had the second, and then the first came round in an eddy off her bow and he had that too. He took them into the house in the net and held them out to Ned in his glove, two rough grey stones the size of a thumb's end, warm through the leather, and Ned said "Hm," and put out his hand.

"The duke'll have those," said Ned.

"He'll have the next ones. I'm keeping one."

"You're not."

"I'm not," Cade agreed, and began to sing, at the winch, flat on every third note, which was the sign on every run that the racks were full and the night was going well; and Tobe, on the bench, said "Oh, no," into his mask, as he did every run.

At an hour and forty the rail cracked.



Cade heard it first, though afterward he was not sure he had heard anything.

He was walking aft from the bow along the starboard rail with the pole under his arm, past the open hatch, when something went below him in the hold: a small dry tick, like a slate splitting in a cold grate, from the stern end, under the house, where the rail came down from the helm post and ran forward under the deck to the lift stones. One tick. He stopped and listened, and there was nothing else.

Then the deck went soft under his boots.

Not a lurch. A settling, the thing a lighter does when her keel takes the ground on a falling tide and her whole weight begins to be held up by something less. A hand's breadth, and another. Through the panes he saw Ned's head come round.

He was in the house in four steps, with the pole on the deck behind him and the door dogged behind him, and his mask off.

"She's sinking," he said.

"I know she's sinking." Ned had the lift lever over as far as it went. "Bank's on. Bank's full; you've been singing at it for half an hour." His eyes were on the bank's needle on the wall, hard over, the racks pouring into the rail as fast as it would take. "Everything's going in. And she's not getting it."

The reserve's needle, beside it, began to fall.

The six reserve stones beside the lift stones were never drawn on in the Band; they never had to be. Cade watched their needle go down the dial like the level in a cask with the bung out.

"Tobe," said Ned. "The trap."

Tobe had the trap up and was down the ladder with the lamp into the hold at the helm post's foot, where the flasks stood in their quilted coats and the rail came down in its iron sleeve and turned forward. Cade went after him, his mask on again; he did not remember putting it on.

The lamp showed nothing. The sleeve was the sleeve, iron, rimed white with the cold, running forward along the hold's floor between the frames. Tobe went along it on his knees with the lamp held low. Cade went beside him with his gloved hand an inch above the iron, not touching it, the way you feel along a pipe for the place a joint is hot.

Two yards from the helm post's foot, under the after edge of the hatch, his hand went cold.

Not colder than the hold. The hold was cold enough to kill a man in an hour without his fur. It was a cold that moved. A draught, out of the iron, against his palm through the glove, steady, hard, like the draught under a door on a winter night; and he looked at the lamp in Tobe's hand a foot away, the flame upright behind its shutter, and the flame did not lean. Not a hair. There was a wind on his hand and no wind on the flame.

He had felt that once before, at the stone-keeper's drill in the shed, when the duke had let a stone go on purpose to show them: a gust that moved nothing. That's what it is, the duke had said, when what's in a stone goes out of it with nowhere to be. You feel it as weather even if you're nobody. It isn't weather.

"Here," he said. "It's coming out here."

Tobe put his own hand down beside Cade's and held it there. He was a foundry man. He knew what a crack in a thing did before he could see one.

"The stone's gone," he said. "Inside. The sleeve's whole. The rail's cracked under it." He took his hand away. "Everything that goes down that rail goes out of it here. The bank and all."

They went up the ladder.



Ned had done the next thing already. He had the head tank open.

It stood beside the latch on the house's starboard wall in its iron jacket, the tank the cold trap filled every run, and it had Bram's metered valve onto the rail that let it out a little at a time, inside the warm where nothing froze: a long reserve. Ned had turned it all the way. Through the sight slit Cade could see the blue going down.

And she slowed. A little. The deck stopped settling under them and settled more slowly, a finger's breadth at a time instead of a hand's; and Ned watched the gauge on the house wall, the height, and said, flat, "Three miles and three quarters," and then, a while later, "Three and a half," and did not say anything else.

"It's going out of the crack," said Cade. "Most of it. Whatever goes in."

"I know." Ned's eyes went to the latch. "That's the one I'm watching."

Cade looked at it too: the leather lung on its brass frame, and the catch below it that would drop the moment the lung went slack and loose the drogue and fire the horn. It saved a ship whose stones died. The bellows stood full and taut. The lift stones under the deck were not dead. They were drinking with everything they had and getting a trickle of it through a rail that let the rest go as a wind that moved nothing. They would never go still enough for the catch. They would go thin, and slow, and the lung would hang full above them the whole way down.

"It's waiting for them to die," said Cade. "And they won't. Not quick enough for it."

"No," said Ned. "So we'll not wait for it." He looked round at them both. "Say it."

"Ninety," said Tobe. "Four flasks. Bank off, reserve off, flask. Then the next. Six minutes."

"Six minutes to do what?"

Nobody answered him. He had not wanted an answer. He wanted them to know it.

The head tank's sight slit went dark at three and a quarter miles. Ned put the bank lever back, and the reserve's, and said "First," and Tobe went down the trap and opened the first flask a full turn, as the book said, and Cade, at the top of the trap, felt it go through the floor, through the boards, through his boots: a shove, a flood, a long hard push out of the hold and up through the rail; and most of it, he knew, going straight out of the crack two yards forward in a gale that would not have moved a candle; and some of it, enough, reaching the lift stones in the first breath, so that the Hod came up under them like a boat lifting to a wave, and hung, and Tobe in the hold began to count aloud.

At ninety she was sinking again.

"Second," said Ned.

By the third Cade had stopped listening to the count. He was doing the only sum that mattered, four flasks against the gauge, and getting the same figure every time; and he looked at Ned and saw that Ned had done it too.

"She's too heavy," Cade said.

Ned looked at him.

"Two ton of stone in the racks." Cade was already taking his mask off its hook. "It's no good to her; on, it pours out of the crack faster than she can drink. It's only weight now." He looked forward through the panes at the side doors. "The frames come out. That's what they're built for."

"Over the side." Ned's voice did not change. "The whole season's charge."

"Every stone in them."

Ned looked at the gauge. Three miles. He looked at the chair in the after corner with its strap buckled across it. Then he looked at Cade, and Cade saw him decide, and saw what it cost him, a lighterman who had never in his life put cargo over the side and had told men who did that they were not lightermen.

"I'll heel her," said Ned. "To port first. You'll have the frames run to the door on their own if I give her enough. Tell me when." He put his hand to the trim keys. "Tobe. The third flask, and then come up and help him. Mask."



Afterward Cade could not remember the cold, only that his fingers stopped answering by the third frame and he drove the pins with the heel of his hand, and that the door's dogs had frozen in their seats and he beat them off with the maul. He remembered the door swinging out onto nothing, the black and the stars and, far down, the faint white floor of the cloud. Ned's voice through the speaking tube, "Heel coming," and the deck tilting to port, and the first frame groaning on its skids, and then running, twenty-six full stones in oak, out of the door and gone without a sound; and he had leaned out after it on his line and watched it go down, a faint glow, tumbling, smaller, until he could not see it.

Four out of the port door. Then Ned brought her level, and over to starboard, and Tobe was beside him at the second door with his own maul, and four more.

Two tons. A season.

When the last went, the deck came up under his boots. He felt it come. Not much; but the Hod, lighter by a third of her load, came up a hand's breadth on the fourth flask's last push, and hung there a breath, two, and then began slowly to sink.

They dogged the doors. They went back along the rail on their lines into the house, and Tobe shut the door and dogged it, and the pump began to thump, and Cade took his mask off and stood in the warm with his hands hanging at his sides like a pair of gloves on a line, and looked at the gauge.

Two miles and three quarters.

The flasks were empty. The tank was empty. The reserve's needle lay on its pin. The bank was gone over the side. Under the deck the six lift stones were holding the Hod up with what they had left in them, which was not much, and what came to them down a cracked rail from nothing.

And above a mile you stayed with her. It was the second line in the book. The duke had said why, at the long table, with his hand flat beside Ned's harness: half an hour hanging in the dark under silk, the cold, the westerly, twenty miles east in the sea with your hands dead.

Cade looked at the rail.

A hand's breadth above the floor at the helm post's foot the sleeve was cut away, a hand's width, and the bare stone showed through, worn smooth and a little dark from hands. They had all put their hands on it at the drill, every month. A farmhand can push into a rail for a count of forty, the duke had said. A man who's carried stones for years can do more.

He knelt down on the house floor and put both his hands on it.

He did not know how. At the drill he pushed with a face like a man at stool and felt nothing, and the rail held for his count of forty because the duke said it had. He shut his eyes and pushed the same way now, with the whole of him, at nothing.

And it went.

He felt it go. That was the thing he would try to tell Fletcher afterward, at the yard, and could not, and stopped trying. He felt something come up out of him from somewhere under his ribs, where his back hurt on the ground and did not hurt up here, and go down his arms and out of his palms into the stone, warm, steady, a thing like pouring water out of a jug, if the jug were you; and the rail took it. And under the deck, a long way off, at the other end of what he was pouring into, he felt a thing he had never felt in his life: six small hungry things in the dark under his feet, taking what he gave them.

Tobe knelt down beside him and put his hands on the rail beside Cade's.

The deck stopped sinking.

"Hm," said Ned. Then, quite quietly, to nobody, "Well."

It did not hold her up. Most of what they gave went out of the crack as wind; Cade knew it the way he knew where the crack was. But it held her enough, and Ned did the rest. He put her nose down a little on the trim keys and gave the air a notch, and the air stones in her channels, which had nothing to do with any rail and were as full as at the top, pushed her forward; and she went down and forward together on a long slow slant, the way a gull comes off a cliff with its wings set, and the westerly ran behind her like a mill race and carried her east.

"Coast," said Ned. "We'll want water under us. I'll not put her into a field in the dark." He looked at the gauge. "Count."

They took turns, one pouring while the other rested his hands beside him on the stone, and both together when the gauge went too fast; and Ned called the height every quarter mile and nothing else, and the frost went out of the panes, and the cloud came up under them, and the panes ran wet.

Somewhere in the cloud Cade's hands began to burn.

Not hot. The other way. It began in the middle of his palms, where they lay flat on the stone, as a burn from a frozen iron begins, sharp and then dull; and it went up into his wrists, and he kept pushing, because Ned said "A mile and a half," and then "A mile and a quarter," and the house was going down through the cloud on two men's hands and the air.

"A mile," said Ned.

Below a mile, go. It was in the book under Ward's line. Ned was looking at the chutes on their hooks.

"She floats," said Tobe, with his eyes shut, pushing.

"She floats," Ned agreed. "And three men in the sea at night off a marsh come up in the morning with the crabs on them, chute or no chute." He took his hand off the trim keys and put it back. "We ride her in. Keep pouring."

They came out of the bottom of the cloud at half a mile into a dark that had a different dark under it, flat, faintly paler, with a line along its edge that was the coast and the marsh behind it; and the sea came up, black and wrinkled and wide, with no light on it anywhere; and Ned brought her round into what wind there was at the bottom of the sky, as a lighterman brings a loaded boat round to come alongside, and said "Now, both, everything you've got," and they gave it, and she came down on the sea a mile off the marsh flat on her bottom with a noise like a door slammed in a cellar, and bounced, and came down again, and stopped.

Cade took his hands off the rail.

He knelt on the house floor and looked at them in the light of the shaded bulb over the dials. They were his hands. Across both palms, from the heel to the root of the fingers, ran fine raised lines, red, thin as threads, crossing and branching, like the frost that had been in the corners of the panes at four miles. He put the back of one against his cheek. It was hot.

Under the floor, a long way down, he heard water coming into the hold.



She floated. She was a lighter. Her bottom was split aft of amidships where she had come down on it, and the sea came into the hold until it stood a foot under the deck beams and stopped, and she sat low and level with the house dry on her stern; and the three of them sat on the house floor in the dark with their backs to the wall and listened to the water under the floor find its level.

Then Ned got up, because there was a dawn coming, and a dawn meant boats.

They laced the canvas hood down over the cupola, so that she was a lighter with a deckhouse and nothing else. Then Ned stood on her deck in the first grey looking at the lugsail lashed along her rail, its mast stepped and unstepped a hundred times on the lake for the drill and never once raised in earnest; and he stepped it and raised it, and took the axe off its clips on the house wall and cut it through a yard above the deck in six strokes, and let the top go over the side with the sail still furled on its yard.

"A squall," he said. "Two hours after midnight. Out of the north-west, out of nothing, the way they come in June. Took the mast. We were laden, and she was taking water over the side, and we put the cargo over to keep her up." He looked at them both. "Ore. For the works. Our cargo. Over the side."

"Ore," said Tobe.

"And your hands." Ned looked at their hands. "Rope. The tackle ran when the mast went and you held it. Burned your hands on the tackle." He threw the axe down on the deck. "That's all it is. A lighter blown out past the bar in a squall and stove on a sandbank coming in. It happens every year. Nobody looks at it twice."

It was full light when the fishing boat came out of the marsh channel, a black-sailed boat with two men and a boy in her, and stood toward them, and came alongside; and the older of the two men stood in her bow and looked at the lighter low in the water with her mast gone and her three men on her deck with their hands held out in front of them like men who had picked up a hot pan; and asked them what had happened.

Ned told him.

The man looked at the cut mast, and the empty sea, and the sky, where the last of the night's cloud was going off east in rags.

"A squall," he said.

"Out of nothing," said Ned. "The way they come in June."

The fisherman nodded, slowly, the way a man nods at weather he has seen before, and threw them a line.



The Hod was due on the reeds at half past twelve.

Emeric was there at twelve, as he was on every run night now; Ferra with the charge book and a lamp, because it was her book; Imani with her bag, because every man off the Hod had his wrist taken on the bank before he went home. Nobody had told any of them to be there.

At half past twelve the sky over the lake was empty.

At a quarter to one it was empty. Emeric stood at the water's edge with his sense out as far as it would go, which was a long way now, over the lake and the hills and up into the dark; and as far as it went there was nothing.

At one he said, "The ship."

Ward had been on the bank the whole time, a little way off, in the dark, as Ward was on the bank on most nights the Hod flew without his ever having said he would be; and he came now, and stood beside Emeric, and did not argue. He said, "Fletcher," and went for him himself.

The K Discovery came out of her shed at a quarter past one with her lamps dark and Fletcher at the bow helm and Ward at the top one and Emeric in the stone-keeper's station with every stone she had in his head at once, and went up off the lake into the west on the line of Ferra's book, the western run, because that was where the Hod had been going.

He would remember how big the dark was. He had flown at night a hundred times and never thought about its size, because there had never been anything in it he needed to find. They went west along the run's line at a mile, under the cloud, with his sense spread on every side as wide and thin as it would go, over the hill country and the dark farms and the pale strip of the Milford road. Behind them, when he looked back once, the town's west half was lit for the first week of its life, the new lamps along the lanes steady and white, every one the same, on Tess's new current that went back and forth in the wires sixty times a second. He looked away from them, into the dark.

Nothing in the west. Not a note. Twenty miles of hill country with sheep in it and farms asleep in it and a shepherd on a hillside under a sheepskin with his dog, and that was all.

"The westerly," said Ward, at the speaking tube. "Four miles up it runs east."

They ran with it. East, back over the town, over the lake where Ferra's lamp was a spark on the reeds, over the works, over the marsh, with the reeds going by black under the keel and the herons getting up out of them, and out over the coast; and the sky in the east was going grey along the sea's edge, and the sea came out of the dark under them flat and pale and empty.

And there they were.

He felt them before he saw anything. Three notes, low on the water a mile off the marsh, very small at that distance. Ned's, slow and steady. And Tobe's and Cade's, which he knew as well as Ferra's step on the stair, and which were wrong: brighter than he had ever felt them, nothing like a knight's but brighter, with a raw edge on them both that he did not know and did not like.

Alive. Three. On the water.

He said it to the speaking tube, and heard Ward say nothing, and heard Fletcher at the bow helm let out a breath that came up the tube like a gust.

The fishing boat came out of the marsh channel twenty minutes later, in full light, under a black sail, and stood toward the lighter; and the K Discovery held where she was, high up in the morning's first thin cloud over the coast, a thing nobody on the water would look up for and nobody would see if they did, while Emeric watched through the bow's port with Fletcher's spyglass. The lighter, low, square, black, with a stump where a mast had been. Three men on her deck. The fishing boat came alongside. A line went over.

"He's cut his mast," said Fletcher, with the glass. He was quiet. "He's cut his own mast and put it over the side so they'll believe in a squall." He lowered the glass. "There's not a lighterman on the river would have thought of that but Ned."

The fishermen took her in tow, slowly, toward the marsh channel. Emeric watched until the two boats were in the channel and the reeds had them, and then he told Ward to take the ship home.



The yard's launch went down the river at seven with two of Fletcher's men and a bag of silver from Saban, to find out which fishing hamlet the lighter had been towed into and pay its salvage like any lighter's owner would, and bring the crew home.

That left the waiting.

He waited on the shed's slip, because that was where the launch would come back to, with the K Discovery dark in the shed behind him and the lake in front of him going from grey to blue to bright, and Ferra on his left and Imani on his right. Neither of them had slept. Neither had he. Nobody suggested it.

Ferra sat on the slip's edge with the charge book on her knees. At eight she opened it at the night's page, every column empty but the first, and closed it. At nine Imani took his wrist and held it a full minute by Fletcher's yard clock, and did not tell him the number, which told him what it was.

"They're alive," he said. "I felt them."

"You felt them on the water," said Imani. "In a lighter with her bottom out, Fletcher says, and men on the deck holding their hands out in front of them." She looked at the lake. "I'll believe what I see when they come up that river."

Bren came at ten.

He came along the lake path at a walk that wanted to be a run, with last night's straw still in his hair, because somebody at the yard had said something in the street; and Emeric told him: alive, all three, a fishing boat with them, the launch gone to bring them home. Bren listened standing quite still. Then he sat down at the bottom of the slip with his back to them and his arms round his knees, looking at the river mouth.

Ned Tallis was his uncle. It was his uncle's boat in the sea.

After a while Imani went down and sat on the stone beside him, not touching, and looked at the river mouth with him; and then Ferra sat down on his other side with the charge book; and Emeric, at the top of the slip, stood.

Armand came at noon. He came down from the keep in Wexler's coat with his stick, slowly, without anybody's help, which Imani would have had something to say about at any other time; and stood at the top of the slip a little apart from Emeric and looked down the lake at the river mouth with the rest of them.

Nobody said anything about the chair. Imani had grounded him in the spring, after he came down grey and shaking from the foot of the Band, and put him in a chair in the dome at Ferra's line instead, and he had been sour about it for a month. He stood in his thief's good coat with both hands on his stick and his eyes on the river; and when Imani came back up the slip he moved the stick to let her by, and she stayed beside him after that until four, and neither of them looked at the other.



The launch came up the river at four, against the current, with the two yard men at the oars and three more men in the stern sheets with their hands in their laps.

Ned got out first. He came up the slip on both legs at the same pace and stopped in front of Emeric and took his cap off, which he did not do for bishops, and held it in both hands, and took his time, because he had never in his life said a thing that did not need saying.

"She's on a mud berth in the hamlet at the channel mouth," he said at last. "Fletcher's men have paid for her. She'll want a new bottom from the third frame aft." He turned his cap round. "And racks. And a mast."

"You cut your own mast."

"A squall took it." Ned did not smile. "Two hours after midnight. Out of nothing." He looked down at the cap. "The fisherman's telling it already. He saw the mast go, he says. By Sunday he'll have seen it bigger." He turned the cap again. "I'll want to send him something, when it's done. Not money. He'd know what money meant."

Behind him Bren had come up the slip from the bottom and stopped two steps below, and Ned turned his head and saw him, and something went across Ned's face that Emeric had not seen on it in a year of mornings at the reeds; and then Ned put out one hand and took the boy by the back of the neck, hard, and held it, and let it go.

"Your mother," said Ned.

"I know."

"She'll have my ears."

"She'll have both of ours," said Bren, and his voice went in the middle, and he looked at the lake.

Imani had the other two by then.

She had them sitting side by side on the slip's edge with their hands palms up on their knees, and knelt in front of them with a lamp lit in the middle of the afternoon, held low and close.

The lines were still there. Fine, raised, red, thin as threads, across both palms of both men from the heel of the hand to the root of the fingers, crossing and branching. Cade's were worse than Tobe's. She put the back of her own fingers to Tobe's palm, lightly, and took them away.

"Hot," she said.

Ferra's hand went flat on the charge book. Bren, at the top of the slip, saw the lines and went white. Everyone there had been in the square in May with Rylan on the ward steps and the steam coming off him, and everyone had seen Ivo's plates or heard about them. Lines that started and did not close.

Imani turned Cade's hand over in hers and looked at the back of it. Clean. She turned it again, and ran her thumb very lightly along one of the lines on his palm, and watched his face while she did it; and then she looked at his wrist, and the inside of his forearm, up to the elbow, with the lamp, slowly, and then Tobe's.

"It's not tracery," she said.

She said it at once, and plainly, and loud enough for the slip, and Emeric heard Bren breathe out at the top of it.

"Tracery's under the skin. Silver, when it's made, and it doesn't stop at the wrist. These are in the skin. On it. They've stopped where your hands stopped." She set Cade's hand back on his knee. "It's a burn. You've drawn more out through your palms than the skin of a palm was made to pass, for longer than it was made to pass it, and it's burned you on the way out. Like a rope does going through your hands too fast." She looked at Ned, briefly. "So your story's nearer the truth than you meant it to be."

"How long?" said Tobe.

"Weeks. They'll fade. Cool water morning and night and nothing in them heavier than a cup until I say." She stood up and blew the lamp out. "And neither of you is to put his hands on a rail, or a stone, or anything with a stone in it, until I've seen them every day for a fortnight. I'll write it in the crew book myself."

Cade looked down at his hands on his knees.

"We poured," he said, to Emeric, like a boy with stones in his net. "Into the rail. From two miles and three quarters to the water. Your farmhand does a count of forty. We did..." He stopped. "I never counted."

"Twenty-five minutes," said Ned. "By the house clock."

Emeric looked at their palms.

He knew what it was; he had known it at dawn from the raw edge on their notes. Nine months at the mark, three hours a night, at a rate Imani had chosen and Armand had measured and he had asked for and called comfort; and two men who had never had the rite and were not mages had put out of themselves, through a cracked rail, for twenty-five minutes, what no farmhand in the realm could have, and did not know why they could, and were looking at him to be told. Kaldo's first inducted men had cast, at two miles up over the sea at night, because there was nothing else left.

He would tell them. Not on a slip. Not today, with their hands like that. In the long room, all of them, everyone who had ever stood a watch in a house at the mark, and all of it, with nothing kept back. He had owed them that for nine months.

"A flask's a bucket," he said, to Cade. "You saw. You pour a bucket into a cracked rail and the crack has most of it. You two were a tap." He looked at both of them. "That's what kept her up. Not how much. How steady."

Cade thought about that, with his hands on his knees.

"A tap," he said, and almost laughed, and did not, and looked at his palms.

Ned put his cap back on.

"Three aboard," he said. He said it to nobody; to the lake. "Two's the book. We had one more, and it wasn't enough. I had my hand on the levers. They had theirs on the rail." He was quiet. "One flew, and two poured, and there was nobody spare. Not a hand. If one of them had been sick in his mask on the way down, or fainted, or done what that one does with his back and not told anybody..." He did not look at Cade. "Nobody spare."

He turned round and looked at Bren, at the top of the slip, for a long breath, and did not say anything to him at all; and then went up the lake path toward the town, on both legs, at the same pace, to tell Bren's mother before anybody else did.



He fell asleep first.

Imani had never seen him do it. She lay in his bed in the dark with her head on his left shoulder and her hand flat on his chest, and felt him go: one breath, the next longer, his whole weight settling under her the way a house settles at night, and his heart under her palm slowing from sixty-eight to sixty to fifty-four.

She lay still and counted it anyway.

On his other side, in the dark, Ferra moved her head on his right shoulder.

"Is he?" said Ferra. Very low.

"Fifty-four."

"Asleep, I meant."

"That's what fifty-four means." Imani did not move her hand. "He went at the third breath after the stair clock. I've never seen it. Not once. I've never been awake when he went. It's always him lying there doing his sums at the ceiling while we..." She stopped.

"While we sleep," said Ferra, "and he lies there all night with his eyes open looking as if somebody's taken the skin off him, and comes down in the morning looking like a sheet that's been through the mangle, and everybody at the table looks at him and then at us." In the dark Imani heard her smile. "Ward looks at us as if we'd kept him up."

"Ward will look at him tomorrow."

"Tomorrow it'll be us."

They lay still. He breathed between them, slow and deep, with one arm under each of them, and did not stir. Somewhere far down in the keep a door shut, and the stair clock struck the half hour.

"He hasn't argued with me in a fortnight," said Ferra.

"Since the loft."

"Since the loft. I gave him the paper mills on Tuesday with a mistake in them, on purpose. I put the Grayrock mill above the fall, where the March flood comes over the weir. Small, in pencil, where he always looks." Ferra was quiet. "He initialled it."

Imani thought about that for a while.

"Did you fix it?"

"Of course I fixed it. This morning, while he was on the slip." Ferra moved her head on his shoulder. "That's not the point."

"No."

"He had it printed on the bread's paper, Imani. And four boys went up a ladder with a sack of it anyway, and he's carried them up and down the stair for a fortnight like a coal scuttle." Ferra's voice did not change. "Last night he found three men by their notes on the sea in the dark. And tonight he came home, and fell asleep."

"Because they were alive," said Imani.

"Because they were alive and nobody had to be carried."

Imani lay with her hand on his chest, fifty-four, fifty-four, and thought about Cade's palms in her lamp, the red threads that stopped at the wrist; and the boy in her end bed with the grey lines up his forearm that did not close, who looked at them every morning when he woke as if they might have gone in the night. Twenty-five minutes into a cracked rail, and burns that would fade in a month. A sack of bread in a night. The same thing in them, going out or coming in. The total decides where you land, Armand said, and the rate decides whether you live.

"He'll tell them," she said. "The crew. What the mark's done to them. He said so on the slip; I saw his face."

"I saw it."

"He'll want to tell them all of it. With nothing kept back."

"Then let him," said Ferra. "He's owed it them since September." She was quiet. "And he'll do it badly. He'll make it about him, the way he does with a thing he's ashamed of: half an hour of what he did and why he's sorry, and never once what it means for them." She moved closer under the blanket. "I'll tell him. Before. He'll argue."

"Good," said Imani.

"Good," Ferra agreed.

The clock struck one, and then two. Neither of them slept. He did, between them, with his face turned up and his mouth a little open, and once in the night he said something in his sleep in his own tongue, three words, that neither of them knew; and Ferra, in the dark, said them over once under her breath, the way she said a figure she meant to keep; and Imani did not ask her to say them again, and lay with her hand on his heart, counting it, and lost the count somewhere past three, and did not start again.

In the morning he came down the keep stair at seven, rested, with his locs tied back and his face clear, and sat down at the long table and ate four eggs.

Ferra came down at a quarter past with the paper mills' page in her hand, and her collar done up wrong by one stud. Imani came at half past with rings under her eyes like bruises and sat down without a word and drank Maren's coffee black.

Ward, at the end of the table, looked at the three of them, one after another, slowly. Then he got up, and poured Imani a second cup of Maren's coffee without being asked, which he had never done for anybody at that table, and sat down again; and Maren, coming through the door with the bread, saw him do it, and stopped in the door, and went out again.
