Chapter 173 - First Blood at Harrow Bridge

The carter's name was Abel Dunmore, and he had driven paper for the works on the Wayford road for three years, and he had paid Lisle's toll at Harrow Bridge eleven times since the spring without a word, and on the twelfth time he said a word.

Emeric heard it from four people before noon, and none of them told it the same way.

Pennock's runner told it first, at the counting house in Wayford, out of breath at the door: that Dunmore had come up to the pole at first light with two bales and a box of pens, and the toll-keeper had charged him for the box as a bale, and Dunmore had said a box was not a bale, and the toll-keeper had called the men from the hut, and they had pulled him off his cart and beaten him in the road. Then Dunmore's boy told it, who had been on the cart, and who had run the mile to the town: that his father had said a good deal more than a box was not a bale, and that one of Lisle's men had hit him first, and then all of them. Then a woman who kept the ale-house at the bridge's south end told it to Saban, who had ridden down: that the carter had struck the toll-keeper first, or had lifted his whip to, she could not say which, it was dark still. And then Declan told it, at the end of the morning, standing in the counting-house yard with blood on his sleeve that was not his, and he told it the way Declan told everything, which was in order.

He had been on the road with the patrol, twelve of them, riding south from Wayford to meet the day's carts at the bridge and see them through, as Emeric had ordered after the mediation failed to come. They had heard the shouting from the top of the rise. They had come down the hill at a trot and found Dunmore on the ground by the pole with four of Lisle's men round him and a fifth kicking him, and the toll-keeper standing by the hut with his book held to his chest, and the carter's boy gone.

Declan had called out to them to stand off. They had not stood off. One of them had drawn.

"I told them twice," Declan said. "The second time I got down off the horse. I said, in the prince's name, stand off the man. The one who'd drawn came at me." He looked at his sleeve. "Colin Rusk got between us. He was on foot. He'd got down to help Dunmore up." He was quiet for a moment. "Then it was all of them."

Two of Lisle's men dead at the pole. Two of Declan's twelve dead: Colin Rusk, a garrison man of thirty with a wife on the island, with a sword through the body, and Tad Weller, one of Enno's first twenty, nineteen years old, who had been riding at the back and had come up the road at a run with his piece in his hands, not charged, because the rule said not charged on any road, and had swung it like a club at a man who was coming at Declan's back, and had been cut down doing it. The rest of Lisle's men had broken and run into the woods. The toll-keeper had run too. The patrol had pulled down the pole and broken the gate of the toll-yard behind the hut, where the tolls were kept, and found the box, and not touched it, and Declan had put two men on it and ridden up the goat track to the relay post with the rest and pulled the plank off its door and put the keeper's lamps, which had been in the hut all summer under a sack, back on the mast.

"The men in brown," Emeric said.

"Gone." Declan looked at him. "They weren't there, Highness. The ale-house woman says they rode north yesterday at dusk. All of them. Horses and all."

"Yesterday."

"Yesterday at dusk."

Emeric stood in the counting-house yard and thought about a room under the east tower at Kaldo with a window the width of a hand, and a man with a forgettable face standing by the wall, and one sentence.

Everything that had happened at the bridge that morning was true. Every one of the four stories was true, as far as the teller knew it. And there was nothing in any of them that would tell anybody, in a year, in the capital, at a table, who had struck first.

Aldane declared eight days later.

It came by the relay, which was lit again all the way to Kaldo, and by a rider from the north with a printed sheet, and by the fountain in the upper market at Wayford, where a man in Aldane's colours read it aloud twice at noon to a crowd that did not cheer. His grace of Aldane, having learned that the lord of Kaldo's men in arms had fallen upon the servants of the house of Lisle at Harrow Bridge while they kept the toll of that bridge under a lawful charter of the Crown, and slain them, and broken the house of Lisle's gate, and the Crown's mediation being offered and not yet come, and the house of Lisle being joined to his grace's house by marriage and asking his grace's protection, took the house of Lisle and its charter under that protection, and would come in arms to defend them. It did not name a day. It did not need to. Saban's people on the road north had the day already: the host had been mustering at Aldane's southern hall for three weeks. For three weeks before the bridge.

The table was the counting house's long table, with Pennock's ledgers pushed to one end. Emeric and Ward and Saban and Declan, and Pennock at the door, and Rylan by the window.

"How many?" Emeric said.

"Two thousand," said Saban. "About. Near half of it horse. His own knights and his household and his vassals' knights, and foot from the north baronies. And hired mages. A dozen at least. My people saw them at the muster, in grey, with their own guard."

Ward did not say anything. He had the map in front of him, the one Pennock's clerks had drawn for the notice last year, with the bridge and the stream ground and the town.

"Mages," Emeric said.

"A dozen. Maybe more."

And then it came, all at once, the way a sum comes out that one has been carrying in one's head for a season without looking at it. He had had the wool factor's clerk's letter in his hand at the long-room table in the spring and read it twice. He had read Why? in Saban's hand under it. He had put it away because he could not answer it.

Big cheap stones. Hundreds of them. Lamp stones and pump stones, flawed, the size of a fist, bought quietly in the capital all winter and sent north in closed carts. Stones nobody needed a hundred of at once, for any use anybody could name.

Unless you meant to break them.

He had seen it done, years ago, by the high mages: a crystal overloaded on purpose, driven past what it could hold until it let go, and the ground round it for a hundred paces or more gone dead. No mana. No working. Every stone in the dead patch cold, every lamp out, every rail slack, every knight in it a man and no more, for a day, for a week. It was the one weapon the high mages had always had against stonework, and it cost a good crystal every time, and so nobody used it except in a siege. Nobody had ever thought to use it with cheap ones.

A cheap stone would not make much of a dead patch. A hundred cheap stones, laid out in a line across a field and broken one after another, would make a field.

"They're dead-zone stones," Emeric said.

The room was quiet.

"Every one of them. He's going to break them in front of his line. He's going to put a dead field between his host and ours." He heard his own voice, very level. "Everything we have that runs on a stone. The suits. The relay on the ridge. The lamps. The knights' reach. My sense. All of it, dead, in whatever ground he chooses. And his horse goes across it at us."

Everything but one thing, perhaps. There would be two white lights at the foot of a ridge in Kaldo tonight, on half a mile of copper, off a wheel that sawed timber by day, and a row of crocks on a bench in the pattern store that would ring a bell for a week on a Copper's worth of iron, and there was not one stone in any of it. He did not know that a dead field would leave them burning. He thought it would. He had never had the means to find out, and it did not matter now, because between this table and those lights there were five days of road and not a yard of wire on any of it.

He looked at Ward.

Ward had gone grey. Not white; grey, the colour of old ash, all at once, as Emeric had seen him go only once before, in the cellar under the Staple. He sat with his hands flat on the map and looked at nothing.

"It takes his own knights too," Rylan said, from the window. "The dead ground. It takes their reach the same as ours."

"He knows," said Ward. His voice was quite steady. "He's always known. Every high mage who ever broke a stone in a siege knew it: it strips your own men as bare as the enemy's. That's why nobody does it in the field." He looked down at the map. "He thinks it strips us barer. He thinks we're a prince with his tricks and a lot of farmers with sticks. He thinks if he turns off the tricks, it's knights against farmers."

"And is it?" said Saban.

Ward did not answer him at once. He took his hands off the map.

"Once a week," he said. "For years. Every man in my garrison, in plate, in the lower court at Kaldo, with a dead crystal in the middle of the court and every stone on him cold. Sword and shield and nothing else. Because the prince told me once that one day somebody would do this, and I thought he was a boy who'd read too much, and I did it anyway because it was good for them." He looked at Emeric. "They hate it. They've hated it every week of it."

"And the levy?" said Emeric.

"The levy," said Ward, "has never had anything to turn off."

He put his finger on the map, on the stream ground north of the bridge.

"Nothing in a dead field works," he said. "Nothing. Not his, not ours. Except what was never magic to begin with." He tapped the map once. "Powder doesn't care."

Nobody said anything for a while.

It was the first time, Emeric thought, in all those years, that the drill in the lower court had not been an argument. It was not an argument now. It was the plan, and Ward had had it in his hand the whole time, and had not known.

Ward opened his room on the night before the column marched.

He had not meant to. He was in the gatehouse at Kaldo, where he had slept when he slept at all since the prince flew him south from the thorn hill three nights before, and he had the map on the table and the muster rolls and Enno's returns and the waggon list, and he had been at them since supper, and there was nothing left in them to do. He had done it all. The line would march at first light: six hundred and eleven of Enno's, a hundred and fifty of the garrison, forty knights, the waggons and the powder and the jars and Imani's field ward. Kaldo would be held by Armand and what was left.

He sat at the table with nothing to do and his hand on the band on his wrist, which he had worn since the winter and never used alone.

He had learned to rehearse in the room. That had been years ago, when the prince first bound them, before the war. He had found that he could walk ground in it. Ground he had seen once and remembered. He could walk it and put men on it and move them, and see where the dead ground was and where a line would bend, and do it a hundred times in an hour, the way he had done it in his head all his life on the eve of every fight, only clearer. He had done it at the table with the prince's hand on the stone. Never alone.

He did not try to picture anything. He thought about the stream ground north of Harrow Bridge, which he had ridden across four times, and how it lay. The bridge, and the road climbing out of the valley. The ridge on the west side with the relay post on it. The stream ground itself, flat and wet and open, a mile of it between the woods and the town's first fields. Where a man would put a line. Where a man would put a dead field, if he wanted his horse to have the whole of the open ground to run in.

And he was there.

It was not a room. It had never been a room, for him. It was the ground, grey and clear and very quiet, with the sky above it the colour of lead, and the ridge and the bridge and the woods, and he was standing on the ridge by the relay post looking down on it.

He did not know how he had got there, and he did not care. He found that he did not care at all. He stood on the ridge in the grey quiet and looked at the ground and felt, for the first time in eight days, since the prince had said dead-zone stones at the counting-house table, that there was nothing in the world he would rather be doing than this.

He put the line on it. He put it on the south edge of the open ground with its back to the stream and the bridge behind it. He put Aldane's dead field in front of it, where a duke would put it, across the open ground, and walked Aldane's horse into it from the north. He watched them come. He moved the line back fifty paces and watched them come again. He put an earth bank in front of the line, a short one, the height of a man's waist, and moved it, and moved it again, and put it where the dead field's edge would be if the duke broke his stones where a duke would break them, and stood the line behind it, and walked the horse in again.

He did it until the grey light in the room began to go, and then he came out, and he was sitting at the table in the gatehouse with the map in front of him and the lamp burned down and the first light coming up grey behind the lake wall, and he had his answer, and he had not been frightened once.

The prayer went north in their coats.

Doran had written it. He had brought it to Emeric in the spring, when the levy was first talked of, and asked whether the parish might print a short prayer for the men to carry, and Emeric had said yes, and it had gone to the finishing room under Doran's rule. The proof had been read by the parish, as the rule required: by Doran himself, and his deacon, twice, and marked clean. Nevin's boy had set it on the small press. Two hundred copies on the good sheet, folded small, to go in a coat.

Emeric was at the Western Gate at first light to see the column off, before he flew north again. He saw Doran come down the road from the church at a pace he had never seen the old man use, with a folded sheet in his hand and his face the colour of the sheet, and he saw him look at the column, which had begun to move, six hundred and eleven men in blue in a long line going out under the gate with their pieces on their shoulders, and stop.

"Highness," Doran said.

He held out the sheet. Emeric took it and read it.

It was the soldiers' prayer. It was short and plain and good, and asked the Lord to keep them in the field and bring them home, and to forgive them what they would have to do, and to keep their families while they were gone. It was the proof Doran had read. It was the proof Doran had read except in one line, the fourth, where it should have said Keep us, Lord, in the hour of the fight.

It said Keep us, lord, in the hour of the fight.

One letter. The capital gone from a word. Emeric looked at it and did not understand, for a moment, why a priest's face should be that colour over one letter.

Then he did.

"The boy dropped the line," Doran said. His voice was quite steady and very quiet. "After I'd read it. He was locking up the forme and the fourth line fell out of the stick, and he set it again from the case without calling me, because he thought it was the same line and the same letters, and he took the first letter from the wrong box." He looked at the sheet in Emeric's hand. "I found it this morning in the one they gave me. Two hundred. They were handed out last night at the muster."

Emeric looked at the column going out under the gate. Six hundred and eleven men, and two hundred of them with a folded sheet in an inside pocket that asked, in print, on good Kaldo paper, not their God but their lord to keep them in the hour of the fight.

"Can we call them back?" he said.

Doran looked at the column too.

"No," he said. "No, Highness. We can't."

The forward depot burned on the fourth night of the march.

It was at the ford a long day south of Wayford, where the column had camped on its way north two summers ago, in a stone barn that Saban had leased from a farmer on the south bank in the spring, and it held the reserve: the powder for the line beyond what each man carried, and the spare cartridges, and the lead. Twenty men guarded it. It burned at the third hour of the night, from the back, where the thatch came down to the ground on the woodland side, and the guard got nine casks out through the front before the roof came in, and a man called Owen Tarle went back for a tenth and was carried out with his coat on fire, and lived. The rest went up at the fifth hour, all at once, with a noise that was heard in Wayford.

Two of the men who had fired it were caught in the woods by first light, in green coats with the white band.

The relay brought it to Emeric at the Ambry house in Wayford before dawn. He read the slip standing up. Nine casks. The line would reach the ford by the next evening, and the bridge the day after, and Aldane's host, by Saban's riders, was three days north of Wayford and coming.

Six hundred men with what they carried, and nine casks. It was one fight's powder, if the fight was short. It was not enough if it was not.

There were forty casks in the powder house beyond the beds at Kaldo. Five days away by road.

He sent to Kaldo by the relay, in the cipher, to Ferra and Fletcher both, and then he went to find Ward, who had ridden ahead of the column to Wayford, and told him.

"No," said Ward.

"There's no other way to get it here in time."

"Then we fight on nine casks."

"And if it's not enough?"

"Then it's not enough," Ward said, "and we fall back behind the stream and fight again. What we don't do is put a hull in the air over a duke's host the night before a battle, full of powder, where any man who looks up can see it."

"She goes high. She goes at night, in the dark of the moon, from Kaldo to the ford. She never comes within ten miles of his host."

"She's the size of a church."

"At two miles up she's the size of nothing."

Ward looked at him for a long moment.

"I've told you," he said, "what I think."

"You have."

"Write it down," said Ward, "that I told you," and went out.

Emeric wrote it down. He sent it to Ferra with the rest.

Hull two came up from Kaldo the next night, the second night of the dark of the moon, with Ned Tallis at her levers and Fletcher and Tobe in her hold with thirty-one casks of powder lashed down in the racks where the stone would have been, and she came high, at the top of the band, where the air was thin and still, and she came down in the last hour before light on the south bank of the ford, a mile from the column's fires, in a water meadow Emeric had lit with one shaded lamp.

He had his sense out all round the meadow the whole time, as far as it would go. It was very quiet. Sheep in the next field. An owl. The column's sentries at the ford, a mile off, facing north.

And north of them, on the far side of the stream, far out, at the edge of what his sense could reach, a single horse, standing still on a hill, with a man on it.

He felt the horse stop. He felt it stand. He felt the man on it, very small at that distance, not moving, for a long time, while hull two came down out of the dark over the ford. And then he felt the man turn the horse, and the horse go north, fast, until it was out of reach.

He did not say anything to Ned or to Fletcher. He helped them get the casks off. Hull two went up again empty before light, and was gone into the dark in a count of thirty, and there was nothing over the water meadow but stars.

Fletcher laid the third keel on the lower water the week the column marched. He had had the timbers seasoning for a year. She was not for cargo. He had asked Emeric what she was for and Emeric had said reach, and Fletcher had nodded and gone away and drawn her long and narrow.

The column crossed Harrow Bridge on the seventh day, at noon, in the sun.

Emeric sat his horse on the ridge by the relay post and watched it go over. The line in its blue coats four abreast, with the pieces on their shoulders and Enno walking at the head, and the garrison behind, and the knights, and the waggons with the nine casks and the thirty-one, and Imani's field ward in three carts with a red cloth on each, and the broken pole lying in the fern at the north end where Declan's patrol had thrown it. The toll-house was empty. Somebody had nailed its door shut.

Beside him on the ridge the relay keeper was up on the roof of the post at the mast, with his lamps, which had come back to him from under a sack, working the shutters. He had been working them since the column came in sight. Emeric could hear the clack of them above his head. Down the line southward, on the next ridge and the next, he could see the other posts' lamps answering, small and bright in the daylight, passing it back to Kaldo mark by mark: the column over the bridge, the hour, the count.

The line went up the road out of the valley toward the stream ground, and the keeper's lamps went on clacking over Emeric's head, and down the ridges to the south, one after another, the marks went home.
