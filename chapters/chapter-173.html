Chapter 173 - A Week Ahead

The message came up the Wayford wire on the twenty-second of March in the rain, and every letter of it was there.

Saban read it standing in the telegraph room with the slip in one hand and his hat still dripping in the other, and then he read it again, and then he went down the tower stair and across the yard and up the keep stair to the long table, where the morning's business had just begun, and put the slip down on the table in front of Emeric without a word, and sat down in his own chair and took off his wet coat.

It said that the wool buyer from the north had left Wayford on foot on the eighteenth, going south, and was stopping a night at every parish on the road; and that he was very interested in the flocks, and asked every shepherd how many dogs his lord kept, and wrote down the answer.

Emeric read it twice.

"Your man's not a wool buyer," he said.

"My man's whatever I pay him to be on a public wire," said Saban. "Which this week was wool." He hung his coat on the back of his chair. "Church Visitor. One of the old ones, the ones that walk. They go round the southern parishes every few years and read the books, the baptisms and the burials and the tithe, and sit at the priest's table and ask him how his flock does. This one's been asking every priest on the road how many mages his lord keeps. Not the lord. The priest." He folded his hands. "My man stood him a supper at the crossing inn on the seventeenth and he asked my man too. My man said he didn't know. He said that was a pity, and paid for his own supper after all, and went to bed."

"Dogs," said Ferra.

"Mages," said Saban. "Dogs was mine. I'm rather pleased with dogs."

"How far?"

"Walking, stopping a night at every parish. There are nine parishes between the crossing inn and our gate if you count the chapel at the ford, which he will." Saban looked at the slip. "A week. Perhaps eight days. He's slow and he's thorough and he doesn't seem to be in a hurry about anything at all, my man says. Which is what my man said he didn't like about him."

Nobody at the table said anything.

Then Armand, at the end, opened his eyes.

"He'll lodge with Doran," he said. "They always lodge with the priest. And he'll read Doran's book, every page, because that's his work." He was quiet. "And some of the old ones read more than books. Not many. One in a dozen, perhaps. The Church takes its Visitors from its chamber priests when it can, and its chamber priests from the boys who can feel a stone across a room." He looked down the table at Emeric. "If this one counts mages, it's because he can."



They sat until noon over what to hide, and the first thing they agreed was what not to.

"The lamps stay lit," said Ferra. "The wall lamps, the gate lamps. Every merchant in the south has seen them. The still's fire stones stay, the cold stones stay, the stone bank stays. Kaldo buys stones and burns them and everyone from here to the capital knows it, because I've sold half of them the ice." She turned a page. "Orrin and Vesk stay. Paskin stays. Ambrose and Sorrel and Linnet and the fifteen students stay. We hired them, in the open, and they're on the barony's roll at the posting house in Wayford for anybody to read. If we hide a mage who's on a public roll, the man finds the roll, and then he finds the mage, and then he wonders what else we hid." She closed the book. "He's coming to count mages. We let him count mages. He'll count twenty-one and write down that the prince is spending a great deal of money on hedge mages and students to dig drains, which is what every lord in the south already says about us in his cups."

"And everything else goes away," said Ward.

"Everything else goes away."

Everything else was a longer list. Ward wrote it, on the back of the minutes, in his square careful hand, with Hale reading over his shoulder and correcting his spelling of crate, which Ward allowed him to do for the first and last time.

The ship stays in the shed. Doors chained. The suits into crates. The bands under cuffs. The chair on its wheels. The mana printer covered. The forge shut, and its door; no runs till he's gone. The cellar under the keep locked and Armand's key in Armand's pocket. No night flights. Nothing off the ground that isn't Sorrel's bag. Nobody delves.

And the Wayford line.

Paskin was not at the table. He was two miles north at the second cutting with the students, and somebody would have to go and tell him. Emeric looked down the list at it and did not like it any better on paper than he had in his head.

"Four earth mages standing on the capital road in a row with a hill opening in front of them," said Ward. "For a man who's walking the capital road to count mages." He put his pen down. "He'd see them before he saw the town."

"If they come off the line," said Ferra, "Fennick's crews dig it the old way, with powder and picks, for as long as he's here. And after, because you won't put them straight back the day he leaves, not with the posting houses watching." She was writing. "That's the summer. A season, near enough. The forty miles to the river don't get to the river this year."

"Then they don't," said Emeric.

Ferra looked at him over the book.

"I'm not arguing," she said. "I'm writing it down." She wrote. "So that next spring, when Odo stands at this table and asks why he's a season behind, I can show him the page with your name on it."



Paskin took it badly, which Emeric had expected, and Fennick took it very well, which he had expected too and found almost worse.

"Powder," said Fennick, in the cutting, with his hands in his pockets, looking up at the raw clay face of the second ridge where the students had stopped the day before. He said it the way another man might have said wine. "The whole crest. Me and my lads. For a month."

"Longer, probably."

Fennick's eyes went very bright.

Paskin, standing at the top of the bank with his pipe in his teeth and the three students behind him in a row, said nothing whatever until Fennick had gone off down the cutting to look at the clay with his lads, whistling. Then he took the pipe out of his mouth and spat, carefully, off the bank, away from everybody.

"He'll take a month to do what we'd do in four days," he said. "And he'll bring down the side of my cutting the first time he gets the charge wrong, and he'll get the charge wrong because he likes it when it's wrong."

"He's very good."

"He's very good at noise." Paskin put the pipe back. "A man walks the capital road with Fennick's lads blowing a hill to bits beside it, and he'll not look at the hill. He'll look at the smoke." He was quiet. "Maybe that's what you want."

Emeric had not thought of it that way. He looked down the cutting at Fennick, who was standing with his face a hand from the clay, smelling it.

"It might be," he said.

"Hm," said Paskin, and took the students home.



Kaldo spent the week hiding things, and it was not good at it.

The suits went into crates on the twenty-fourth. There were six of them now, and Rylan's on its rack, and the crates had to be built, because there was nothing in the keep big enough; and Fletcher's men built them in the shed out of the timber that had been put by for the ship's spare locker doors, and Fletcher said a number of things about that. Ward's suit and Osric's and Declan's each had the lion on the breastplate that Bram had cut for them, and Osric would not let his go into its crate until he had wrapped the lion in a blanket.

"It'll scratch," he said.

"It's going in a box for a week," said Ward.

"It'll scratch in the box."

Hale painted the crates. He was the only person in the keep with a hand that looked as if it belonged on a crate, and he took a great deal of care over it, and when he had finished every crate in the shed said in neat black capitals STOVE PARTS, MILFORD, which was where the iron stoves went when they went anywhere. Emeric looked at the seven crates in a row, all of them a little longer than a man was tall, and said nothing. Fletcher looked at them and said that he had never in his life seen a stove that needed a box that shape. Hale said that Milford's stoves were very large. Then he went and painted the same words on the box the printer's spare type came in, for practice.

The mana printer was in the cellar, where it had been for two years, and nobody outside the six had ever seen it: an iron frame and rollers on a stone bed, and at its heart the stone that inked the plate, which was what made it a mana printer and not a press. Anybody who got as far as the cellar stair with a lamp would feel that stone before he saw the frame. So the stone was emptied and wrapped and put in the box under the forge door with the other things that had to sleep for a week, and the frame was covered with sacking and roped, and a card was pinned to the sacking that said PRESS, BROKEN, in Hale's hand. By the next morning somebody had added, underneath, in chalk, VERY. Bram said he had no idea who. He had chalk on his apron, and on his cuff, and he had called it the stupidest name for anything he had ever heard on the day it first worked and had used no other since.

The bands went under cuffs. That was the knights and the six, ten wrists, and the bands were silver and could not be hidden by the sleeves of a working shirt, which rode up; so Maren and the girls in the laundry sewed cuffs, deep ones of brown wool, buttoned, to go over the band from the wrist almost to the elbow, like a clerk's sleeve guards. Ward wore his without a word. Rylan said his made him look like a counting clerk. Declan's was too tight, and he said so every hour, and Maren took it back and let it out and gave it back to him and it was too loose, and he said so. Osric said nothing about his at all, for two days, and then appeared at the dawn drill on the third with both cuffs embroidered round the buttons with small red flowers, and when Ward asked where he had got the flowers said that a girl in the laundry had done it for him, and would not say which girl, and was very pleased with himself for the rest of the week.

Emeric's own cuff he wore to the long table on the first morning and Ferra looked at it and looked away, and Imani looked at it and did not look away.

"It's a sleeve guard," he said.

"It's a sleeve guard on a prince," said Imani. "Who doesn't write."

"I write."

"You don't write enough to need a sleeve guard." She turned back to her list. "Spill something on it. Ink. Then it'll look as if it's for something."

He spilled ink on it that afternoon, on purpose, in the counting room, at Ferra's desk, while Ferra watched him do it with no expression at all; and it looked, after, exactly as it had looked before, but with ink on it.



The chair was worse.

Tess's chair had gone on its wheels in the streets since the first day, by Ward's rule and her own: wheels down in the town, the stones lightened, the motor turning the wheels. It had been her rule as much as his. She knew what the chair was. But in the works and the keep and the line room, where nobody from outside came, the chair lifted when she wanted it to, and went up the keep stair on the air like a leaf going up a chimney, and over the shed's slip, and across the line room from bench to cells without touching the floor; and that was where she lived, now, most of her days.

A mage who could read a man's note from across a room could read a lit gravity stone through the floor of a house.

So for a week, the stones were shut. All of them. The air lattice, the lift, the Heavy, everything that made the chair what it was. What was left was a chair with two big spoked wheels and two little casters and a motor in the hub, and the hand rims; which was more chair than anybody else in Randillia had, and which could not climb the keep stair.

Tess sat in it at the bottom of the keep stair on the morning of the twenty-fifth with the stones shut and looked up the stair.

Osric was the one who said he would carry her up. He said it nicely. He had carried her before, in the winter after the dynamo house, before there was any chair at all, every morning from the ward to the works, and he did not mean anything by it except that he was there and the stair was there.

"No," said Tess.

"It's only the one stair."

"No." She looked at the stair. "I'll go in the line room at the gate tower. There's a bench. I'll take the book." She turned the chair round, on the motor, which hummed. "For a week."

"Tess," said Emeric.

"For one week," said Tess, "I'll live in a room I can get to on wheels, like a sensible woman, and you'll come to me, all of you, when you want something, and I'll be very interested to see how many of you do." She did not look round. "Mind the cobbles," she said to Osric, who was standing in her way; and he moved; and she went across the yard on her wheels in the rain toward the gate tower, quite slowly, with her back very straight.

By the third day of it, Emeric had been to the gate tower nine times. He counted. So did she.



The ship was the easiest, and the hardest to leave alone.

She was already in the shed. She had been in the shed every day of her life except the nights. On the twenty-fourth, in the afternoon, he went down with Fletcher and walked her stem to stern with a lamp, the old way, and went round her cases and closed every neck, the main matrix and the trim group and the thrust stone in the propeller housing, one by one, until he could stand on the slip with his sense out and feel nothing from her at all, nothing but the faint cold note of stones asleep in their glass, which he did not think anybody but a delver could feel through her oak and her copper and the shed's brick. Then the doors were shut and the chain went through the hasps and Ward put the padlock on it himself.

Fletcher stood looking at the doors.

"She's never been chained," he said.

"It's a week."

"I know it's a week." He did not move. "A man comes asking about a light over the marsh, and the light's on the other side of a door with a chain on it, and every fisherman on the river knows what's behind the door." He turned. "You can chain a door. You can't chain a fisherman."

"The fishermen think it's a boat."

"The fishermen," said Fletcher, "think it's a boat that goes out at night with no sail and comes back before dawn with no fish. They think it's a smuggler. Half of them think it's yours and the other half think it's Saban's." He started back up the bank. "Which is fine. As long as nobody asks them what it smuggles."



Armand took the readings early.

He said so on the twenty-third, at the table, the day after Saban's slip, in the voice he kept for things that had already been decided: that the year's readings, which he took every year in the last week of April, would be taken this year in the last week of March, starting tomorrow, and finished before the Visitor reached the gate; and that the book would be in his box, and the box locked, and the key on his chain, before the man set foot in the town.

"Why?" said Ferra.

"Because if he asks to see my sheets I want to be able to tell him there's nothing to see, truthfully, and show him an empty desk." Armand did not open his eyes. "And because I'd rather he didn't watch me read the barony. He'd learn more from watching me do it than from the sheets."

So Linnet brought them in, the still crew and the matched townsfolk and the stone handlers and the knights and the six, at twice the rate she had ever brought them before, from first light to last for five days, to the stool by the window in Armand's room; and Armand read them, three times each, and wrote, and blotted, and did not stop. He looked very old by the fourth day. Linnet made him eat at noon, standing over him, and he ate.

Emeric went in on the last afternoon, the twenty-eighth, and sat on the stool and held out his wrist, with the cuff unbuttoned and turned back off the band.

Armand read him three times. He wrote. He blotted. He did not turn back to last year's page, which he had done for Osric last spring, and had done for Declan, and had done this week, Linnet said, for the still crew's whole first bench, one after another.

"Well?" said Emeric.

"Noise," said Armand.

"You turned back for the still crew."

"I turned back because Linnet had put them in the wrong order and I wanted to see whether I'd read Mott first or last a year ago." Armand closed the book. "And I'd thank you not to stand outside my door counting the pages I turn." He took the book in both hands and held it a little while on the board across his knees, not looking at it. Then he reached down beside his chair for the box, and put the book in it, and locked it, and put the key on the chain round his neck under his shirt.

"If he reads my sheets," Armand said, "he'll read them the way you do." He looked at the box. "Not the way I do."

"And how do you read them?"

"Carefully," said Armand. "And slowly. And for five years more." He shut his eyes. "Go away. I've read four hundred people this week and I'm tired."



Vantic went to Wayford on the twenty-sixth, on the Ducatis House's business, which Ferra mentioned at the table in a line, in passing, among the wool prices, as a fact about the House's ledger and its factor's travel. Emeric heard it and did not think about it, then.

On the twenty-ninth the wire said the wool buyer had spent the night at the chapel at the ford, nine miles north, and had asked the chaplain whether it was true that a light could be seen some nights moving over the Kaldo marsh, and had been told that the chaplain did not go out at night, and had seemed disappointed.

That afternoon a little after three, from the second ridge, there was a long flat thump that Emeric felt in the keep's floor, and a column of brown dust went up off the crest of the hill and drifted south on the wind across the capital road, and every carter on the road stopped to watch it; and Fennick's voice came faintly down the valley after it, cheering.

Emeric went to the gate tower and up the stair to the line room there, where Tess sat at the spare bench in her chair, on its wheels, with her book and a cold cup of tea and a relay in pieces.

"Twelve," said Tess, without looking up.

He sat down on the clerks' bench across from her.

"He'll be here the day after tomorrow," he said.

"I know. I read the wire before you did. I'm in the room with it." She put down the screwdriver. "Will he come up here?"

"I don't know."

"If he does," said Tess, "I'll tell him about relays." She picked the screwdriver up again. "For an hour. In detail. He'll never come back."
