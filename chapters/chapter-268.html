Chapter 268 - The First Deposit

Ward asked for the yard on the third Monday in May, at six, before the class came in, and he asked for it the way he asked for anything he had been thinking about for three weeks: in passing, at the foot of the keep stair, with his gloves already on.

"Blades," he said. "Blunt. The north end. Before they come."

Emeric had been waiting for it since the week. He had known Ward would ask the way you know a cart is coming up the lane by the sound before it turns the corner; he had felt Ward looking at him across the hall at supper since the knee stopped clicking, and in the yard at the drill, and once on the wall walk at night when Ward thought he was watching the river. Ward had measured every inch of him since he was seven. He would not believe a new inch until he had put a blade against it.

"Rules," said Emeric.

Ward stopped on the bottom step.

"Mine," said Emeric. "No glyphs. Nothing out of the hand, nothing out of the body. Nothing I couldn't have done the day before I ate it." He did up his cuffs. "Only what a knight has. If I can't do it with what you've got, I don't do it."

Ward looked at him a long time.

"Why?"

"Because I'll not hurt you with something you can't answer."

"You'll not hurt me with anything," said Ward, and went out into the yard.



The north end of the yard was in shadow at six, under the wall, with the dew still on the cobbles and the drill frames standing empty at the far end and the smoke from Maren's kitchen chimney going straight up into a white sky. Rylan was there. Emeric had not told him and neither, by the look of him, had Ward; he was sitting on the mounting block by the armoury door with a cup in his hands, as if he always sat there at six, which he did not. Declan was beside him. Osric was not there, because Osric was at the works, which meant Osric did not know, and that Rylan had found out by some other road.

The blades were on the rack, two of the long practice swords with their edges ground round and their points capped in leather, as heavy as the real thing and a finger longer. Ward took one and weighed it and gave Emeric the other.

They walked out to the middle of the north end and faced each other at two paces, and saluted, and Rylan put his cup down.

The first exchanges were the old ones. Ward came in from the left high, the way he had come in from the left high at the first lesson in the capital, when Emeric had been a boy with a wooden sword and a crown he did not remember having; and Emeric took it on the flat and turned it and stepped through, and Ward was not there. They went round. Ward pressed, three cuts, a feint, a thrust that came from nowhere, the way his thrusts always came; Emeric met them, all of them, and gave back two of his own, and Ward met those.

It was even. It was the old evenness: the one they had arrived at in the years after the Rowena trip, when Emeric had found out how to drive his mana into his own legs and arms and back the way a knight did, a hybrid's trick that Ward had never seen done by anybody who also cast, and had used it to close the gap that twenty years of Ward's life had put between them. It had closed the gap. It had never held. He had always had to ration it, to save it for the moment, to run it down his arm for the cut and pull it back for the step; and by halfway through any long drill he was fading, and Ward knew it to the breath, and waited for it, and won.

He was not fading.

He noticed it at the first long pause, when they broke apart and circled with the blades low, both breathing. He had been driving it into his legs since the salute, and his shoulders, and his hands on the hilt, all of it at once, without thinking about where to save it, because he had forgotten to think about where to save it. And there was more. He went looking for the bottom of it, the way you put your foot down in a river to see how deep it is, and did not find it.

Ward came in.

Emeric took the first touch.

He did not know he was going to until he had. Ward's cut came down from the right and he went under it instead of meeting it, low, and up inside Ward's arm, and his blunt edge came across Ward's ribs under the left arm, lightly, the touch a teacher gives a student to say there, that one; and stopped.

Ward stepped back.

On the mounting block, Rylan had stood up.

In eleven years of the yard, nobody had seen it. Ward had been touched, at drill, by his own knights, now and then, a glancing thing on the forearm in a melee; but not by Emeric, never, not once, not even when Emeric had been on his best day and Ward on his worst. Ward had touched him ten thousand times. That was what the yard was. It was a place where Ward touched you, and you learned why.

Ward looked down at his ribs. Then he looked at Emeric.

He did not say anything. He came in again, at once, from the left high, and feinted the left high and came round from the right low, a move Emeric had seen perhaps four times in his life and never seen coming; and the blunt edge took Emeric across the thigh, hard, the way Ward's touches always were, so that he would remember it. The second touch, Ward's.

"Again," said Ward.

They went again.

Something happened in the third exchange that Emeric could not, afterward, put in order, though Imani made him try for her book and Ward made him try for his.

He was holding everything. That was the first thing. He had been holding his sense open, as he always did, since he woke; and the body, driving it, as a knight did; and somewhere in him, without deciding to, the door: the hair-width door to the slow place, the one he held open to see the overlay, that he had been holding open by habit at drill for two years because it made the yard look like a drawing. And now he was holding all of it, all at once, and it was not costing him anything he could feel, because there was so much more to hold it with than there had been.

Then Ward moved his weight.

He did not move. He only shifted it, the way a man does before he moves: onto the ball of the left foot, a hair. And Emeric saw, quite clearly, at the edge of his sight, like a reflection in a window at dusk, another Ward. Pale. Thin as breath. Stepping forward off that left foot with the blade coming round from the high right, a full pace ahead of the real one.

And beside it, fainter, another, going back.

And another, very faint, hardly there, coming in low.

He did not have time to think about what they were. The pale one stepping forward was so much sharper than the others that it was almost solid. The real Ward came forward off his left foot with the blade coming round from the high right, a heartbeat after the ghost, along the exact line the ghost had gone; and Emeric was not there, because he had stepped off that line while the ghost was still on it.

He stopped blocking.

He did not decide to. It was only that there was no reason to block a blade that was going to go past him, and every blade that came now was going to go past him, because he could see where it was going before it went. Ward came in high and there was a ghost going high, and he leaned a hand's breadth, and the real blade went through the place he had been. Ward came in low with a ghost a breath ahead of it, and he lifted his foot. Ward feinted and the ghosts split into three, all pale, and he waited, and two of them faded as the real Ward chose, and he moved off the line of the one that stayed. He was close now. He had come inside without meaning to, inside Ward's reach, where a long blade was useless and a man with a long blade had to step back to use it; and every time Ward stepped back he followed, and stayed, a finger's width from the edge every time, closer than he had ever stood to Ward with a sword in his hand.

He did not strike. His own blade hung in his right hand, low, forgotten. His rule.

Ward was very quiet. His breathing had changed. He had gone from cutting to cutting faster, and then to cutting in the patterns he did not use at drill, the ones from the war, the short brutal ones, and then to something else, something Emeric had never seen him do: he stopped attacking the man and attacked the space round him, wide flat cuts that would have taken anybody standing within a pace, to drive him out from inside the reach. Emeric saw the ghosts of those too. He went under one and over the next and stayed.

It went on for a long time. The sun came over the wall and put the north end in light.

Ward lowered his blade.

He did it slowly, and stood with the point on the cobbles, breathing, with the sweat running down his face into his collar. Emeric stopped where he was, a hand's breadth from Ward's chest, and found that he was breathing hard too, and that there was an ache behind his eyes like the start of a migraine, sharp and bright and getting brighter.

"I can't hit you," said Ward.

"No."

Ward looked at him from a hand's breadth away. Nobody in the yard moved.

"Then it's not a draw," said Ward.

"It's not a win either. I didn't hit you."

"You didn't try."

"I said I wouldn't."

Ward looked at him a moment longer. Then he stepped back, one pace, and saluted with the blunt blade, properly, the full salute that he gave at the end of a drill to the man who had done the drill best, and that Emeric had seen him give a hundred times to other people.

"A draw," said Ward.

He handed his blade to Declan, who had come off the block to take it, and walked out of the north end and across the yard and in at the armoury door, and shut it behind him.

Rylan had sat down again on the mounting block. He picked up his cup and looked at it and found it empty.

"Well," he said.

The class was coming in at the gate in a long untidy column with Mira Holt at its head, and they had seen the end of it from the gate, some of them, and were talking. They had seen a man who could not be touched. They had not seen any of how. They would say so all day, and invent the how, and the how they invented would be wrong in every way that mattered; and it would be round the base by Friday that the duke had walked through the captain's blade like smoke.

Emeric gave his own blade to Declan, who looked at it as if it might bite him.

"Bed," said Rylan, still looking at the cup. "You've got a face on you like a man with a fever."

"It's my eyes."

"Then bed with your eyes," said Rylan. "And when you're up, you're going to tell me what that was. And then you're going to tell Ward. In that order. Because he's going to want to know, and he's going to want not to have asked."

Emeric slept until three in the afternoon, with the shutters closed and a wet cloth over his eyes that Imani had put there without a word; and when he woke, the ache had gone to a dull throb, and Ward was sitting in the chair by the hearth in his shirt with a book he was not reading on his knee.

"The ghosts," said Ward, when Emeric had told him. He had listened to the whole of it without a word. "You see where I'm going to be."

"Where you're probably going to be. Where you've decided. If you haven't decided, I see three or four of you and they're all faint."

Ward was quiet a while.

"Then I'll not decide," he said, "till it's too late for you to see me."

Emeric looked at him from under the cloth.

"I don't know if that works," he said.

"Nor do I." Ward closed the book he had not been reading and stood up. "I've time to find out."



Joss came to the tank on the Wednesday after, at seven in the morning, because Imani had asked for seven and he had been sitting on the bench outside the ward door since a quarter to six.

Emeric was there because he had been in the tank himself, at half past six, for the second time. The first had been the morning after the week, when Imani had walked him down from the keep before breakfast with her hand under his elbow and put him in the long warm bath with his head in the sling, and run the frame over him, and taken it off the printer, and looked at it; and it had been fog. Grey, from edge to edge, with a darker grey somewhere in the middle that might have been his heart. She had run it again, slower, and got his ribs and the top of his lungs and nothing below them, and the twenty minutes had run out, and she had made him get out and stood by the bath with the two sheets in her hands, looking at them, as angry as he had ever seen her.

"I can't see you," she had said.

"It's the density. It's the rule. You said yourself, a mage gets a chest."

"I know what I said." She had not looked up from the fog. "I didn't know it would be you."

This morning she had done his chest again, crawling the frame over it a hand's breadth a minute for the whole twenty, and got it: the ribs pale and clean, the heart a dark fist, the lungs clear, and there, low on the left under the ribs, nothing at all where the stone had been. She had pinned it on the ward wall beside the morning's other sheets and written under it, in the hospital's hand, Duke, chest, twenty minutes, one view only, and the date; and then she had gone to the door and said "Joss," and the boy had come in.

He came in as he had come into the yard: very still, as if something in the room might go for him. He looked at the bath, steaming a little, and at the brass frame hanging over it on its rails, and at the printer at the bath's foot with its paper and its wires, and at the sheets on the wall; and at Emeric, at the end of the room, in his shirt with his hair still wet; and away again, fast.

"Shirt off," said Imani. "Boots. Breeches you can keep. Into the water, on your back, head in the sling. It's warm. Nothing's going to touch you but the water." She was already at the frame. "Then lie still. If you need to move, say so first. If you need to stop, say stop."

He did as he was told. He lay in the bath with his eyes on the ceiling and his hands at his sides, and the left one, under the water, curled against his hip and shook, the small steady shake that had not stopped since the cellar, so that the water over it stood in fine rings.

"Arm first," said Imani. "Then the rest, if there's time."

The frame came down over his left forearm.

It went slow. Not as slow as it had gone over Emeric; Joss had the one ring at the wrist and nothing else, a knight's first step that his body had been forced into in a night and that had stopped there. But slower than a commoner. Emeric watched the printer's head go back and forth over the paper on its rail, and the arm come up out of the paper a line at a time: the two long pale bones, the wrist's small ones packed at the end, the grey of the muscle round them. And along the inside of the forearm, from the wrist ring up toward the elbow, a line.

It was faint. It was a thread of darker grey in the grey of the muscle, running close along the bone, the length of a hand, thinner than a pencil line. Emeric would not have seen it on the paper if he had not known where to look. Imani had seen it before the head had finished the line.

She did not say anything. She let the frame go on up the arm to the elbow and then ran it back down again, slower still, over the same line, and the printer drew it again on a second sheet; and she took both sheets off the printer and held them up to the window side by side.

"There," she said.

Joss turned his head in the sling.

"Don't move."

He turned it back.

"There's your nerve." Imani was talking to Emeric, but she was looking at the sheet. "That's where the tracery went over it. That pale, there, along it, that's the scar. And that," her finger on the dark thread, "that's the nerve. It's thin. It's very thin. But it's there, all the way along. It's not cut." She lowered the sheets. "It's not cut, and it's not dead. A dead nerve doesn't bend the field. That one's bending it."

The water over the boy's left hand had gone very still.

He had stopped shaking. He had stopped because he was holding it still, holding every muscle in his arm so hard that the cords stood up under the skin; and he was looking at the ceiling, and his jaw was shut.

"Joss," said Imani.

"Yes, miss."

"Let it go. You'll cramp."

He let it go. The hand shook again under the water, in its fine rings.

"I can't promise you anything," said Imani. "I want you to hear that before you hear the rest. Nobody's ever done this. Nobody's ever had one like you." She put the sheets down on the bath's edge. "But a nerve that's thin and alive can be fed. The food you ate in the cellar was too much, too fast, and it went through you like a flood through a field and took the top off. The base feeds a class the same food a little at a time and works it off them as fast as it goes in, and it doesn't take anything off anybody. If you ate that way, a little, under me, and worked it off, under Ward, a nerve might come back. Slowly. Over a year. Over two." She looked at him. "Or it might not, and you'd be a year older, and a lot stronger, with a hand that shakes."

Joss lay in the warm water and looked at the ceiling.

"The Proving," said Imani. "That's what it's called. It's for men going north to the floor. You're not going north to the floor, nobody's sending you. But the eating and the working are the same, and it starts in June, at the base, with a medic over every man." She paused. "I'm the medic. I'd put your name down. If you wanted it."

For a while he did not say anything at all.

Then he said, to the ceiling, "Captain Ward'd have to say."

"He would," said Imani.

"He give me a broom."

"I know."

"I'd not want him to think I wanted more than the broom." His voice had gone thick. "When he give it me. I'd not want him to think I was asking."

Emeric had not said anything since the boy came in. He said something now.

"You're not asking," he said. "She is."

Joss's eyes came off the ceiling and onto him, for the first time; and stayed there, a long moment, with the water steaming round him and his left hand shaking in it.

"Yes, sir," he said.

Imani wrote his name on the Proving's sheet that afternoon, in the hospital's hand, under the line for the crown's men, which was empty yet, and the lords' sons, which was empty yet, as the first name on the list. Under it she wrote, Not for the floor. For the arm. And under that, Ward to say.

Ward said yes at supper. He said it without looking up from his plate, and without asking any questions, which was how Imani knew he had already decided before she asked; and when Rylan, across the table, opened his mouth, Ward looked at him, and Rylan shut it again.



The bank opened on the last Monday in May, in the old tithe house on the market square, and the first person through its door at nine o'clock was Maren.

Nobody had arranged that. Vantic said afterward that nobody could have; that she had considered, in April, whether there ought to be a first depositor put up for it, the way a lord is put up to cut a ribbon, someone the town would see and trust, and had decided against it, because a bank whose first customer had been arranged was a bank that would arrange other things. So the doors opened at nine and the clerk stood behind the long counter with Ottoline's tabulator at his elbow and his pen in his hand and nobody came in; and at two minutes past nine Maren came across the square from the castle in her apron, with a stone jar under her arm that had once held pickled onions and now held, she told the clerk, putting it on the counter with a thump, eleven years.

Eleven years of what, said the clerk.

Of what she had not spent, said Maren. Of the duke's pay, which was more than she had ever had and more than she had ever been able to spend, because she was in the kitchen from five till nine and there was nothing in Kaldo to spend it on at the hours she was not. It had been in a jar under the floor of her room since the first winter, she said, and then in the jar and another jar, and then she had sold the second jar's worth for a silver chain for her sister's girl and put the rest back. She had heard there was a place now that would keep it for her better than the floor. She had heard it would give her a paper with her name on it that said how much there was, and that she could take the paper to the counter any morning and get it back, and that in the meantime the place would lend it to somebody and pay her something for the use of it, a little, every quarter.

"That's about the size of it," said the clerk.

Maren looked at him. Then she looked round the bank: the long counter of Kaldo oak, the brass rail, the tall windows onto the square, the green baize on the clerks' desks, the iron door at the back with the House's arms on it that went down to the vault; and at the far end, behind a rail of her own, at a plain desk with nothing on it but a pen and a book and her own two hands, Vantic, who had not looked up.

"Who to?" said Maren.

"Madam?"

"Who'll you lend it to?"

The clerk opened his mouth, and Vantic said, from her desk, without raising her voice, "To the brickfield. They want a second kiln. They'll pay the bank for it out of the bricks over three years, and the bank will pay you out of what they pay it."

Maren considered that. She knew the brickfield. Everybody knew the brickfield; it had made the bricks for the hospital and the school and half the lower town.

"And if the bricks don't sell?"

"Then the bank pays you anyway," said Vantic, "out of what it has, because that's what a bank is. And the brickfield loses its kiln, and I lose the use of my own money for a while, and I'll have been wrong, and it'll go in a book with my name on it." She turned a page. "The bricks will sell."

Maren looked at her for a long moment, the way she looked at a fish on the quay before she bought it.

Then she unscrewed the lid of the jar.

It took the clerk forty minutes to count. Most of it was copper and some was silver and there were, at the bottom, under a twist of cloth, three of the old gold Rands from the first winter, when Emeric had paid the castle's people out of his father's purse because there was nothing else to pay them with; and Maren had kept those three, she said, because they had his father's face on them and she had thought, at the time, that a duke who paid a cook in his father's gold was either a fool or a man who would last, and she had wanted to have something to show for it either way.

The clerk counted it twice. Ottoline's tabulator took the sum through its keys and printed it on a card, and the card went into a drawer, and the clerk wrote a note.

It was the first one. It was printed on Kaldo paper from the mill on the river, the hard white paper that the Almanac was printed on and the school's books, but heavier, and made in a mould of its own that the paper mill had cut over the winter; so that when Maren held it up to the window, as the clerk told her to, there was a bird in it. Not printed. In it. A pale shape in the body of the paper itself, where the pulp had lain thinner over a wire in the mould: a falcon, stooping, with its wings half closed and its head down, going at something nobody could see. On the face of the note, in an engraved border so fine it looked like lace, the House's arms and the words HOUSE DUCATIS, KALDO, and the sum in figures and in words, and a number, and a space at the bottom where the clerk wrote MAREN in capitals; and under it, a signature that was not a name, in a hand that was small and square and did not lean: Vantic.

Maren turned it over, and back, and held it up to the window again, and looked at the bird.

"What's that for?"

"So you know it's ours," said Vantic.

Emeric found out about the bird at half past four that afternoon, in Ferra's room, with the first note in his hand, which Ferra had bought off Maren with its face value and one Copper over so that it could go in the barony's books as the first note of the House of Kaldo; and he held it up to the window as Maren had, and saw the falcon.

"I drew a tree," he said.

"I know you did," said Ferra.

"I drew it for her in March. The tree on the arms. In a ring. I gave it to the mill."

"I know. She told the mill thank you, and asked them to cut the falcon instead." Ferra was writing. "She said a tree was the duke's. Every mark in the barony is the duke's. The bridges, the pumps, the carts. A note's a promise, and the duke didn't make it. The House did." She did not look up. "Also she said the tree looks like a broccoli in a watermark. She said it nicely."

He looked at the bird.

"Why a falcon?"

"I asked her that." Ferra blotted the line. "She said there's a pair on the cliffs at the family seat. Over the harbour. They've been there as long as the House has. They go down at a pigeon faster than anything in the world, and they don't miss, and nobody's ever been able to tame one. Her grandfather tried." She put the pen down. "She said she didn't want anything on her notes that somebody had tamed."

He looked at the bird for a while longer, stooping in the paper with its head down.

"It's better than the tree," he said.

"Yes," said Ferra. "She knew that too."



The correspondent agreement was signed on the Thursday, at the family seat, and Emeric and Ferra waited outside.

They went down the coast in the public lift hull. That had been Vantic's own condition; she had written it into her letter in April, on the line under the date she wanted: that she would go in the hull the House paid for a seat on, the way any passenger paid, and not on any ship of Kaldo's that a passenger could not buy a seat on, because if she came in to her father's harbour on the duke's own ship the partners would see the duke's ship and not her. So the public lift hull it was: the long grey passenger hull with its paired gravity stones and its cabin of plain benches and its brass rails, that flew the Milford and Grayrock route for anyone with the fare; hired out for the day at the House's cost, with Old Edrin at the casting seat in the bow in his sheepskin coat and his pipe unlit in his teeth because Imani had told him what she would do to him if he lit it inside a sealed hull.

Emeric and Ferra paid for their seats. Ferra had insisted. She had written them into the House's account as passengers and paid the House out of the barony's purse, a Silver each, and given Vantic the receipt in the hull at the start, and Vantic had read it and put it in her book without a word, and Emeric had seen the corner of her mouth go.

It was a long flight. Down the river to the coast, and along the coast south and west, over the grey water, past headlands Emeric had only seen on the map. Vantic sat on the bench across the aisle with her book open on her knee and a pen in her hand and did not write anything for the first hour; then she wrote steadily for the second; and in the third hour, when the coast turned south and the family seat came up out of the haze ahead, a white town on a hill above a harbour with a long grey house at its crown, she closed the book and put the pen in its spine and sat with her hands folded on it, looking out of the window, and did not move again until they were down.

Old Edrin set the hull down on the long field above the harbour, behind the house, where the House's horses grazed, as gently as if he were setting down a basket of eggs; and the horses ran to the far end of the field and stood there with their heads up and their tails out, and did not come back while the hull was there.

Vantic got up.

"Wait here," she said.

"We'll walk down to the harbour," said Ferra.

"Wait here," said Vantic, "please," and went down the steps and across the field to the house; a small straight figure in grey, with her book under her arm, going up toward the great grey house where she had been a third child and a girl and a mage that nobody counted; and she did not look back.

They did not walk down to the harbour.

They sat on the hull's bottom step in the sun, the two of them, and watched the house. Old Edrin came and sat on the step above them and took his pipe out of his teeth and looked at it, and put it back. Out over the harbour, high up against the white sky above the cliffs, two dark shapes went round and round on the wind, tilting.

"There," said Ferra.

He watched them. One of them folded, all at once, as if it had been shot, and dropped. It went down the face of the sky like a stone down a well, faster than he could follow, and was gone below the cliff's edge. The other went on round.

"She said they don't miss," said Ferra.

"Nothing doesn't miss."

"I said that too." She was watching the one that was left. "She said it's not that they don't miss. It's that they go down again."

They waited two hours and a quarter.

At a little after three, Vantic came out of the house. She came out of a side door, alone, and came down the field the way she had gone up it, at the same pace, with the book under her arm; and when she was ten yards off Emeric stood up, and Ferra did not.

Vantic stopped at the foot of the steps.

"Signed," she said. "Both houses honour both papers. Lunch?"

Ferra looked at her for a moment from the step, and then down at her own book, and counted on her fingers on the cover where nobody but Emeric could see.

"Seven," she said.

"Seven what?" said Vantic.

"Words." Ferra stood up and brushed the step off her skirt. "I'd a Copper on under ten with the pilot."

"I'd a Copper on under five," said Old Edrin, from behind them, "and I've lost it, and I want it said that 'lunch' oughtn't to count." He got up. "There's a pie shop on the quay that does a lamb pie you'd sell your mother for. I've been told."

Vantic looked at the three of them, at the pilot and the duke and the Chancellor of four baronies on the bottom step of a hired hull in a horse field, betting on her.

For the first time in all the years Emeric had known her, she laughed. It was short and surprised and it came out of her as if it had been in there a long time.

"The quay," she said. "I'll pay." She turned to go. "It's on the House."

They ate the pies on the harbour wall, in the sun, with the gulls watching; and they were good pies, though nobody sold his mother. Vantic ate hers looking up at the cliffs, where the falcon that had gone down had come up again, and the two were going round together on the wind; and halfway through the pie, without looking at either of them, she said that her brother Cassian had signed the agreement for the coast houses, in his own hand, below hers, without being asked, and had asked her to tell the duke that he would come to Kaldo in June to look at the bank, for a week.

"And Tullio?" said Ferra.

Vantic looked at the cliffs.

"Tullio wasn't there," she said, and finished her pie.



They were home by ten, with the lamps lit along the canal ring and the station's clock showing the hour to the lower town, and Vantic got down at the House's door in the square with her book and a nod, and the hull went on to its berth at the port.

Imani was waiting at the top of the keep stair.

She was not in her ward coat. She had her hair down, which she did only at the end of a day, out of the knot she wore it in to keep it out of her work; long, and curling, and a little wild from having been tied up since six. She had a candle. She stood on the top step with it, in a plain dark dress with the sleeves pushed up, and looked at the two of them coming up the stair, Ferra first with her book and Emeric behind her; and she did not say anything about the pie, or the falcons, or the bank, or anything at all.

"The cuffs marks," she said.

Emeric stopped on the step below her.

"The new skin. On the wrists. And the ankles. And everywhere else." She held the candle a little higher, so it was on his face. "I've looked at it every morning for a month, and written it down, every morning, and it's healed. I wrote that this morning. Healed." She looked at Ferra. "I'm off the ward until six. Bettany has it."

Ferra looked at her for a moment; and then she closed her book and put it in her pocket, which she did not do, and held out her hand for the candle.

"Then I'm not counting anything tonight," she said.



They were slow.

That was the thing he remembered afterward, of all of it: that nobody hurried. They had hurried before. There had been nights in the winter, before the pod, when the three of them had come together in a rush because the day had been long or the night was short or because they were young and could, and it had been laughing and clumsy and quick and good, and he would not have given any of it back. This was not that. This was a room with one candle in it and the shutters closed and the town's lamps showing in thin gold lines round their edges, and the two people he loved best in the world, taking their time.

Imani undid his shirt.

She did it the way she did everything with her hands, which was carefully and completely and without any fuss, a button at a time, down; and when it was open she did not take it off him. She put her hands flat on his chest under it, both of them, and left them there, and shut her eyes.

He felt her sense come into him. Thin, cool, careful, the way it had come into him in the dome on the third day when he had been too far gone to know anything but that it was her. It went in through her palms. It did not go deep. It went along the skin, slowly, under her hands and then away from them, outward, across his chest and round his ribs and up into his shoulders, the way you run your hand over a table you have made to feel for any rough place you missed.

"What are you doing?" said Ferra, from the bed.

"Looking," said Imani, with her eyes shut.

"At what?"

"At all of it. Every bit of skin he's got. I've never seen it from the inside." She opened her eyes. "It's new. Did you know that? Not just the wrists. All of it. The stone went through every channel he has, and the channels run under every bit of skin, and when it was done the skin over them had been made again. Finer. Like a baby's." She took her hands off him. "I've been wanting to touch it for a month and I've had to write it down instead."

"You could have touched it," said Emeric.

"I was his healer for a month," said Imani. "I'm not his healer now." She took the shirt off his shoulders, and it went down his arms and onto the floor. "Now I'm going to touch it."

Ferra had stood up off the bed.

She came across the room in the candlelight, and stood beside Imani, and looked at him, and at the shirt on the floor, and at Imani; and then, very deliberately, she reached up and took the pins out of her own hair, one by one, and put them in Imani's hand, and Imani put them on the chest by the candle without looking where she put them. Ferra's hair came down over her shoulders, red even in that light, red as a forge. She stepped close to him. She put her face against his chest, against the new skin, just above the heart, and breathed in, and stayed there.

"You smell different," she said into it.

"How?"

"I don't know. I'm not writing it down." She lifted her head and looked at him. "I'm not writing anything down tonight. I said."

He kissed her.

He had kissed her a thousand times. He kissed her now as if he had never done it, slowly, and she let him, with her hands flat on his chest where Imani's had been; and Imani stood beside them in the candlelight and watched for a moment, with that look on her face she had when she watched something done well, and then put her hand on the back of his neck and turned his head and kissed him too, the same way, and Ferra did not move her hands.

The new skin felt everything.

He had not known. Imani had told him, in the dome and after, that it was finer, and he had heard it the way you hear a healer tell you things about your own body, as a fact in a book. He had not known what it would be to be touched with it. Ferra's fingers went down his ribs and he felt every one of them, separately, every ridge of every fingertip, as if the skin were listening. Imani's mouth was on his throat and he could feel her breath before her lips, and her lips before the warmth of them, as two things and then three. It was like standing in a room he had lived in for years and finding that someone had taken all the dust sheets off the furniture.

Imani felt him go still. She lifted her head.

"Too much?"

"No," he said. "No. It's only. Everything's louder."

"Good," said Imani, and smiled, slowly, the smile she did not give anybody else, and took his hand and put it on the laces at the back of her dress.

He undid them. He was slow about it, because his hands had stopped being quite steady, and because Ferra was watching his hands, and because there was no reason in the world to be quick. The dress went. Imani stepped out of it, brown and bare in the candlelight with her hair all down her back, and turned to Ferra, and undid Ferra's for her, the hooks down the front one by one while Ferra stood with her chin up and her eyes on Emeric and her face going pink from the throat upward, the way it always did, as if she had not done this before, as if she had not done this with the two of them a dozen times since the winter and every time been the first to laugh.

She did not laugh this time.

They went to the bed together, the three of them, and lay down in it, and for a long time that was all: lying close, in the warm, with the one candle, skin against skin. Ferra's leg over his. Imani's hand on his chest, flat, over the heart. Kisses, slow and many and not going anywhere, his mouth and Ferra's, Ferra's and his, his and Imani's, and then, once, very lightly, as if by accident, Ferra's and Imani's, the two of them over him, which they both pretended had not happened and which neither of them stopped. Their hands went everywhere. His went everywhere they would let them. Every place they touched him the new skin lit like a struck match.

"Count," said Ferra, at some point, against his shoulder, breathless, laughing. "Go on. You always count."

"I'm counting," said Imani.

"What?"

"His pulse." Imani had two fingers on his throat, under his jaw, and her eyes were half shut. "Ninety-four. Ninety-eight." He kissed her fingers. "A hundred and. I don't." Ferra bit his shoulder, not gently, and he made a sound he had not known he made. "A hundred and." Imani took her fingers away from his throat and put them in his hair instead and pulled his mouth down to hers. "I've lost it," she said against it. "I've lost count. I've never lost count in my life."

"Write it down," said Ferra.

"Shut up," said Imani, and pulled her down too.

What came after came slowly, and it came for a long time, and the candle burned down to a stub in its dish on the chest and guttered and nobody got up to see to it. He was careful of them, and they of him; and then not careful, and then careful again, because the night was long and none of them was going anywhere, and it did not have to be one thing. He learned what the new skin was for. He learned it with Ferra over him in the last of the candlelight with her red hair coming down round both their faces like a tent and her eyes wide open on his, not looking away, the way she never looked away from anything she had decided to do; and with Imani's mouth at his ear, saying his name, only that, over and over, low, as if it were the only word she had kept. He learned it again later in the dark with the two of them the other way round. He learned it, in the end, in the grey before the dawn, with Imani asleep against his back and Ferra asleep against his chest and his own heart going like a hammer, slowly coming back down, and the skin of his whole body humming where they touched him, everywhere, like a bell somebody has struck and walked away from.

He did not sleep.

He lay there between them in the dark with their bodies knotted round him, Ferra's arm across his chest and Imani's knee in the small of his back and both of them breathing slow and deep and even, and the heat of the three of them under the blanket like the heat off a banked forge. He could feel every place they touched him. He could feel their hearts, both of them, slow. He could feel, with his sense, open by habit, faint and small, the town round them going on with the night: a cart on the bridge, the station's night boy walking the platform, the dome's slow note in the ward below with the tree folded up in the dark; and further, the base, nine miles off on its rise, with the class asleep in their long white rooms; and Ward in his room along the gallery, not asleep, sitting up, deciding things late.

He thought about ghosts.

He thought about a pair of falcons going round on the wind over a white town, and one folding, and going down. He thought about a clerk counting eleven years of copper out of a jar. He thought about four hundred and ninety-four, on a wall, in Ferra's square hand, and a king in a cipher. Bring me a road. He thought about everything he could do, now, that he could not do a month ago, and about the fact that he had not the least idea, yet, what most of it was for. Imani stirred against his back and said something in her sleep that might have been a number. Ferra's hand on his chest closed on nothing, and opened.

He lay awake until the shutters went grey, and then gold, and the station's clock in the lower town struck six, and Imani woke all at once the way she did, the way a healer wakes, and sat up in the bed with her hair everywhere and looked down at him.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"No."

"At all."

"No."

She looked at him a moment longer. Ferra had woken too, and was lying on her side looking at him from under her hair, with one eye open.

"I slept," said Ferra. "I slept beautifully. I think I slept better than I have since I was nine." She stretched, all the way, like a cat on a warm wall, and lay back. "You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"The new skin," said Imani, in her healer's voice, getting out of the bed to look for her dress. "It wants rest. I'll write that down." She found the dress. She held it up. "He'll not get any."

"No," said Ferra, comfortably, into the pillow, with her eyes shut again. "Write that down too."
