Chapter 368 - Ride-Up

They took BR-01 out into the middle of the bay on a coal lighter, because a coal lighter was flat and wide and nobody would miss it, and because the rule was that a relay climbed over water or not at all.

It sat on the lighter's deck on a cradle of oak, finished. It had its blades on now, two long ones on the short mast, turning a little in the morning air off the land. It had its six lift stones in their sockets round the rim, Bram's, glowing the faint blue of a full bank, and the pin behind its glass port in the middle of its underside, Tess's, clear and steady, with its charge round it like breath on a cold window. Ambrose's sets were in, and the battery. Tess's decider sat in its cradle under them, a grey crystal no bigger than a goose egg with lines running through its inside that nobody but Tess and Emeric could read, and fixed to the floor plate beside the pin, in a short brass tube looking down through a sealed window of its own, was Ivo's mirror. Ivo had ground it himself. He had said so to everybody in Kaldo who'd stand still.

It was the sixth of October. The water was flat and grey and the sky had nothing in it but a little high cloud, very far up, like combed wool.

"You're sure," said Ward.

"I'm sure."

"Because you can send it up on its own. That's what the bank's for. It climbs by itself. You said so in September."

"It climbs by itself," said Emeric. "And then it sets its pin by itself five miles up, for the first time ever, with nobody there, and if the pin doesn't take it drifts off east on the wind and comes down on its chute somewhere over the sea and Tess cries." He was buckling the harness. "The first one wants a hand on it. The voyage ones won't."

"I wouldn't cry," said Tess, from the lighter's stern, on a coil of rope. "I'd be cross."

"She'd be cross," Emeric agreed.

Ward looked at him for a long moment, and then at the harness, and then at what Emeric wasn't wearing, which was a Band suit.

He had a coat on, his old flying coat, and his ordinary clothes under it, and a mask round his neck on a strap with a small bottle on his hip for breath, and that was all. He didn't need the suit. He hadn't needed one since the pod; he'd gone up off the K Discovery's rail to the Well in his shirtsleeves three winters ago, and everybody on the K Discovery had watched him do it and nobody had liked it. The cold would bite. The air up there was thin enough that he'd want the bottle for anything longer than a minute or two. But the suit was for bodies that would bleed, and his didn't.

"I'll go up with you," said Maeve.

She'd come out on the lighter with Ferra and Imani and nobody had asked her why, and she'd stood at the rail watching the stones being checked with her braid over one shoulder and her hands in the pockets of her borrowed oilskin. She'd been at the Works yard every morning at six for seventeen days. His arm didn't burn any more. It still ached afterward.

"No," said Emeric.

"Why not?"

"Because you can't fly."

"I don't need to fly. You're flying. I'd hold on." She nodded at the drum, at the two steel rings on its upper body where the harness clipped. "There's two rings. I'd take the other one. In a suit. Mira's got a suit that'd fit me, she's not much taller than me, and she's down on the ground all week with Ward doing knots."

"You'd need to ask Mira first."

"Give it time," said Maeve. She said it lightly, looking at the drum. "I doubt she'd refuse."

Something went across the back of Emeric's neck, about as fast as one of Maeve's bolts and about as hard to say where it had come from.

"Hmmph," he said.

Maeve turned her head and looked at him. "What was that noise?"

"Nothing."

"You made a noise."

"I didn't make a noise." He finished the buckle. "No. You're not coming. Not this time. The first one's mine."

Behind him, at the rail, he heard Imani say something under her breath to Ferra, two words, and he heard Ferra choke.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing," said Imani. She didn't look round. "Like Bram."

He didn't know what that meant. He knew roughly what it meant. Bram made that noise; Bram made it at the dinner table whenever either of his girls said a boy's name, and Bram's girls had been saying boys' names since they were eleven and Bram had been making the noise the whole time, low, in the chest, like a dog that hasn't decided yet. He hadn't known he could make it.

He looked at Maeve. She was looking at the drum again, quite innocently, and there was nothing in her face at all.

A friendship, then. Somebody among the knights, by the sound of it, and she'd got fond. That was all right. He'd keep an eye on it. He'd keep an eye on whichever of them it was, in fact, and they'd know he was keeping it, and he found that he was looking forward to that more than a king ought to.

"Up you go, then," said Ferra, wiping her eyes.



Tess sent the bank up to full, and the lighter's deck went light under the drum, and BR-01 lifted off its cradle.

It went up slowly. That was the design; a relay's bank had to carry it five miles, and a bank that spent itself on speed would set its pin with nothing left. Emeric went with it on the harness, one hand on the ring and the other free, with his own lift doing no more than keeping the strap slack. He wasn't flying it; he was riding it, the way you ride a cart going up a hill, with your feet on the step. The lighter dropped away under them. Ferra and Imani at its rail, and Tess on her rope, and Ward with his arms folded and his face set, and Maeve with her hand up, shading her eyes. Then the bay round the lighter. Then the harbour mouth, and the sea wall in its long arm with the light at the end, and Ilenne's oval out in the middle of the channel where the anchors had gone down. Then the whole of Kaldo.

He'd seen it from the air a thousand times. He looked anyway. The ring of the walls and the castle in the middle, the wards going down to the water, the river with its glass towers along it catching the low sun, the rail yards, the works, the long straight line of the Grayrock road going away north-west into brown country. Smoke from a hundred chimneys going straight up, because there was no wind yet. At two thousand feet he could see the trains. At five thousand he couldn't, and the whole of the city was a thing he could have covered with his thumb.

The cold came at about two miles.

He'd known it would. He warmed himself the way he always did, from the inside, the knight's way, the mana held in and turned to heat; and for a while that was enough. Then the wind started. Up past three miles it came out of the west, steady and hard, and it went through his coat as if the coat were paper, and it took the heat off the outside of him faster than he could make it inside. His face went first. Then his hands. The hand on the ring stopped telling him it was there.

He thought about it the way he'd have thought about it on the other side of everything, standing in a cold yard with a jacket that had wires in it: you don't fix a cold skin by eating more. You put the heat where the loss is.

So he put it there. He took the same padding he used inside, the brace he'd learned in the cave after the golem cracked his hand, and laid it over the outside of himself instead, skin-close, all over his arms and his chest and his back, and told it to be warm.

Warm was hard. Warm was a lot of instruction to hold in a wind like that. It kept slipping, going cold in one place while he held it in another, like a man trying to hold a blanket round himself in a gale. So he pushed harder.

And his coat caught fire.

It went all at once, the whole of the left sleeve and then the front, with a soft whump that he heard even through the wind; and he looked down at himself, at four miles over the sea, hanging off a rain barrel on a strap, and he was burning. His arms were burning. Not his coat on his arms: his arms. The fire was coming off his skin, out of the padding, a thin bright orange sheet of it, steady as a lamp flame, and the coat was going up in it because the coat was in the way.

It didn't hurt.

He noticed that second. First he slapped at his sleeve with his free hand, out of a reflex older than any world, and the free hand was burning too, so that didn't help. Then he noticed that it didn't hurt. It was his. His mana, his instruction. The fire knew whose it was, and it sat on him like a second skin and didn't so much as warm him past comfortable; and the coat, which wasn't his, burned off his arms and his chest in strips and went away down the wind in little black flakes, and the cold came straight back in through the holes.

He laughed out loud, five miles up, and the wind took it away.

He tried it on his face. He didn't decide to. He'd got the thing and he wanted to know how far it went, and the face was the part that was coldest, so he let it creep up his throat and over his jaw.

And he couldn't breathe.

The fire ate the air. The thin air up there was little enough already, and the flame took what there was before it got to his mouth, and what came in was heat, hot and empty, like breathing out of an oven door. He pulled it off his face at once and dragged the mask up off its strap and breathed from the bottle, hard, three times, with his eyes watering.

So. Over the arms, the chest, the back, yes. Over the face, no. You could have the whole of yourself covered, but then you didn't breathe; and up here, where he was already living on a bottle and borrowed breath, that was a very short clock indeed. He filed it. It was interesting. It wasn't anything he'd want to do again on purpose, he thought, and he let the fire go down off him, and it went, and the cold came in, and he warmed himself from the inside again as best he could and mended the sting on his cheekbones and the backs of his hands as he went, the small quick way that he'd taught himself from watching Imani, and rode on up in his shirt and the remains of a collar.



The Band's foot was at four miles and a bit, and he felt it the way you feel the edge of warm water in a cold lake. The air thickened round him, not in weight but in what it carried. He went on up through it. BR-01's stones felt it too; he could hear them drink.

At five miles Tess's decider woke.

He felt it do it. A small hard click in the grey crystal under Ambrose's sets, like a latch going over in another room, and the pin behind its glass port took hold. Not of anything he could see. It took hold of the Band itself, the way the Well's pin did, of the place in the air where the drum was, and held it there; and the drum stopped climbing, and stopped drifting, and hung. The wind went past it and it didn't move. He took his hand off the ring and it still didn't move. He hung on the strap beside it, held up by his own lift, in the blue-black and the cold and the enormous silence, and watched it hold.

The whip antenna had unfolded below it. The boom had swung out on top with the two beams on it, pointing away from each other, one toward the Well, one out to sea at nothing yet. He heard the sets come on: a faint warm hum through the plate. Then, through the mask's earpiece, on his own field set, the first beat. Short. A burst of nothing he could read, in Ferra's cipher, going down the Well's beam.

A long moment later the Well answered.

And then, very faint, under all of it, Ambrose's voice from the line room in Kaldo, five miles down and a mile or two inland, through a crackle like frying fat: "BR-01, line room. I have you. I have you clean. The Well has you." A pause. "Tess says it's not a dustbin."

"BR-01," said Emeric, into the mask. "Holding. Pin's set. I'm letting go of it now."

"Don't," said Ambrose, alarmed.

"I mean the strap."

"Oh," said Ambrose. "Yes. All right."

He unclipped the harness.



Then, because he was there, and because he would never again be the first man to hang beside the first one, he looked.

He had seen the Band before. He had seen the cloud deck from above and the sea from above and Kaldo from above. He had never stopped, at this height, with nothing to do, and let himself see all of it.

The horizon was a long way off. Two hundred miles; he knew the sum. It ran all the way round him in a ring, and inside the ring was the world.

The sea to the east and south, grey-blue going to silver at the edge, with the cane islands scattered on it like crumbs and South Sentinel low and green. Kaldo under him, a coin. The coast going away north, cliff and bay and cliff, with Duvrenne somewhere up the end of it, past the haze. And inland, west, the whole of Randillia laid out like a map on a table: the brown and green of it, the patchwork of the farms, Milford's long fields, the hills where Grayrock was, a glint that was the river, a glint that was another river, a darker smudge that was the forest past Wayford. And past that, past the last thing he had a name for, more country. More hills. A long low line of something that might have been mountains, blue with distance, at the very edge. And beyond the mountains, he supposed, Solreth's plains, and somewhere north of them Velmoor's forests and its coast, and the great forest that the old maps called by a name nobody used. He couldn't see them. He knew they were there.

He had never spoken to anybody in any of it.

It came on him quite slowly, hanging there, the way the cold had. Not to anybody. He had fought Duvrenne and then signed a treaty with it, and that was a war and a paper. He had fought the capital and the Church, and that was a war too. He'd refused the Empire's envoy. He'd sat at his own crowning and watched an envoy from Solreth read a letter and an envoy from Velmoor bow, and he had shaken their hands, and Ferra had written down that the Solreth man's calendar was older than the Church's; and that was all. That was the whole of it. Sixteen years, and every person outside his own borders he had ever dealt with had been an enemy or a paper. He had never sat down with any of them and asked what they ate.

When had they become like this? He hadn't meant it. Nobody meant it. It was distance, and the cost of a road, and news that came a month late. It was what every government did when it had enough to do already: you minded your border, and past the border was weather.

And he was going to sail past the last islands in the spring, through the wall, to the door of a queen who had never in a thousand years seen a man of his people. The first of them in a thousand years. And he had been going to send a deputy.

No. He hadn't been. He'd only been letting everybody assume he might, because it was the sensible thing and he hadn't wanted the argument. He knew now that he wasn't going to. He'd never once spoken to anybody past his own border except as an enemy. The first people past the wall in a thousand years were not going to meet a man he'd sent. They were going to meet him.

He'd have to say that to his father. He didn't look forward to it.

He looked down at BR-01, hanging beside him with its blades turning in the Band's wind and its beams pointing east and west, and Ivo's mirror in its brass tube looking down through its window at the bay. He thought, idly, the way you think in a warm bath, that from up here, with glass like that in it, a drum could see the whole of that. All of it. Every road and every port, Duvrenne's harbours and the capital's gates and whatever was moving on the Grayrock road, all the time, day and night, so long as the sky was clear. Not just the bay. If he put glass in every drum.

He put that thought down carefully, the way you put down something that might be hot, and didn't pick it up again.

He had another one, under it, that he didn't like at all. It came from the other side of everything, out of a book, the kind of book men wrote late at night about wars that hadn't happened yet. From this height, a rod of iron dropped straight down would be going faster than any shell when it struck. You wouldn't need powder. You wouldn't need a gun. You'd need a rod, and a height, and something to let go of it.

He looked at that one a long time, five miles up, in his burnt shirt. Then he put it away somewhere further back than the first, where he kept the things he'd decided not to build.

The last thought was the one he let stay. It was the one he'd come up with, if he was honest. The ring of country under him, two hundred miles in every direction, and all of it dark at night except where Kaldo was. Kaldo was the only light. He could see, quite clearly, the shape of what it could be with a line of these drums laid across it: Milford and Grayrock and Wayford and the capital and every town in between talking to each other the way Kaldo's wards did, the trains running to times that the whole kingdom kept, the weather known before it came. Roads, and rails, and wires, and light. The whole of Randillia lit up at night the way Kaldo was, and then past it. He could see it as plainly as he could see the rivers.

Not yet. Not this year. He'd write it on a list, at the bottom, with two question marks, like the train.



He had one more thing to do before he went down, and he'd been putting it off for the last ten minutes, and he knew it.

The net had been passing traffic clean for a quarter of an hour. Ambrose had sent the line room's test, and the Well had sent it back, and the beats were going down the Well's beam on their wandering count, and Tess had come on once to say that the decider was hearing its second neighbour, which was the Well, and that it hadn't accused anybody of anything yet. It was proven. The tech came first. That was the rule; he'd made it himself, in September.

So he turned the field set's dial to the call sign he'd had Ambrose set in the kit back of a golem's armour, in the castle, in Kaldo, and pressed the key.

She answered before he'd let go of it.

"Emeric?"

It came through the crackle very small, very far down, and entirely her: the voice he'd been given back in his own head through the bridge. He had built the set she was hearing him on. He had built the ears she was hearing it with.

"It's me," he said.

"Where are you? You sound like you're down a well."

"I'm up one. Five miles. Over the bay." He looked down at the coin of Kaldo, and the castle in the middle of it, and somewhere in the castle a woman in a body of plate and glass sitting very still with her hand on her own side where the set was. "It works. The first one. It's up, it's holding. You're the first call."

A pause. A long one, on the line.

"Am I," said Amara.

"After the line room. Ambrose had to check it. You're the first one that matters."

"Well," said his mother. He heard her decide, at the other end, not to say what she had been going to say; he knew the sound now. "Is it cold?"

"Very."

"Have you got a coat on?"

He looked down at himself. At his shirt, with no sleeves left on it to speak of, and the black edges, and a little curl of smoke still coming off one shoulder.

"Some of one," he said.

"Come down, then," said Amara, "before you lose the rest of it," and he heard her laugh, the real one, very faint, five miles down, and then Harrold's voice somewhere behind her asking who it was.



He came down on the lighter's deck half an hour later out of a clear sky, on his own lift, in the remains of his shirt, with the mask round his neck and soot on his face, and the four of them at the rail looked at him.

"You were wearing a coat," said Ward.

"I was."

"Where is it?"

"Halfway to South Sentinel by now, I should think, in small pieces."

Imani came across the deck, took him by the chin, turned his face to the light, and looked at the backs of his hands, and then his cheekbones, which were pink, and then his arms, which weren't anything at all.

"Your clothes burned," she said.

"Yes."

"And you didn't."

"No."

She looked at him for a long time with her sight open. He felt her go over him, careful and narrow, the way she did, and find nothing.

"You're going to tell me about that," she said, "tonight, with the door shut, and Ferra's going to write it down." She let go of his chin. "And you're not going to do it again until you have."

"I wasn't going to do it again."

"That's what you said about the Crumple."

Above them, five miles up, too small to see, BR-01 hung where he'd left it with Ivo's mirror looking down on all of them, and every quarter of an hour, at a time that wandered, it said to the Well, in Ferra's cipher, down a beam nobody else could hear, that it was there.

Maeve was looking up at the place where it was. Then she looked at him, at the burnt shirt and the soot, with her head on one side.

"Next time," she said.

"Hmmph," said Emeric, before he could stop it, and Imani turned round and walked very quickly to the other end of the lighter.
