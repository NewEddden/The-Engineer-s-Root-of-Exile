Chapter 348 - Two Queens

The ward kept a kettle on a fire stone in the back office, and on the fourth of August, at a quarter to seven, Emeric made coffee on it.

He had been doing it for a week. Tamsy Brook's girl had shown him once, at the counter, with the patience of somebody showing a dog where the door was, and he had gone away with a paper of ground beans and the belief that it was an engineering problem. Water, heat, time, a filter. Ottoline had made him the filter, a brass cone with a punched plate.

It was not an engineering problem. He did not yet know what kind of problem it was.

He carried two cups through the back passage, past the linen press and the door of the long room where Danner slept, and shouldered into the office. Imani had her feet on the desk and the night book open on her knees. Ferra had the other chair and the other half of the desk, with her own ledger and the morning's post squared beside it, because the ward was where Imani was in the mornings and Ferra had stopped pretending her books needed the records hall.

"What is it today?" said Imani, without looking up.

"Coffee."

"You said that yesterday." She took the cup and smelled it and held it out at the length of her arm. "Ferra. Smell this."

Ferra didn't smell it. She put one finger against the side of the cup, the way she'd test a stone.

"It was boiling when he poured it," she said. "It's still too hot. He boiled the water with the grounds in it."

"You're not meant to boil it?"

"Tamsy's girl told you not to boil it. I was standing next to you."

"I thought she meant not for long."

Imani drank some anyway, and made a face that went on for a while, and drank some more.

"It's not worse than yesterday," she said.

"Thank you."

"Yesterday was a punishment. This is only a mistake." She put the cup down on the night book, which Ferra moved out from under it without a word and replaced with a saucer.

He could make a pressure vessel hold at four hundred pounds to the inch. He minded the coffee more than he could explain. Ferra looked at him over her cup with the expression she wore for a column that wouldn't add.

"He'll get it," she said to Imani. "He got the glass furnace right in the end."

"He got the glass furnace with Bram."

"Bram won't help with this. I asked."

Ferra's ledger was open at a page he had not been shown. It had a date at the top, in October, ringed twice, and under it a column in her small upright hand headed Not yet decided, which was long, and a second column headed Decided, which was not. Doran's name was in the first column with ask beside it. So was Rowena's. So was the dome after?, with a question mark she had gone over twice, as if it might come off.

Imani leaned across and read it upside down.

"You've put me in it."

"You're in it twice," said Ferra. "Once under guests, by mistake. I'll take you out."

"Leave me in. I'd like to be a guest. I'd sit at the back and eat."

"You'd sit at the back and tell the cook his sauce had split."

"Only if it had."

Nobody else had seen the page. The six knew, and Duvrenne's people, and nobody else. The wedding would tell them. In the meantime it was a ringed date and a column, and Ferra ran her pen down the column most mornings the way other women might have touched a ring.

Under the ledger the morning's post sat in its pile for the boy: a coal bill, a reply to Grayrock about their bridge, a note to the Motion Shop about brake blocks, and three plain envelopes addressed in three different hands, which would go out with the ordinary mail like everything else and tell the keepers, in Ferra's cipher, one word each, that Kaldo was still there. She sent them every month. Nobody remarked on them. That was what they were for.

The telephone went in the front hall.

It was a loud bell and a new one, and the girl on the front desk had been told to pick it up on the second ring and not the first, so as not to look as if she'd been waiting. She picked it up on the first. They heard her say yes twice and then how long, and then she was in the passage.

"The west quarry," she said. "A crane chain's let go. A man under the hook. They've got a strap on his leg and they say there's a lot of blood."

Imani took her feet off the desk.

"Send it," she said. "Table two. Tell them to scrub and lay out the fine gut."

"It's gone already," said the girl. "They heard. They were sitting in it."

Emeric went out into the yard in time to see it leave.

The Motion Shop had built it on the crawler's engine and a cart's sprung frame, with a box body behind the driver's bench, two stretchers on rails inside and a lamp stone in the roof. They had painted THE WARD on both sides in plain letters. Emeric had called it an ambulance the first time he saw the drawings, and Wren had written ambulance on the drawings, and it was an ambulance. This was its first call. The driver was a carter who'd driven the brewery's drays for twenty years and had been made to learn to stop, which was the part he found hard. Two of Imani's medics sat in the back with the doors latched open, hanging on.

It went out of the yard with its bell going, toward the west road. People stepped back off the street for it.

Imani was already in the scrub room with her sleeves up and the brush going.

"You can watch," she said, "from the gallery. Wash anyway."



It came back in twenty-six minutes. Imani counted them, because she always counted, and because twenty-six was better than the forty it would have been on a cart.

The doors swung, and the medics brought him down the passage at a jog with the stretcher between them and the driver behind with the chest, and they had done it right. She saw that before she saw anything else. The strap was high on the thigh and wound tight with a stick through it, the way she'd taught them, and someone had written the time on the man's forehead in wax pencil: 7.18. Below it, smaller, because the medic who'd written it was the one who'd asked her a dozen questions in the spring and wrote down all the answers, strap.

"Seven eighteen," said the medic. "Torn on the inside, high up. It was spurting. It's not now."

"It's not now because you've stopped his leg," said Imani. "Good. On the table. Mind the strap."

He was a big man in a stone-coloured shirt that had been cut off him, with quarry dust in the hair of his arms and his face grey under it. He was awake. He looked at her and at the lamps and said, very reasonably, that he'd like to keep the leg if it was all the same to everyone.

"So would I," said Imani. "Lie still."

She looked.

The sight had been hers since before the stone. Since the stone it was finer up close than anyone's, finer than Emeric's, and she still hadn't got used to how much of it there was. She went in past the skin and the fat and the torn muscle, which was a mess and could wait, to the big vessel that ran down the inside of the thigh. The hook had taken it half across, a long ragged tear, and the strap above it had it pinched off like a hose under a boot. Below the strap the leg was cold. Thirty minutes cold. She could have another hour, at a push, before the leg began to die from the bottom up. She didn't want to push.

The old healer from the capital was in the gallery with Emeric, the man who had told her in May that nobody grew a limb. He had not said anything about lumps for weeks.

"Gut," she said, and the nurse put the curved needle in her hand already threaded, fine as hair. "Loosen the strap when I say. Not before."

"Madam," said the medic, "it'll go like a fountain."

"It won't."

In the first week after she woke she'd sat in the dome with a basin of water on her knees and held it still. Not calm. Still. Dead still, so that Ferra dropped a pin into it and the pin lay on the top like a pin on glass, and nothing moved, and Imani had laughed so hard the basin went over. Blood was mostly water. It had taken her most of June to believe that it knew it.

She reached in, not with her hands, and found the column of blood in the vessel above the strap, and held it.

"Now," she said.

The medic loosened the stick, a turn and then another. The strap went slack.

Nothing came.

The blood lay in the vessel above the tear like a thumb was on it, from inside, and it didn't press and it didn't pulse, and below it the torn ends lay open and nearly dry. She could feel the heart behind it all, a long way up, pushing, and the push arriving and stopping where she'd told it to stop, and going round by the small vessels instead, the way it would have to anyway when she was done. It was like holding a door shut against a crowd with one finger. It wasn't heavy. You just couldn't think about anything else.

She sewed.

The ends first, drawn together where they wanted to meet and not where they'd fallen, because a tear was never straight and a body mending by itself mended the way it had been left. If she just mended it she'd have a vessel healed in a pucker, narrow where the tear had been, and in ten years he'd have a foot that was cold in winter and couldn't say why. So the stitches went in first, small and close, a hair apart, the gut that the body would take up in a few weeks once it didn't need it. Then, when the shape was right, she mended along the line of the stitches, telling the wall where it went, and the wall listened and closed.

She let go of the blood a little at a time.

It came down into the mended vessel and filled it, and pushed, and the vessel held. She watched the colour come down the leg under the skin like a tide coming over sand.

"His foot's pink," said the nurse.

"I know."

"It was white."

"I know. I'm looking at it."

The muscle she did in layers, the deep first, and closed the skin with ordinary thread, and left a line she'd take off in a month if he wanted it off. Quarrymen mostly wanted to keep the line.

The old healer came down from the gallery when she'd finished, and stood at the foot of the table with his hands behind his back, and looked at the leg for a long time.

"How long did you hold it?"

"Since you started counting."

"In the capital," he said, "we'd have taken the leg off at the hip. And he'd have died of the fever on the fourth day." He said it the way a man reads out his own accounts. "I was quick. They said I was the quickest in the city."

She didn't know what to say to that, so she said nothing, which Emeric would have told her was correct and Ferra would have told her was rare.

The quarryman had been awake through all of it, on the draught the nurse had given him for the pain, watching the ceiling. He turned his head.

"Is it still on?"

"It's on."

"Will it work?"

"If you do what you're told for a month." She stripped off her gloves. "Which nobody ever does. So six weeks."

On her way to wash she passed Danner in the long room's doorway, in his shirt, squeezing a ball of rubber in a left hand that was still too pink and too thin and that had not existed in April. He squeezed it twice a minute because she'd told him to and had been doing it for nine days without being reminded, which made him the best patient she had.

"Was it bad?" he said.

"It was a leg." She looked at the ball. "Harder. You're cheating on the last finger."

He was. He stopped.

She washed her hands and her arms to the elbow and stood in the scrub room with them under the tap for longer than they needed, and when she turned the tap off her hands were perfectly steady. It was her knees that had opinions. She sat down on the bench and let them have them, and after a while Emeric came in and gave her a cup of something from Tamsy's that was properly made, which she drank without comment, because some days you let a man win.



The punt went over two days later, at dusk, under the middle arch of the north bridge.

Emeric was on the bridge with Imani. They'd been in the dome all afternoon with Amara and the feed gauges, and his father had come in at six and taken over the talking, and they'd left the two of them by the tree and come out across the gravel into the evening. The lamps along the moat had come on a minute before, on the wire, all the way round the ring at once, and the willows had gone from grey to green under them.

He heard it before he saw it. A scrape, and a voice, and then a sound he didn't know, heavy and hollow, like a door slammed underwater.

Imani was at the parapet. Below them, on the dark water inside the arch, the long flat bottom of a punt rolled up into the lamplight, black and shining, and turned the rest of the way over and lay there like the back of something asleep. There had been coping stones on it. He'd seen it come round the bend a minute earlier, low in the water with dressed stone for the masons who were relaying the moat wall on the east bank, and two men poling it, one at each end.

There was one man in the water. He came up and went down again.

The boat stage was at the foot of the bridge. The man who hired the boats out was already on the end of it with a boat hook, and his boy behind him with a lamp, and the man leaned out and got the hook into a coat collar and hauled, and the boy put the lamp down and got hold of an arm, and between them they had the man on the planks by the time Emeric had come down the bank steps. He took them three at a time and didn't remember doing it.

The man on the planks wasn't breathing. He was grey, and his mouth was open, and there was moat weed in his hair.

"He's gone," said the boat man. "He's gone, I saw him go under."

"He's not gone," said Imani. She was on her knees. "He's full."

She put her hand flat on his chest.

Emeric felt her do it. He'd felt her hold the basin, and the blood in the quarryman's leg from the gallery, and this was the same thing turned the other way, not stillness but a pull. The water came up out of the man. It came out of his mouth and his nose in a long sheet that ran over the planks, more of it than seemed possible from one chest, moat-green, with a thread of weed in it, and Imani turned him on his side as it came so it ran off him and not back in.

He coughed.

It was a terrible cough, the kind that turns a body inside out, and it went on and on, and then he was sick, more water, and then he was breathing, in great dragging whoops, with his face in the wet planks. Imani held his head off them with her hand under his cheek.

"That's it," she said. "That's it. Keep doing that."

The boat man's boy had begun to cry, quite quietly, holding the lamp.

The man on the planks got his breath. He got enough of it to lift his head. He looked at the water, and at the black hull lying under the arch, and said one word, hoarse, more of a shape than a word.

"Edwy."

Emeric looked at the boat man.

"His brother," said the boat man. "The Bracks. They were both on her." His face changed. "I only saw the one go in."

Emeric was already sending.

He had learned to sense through walls years ago and through water not long after, and the moat here was nine feet and dark and full of silt, and his sense went down into it like a hand into a pond. The hull. The poles, floating. The coping stones, eight of them, on the bottom where they'd slid off the deck when she rolled, in a heap against the bridge pier. Weed. A dead branch. And under the stones, at the foot of the pier, with his legs pinned and his arms up, a man, not moving at all.

He took the stones off.

He didn't lift them. He pulled, the way he'd learned in a hundred mornings at the base, the whole heap at once, up and out and away from the pier, and the water heaved under the arch and the stones went over onto the bank beyond the stage in a long slither of mud, and the boat man shouted. His right hand, still sore, went hot to the wrist. He was in the water by then. It was warm on top and cold underneath, and he went down along his own sense with his eyes shut because they were no use, and found an arm, and a coat, and brought him up.

They got him onto the stage between them. The boat man took his shoulders. The brother had sat up, and was holding onto the stage's post with both hands as if the stage were moving.

Imani put her hand on Edwy Brack's chest.

The water came out of him the same way. More of it. She turned him the same way. He didn't cough.

She kept her hand there. Emeric knelt on the other side and put his own hand on the man's wrist, which told him nothing he didn't know, and then, because he could, he looked in.

The heart had stopped. That he'd seen before. He'd seen Amara's stopped by the tree, and put his hand on her and put a burst of lightning through her the way a man on Earth would have done it with paddles, and it had started. His hand was already moving. He could feel the charge gathering in it.

Imani took hold of his wrist.

She didn't grip it. She just put her hand round it, and he stopped.

"It isn't his heart," she said.

She was looking past the heart. He looked where she was looking, and it took him longer to see than it had taken her, because her close sight was better than his and always had been. The brain was dark. Not quiet. Dark. It had gone without blood for longer than it could bear, and it had begun, from the inside, in its deepest places first, to come apart, and the coming apart had a look to it that he had read about once in another life and had never seen. It was very small and very thorough.

"How long?" said Imani, to the boat man, without looking up.

"The lamps had just come on. When she went over. I saw them come on and then I heard her go."

Twenty minutes, near enough. The brother had been under for three, perhaps, before the hook found his collar. Edwy had been under the stones the whole time.

Imani took her hand off his chest.

"I can't," she said.

That was all she said. She sat back on her heels on the wet planks and put her hands in her lap and looked at them.

The brother had let go of the post. He came along the stage on his knees, because his legs weren't working yet, and stopped beside Edwy and looked at him. He was older than Edwy by a few years, Emeric thought, though they had the same broad nose, the same big lighterman's shoulders, the same weed in their hair. He didn't say anything. He didn't touch his brother.

Then he said, "She's on the pier."

Nobody understood him.

"The punt. She's fouling the arch. The night boats come through at ten with the coal for the dome." He looked round at the boat man. "She'll have to come off or they'll go into her."

"We'll get her off," said the boat man. "Don't you worry about the punt."

"She'll have to come off."

"We'll do it."

The boy had fished Edwy's cap out of the water with the hook. He held it out. The brother took it and wrung it out, carefully, twisting it the way you'd wring a cloth, and then he shook it out and pulled it straight and laid it on Edwy's chest, and then moved it an inch, so it sat square, and left his hand on it.

"He couldn't swim," he said. "Thirty years on the water. Never learned. Said he'd no need, he'd never go in." He kept his hand on the cap. "He never did."

People had come down the bank steps by then. A watchman, and two of the masons from the east wall in their aprons, and a woman from the houses on the far side with a blanket, who put it round the brother's shoulders without asking and stood behind him with her hands on it. The ambulance came at a quarter past nine. Somebody had run to the telephone in the bridge house. It came down to the stage road slowly, without its bell.

Imani rode in the back with Edwy Brack. Emeric walked behind with his boots full of the moat and the brother beside him in the blanket, and neither of them said anything all the way up the hill, and at the ward door the brother stopped and looked back the way they'd come, toward the bridge and the lamps on the water, and said the punt was his, really, Edwy only worked it.



She was on the ward steps at midnight with her knees up and her arms round them.

He'd been home and changed and come back. Ferra was inside, at the front desk, writing something to the masons' master about the coping stones in a voice he could hear through the door. He sat down on the step below Imani's, so his head was at her knee, and didn't say anything.

Down the hill the lamps went round the moat in their ring, and the night boats had got through.

"In the delve," she said. "In the sleep. You had ten things to teach me."

"Yes."

"I took nine. I said I'd have the last one when I needed it."

"I remember."

She put her chin on her knees.

"Go on, then."

He thought about how to say it, and then said it as short as it would go, because she'd seen it already and only wanted it named.

"The brain burns more than anything else in the body and keeps the least back. Four minutes without blood, five, and it starts to die, and it dies from the inside first, where you don't see it yet." He looked down at the moat. "What a person is isn't in the cells. It's in how they're joined to each other. Every joining, all their lives. You can make new cells. You could make him a whole new brain, probably, the shape of the old one. You couldn't make the joins, because nothing knows where they were. Not his body. Not you. It isn't kept anywhere else."

"So it's gone."

"It's gone."

She was quiet for a long time.

"Twenty minutes," she said.

"He was gone at five. You were on the bridge at four."

"I know." She let her breath out. "I wrote him in the book. Edwy Brack, lighterman. I didn't know if Edwy had a y or an ie. I asked his brother. He didn't know either. He said nobody had ever written it down."

The door opened behind them, and Ferra came out and sat on the step beside Imani without a word, and put her coat round both their shoulders, though it was August and not cold, and the three of them stayed there till the boat under the arch had gone.



The Church's air crews went home on the twelfth.

They came down from the sheds where they'd been kept since the winter in two columns, with Osric's people on either side and nobody in irons, carrying what they'd come with and what they'd been given: their own boots, mended, a coat each from the mill, a canvas bag. There was bread in the bags, and cheese, and a water bottle, and nothing else, because Saban had gone through each one himself the evening before and taken out a lamp stone that one of them had prised off a wall and a bicycle bell that another had won at cards.

"He can keep the bell," Emeric had said.

"No," said Saban. "Somebody in Ostrel will take it apart."

"It's a bell."

"It's a Kaldo bell. Wren filed it." Saban had put it in his pocket. "They'll take a bell apart and find nothing in it and decide that's the secret."

The K Endeavor lay at the east quay with her ramp down. She'd carried them in from the white hulls on the night of the battle, the clerks writing a card for each man as he came up out of the dark, and she'd carry them out. Saban had chosen where she'd set them down, beyond the border, on a road of his choosing, under a flag of truce, at an hour he had told nobody.

Ferra stood at the foot of the ramp with the cards.

"Three hundred and forty-one by the cards in the winter," she said, when Emeric came to stand beside her. "Eleven asked for rooms, and have them. Every other name's ticked." She turned a card over. "Imani's women saw each one yesterday."

They came up the quay in their columns, browner than they'd been. Some of them had learned to lay a course of brick under a Kaldo mason who shouted at them in exactly the voice he used for his own apprentices, and one of them, Ferra said, had asked to stay and been refused, because he wanted to stay for the mason's daughter and the mason's daughter had been asked.

Doran was on the quay in his old black cassock and the red hat Ostrel had sent, which didn't fit. He'd asked to come. He stood a little apart from everyone with his hands folded, and as each column came up he made the sign of the Eye over it, without fuss, the way he'd make it over a sick bed. Some of them stopped and knelt on the stones to take it. Most of them looked at him, and at the hat, and made up their minds about something, and went on. One officer at the head of the second column, a tall man with a scar through his eyebrow, looked at Doran with real dislike and walked past with his eyes front, and Doran blessed him anyway, at a distance, and the officer's neck went red.

"He'll tell them in Ostrel I did that," said Doran to nobody, mildly. "Good."

Piran, whose two ribs had been strapped on the K Endeavor's deck in the winter, was the last but one up the ramp. He stopped at the top and looked back down at the quay, and found Emeric there, and didn't wave or nod or do anything a man could be reported for. He looked for a while. Then he went in.

They had begun the loading late, by Saban's order. By the time the last column came up the light was going, and along Gate Avenue and the quay road and up the hill in their rings, all at once, the lamps came on.

The men on the ramp stopped.

It went back along the column like a word passed. They'd seen it every night for six months from behind the fence, from the doors of the sheds; this was the first time they'd seen it from the open, with nothing in the way. The whole town lit, ring on ring, up to the castle, with the dome a pale shape in the middle and the moat a circle of light round it.

A young one near the top of the ramp, sixteen or seventeen, with ears that stuck out under his cap, turned to the garrison man at the rail beside him. The garrison man had the crown's device on his breastplate in blue enamel, a little crooked.

"Are they real?" said the boy.

The garrison man looked at him.

"The lanterns. Are they real?"

"They're lamps," said the garrison man.

"But are they..." The boy didn't have the word. "In Ostrel they said it was a glamour. On the town. To make it look like more than it was."

The garrison man thought about it, with every appearance of giving it his full attention.

"I've got one in my kitchen," he said. "Over the sink. My wife does the washing up by it." He jerked his head at the ramp. "Go on. You're holding up the line."

The boy went on. At the top he looked back once more at the lit town, the way the brother had looked back at the bridge, and then the dark of the hold had him.

The ramp came up at twenty past eight. The K Endeavor lifted off the quay with her running lights hooded and went away over the dark fields, low, until there was nothing to see of her, and Saban stood at the quay's edge with the lamp stone in one pocket and the bicycle bell in the other and watched the place where she'd been.

"They'll tell it," he said. "Nobody will believe them. Then their sons will."

"Is that what you want?"

"It's what's going to happen," said Saban. "I only want it on the record that I knew."



On the morning of the fourteenth, Abloh's figures were still on the loft wall from the spring, chalked in his square hand down the plaster beside the window: the six's grey suits, the crowning coat, a column for Waylon with a line through it where Waylon had refused a second fitting. He hadn't rubbed any of them out. He said a wall was a ledger and Ferra could keep hers on paper if she liked.

Ferra had hers open before the door shut.

"Both of us," she said. "Taken in January. To the eighth." She held the book out to him, open at the page.

Abloh didn't take it. He had three pins in his mouth and a tape round his neck, a proper tailor's tape now, cloth, marked in inches, that Dov had marked for him off a steel rule in the winter, and he looked at the book the way he looked at any measurement somebody else had taken.

"January," he said, round the pins.

"Yes."

"Before the sleep." He took the tape off his neck. "Arms out."

Ferra put her arms out. She did it with the book still in one hand, and he took it off her and put it on the bench without looking at it, and went round her with the tape, shoulders, back, the length of the arm from the point of the shoulder to the wrist bone, and wrote each one on the wall in chalk under nothing, a new column, and then stood back and looked at it beside the old one.

"Half an inch across the back," he said. "And the arm."

"I haven't grown."

"Not up." Abloh took a pin out of his mouth. "You came out of that sleep with more of you where you carry things. You'll have noticed the doors."

Ferra had broken two door handles since the spring and a kettle lid. She picked up her book and crossed out a figure, and wrote his under it, and didn't say anything, and Imani, by the window, made a small noise into her sleeve that she turned into a cough.

"You next," said Abloh, without looking round.

"Me?"

"You were in the same sleep."

Emeric had a roll of Bram's tracing paper under his arm, tied with a bit of wire, and he waited until Abloh had finished with Imani and written her column up beside Ferra's on the wall. Then he untied it and spread it on the cutting table and put the chalk box on one corner and Abloh's shears on the other.

There were three sheets. He'd drawn them at the long table over four nights, with Ferra reading figures across the table to him and Imani asleep on the bench under the window with her boots on, and he'd drawn them the way he drew anything he wanted somebody else to build: the lines that carried the weight and nothing on them.

The first two were the same shape, near enough. A woman's outline, standing. The shoulders bare, the line of the dress coming across below them. Close through the body to the waist, and from the waist the skirt going out wide and on, all the way to the floor and then further, behind her, along the floor, so that the drawing ran off the end of the sheet and he'd had to tape a second strip of paper to it to finish. From the crown of the head, a line that came down past the shoulders and the back and the skirt to the floor as well, drawn faint, with a note beside it in his small hand: thin. You see through it.

The third was a long coat, open, to the boot, falling from the shoulders straight down like a cloak.

Abloh looked at them for a long time. He put his spectacles on, which he did for work he hadn't seen before, and bent over the first sheet with his hands flat on the table either side of it.

"There's nothing on them," he said.

"No."

"You've drawn me a woman in a sack with a tail."

"I've drawn you where it hangs from," said Emeric. "What goes on it is yours."

Abloh looked up at him over the spectacles.

"Black and gold," said Ferra, from behind them. "It's the house's."

"It's the house's because I put it there," said Abloh, and bent back over the drawing. He put one finger on the tail of the skirt where it ran across the taped strip. "What's this?"

"A train."

"Behind her. On the floor."

"Yes."

"On Doran's floor. Down the lane. Up the castle steps." Abloh measured it against his hand, then against the tape. "That's a floor's worth of silk dragging after her, picking up whatever the town's dropped. Who carries it?"

"Nobody. It follows her."

"Hm." He moved his finger to the faint line from the crown of the head. "And this."

"A veil."

"Over the face."

"Until the end."

Abloh took his spectacles off, and folded them, and looked at Emeric with his head on one side, as he'd once looked at a coil of copper wire he'd been asked to wax and had never seen the like of. He had sewn felt boxes for things he didn't understand and harnesses for men jumping off airships. He'd stopped asking, mostly, where Emeric got his ideas. Emeric could see him decide not to ask now.

"Anything else?" he said.

"One thing." Emeric rolled the coat sheet back a little so it lay apart from the others. "I don't see them. Not your drawings, not a fitting, not a sleeve through a door. Not till the chapel."

Abloh was quiet.

"You looked last time," he said. "At the capital. Through the door. A hand's width."

"Not this time."

"Why?"

"It's bad luck."

Behind him, Imani was looking at the floor with great attention. Ferra was writing something in her book that didn't need writing. Neither of them had laughed in bed two nights ago when he told them, in the dark, about the dress nobody was supposed to see, and neither of them was going to laugh now.

Abloh put the three pins back in his mouth, one by one, and took them out again.

"All my life at a sail," he said. "I've had masters stand at my elbow and tell me where the reef points go. I've had an owner lift the canvas off the loft floor while I was cutting it, to see what he was paying for. Since I took up coats I've had the six in and out of here like it was a tavern, and you, at the capital, at a door." He looked at Emeric. "Say it again."

"I won't look."

"Good," said Abloh. He rolled the coat sheet flat and turned it toward himself. "Arms out. Then go away."



Imani watched him go down the loft stairs with his hands in his pockets and the expression of a man who had been sent out of his own house, and when the street door had shut Abloh went to the window and watched him all the way across the yard and round the corner by the rope store, as if he expected him to come back and look through the keyhole. Then he came back to the table.

He picked up a stick of charcoal.

"Roses," he said.

"Roses," said Ferra. "His mother's."

"His mother's and his." Abloh was already drawing, over the faint lines of Emeric's bones, quickly, the charcoal squeaking a little on the tracing paper. "You had them last time, all down your skirt, and it was right, last time, because you were walking up that hall beside him and the capital had to see whose you were." He drew a long line up the bodice, and another across it, curving. "Not this time. This time you're not walking beside him. You're walking to him." The line put out a short stiff branch, and a leaf, and a thorn. "So you get the cane."

Imani leaned over the table. Under his hand the black of the skirt was filling, in a few strokes, with something that climbed: long gold canes running up from the hem in the diamond shapes Ferra liked, crossing and crossing again, with leaves on them and thorns, and no flowers anywhere.

"No roses at all," she said.

"A rose is the last thing the bush does," said Abloh. "All the year before that, it's this." He tapped the cane with the charcoal. "Let him have the flower. His mother had the flower. You two are the bit that holds it up."

Ferra said nothing for a moment, which was rare enough that Imani looked round at her. She was looking at the drawing with her mouth very firm.

"Yes," she said.

Abloh drew Ferra's in a quarter of an hour, and talked while he did it, mostly to the paper. A collar of gold standing up close round the throat, and from it chains, fine ones, falling across the bare shoulders and the top of the chest and joining at the breastbone over a drop of clear stone. Sleeves of something so thin you'd see her arms through it, gold-edged. The back open almost to the waist, with the chains crossing it. The skirt in panels, layer over layer, each edged in gold, so that when she walked they'd move against each other and show the gold underneath. He drew the train out to the end of Emeric's taped strip and then, after a moment's thought, past it, onto the table.

"The back," said Ferra.

"Nobody in that chapel's going to see your back but the people behind you."

"That's everybody."

"That's the idea," said Abloh, and started on Imani's.

Imani watched the bodice come close and the shoulders come off bare and the front come down, lower than anything she had worn in her life, and the same thin sleeves, and the same open back, and the skirt fuller than Ferra's and heavier, and she put her hand flat on the paper.

"No," she said.

Abloh stopped.

"I've worn a collar to my chin for fourteen years," said Imani. "In the ward. In the street. At the capital, I wore it to my chin and you let me."

"I did."

"If somebody faints in that chapel, I'm on my knees. I'm not kneeling on Doran's floor with my shoulders out and a tail of silk behind me that I can't get my legs out of." She heard her own voice and didn't make it any softer. "I'm a healer."

"Not that day," said Abloh.

"Every day."

He looked at her for a while, with the charcoal in his fingers. Then he moved her hand off the paper, quite gently, and drew.

He drew a collar. Gold, standing up from the dress close round her throat to just under the jaw, stiffer and higher than Ferra's, with a dark stone set in the front of it. He drew it carefully, and shaded it, and sat back.

"There's your throat," he said. "Covered to the chin. Same as always." He touched the drawing at the waist, then at each shoulder. "Hooks. Here, here and here, under the gold, where nobody sees. The outer skirt comes off at the waist and the sleeves come off at the shoulder. Pull those three and you're in a plain black dress you can kneel in, in the time it takes to say you're going to." He looked up at her. "The collar stays on. The rest of you is mine for one day."

Imani looked at it for a long time.

"Three hooks," she said.

"Three."

"Show me where. On the day. Before I go down the stairs."

"I'll show you now," said Abloh, "and then on the day, and you'll still pull the wrong one," and Imani, who had expected to be angry for the rest of the morning, found that she wasn't.

Ferra had been waiting. She said, "I want a pocket."

Abloh turned round.

"In the skirt," said Ferra. "On the right. For the book."

"In that."

"Yes."

"Over my dead..."

"Then that's settled," said Ferra, and wrote it down.

Abloh looked at the ceiling of his own loft for some time. He drew the pocket in, small, on the right, hidden in a fold of the panels, with a line under it so heavy the charcoal broke.

"And on top," he said, when he'd found another stick.

"On top of what?"

"Your heads." He tapped the faint line of the veil where it came from the crown of the drawn head. "That's a lot of thin silk coming off nothing. What's holding it down?"

"Pins," said Imani.

"You're marrying a king." Abloh put the charcoal down. "You go into that chapel the Chancellor and the head of the ward, and you come out of it queens. I'm not sending a queen up the lane under a veil held on with pins." He drew it, a few quick strokes over each head: a crown, gold, rising in points, with something clear caught in the points. "That's not my work. That's a smith's."

Ferra looked at the drawing. Then she turned a page in her book and wrote, under a heading Imani couldn't see from where she stood, two words.

"He's doing the rings," said Imani.

"He doesn't know that yet either," said Ferra.

Abloh had rolled the drawings and was tying them with Emeric's wire. "When?" he said.

"October."

"Which day?"

"It isn't decided."

"Has the priest been asked?"

Ferra closed her book.

"No," she said.

Abloh looked at her over the roll.

"You've come to me before the priest," he said.

"You take longer," said Ferra, and took a folded sheet out of the back of the book and put it on the table. It was a list in her hand: Ward, Bram, Rylan, Declan, Osric, Arthur, Bren, and on down. "Grey. Like the crowning. Fourteen."

Abloh read it. He read it twice.

"Three of those," he said, "and a coat, and fourteen suits. In seven weeks. With harvest."

"Your girls..."

"My girls can do eleven." He put the list under the roll of drawings. "I'll do the other three. And the rest." He went to the top of the stair and shouted down it, and two of his girls came up, wiping their hands, and he said to them, in the voice he used on the sail loft floor: "Nobody goes home till I say. From today. And nobody tells him a thing."

"Not even the colour?" said the younger one.

"He knows the colour," said Abloh. "He's not having anything else."



Doran buried Edwy Brack from the old chapel on the ninth, with the lightermen of the river standing in the lane because there was no room inside, and afterwards walked out of the north gate and down the new road to the salt meadows, where the ribs stood chest high out of the grass in a ring and the printer's gantry stood over them like a heron. He stood among them for a long time, looking at the water Edwy had drowned in, a mile up the river.

"It wants a name," he said. "Ferra says the book wants a name to head it."

"Saints' names," said Emeric. "That's what's done."

"It's what's done in Ostrel." Doran put his hat on. "I'll call it for him. Saint Edwy's. He was drowned bringing stone for the town's wall, and he never had his name written down till Imani asked his brother how to spell it. Let it be written up somewhere it'll stay." He looked at Emeric. "Ostrel will say he's not a saint."

"Will you argue?"

"No," said Doran. "I'll let them try and prove it."



The piano spoke for the first time on the sixteenth, in the Motion Shop, at about ten at night.

It had begun as a question Fisk asked on the second day: how did Emeric mean to make a keyboard that knew how hard it was struck, when an organ's key was only ever open or shut? Emeric had drawn him the answer on the back of an organ sheet, and the answer had become a thing in its own right, the way answers did in Kaldo, because Bram had seen the drawing on Tess's bench. A frame of cast iron, to hold the pull of two hundred strings without bowing, which no wooden frame could. Bram's steel wire, drawn through his finest plates, the same wire that had carried the town's first current; three strings to a note at the top and one thick wound one at the bottom. A soundboard of split spruce. And for each key a little hammer that flew up and struck its string and fell straight away from it, so the string could ring: the action, Ottoline's, two hundred and some parts to every key, every one of them on a pin, and a felt on every hammer that Abloh had made in three thicknesses and told her to try.

Fisk sat down at it with the whole shop watching. Tess. Dov, with grease to the elbow. Ottoline with her loupe still in her eye. Bram in the doorway. Emeric, who had drawn every part of it and could not play a note on it.

Fisk played one key. Softly. The note came up out of the black box and hung in the shop under the lamps, and went on hanging, and faded, and was still there.

He played it again, hard. Same note. Louder.

He sat with his hands in his lap for a moment. Then he played a chord, low, quiet, and let it go, and played the same chord loud enough to rattle the tins on Dov's shelf, and then, without looking up from the keys, one of his own hymns, slowly, the one from the old chapel, so that every person in the Motion Shop heard what it had been trying to be on the wheezing cupboard all those Sundays.

Nobody said anything when he stopped. Ottoline took her loupe out.

"What's it called?" said Fisk, to the keys.

"A piano," said Emeric.

"Why?"

"Because it plays soft. And loud. And everything between, depending how you touch it."

"Then it's misnamed by half," said Fisk, "but I'll let it stand." He put his hands back on the keys. "I want one in the church by the gallery and one in the school, and the girl from the fountain step is to have lessons on it whether she likes it or not, and if anybody in this shop has been told what the third tune is for, they're to keep it behind their teeth until October or answer to me."



The auditions were in the school hall on the eighteenth and nineteenth, with the doors shut and Fisk at a table on the platform with a lamp, a pencil and the expression of a man about to be disappointed by every person in Kaldo in turn.

They came anyway. They came because Ferra had put a line in the Kaldo News that the cardinal's church wanted players for its opening, paid at the works rate, and the works rate was more than most of them earned from their trades. Fiddlers from every ward, forty of them, who had played at weddings and wakes and harvest suppers all their lives and never once in a room where anybody could hear what the others were doing. The four men from the choir school, who could read music and had never held an instrument made after their grandfathers' time. Two old men from the capital's court band who had come south with Fisk and whom he refused to look at. A brewery's drum-and-sackbut band that played for the dray horses' fair. Noll Finch, with the pipe he'd carried since he was twelve, who stood on the platform and played Fisk a tune from the Hod's cabin with his eyes shut, and was told to come back on Monday and learn the flute.

And the instruments, which were the other half of it, and which had been coming out of Kaldo's shops for a fortnight. The fiddle maker on the river road, who had made fiddles for thirty years and been asked to make them bigger: a fiddle you held under the chin but deeper, the viola; one the size of a child that you sat behind and played with a bow across your knees, the cello; and one taller than a man, the double bass, which he had built twice because the first one's neck pulled straight off its body under the strings in the night with a noise that brought the watch. The brass works, which had made pipe fittings and boiler gauges for eight years and now made trumpets with three valves that let a player change the length of the tube under his fingers, and horns coiled round on themselves like a sleeping snake, so a man could play every note there was instead of the handful his lips could find on a straight tube. Ottoline, again, who had looked at the shawm the court band's old man played and the flute Noll played and said they were all holes and no sense, and fitted them with keys on pins and springs so a hand could close a hole it couldn't reach: the oboe, the flute, and a new thing with a single reed that Emeric drew for her and called a clarinet. Kettledrums from the copper works, two pairs, tuned with screws round the rim.

Fisk heard every one of them. He took fifty-eight. He sent home a fiddler who had played for the old baron's wedding and would not be told, and took a girl of fourteen from the school who had never played anything but whose ear he tested at the piano and said was the best in the room. Jenet Strand he put at the front of the fiddles on the first morning, and told her on the second she'd be leading them, and on the third that she'd be leading them into the ground if she didn't stop rushing.

"What are we for?" one of the cellists asked him, on the nineteenth, at the end, when the doors had been opened again and the hall was full of people putting things in cases. "The opening of the church? Is that all?"

"That's all you're told," said Fisk, and went out.



His mother asked for Sabina on an evening in the middle of the month, at the end of a long talk about nothing much, by the tree.

His father had gone home. Ferra and Imani were across the dome at the east bed, where two of Amara's stones were in the ground, and Ferra was writing the bed's readings into her book, as she did every evening. Emeric had been telling his mother about the coffee. She'd laughed at the coffee. Her laugh in his room was exactly the laugh that had come back to him on the dome floor in February, the first thing through the door when the wall went, before her face and before the road.

Then she said, "Bring me Sabina."

He didn't answer at once.

"She's here. In Kaldo. Harry says she walks every morning to the market and back and buys nothing." His mother's voice in his room was quite level. "I'd like to see her."

"She didn't like you."

"No. Nor I her." Amara sounded amused. "That's no reason. Ask her. Don't tell her. She'll come if she's asked properly; she was always correct about being asked."

So he asked her properly.

He went in the morning, alone, and was shown in by Mabry, who had been Sabina's woman for thirty years and looked at him as if he were something the cat had left on a good rug. Sabina received him standing. She was in grey, as she had been every time he'd seen her since the war, with her hair dressed as if for court and the plain band of the anklet out of sight under her skirt. He told her his mother had asked to see her. He said it once and didn't add anything.

Sabina looked at him for a long time.

"Asked," she said.

"Asked."

"Not sent for."

"She said you'd want to know the difference."

Something went across Sabina's face and away again before he could read it. She said she would come at four, if four was convenient, and he said it was, and Mabry showed him out.

She came at four exactly. Mabry stopped on the gravel outside the dome's door as if there were a line drawn there, and there was not, and Sabina went in alone.

She stood in the warm green air under the glass and looked first at the tree, all of it, from the roots to the crown, the long pale trunk and the hollow crystal hanging in it like fruit, and then down at the mattress at its foot and the woman lying on it under a light blanket with her hands folded, breathing.

She looked at that for longer.

"She's thinner," she said.

Emeric held out his hand.

"Skin, I'm afraid," he said. "It doesn't go through a glove."

Sabina took her right glove off, a finger at a time, and folded it over the other in her left hand, and gave him her hand as if she were being handed down from a carriage. It was cold and quite dry. He took her down, the way he had taken his father in the spring, not into a room of her own, which she hadn't got, but into his, and held the door of it open behind them and let the dome's mana in, and let his mother in.

He felt Sabina see her. He felt her stop.

Then he did what Amara had asked him to do, at the end of that talk, when he'd said yes. He went and stood at the door of his own room with his back to the two of them, and put his attention outside it, on the gravel, on Mabry's feet shifting, on the hum of the dome's feed and the tick of the gauge by the reservoir valve and Ferra's pen at the east bed. It was harder than listening would have been. Attention was the thing a room was made of, and he had to keep it on the door and the gravel and not on the two women behind him, and not even on whether they were talking, for the better part of forty minutes.

He heard nothing. He made very sure he heard nothing.

When Sabina's hand moved in his he brought her up.

She took her hand back and put her glove on, a finger at a time, and smoothed the back of it. Her face was entirely composed. Her eyes were not wet. She looked once more at the woman on the mattress, and then at him.

He nearly asked. He got as far as opening his mouth.

"No," said Sabina. She settled the glove at the wrist. "That was ours."

She went out. Mabry fell in at her shoulder on the gravel without a word, and they went away together over the north bridge and along the moat under the willows, two women in grey at the pace of a procession, and did not look back.

His father had spent the afternoon at the knights' base not thinking about it. He'd told Ward so, Ward said later, about every quarter of an hour.



He had asked two days after his mother went. She had told him nothing about it. He had expected her to tell him nothing, and she had told him nothing so completely that he understood she'd been asked to and had agreed, which was more than he'd ever known her agree to.

So Corbin had gone to the keep's coal door at seven, when the waggoner came, and said to the waggoner's broad back, in the hearing of the yard, that he would like to speak to the duke, if the duke would see him. The waggoner had gone on shovelling. By noon Emeric had sent a note, in Ferra's hand, which said the twentieth, four o'clock, the dome. Nothing else.

The other times he'd gone into a delve, he hadn't asked.

He walked to the dome at a quarter to four in his brown coat with the anklet under his hose. It was warm against the bone from the walk. The waggoner was somewhere behind him with his cart, at the distance of a man who happened to be going the same way. Corbin had begun, he thought, to find the waggoner restful, which was humiliating and also true.

The dome was hot. Nobody had told him that. The air under the glass was thick and green and it pressed on him, on the skin and behind the eyes, the way a summer afternoon presses before thunder. His brother was waiting by the tree.

His half-brother. He still corrected it in his head. He was not sure any more who the correction was for.

"You'll feel the mana," said Emeric. "It's dense in here. It won't hurt you."

"It's hurting me now."

"It's pressing. It won't hurt you." Emeric looked at him. "You can still say no. Nobody will ask you why."

Corbin looked past him at the mattress under the tree, and the woman on it.

He looked at her. She was smaller than he remembered, which was only that he was bigger, and her hair had been brushed and plaited by somebody who did it every day, and her hands were folded on the blanket.

He gave Emeric his hand.

The going down was not like the other times. He'd been ready for the other times. The other times there had been a pull at the back of the head and a long falling, and a sweetness at the bottom of it like incense, and then a room. This was a hand holding his, warm, and a step down, as if into a cellar in the dark with someone in front of you who knew where the stairs were. And then a room.

It wasn't much of a room. It had a door, which stood open behind them, and walls he couldn't quite look at. There was nothing in it to see.

Except her.

He checked the corners. It was a habit now, the one useful thing he'd learned in the yard, on his knees, with his forearm open. The rooms Augustine had shown him had all been wrong in the same way, once he knew to look. Nobody behind the desk. Everything seen from a doorway, at a grown man's height, by a man who'd never been in the room. He looked for the empty place behind the desk. There was no desk. He looked for the doorway.

She was watching him do it.

"You've got tall," said Amara.

He'd thought about what he would say for two days and a night. All of it went.

"I was tall," he said. "When you knew me."

"You were thin," said Amara, "and you were on the stairs."

And she gave it to him.

It wasn't a Seeing. He knew within a breath it wasn't. It was too small and too plain and nobody had built it to frighten him or to please him, and it was seen from the wrong place to be a lie. It was seen from a chair. From a desk under a window, with a page of something half written on it and the pen laid down across the page, at the height of a woman sitting, through a door that stood open onto a stone landing and the top of a stair.

On the third step down, with his back to the wall and a book on his knees that he wasn't reading, there was a boy.

He was very thin. His wrists came out of his sleeves. He was nine, perhaps, or ten, and he was sitting very still in the way of a child who has been told to sit still a great deal and has found a place where nobody will tell him, and he was listening. He wasn't looking in. He was looking at the book. But his head was turned a little, the way you'd turn your good ear to a sound, toward the open door and the scratch of the pen.

The boy was exactly as small as Corbin had been. The doorway was exactly the width it had been. Nobody had made it larger to make him look smaller.

"You came most afternoons for a year," said Amara. "When your tutor let you out early. You sat there and never came in."

"You never asked me in."

"No. I left the door open." She said it without any reproach at all. "Every afternoon. I thought, if I ask him, he'll go. Children do. I thought, if I leave it open, one day he'll come in by himself and pretend he wanted a book." The landing went, and the room was only the room again. "You never did."

He could not, for some time, say anything.

"Your mother and I never liked each other," said Amara. "That was ours. It was never yours to carry."

He had come with something to say and he said it now, before she could, because if she said it first he would not be able to bear it.

"The chapel," he said. "The note."

She waited.

"I wrote it on a slip and gave it to him at the rail. The road. The road you'd take to the hill house, and the day. I'd heard Father say it at table." His voice was doing something he couldn't stop. "I asked him to pray for the boy on the journey. For my brother. I wanted to be the one who'd asked. I thought..." He didn't know what he had thought. He had been thirteen. He had thought it would be a thing he had done, that was good, that someone would know about. "He asked me which road. Very kindly. So that he could pray for the right one."

"I know," said Amara.

He'd known she would. He hadn't known what it would do to hear it.

"Harry told me in the spring. Emeric told him." She came a little nearer, or the room brought her nearer; he couldn't tell which, and it didn't matter. "You were thirteen and you asked a priest to pray for your brother. I'd have told him the road myself, if he'd asked me nicely." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "He'd have asked me nicely."

Corbin stood in his brother's room in the dark, with the dome's heat coming through the door behind him, and his face was wet, and he did not know when that had started, and he could not have stopped it if he had been offered his whole life back to do it.

She didn't touch him. She couldn't. She stayed where she was, near enough, and let him.

After a while Emeric's hand moved round his, the smallest pressure, a question, and Corbin pressed back, and was brought up.

The dome was very bright. The air was green and thick and hot and full of the hum of something he didn't understand, and his knees were on the boards at the foot of the mattress, though he did not remember kneeling. The woman under the blanket was exactly as she had been, breathing, with her hands folded. He looked at her for a while. Then he got up, and nodded to his brother, once, which was as much as he had, and Emeric nodded back and let him go without saying anything, which was more than he'd have managed in Emeric's place.

He went out onto the gravel.

It was half past five. The sun was on the moat and the willows, and the masons on the east bank had the new coping half laid, and the stones that had gone into the water on the sixth sat in a row along the top of the wall with the mud washed off them, as if nothing had happened to them at all. The waggoner had left his cart at the end of the north bridge and was sitting on the tail of it, eating an apple.

Corbin walked as far as the cart and stopped.

The waggoner looked at him, and at his face, and went on eating the apple. When he'd finished it he reached behind him into the cart and found another, and wiped it on his smock, and held it out.

Corbin took it.

He didn't eat it. He stood at the end of the bridge with the apple in his hand and the anklet warm on his bone, and looked back at the dome, and said nothing at all, and the waggoner said nothing back, and after a while the waggoner got down and picked up the shafts and they went up the hill together, at the pace of a man who happened to be going the same way.



Emeric sat on the tram bench at the top of Gate Avenue for a quarter of an hour, because his legs told him to.

Holding the room open cost him. It always had. Twice in a week, with forty minutes at the door the first time and nearly an hour the second, had taken him down nearer empty than he liked to be in the middle of the town, and he'd walked up the hill with the slight ringing in the ears that meant he'd spent more than he'd noticed. He sat and let it come back.

It came back fast. In the woods past Milford, after a day's casting, it took him most of an afternoon to fill. Here it took the time a tram took to come up the avenue and go down again. He sat through a second tram to be sure, and it was the same, thin and from everywhere, as if the street were leaning on him. He put it down to Kaldo, fourteen years of stones in the walls and the water and the people, and that was certainly part of it. He took out his notebook and wrote refill, town v. woods, much faster in town. Why so much? and underlined why, and got up.

In the morning he went out into the inner ward before anyone was up, with a hazelnut in his pocket, and stood under the old pear, and called.

September came down the pear, once, and took the hazelnut off his palm with great dignity, and went back up, and did not come down again all week.
