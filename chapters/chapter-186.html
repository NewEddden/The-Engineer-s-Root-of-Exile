Chapter 186: The Visitation

Father Benedek Orme came to Kaldo for a single letter of the alphabet, and meant to be gone in two days.

He was the Visitor of the southern parishes, which meant that he rode a circuit of thirty-four churches between the Wayford road and the sea, and slept in thirty-four presbyteries, and read thirty-four parish books every two years, and was answered to by thirty-four priests who were glad to see him and gladder to see him go. He was sixty-one. He had ridden the circuit for nineteen years on a succession of patient brown mules, with a clerk and a second mule for the books, and he knew every ford and every inn and every priest's housekeeper's cooking between Milford and the marsh. Kaldo was the last church on the circuit and the one he visited least, because it was a long way down a bad road, and because in nineteen years Father Doran had never given him the smallest cause for concern.

He had come this time because the cardinal asked.

The letter had reached him in the summer at Grayrock, in Augustine's own hand, which Benedek had seen perhaps four times in his life. It was short and friendly and asked for nothing that a Visitor would not do anyway. When you are next in Kaldo, it said, I should be glad if you would look at their printing. I have a soldiers' prayer from there with a small letter in it where a large one belongs, and I should like to know how a small letter gets into a prayer in a parish that reads its proofs. It was not an order. The cardinal did not give orders to Visitors about letters of the alphabet. It was a question from a clever old man who liked to know how things happened, and Benedek, who liked the same thing, had moved Kaldo up the circuit by a month to answer it.

He rode in at the Western Gate on a cold bright afternoon at the end of the autumn, with Brother Aske behind him on the book mule, and Father Doran was waiting under the gate arch in his winter cloak with his hands in his sleeves.

"Father Visitor."

"Father Doran. You're looking well."

"I'm past sixty," Doran said. "So are you. We're both looking as well as we're going to."

The printing took a morning.

Doran had the proof book ready. It was a stout quarto bound in plain calf, and it held every sheet of anything touching scripture that had been printed in Kaldo in three years, the proof and the finished sheet side by side, with Doran's initial on every proof and the date. Benedek went through it at the presbytery table with his spectacles on while Aske made a fair copy of the rule in the finishing-room book: Nothing of scripture to be printed in Kaldo, on any press, without the proof first read by the parish. Under it someone had written at no cost.

The soldiers' prayer was there. The proof was clean. Keep us, Lord, in the hour of the fight. Doran's initial beside it. And on the facing leaf, the printed sheet as it had been issued, and the small l.

"I didn't see it until the muster," Doran said. "Two hundred of them had gone out by then."

Benedek walked down to the type foundry after dinner and spoke to the punch-cutter, a spare grey man called Nevin who had cut every letter in the case himself and could tell by touch which box any piece of type had come from. The compositor's boy was sent for. He was eighteen now, and had been sixteen when he did it, and he told the whole of it without being asked twice: the forme lifted after the proof, the fourth line dropped on the floor and pied, the rush to reset it before the muster, the capital taken from the lowercase box because the two boxes stood side by side and he was frightened and his hands were shaking. He had been made to stand at the foundry door for a week with the sheet pinned to his coat. He said so without resentment. He had deserved it.

It was an error. It was the kind of error that is always an error. Benedek had seen a hundred of them in a hundred parish books, where a clerk had written a child's name wrong in the baptisms or a date in the burials, and he had never once thought any of them meant anything except that the clerk was tired.

He could write it in two lines. The error was the compositor's, after the proof. The parish's rule is sound and is kept. He could ride back up the Wayford road in the morning.

He walked back up the market street to the presbytery at dusk, in no hurry, looking about him, because he had not been to Kaldo in two years and a great deal had changed.

The street was lit. That was the first thing. There were iron posts along it with lamps on top that burned white and still, whiter than any oil, and made the setts shine, and the people in the street went up and down under them as though it were a summer evening. He stopped under one and looked up at it. It hissed, very faintly.

A girl of about seven was standing under the next post with her head back, looking at it too. When she saw him looking she came over, as children do to priests, and took his hand without asking, and said, "Father, are you lost? Father Doran's is that way," and pointed.

He was not lost. He let her lead him anyway.

He felt it in the first three steps.

It was not a thing he had felt in thirty years, and not a thing he had ever expected to feel again. When he was young, before the circuit, he had served nine years in the chamber under the cathedral as one of the rite's priests. He had been one of the eight: the eight priests at the eight places round the frame, who keep the rite through the fourteen hours with a hand on the young man, and who feel, through that hand, the young man fill. You learned it in the first year. You learned to feel a man's capacity through his skin the way a miller feels the fineness of flour between finger and thumb: the thin dry lightness of an ordinary man going in at dusk, and then, by the tenth hour and the twelfth and the fourteenth, the weight coming up in him, the density, until by dawn the wrist under your fingers felt like something poured full and still pouring. After nine years you could feel it without touching, if you stood near enough. Standing in the cathedral nave on the morning of the knighting, with the new knights filing past, it was like standing near a row of hearths.

He had not felt it since, except in knights and in the Circle's masters. Knights he felt all the time. You could not stop, once you had learned. He felt it in every knight who held a door for him and every knight at every lord's table on the circuit, and he had never once felt it in anybody who was not a knight or a mage.

The child's hand in his was dense.

Not like a knight's. Not poured full. But dense, with a weight in it that a child's hand does not have, a small steady fullness under the skin, like a cup of water where you would expect a cup of air. He stopped walking. The child tugged. He let her tug him on, and looked down at the top of her head, and did not say anything.

At the presbytery door she let go and ran off. A baker was putting up his shutters two doors down, and called goodnight to the priests, and Benedek went over to him on some excuse about bread for the morning and paid for a loaf he did not need, and let his fingers touch the man's as he took it. Dense. A carter came up the street with an empty cart and stopped to ask Doran about a christening, and Benedek stood near him, a foot away, as he had stood near the new knights in the nave, and felt the warmth come off him like a hearth.

A baker. A carter. A child. He stood in the lit street with the loaf in his hands and a feeling in his stomach he had not had since the chamber, which was the feeling of being in the presence of something that was not supposed to happen.

He did not ride home in the morning.

"I'll stay for the visitation proper, if you'll have me," he told Doran at breakfast. "It's been two years. I may as well do it while I'm here."

Doran was pleased. Doran had never had a bad visitation in his life and was proud of his parish, and said so, and took him round it.

Benedek spent a fortnight in Kaldo. He looked in all the places a sensible man would look.

He looked at the works, where the prince's smith, a big quiet man called Bram with burn scars on both forearms, showed him the converter and the rail mill and the forge with a patient courtesy that answered every question and volunteered nothing. There was a great deal of fire and noise and stink and iron. There was nothing in any of it Benedek could see that was against the law of the realm or the teaching of the Church. Iron is not a sin.

He looked at the school, where a young teacher called Orrin taught a hundred children their letters from printed primers in a long whitewashed room with clear glass in its windows. The last page of the primer carried a psalm, the psalm of the lamp in the window, and the proof of it was in Doran's book with Doran's initial. The children read it aloud for him. He walked up and down between the benches while they read, and passed close to each of them, and felt them. Not all of them. Most. The ones from the island.

He looked at the mage. There was an old man called Armand, who had been a great master in the Circle once and had come south with a cart full of his students to build the prince a wall, and who received the Visitor in a study full of printed books with the politeness of a man who has received a great many visitors in his life and expected most of them to be tiresome. His hands shook when he poured the wine. He showed Benedek the founding stone of what he called his school, and the printed books of hand-forms that he taught from, and talked for an hour about the Circle's history with an erudition that Benedek found charming and a care, round certain subjects, that he found instructive. He did not mention the chamber once. Benedek did not mention it either. When they shook hands at the door, the old man's hand was dry and light and trembling, and had in it a density that Benedek, who had felt the hands of the Circle's masters, would have called a master's, and something else under it he could not name.

He looked at the new city. Doran took him out on a borrowed horse to see it, down the road past the lower water to the flat where the wall now stood all round, closed, grey and new, with its gates unhung. Inside it there was a great house on a hill with the drum of a dome on it and scaffold round the drum, and rings of water round the hill, and one quarter of streets and houses and three quarters of nothing but pegs in the grass. Doran showed him the church's plot, with its footings in. It was on the second ring, on the north side.

"Not in the middle," Benedek said.

"No," Doran said. "The offices are in the middle. The records. The courts." He looked at the dome drum for a while. "I asked, the day they cut the datum. Mildly. I was shown the plan. It was on a ring street." He turned his horse. "I said thank you."

He found nothing. That was the truth of the fortnight, and he wrote it down each night in his own book because he was an honest man. The works were lawful. The school was lawful and taught its children well. The mage was a licensed master of the Circle doing lawful work for a lawful lord. The printing was sound, the rule was kept, the error was an error. The prince, whom he met once, for a quarter of an hour, on the steps of the keep, was courteous and a little absent and looked at him with a directness Benedek did not expect and did not enjoy, and said nothing whatever that a Visitor could have written down.

Every morning of that fortnight he walked down to the market square before breakfast and drank a cup of water from the brass tap at the foot of the tall timber tower there, because Kaldo's water was famous up and down the circuit and he had never tasted better. It was cold and clean and a little sweet. There was a well standard for it, printed and pinned to the tower's door, the whole of it in plain words, how deep and how far from any midden and how often to be cleaned, and he had copied it for his other parishes years ago. It was the one thing in Kaldo he approved of without any reservation at all.

He never once looked at the tower itself. It was a water tower. Nobody in the world looks at a water tower.

On the tenth day Doran gave him the parish book.

That was the heart of every visitation, and Doran brought it out in the evening at the presbytery table with the lamp trimmed high, and set it down in front of him with a small proud gesture, the way a man sets down a thing he has made with care. It was the parish book of the church of Kaldo on the island. Doran had begun it the year the prince came, the old book being full, and had kept it the same way since. Baptisms at the front. Marriages in the middle. Burials at the back. Every entry with the name and the date and, in the burials, the age at death and a word or two for the cause if the cause was known.

Benedek read the burials.

He read them first as a Visitor reads them, looking for irregularity: a burial with no name, a suicide in consecrated ground, a stranger buried without inquiry. There was nothing of the kind. Doran's book was as clean as his proofs.

Then he read them again, as a man reads them who has read thirty-four parish books every two years for nineteen years and knows without counting what a year's burials look like in a parish of two thousand souls.

In a parish of two thousand, in the south, in a year without plague or famine, you buried sixty. Sixty-five. One soul in thirty or so. A third of them were children under five, and most of the rest were old, and a handful were young men who had fallen off something or drowned in something or been kicked by something, and a handful were young women who had died in childbed. That was the shape of it. It had been the shape of it in every parish book Benedek had ever read. It was the shape of the world.

Kaldo's first year in Doran's book had that shape. Sixty-eight burials. Twenty-four children under five.

The second year had forty-nine.

Benedek looked at that for a while. Then he turned back to the margin where Doran had written, in small letters, the year's events as a priest notes them for his own memory, and found it: The prince's map of the wells. The midden by the market well filled. The new well at the gate. He nodded. He knew that. He had copied the well standard himself. Clean water and a filled midden would do it. He had seen the same thing, on a smaller scale, in two other parishes that had taken up the standard. Fewer children dead of the flux in the summer.

After that the parish grew, and grew fast. Doran noted it in the margins, year by year: the works, the mill, the families coming for work, the debtors off the south road. The burials grew with it, more slowly. By the year before the second step the island parish held four thousand and some, and Doran had buried ninety-two. One soul in forty-five. That was a good figure. It was better than any other parish on the circuit, and Benedek could account for every point of it: the water, the drains, the wages, the doctor's ward. He had half expected it.

Then, three winters ago, it fell again.

Sixty-one, that winter's year. Fifty-two the next. Forty-seven the last, with the parish grown to four and a half thousand. Benedek had the census printed by the barony's census office, which Doran kept with the book, and he set the two side by side and did the sum with a pencil in the margin of his own notebook, and then did it again.

One soul in ninety-five.

He had never seen a parish book with a figure like that in it. He did not believe there was one anywhere in the realm.

And it was the children. Nineteen children under five, in the three years since. The book's first year alone had buried twenty-four, out of half as many souls. Benedek counted them twice. In the same three years he had buried very few of the old. He went down the age column. People were dying at seventy-four, at seventy-nine, at eighty-one. A woman at eighty-six. He had walked past a cooper's shop by the water steps that morning and seen a small bent man of past seventy hooping a barrel with a mallet, steadily, as a man of forty would, and thought nothing of it.

He sat back.

There was no margin note three winters ago. Nothing about a new well. Nothing about a midden. Doran had written, for that winter: The snow. The market tower built. And nothing else.

He went on down the burials, slowly now, because he did not know what he was looking for. Near the end of the year before, among the ordinary names, he found an entry where Doran's pen had stopped. You could see it. There was a small round blot where the nib had rested on the page too long before it wrote.

Wat, of Wayford. Out of the cellars of the Staple, in the lord's ward. Buried by the lake, having no family here. And then, after the blot, in the age column: Thirty-one.

"You stopped here," Benedek said.

Doran leaned across and looked. His face changed very slightly.

"I did," he said. "I've never been told a thing I believed less. And I'd less reason to doubt it than anything I was ever told." He sat back. "Mistress Imani had it from the Staple's own book, in the dead woman's hand. He looked eighty. He was thirty-one."

"What was done to him?"

"I don't rightly know," Doran said. "Nobody's ever said it to me plain. Something in those cellars. Something the lords did. They hanged them for it." He looked at the page. "The ward will have more. Mistress Imani keeps a register. Every soul in and out, and what was wrong with them, and what was done. She'll give it you. I'll ask her."

He asked her. The ward's register came up to the presbytery the next morning in the arms of a young woman called Tamsin, one of Imani's students, who set it on the table with a note in Imani's square hand that said only For the Visitor. Return it by the end of the week. I. It was a great heavy book, three years of it in one binding, and Benedek went through it in two days with Aske at his elbow copying what he told him to copy.

He found Wat in it. Weight on the grain beam, week by week, going down and then stopping and staying. Pulse against the sand glass. Gums. Nails. The walk to the door. Looks seventy, by any measure I have. And at the end, a line: Died in the eighth week. Cause not known.

He found, in the ward's admissions and deaths, the same shape as in Doran's book, from the other side: fewer children coming in with the summer flux, and those that came in going out again; old people coming in with the ordinary things old people have and going home.

And at the very back of the register, on the last leaf, after three years of admissions and deaths and cures, in the same square hand but smaller, as though written quickly and not meant to be looked for, he found a single entry that had nothing to do with anything else in the book.

Second week of autumn. Sir Ward, of the garrison, came to me unasked and said, in his own words: I felt it in my teeth. The night the prince went down into the cellar at the Staple. The men in the hall felt it too, and thought it was the building settling. I have written it as he said it.

Benedek read it three times. He did not know what it meant. He knew that it meant something, because nobody writes a thing like that at the back of a register without a reason, and he knew that it was not his to understand. He had Aske copy it exactly, with the date, and the words at the back, in the same hand, and went on.

He wrote the report on the last two nights, in his room at the presbytery, by one candle, with the door shut.

He wrote it carefully. That was what he was for. He did not write a single word that was not true, and he did not write a single word that went further than what he had seen. He was a Visitor. He was not a theologian and not a mage and not a judge, and he knew exactly how much weight each sentence he wrote would bear, and he did not put a feather more on any of them.

He wrote that the error in the soldiers' prayer was the compositor's, after the proof, and that the parish's rule was sound and was kept.

He wrote that the parish was well served by its priest, and that the parish book was the best-kept book on his circuit.

He wrote the burials. The figures year by year, the two steps down, the census beside them, the sum. He wrote that the first step followed the prince's map of the wells and was accounted for by clean water, as in other parishes. He wrote that the second step, three winters ago, was accounted for by nothing he could find.

He wrote what he had felt. He wrote it plainly, as a former priest of the chamber writing to the cardinal who kept the chamber, in the words the chamber's priests used among themselves: that in the hands of a child in the market street, and a baker, and a carter, and the children of the school, and a great many others whom he had passed or touched in a fortnight, he had felt a weight he had felt before only in knights. Not so great. But of the same kind. He wrote that he had felt it in the old and in the young, in those who worked at the barony's works and those who did not, in the island people and in the people of the new city who had come from the island, and less, or not at all, in those who had come to Kaldo lately from elsewhere.

He wrote the entry for Wat, and the doctor's line beside it. He wrote Sir Ward's words from the back of the register, exactly, with the date, and wrote that he did not know what they referred to.

Then he sat with the pen in his hand for a long time.

The Church held that a man's capacity was given, not made. It was given once, at the chamber, through the rite, over fourteen hours, by the grace that the rite called down and the eight priests at the eight places kept. It was violent. Everyone who had served in the chamber knew that it was violent. The young men screamed, some of them. They came out marked, all of them, the tracery up their arms like frost on a window, some to the elbow and the ones who had fought it to the waist, and it stayed. Some of them, a few in every year, did not come out at all. It was violent because it was holy, and a holy thing is not made gentle for our convenience.

If a town's ordinary life, its daily bread, whatever it was, did quietly across ten years what the chamber did in one night with eight men and fourteen hours, to children and bakers and carters, without a mark on any of them.

Then the rite was a method. And a bad one.

He did not write that. He was a Visitor, and it was not a thing a Visitor could write, and it went further than anything he had seen. He wrote only the last sentence of his report, which was true and went no further than the burials:

I have not found the cause. I do not think it is to be found in the works, the school, the mage or the printing, all of which I examined and all of which are lawful. I think it is to be found in something the people of this parish have in common that the people of my other parishes do not. I do not know what that is. I think Your Eminence will wish to know.

He signed it and sealed it and addressed it to Cardinal Augustine at the cathedral close, in his own hand.

He left on a grey cold morning with frost on the setts, and Doran walked him down to the Western Gate with his hands in his sleeves.

The crown post rider was at the gate with his bag, as Benedek had arranged, because a Visitor's report to a cardinal did not go by the carrier. Benedek gave him the letter himself. The rider put it in the bag with the others, the ordinary post of the barony going north, and buckled the bag, and swung up.

Benedek went down the gate steps to his mule, and Aske handed him up.

He looked back from the saddle. Doran was standing under the gate arch in his winter cloak, a man past sixty who had kept a parish book since the year the prince came, more carefully than any book on the circuit, and had handed it over at the presbytery table with a small proud gesture, the way a man hands over a thing he has made well. He had no idea. Benedek could see it in his face. He had written down every soul in Kaldo, one after another, year on year, faithfully, with the age and the cause, and had never once added them up.

Doran raised his hand.

Benedek raised his, and turned the mule's head north, and rode out after the post.
