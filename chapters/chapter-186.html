Chapter 186 - Under Kaldo's Seal

Imani stitched Jabari's forearm on the keep kitchen table at seven in the morning, between the bread and the butter, because it was the warmest room in the keep and the only one with a table long enough for him.

He had not told anybody about the arm. He had climbed the ladder with it and sat on the deck with it and flown four hours home with his sleeve over it, and it was only in the kitchen, when Maren put a bowl of porridge in front of him and he reached for the spoon with his left hand instead of his right, that Imani, at the other end of the table, put her own spoon down and said, "Sleeve," in the voice she used in the ward; and he looked at her, and rolled it up. A cut the length of a finger along the outside of the forearm, from a short sword, clean, deep enough. It had stopped bleeding on its own somewhere over the sea.

"You'd have let that go bad," said Imani, threading the needle by the window, "rather than say so."

"It's not bad."

"It will be by Thursday if I don't close it." She came back to the table and sat down and took his arm and laid it on a clean cloth between the porridge and the loaf, the way she laid out every arm. "Don't look," she said. "Big men always faint when they look."

Jabari looked. He did not faint. He watched every stitch go in, eleven of them, with an expression of great interest, while Maren stood at the end of the table with her arms folded and her ladle in one hand and watched him watch, and said nothing, and then at the ninth stitch went to the fire and came back with a second bowl of porridge and put it beside the first.

"For after," she said. "You'll want it. They always do."

"Thank you," said Jabari, to Maren, gravely, with his arm on the table and the needle in it.

"Don't thank me. Eat it." Maren looked at him, a long look, up and down, the whole size of him in the kitchen chair that was too small. "You're the one who stood in a hedge all night for her, are you." It was not a question. She went back to the fire. "Well. You'll do."

Nobody in the town knew where the prince had been. That was the strange part, that morning, and the part Emeric thought about most, sitting at the kitchen table's far end with his own porridge in the steam and the light coming grey through the high windows. The town had gone to bed on the eighteenth with the prince in his keep, and the wall lamps lit, and the chancellor at her window in the counting room all night where the watch could see her. It had got up on the twentieth with the prince in his keep, and the wall lamps out for the morning, and two more people in the keep than there had been, which the kitchen girls had already told the dairy, who had told the north well. By noon the lower ward would know that a tall girl and a very big man had come up the keep stair at dawn. By evening it would know her name, because it remembered the green-and-silver carriage from February, and the four greys, and the steward on his tall bay with a face like a shut door.

It would not know how she had come. Nobody would ever tell it. And Emeric found, sitting there, that he minded that less than he had thought he would; that a town which had stopped asking how its prince did things, and simply waited to find out what he had done, was a town that trusted him more than it had any reason to, and that he would have to keep earning it for the rest of his life.



Isolde slept eleven hours in Ferra's spare room.

Ferra had a room beside her own, along the passage under her name, that had been a clerk's in the old baron's time, and had a bed in it and a chair and a window over the river and nothing else; and she had made the bed up herself on the evening of the eighteenth, before the ship went, with the good sheets, and left a jug of water on the chair and a towel over the back of it, and shut the door. Isolde went into it at dawn on the twentieth with her one small bag and shut the door behind her and did not come out until the bells for the evening service, and then only to ask, standing in the passage in her shift with a blanket round her and her hair everywhere, whether there was anything to eat, and whether it was true, and whether she had dreamed the sling.

"It's true," said Ferra, from her own doorway. "There's soup. You didn't dream the sling."

Isolde looked at her along the passage.

"I'm going to be married," she said. "Aren't I. Tomorrow."

"If you say yes to Father Doran," said Ferra. "In front of the town. Out loud." She folded her arms. "He'll ask you first, tonight, on your own. He wants to hear it with nobody in the room. He said so."

"Then I'll say it to him tonight on my own," said Isolde. "And again tomorrow, in front of the town. And again every morning after, if he likes, to anybody who asks." She pulled the blanket closer round her. "I've no dress."

"Abloh has a dress."

"Whose?"

"Mine," said Ferra. "The grey one. From the chancery." She did not unfold her arms. "You're taller than me. Abloh's letting down the hem. He's been at it since noon and he says it's a crime against good wool and he'll have it done by the morning." She looked at the other girl in the blanket in the passage. "It's the best thing I own. I've worn it once. I'd like it worn by somebody who chose."

Isolde did not say anything for a long breath. Then she came along the passage in her blanket and her bare feet, and put her arms round Ferra, who stood very straight in her doorway with her own arms still folded between them and let it happen, and did not unfold them, and did not step back either.

"You'll make me cry in your passage," said Isolde, into her shoulder.

"Then go and eat the soup," said Ferra, "and cry in the kitchen. Maren likes it."



Doran saw them on the evening of the twentieth, one at a time, in the vestry, with the door shut.

He had not been told everything, and he had not asked for everything. He had been told, on the morning of the eighteenth, in his own kitchen, by Emeric, that there would very likely be a lady in Kaldo by the twentieth who wished to marry, and a knight who wished to marry her, and that her father had not been asked and would not have agreed; and Doran had looked at him across his kitchen table for a long time over his cold tea, and then said that he would want to see them both alone, each of them, and that he would want to hear the word from each of them with nobody else in the room, and that if he did not hear it he would not do it, prince or no prince, and Emeric had said that was why he had come to Doran and not to anybody else.

"The Church asks two things," Doran said, in his kitchen, that morning. "That they both say yes. And that it's done where anybody can see." He turned his cup in its saucer. "It asks a father's blessing. It always has. It doesn't need it. It never has, though there are men in the capital who'd like it to." He looked up. "I'll not marry a girl to anybody she hasn't chosen. Not for her father and not for you."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Doran. "I'm saying it so it's said."

Jabari went in first. He was in the vestry for a quarter of an hour, and came out with his face doing nothing at all, and sat down on the bench in the church porch with his bandaged arm across his knees and looked at the floor.

Isolde went in second. She was in the vestry for more than an hour.

Emeric sat on the bench across the porch from Jabari and waited, and Jabari sat and waited, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing either of them could have said that would have been any use; and after the first half hour Jabari began to look up at the vestry door every time the old wood creaked in the cold, and after the first hour he stopped looking at the floor at all and simply looked at the door.

When it opened, Isolde came out first. Her eyes were red. She was smiling. She looked at Jabari on the bench, and he stood up, and she walked past him without stopping, and touched his good arm on her way past, once, with two fingers, and went out of the porch into the dark to walk back up to the keep.

Doran came out after her, slowly, putting on his coat.

"She asked me questions for an hour," he said, to Emeric. "About marriage. About what I'd seen of it, in thirty years of marrying people. Good and bad." He buttoned his coat. "Nobody's ever asked me that before. They ask me what to say and where to stand." He looked out of the porch after her. "I told her. All of it. The bad as well." He turned to Jabari. "She said yes at the end of it. She'd have said it at the start. I think she wanted to know she still would, after." He put his hat on. "Ten o'clock. The doors open. Don't be late, and don't be early, and don't faint. Big men always faint."



The town came.

Nobody had asked it to. There had been no banns; there was no time for banns, and Doran had said the Church's rule about banns was for strangers marrying strangers, and that he had known both of these people for a whole day, which was longer than he had known some couples he had married. There had been no notice on the church door. There had only been the kitchen girls, and the dairy, and the north well, and by the morning of the twenty-first every soul in the lower ward knew that the green-and-silver girl from February was to be married in Kaldo's church at ten o'clock to a knight nobody had ever seen, who had come up the keep stair at dawn with his arm cut.

So at half past nine the church was full, and the churchyard, and the lane down to the new terraces; and the church's west doors stood open on their hinges to the grey morning, as Doran had said they must, and the town stood in them and outside them and on the churchyard wall, and looked in.

Maren had baked. Of course Maren had baked. There were trestles along the churchyard's south wall under cloths, and the cloths were humped over things that smelled of honey and of the new white sugar from Milford, and two of the kitchen girls stood guard over them with wooden spoons and the expressions of soldiers.

Emeric stood at the side, by the pillar, where the holder of the barony stood at a wedding, with nobody near him. It was not his wedding. He had nothing to do but watch it.

Ward stood for Jabari. He had been asked on the K Discovery's deck, over the sea, in the dark; Jabari had asked him in a low voice, and Ward had looked at him for a long time and then said yes, and nothing else. He stood at Jabari's right hand now at the chancel step, in his best grey, with his hands behind his back, and Jabari stood beside him in a borrowed coat of Declan's that was too short in the sleeve, with his bandaged arm under it and his face grey and his eyes on the west doors.

And Ferra and Imani stood for Isolde.

They came in at the west doors with her, one on each side, at ten o'clock exactly, as the bell stopped. Ferra in her second-best, which was brown. Imani in a dark blue that Emeric had never seen her wear, with her hair not tied up for the ward but pinned, high, and a sprig of something green in it that one of the girls from the north well had given her at the door. And between them, in a grey dress of fine wool from the capital that had been let down four fingers at the hem overnight by a tailor who said it was a crime, with her hair up and her mother's ring on her hand and nothing else, Isolde Sallow, walking up the aisle of a church in a barony that had refused her, to marry a man who had stood in a hedge.

The town was very quiet.

Doran asked her in front of all of them. He asked her slowly, the old words, the ones every soul in that church had heard said at every wedding since they were children; and at the end of them, the question.

"Yes," said Isolde.

She said it loud. Loud enough for the churchyard. Loud enough, Emeric thought afterward, for the lane down to the terraces, and possibly for the north, if anybody in the north had been listening, and he thought that somebody in the north probably was.

Jabari said it after her. He did not say it loud. He said it in a low steady voice that carried anyway, the way Ward's did, and when he had said it he shut his eyes, and opened them, and did not faint.

Doran married them.

Then the lines. Ferra had them ready on the vestry table: three sheets of the mill's best, the sized paper, each with the same words on it in Hale's fair hand, the names, the date, the place, the priest, the witnesses; and the witnesses signed them, Ward and Ferra and Imani, and the holder of the barony signed them at the foot, and Ferra took the barony's seal out of its box and pressed it into the wax at the foot of each one, hard, three times, so that the barony's arms stood up off the paper clean and deep.

"One for the records hall," said Ferra, blotting. "One for the crown's register, by tomorrow's post." She held up the third. "One for the north."

"For my father," said Isolde.

"For anybody in the north who asks," said Ferra. "Your father first."

When they came out of the west doors into the churchyard, the town did not cheer. It did something Emeric had not heard it do before, at a wedding or anywhere: it was quiet, all of it, for a breath, as if the whole of the lower ward had drawn in its breath at once; and then a woman on the churchyard wall, a big woman from the dock quarter with a child on her hip, said loudly to nobody in particular that the girl had chosen, had they all heard, out loud, in front of everybody, the girl had chosen; and then it cheered.



Isolde found him by the trestles, afterward, with a honey ring in her hand and the grey dress's hem already dark with churchyard mud.

"So," she said. "I suppose this means you're finally rid of me."

"Tragic, really."

"Isn't it." She bit the ring. "I'd been looking forward to haunting your dinners for years." She chewed, and looked across the churchyard at Jabari, who was standing by the wall surrounded by the garrison, being slapped on the back by Osric and looking as if he would very much rather be standing in a hedge. "My aunt will say you did it to spite her."

"I know."

"She'll be half right. That's the worst kind of thing my aunt says." Isolde looked at him sideways. "My father will say you stole me."

"He'll have the lines by the end of the week. In Hale's hand. Under the seal." Emeric looked at the church. "And the crown's register will have them. And anybody who asks."

"He won't read them."

"He will," said Emeric. "I'm going to make sure he reads them."

Isolde stopped chewing. She looked at him for a while, in the grey light, with the town round them eating Maren's honey rings.

"What are you going to do?" she said.

"Nothing that'll hurt anybody in your father's house," said Emeric. "The chancellor's made sure of that. In writing." He took a honey ring off the trestle for himself. "Go and rescue your husband from Osric. He's telling him about the cows."

"What cows?"

"There are always cows," said Emeric, "when Osric tells a story."



Ferra was at the west doors, at the end of the afternoon, when the town had gone and the trestles had been carried in and the churchyard was grey and empty and full of crumbs.

She was standing in the doorway with her back against the old oak of the jamb, in her brown dress, with her arms folded, looking down the lane toward the terraces, where Isolde and Jabari were walking slowly up the hill toward the keep together, not touching, a little apart, the way two people walk who have every right in the world to touch and are not used to it yet.

He came and stood beside her in the door.

"I like weddings where the bride chose," said Ferra.

She said it to the lane. Not to him. In the voice she used for a total, flat and level, the voice that meant the figure was right and there was nothing else to say about it.

He heard what was in it. He heard the stay in it, and clause nine, and the table in February with the four baronies' stewards' eyes on her, and every man in four baronies who would one day look at the one check on the holder's power and see the holder's wife; and her yes in February, and her not yet. He heard all of it. It was all in the five words, the way a whole quarter is in the figure at the foot of a column.

He did not say anything. He did not ask her when. He did not say that he would wait, because she knew he would wait, and saying it would have been asking.

He stood beside her in the church door and watched the two of them go up the hill.

After a while she unfolded one arm, and found his hand at his side without looking down, and held it; and he knew that she had heard him hear it, and not push, and that she had wanted to see whether he would.

"Good," said Ferra, to the lane. And let go.



Ward was in the keep yard when he came up, at the foot of the stair, in the dusk, with his helm under his arm.

He had been to the shed. Emeric could see it in his boots, the shed's slip mud, and in the helm, which Ward never carried anywhere but to and from the shed. He stood at the foot of the keep stair as if he had been waiting, which he had.

"The father," said Ward.

"The father."

"Tomorrow night."

"Tomorrow night. And Vane, the same hour." Emeric looked up at the keep's windows, where Ferra's lamp was already lit. "Two teams. You and I and Declan and Osric at Sallow's. Rylan and two of the garrison at Vane's. Imani aboard with the ether." He looked back at Ward. "The same four lines."

"Nobody of the household dies," said Ward. "In either house."

"In either house."

Ward nodded once. Then he looked at the helm in his hands, the violet panes of it gone dark in the dusk.

"I stood up for him today," he said. "In a church. A knight with no land and no money and six men's boots to inspect on a hill." He was quiet. "I've never stood up for anybody in a church."

"How was it?"

"Hard," said Ward. "Harder than the road." He put the helm under his arm. "I'd do it again." And went up the stair.
