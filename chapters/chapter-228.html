Chapter 228 - The Drop

Ferra was at the glassworks to count panes, and she was counting them when the boy dropped the tear.

She had been there all morning. The wired glass was going through Orrin's shop at twenty panes a day, which was every pane Orrin's men could pour and roll and cut and grind between dawn and dark, and every pane of it was entered, because every pane of it was glass the barony had bought twice: once for the dome and once for the net. She sat on a stool by the grinding benches with her book on her knee and the day's count going down the page, and the glassmen worked round her and had long since stopped noticing she was there, which was how she liked a works.

The boy was one of Orrin's gatherers, eleven or twelve, whose work was to carry the glass on its iron from the pot to the table. At the midday break he stood at the pot's mouth with a pontil and a blob of hot glass on the end of it, and a bucket of water at his feet, and let a drop fall off the end of the iron into the bucket. It went in with a hiss and a little puff of steam. He fished it out with his fingers and held it up to the light of the pot's mouth, grinning, and another boy came over to look.

Ferra looked too. She could not help it. It was a bead of glass the size of a pea, round at one end, and from the round end it drew out into a long thin tail, curling, fine as a thread at its tip. A tadpole. Clear, with the pot's fire in it.

The first boy put the round end of it on the grinding bench's iron edge, and took a mallet off the bench, the small one they used for knocking glass off the pontils, and hit it. Hard.

Nothing happened to it. The mallet bounced. The boy hit it again, harder, and it rang against the iron and did not break, and the second boy laughed.

Then the first boy picked it up by its round head and took the tip of the tail between his finger and thumb, and snapped it.

The whole of it went. Not the tail: all of it. The head in his fingers, that a mallet could not break, went to dust in his hand with a small dry crack, like a knuckle, and he opened his fingers and there was nothing in them but a pinch of glittering white powder, and he blew it off his palm into the air, and both boys laughed.

Ferra put her pencil down.

"Tears," Orrin said. "Glassmen's tears. We've made them for the children since before I was a child. My master made them for me." He was at the pot himself, now, with a pontil; the boys had gone to eat. He let one fall into the bucket for her, and it went in with its hiss, and he fished it out and gave it to her, cool now, round and clear in her palm with its long tail curling. "Every glasshouse in the kingdom makes them. The children fight over them."

She held it. It weighed almost nothing.

"Hit it," Orrin said.

She put it on the bench's iron edge and took the small mallet and hit it, not as hard as the boy, and then as hard as the boy, and then as hard as she could. It rang. It did not break. She looked at it on the iron, whole, shining, a bead of glass the size of a pea that she had just hit three times with a mallet.

"Why?" she said.

Orrin shrugged. "It's a tear. That's what they do."

"Glass breaks. That's what glass does. Bram says so every day. A pane breaks if you look at it, and a bottle breaks if you drop it, and this is glass." She held it up. "Why doesn't it break?"

Orrin looked at her with the particular patience of a craftsman asked why about a thing he has done ten thousand times.

"Mistress," he said, "I've no idea. Nobody has. I've never heard anybody ask."

She put the tear in her pocket. She finished the day's count, and entered it, and rode back across the flat to the palace in the afternoon with the tear in her pocket, and she did not take it out, but she put her hand on it in her pocket several times on the way, the way she put her hand on the key to the box.

She put it on the long-room table that evening, in front of him, beside the day's count.

He looked at it. He did not know what it was; she could see that. He picked it up by its head, as the boy had, and turned it to the lamp.

"Don't touch the tail," she said. "Hit the head."

He looked at her. She gave him the small mallet from Bram's bag, which Bram had lent her with an expression, and he put the tear on the table's edge and hit it, and it rang, and he stopped with the mallet in the air and looked at the tear, whole, and then at her.

"Now snap the tail," she said.

He did it very carefully, over a sheet of paper. It went to dust with its small dry crack, and the dust lay on the paper, glittering, and he sat and looked at it.

She watched his face. She had watched his face across that table for more years than she had watched anybody's, and she knew what it did when a thing came together for him. It did not do anything. That was how you knew. It went very still, and his eyes went to a place a little above the thing in front of him, and stayed there, and he stopped breathing for a moment, and then started again.

"Say it," she said.

"It's squeezed," he said. "The outside of it." He did not look at her. He was looking at the powder. "When it goes into the water, the outside cools first. All at once, in the first moment. It goes hard while the inside's still hot and soft. Then the inside cools, after, slowly, inside a hard skin. And as it cools it shrinks. Everything shrinks as it cools. And the inside shrinking pulls on the outside, all the way round, from the inside, and the outside can't shrink any more, it's already hard, so it's squeezed. It's held tight. The whole skin of it, all the way round, is squeezed in, like a hoop on a barrel, like a fist closed on it." He touched the powder with one finger. "Glass doesn't break when you squeeze it. It breaks when you pull it. A crack's a pull, opening. And a crack can't start in a skin that's being squeezed shut. You hit the head with a mallet, and the crack tries to start, and the skin's squeezed so hard it can't open. So it doesn't break."

"And the tail?"

"The tail's thin. It's only skin; there's no inside to squeeze it. So you can snap it. And when you snap it, you've made a crack that's inside the squeeze, past the skin, into the middle where it's all pulled tight, like a bowstring, like a spring wound up. And the crack runs. All of it, everything that was held tight, lets go at once." He looked at the dust. "That's the powder. It's the whole of the squeeze, letting go."

Ferra had her pencil in her hand. She did not write it yet.

"How do you know that?"

He looked up at her then.

"Somebody told me," he said. "A long time ago. Somebody explained it. Why the tears don't break. I know I was told. I know the whole of it, the skin and the squeeze and the crack." He looked back at the powder. "I can't tell you who. I've tried. I can't remember a name, or a face, or where. Only the thing."

She had heard him say that before. Not often. About the pump, once, and the sulphur, and the thing that turned rubber from toffee into a strip that bent double and lay flat again. He always said it the same way, with the same look, as if there were a door in him he knew was there and could not find the handle of. She had never asked about it. She did not ask now. She wrote it down: The tear. The skin squeezed, the inside pulled. E.: he was told, and does not know by whom.

"And the dome," she said.

"And the dome." He picked up one of the wired panes' offcuts, a sliver of clear glass with the iron net in it, from the pile Bram had left on the table. "If you could do that to a pane. Not a drop. A whole pane. Heat it till it's nearly soft, all through, evenly, and then cool the faces of it all at once, hard, both sides, while the middle's still hot. The faces would go hard first, and the middle would cool after and pull on them, and the faces would be squeezed. The whole of both faces of the pane. Like the head of the tear." He put the sliver down. "It wouldn't break. Not when the sun worked at it. Not when the node grew in the day and pressed on its corner. Not when the band leaned on it all night. A bruise at its corner couldn't open into a crack, because the skin round the bruise would be squeezed shut."

"How much stronger?"

"I don't know." He looked at the tear's dust. "Much. Four times. Five. I'm guessing."

She wrote down guessing.

It took four weeks and more glass than Ferra liked to think about, and she wrote down every pane of it.

The first thing was the heat. Orrin's annealing oven, the long low one at the end of the shop where every pane lay overnight to cool slowly so that it did not crack, was too cool; it was built to let glass down gently, not to bring it up. Bram and the prince made another, in a week, beside it: a box of firebrick with a door at each end and fire stones in its walls on a deciding stone that held the heat where it was told, the way the furnace's stones held it for Orrin, and a set of iron rails running through it on which a pane could lie flat on a cradle and be run in at one end and out at the other.

The second thing was the cold. The drop had gone into water. A pane could not; water on a hot pane would have burst it in the first breath, Orrin said, and he had seen it. It had to be air. Cold air, a great deal of it, all at once, on both faces at the same moment, evenly, from edge to edge. The prince drew a frame of iron pipes with holes in them, one above the pane and one below, so that a pane coming out of the hot box on its cradle would slide between them, and at the moment it was between them the air would come out of all the holes at once onto both its faces. And behind the pipes, to drive the air, a box of air stones, which moved air and did nothing else, and could move a great deal of it very fast, very cold, when they were told to, and roared like the band in a storm when they did.

Then they started.

The first pane went into the hot box on a Tuesday and came out when Orrin said it was nearly soft, glowing a dull red, and slid between the pipes, and the air came down on it with a roar that shook dust off the shop's rafters, and the pane went white. All of it. A web of fine white lines ran across it from edge to edge in the first instant, a thousand of them, and it lay on its cradle crazed like the glaze on an old pot, and when Orrin touched it with a pontil it fell into pieces.

First. Crazed. Too hot, or the air too hard, or the air uneven.

The second came out less hot and the air came down less hard, and it did not craze. It went onto the rack by the wall to cool, and Ferra wrote second, held, and at the midday break, while the boys were eating, it burst on the rack by itself with a sound like a pistol, and showered the floor with a thousand small pieces, and nobody was near it, and everybody jumped.

Second. Held from the air. Burst on the rack an hour after, untouched.

The third held. It lay on the rack all day and all night and did not burst. In the morning Bram put it across two blocks and hit it in the middle with the small mallet, and it rang, and he hit it with a larger mallet, and it rang, and he hit it with a hammer, and it went. It went all at once, like the tear: the whole pane in a breath to a heap of little pieces the size of peas, square-edged, dull-cornered, lying in a pile under the blocks. Bram picked one up and rubbed it between his finger and thumb.

"Doesn't cut," he said.

Ferra wrote it down. Third. Held. Took the small mallet, and the large. The hammer broke it. It went to small pieces, all at once. The pieces do not cut.

After that it was the slow part. Some crazed and some burst and some held. They held more often when the heat was even, and they held more often when the air came down on both faces at the same moment, and they held more often when the pane had been ground on its edges before it went in, and they burst more often when it had not. Every pane went in the book: the time in the hot box, the colour it came out, the strength of the air, what it did, and what Bram's mallet did to it the next morning. By the second week one in three held. By the fourth, three in four. The glassmen stopped jumping when a pane burst on the rack. The boys began to fight over the little square-edged pieces the way they had fought over the tears, and Orrin let them have them, since they did not cut.

The rule came in the third week, and it came from a pane that Orrin's man cut.

It had held. It had come out of the air clear and whole and lain on the rack a night and taken Bram's large mallet in the morning, and it was a good pane, but it was a hand too long on one side for the dome's template, because it had been cut wrong in the first place. So Orrin's man laid it on the cutting table and scored it with his diamond along the template's line, as he had scored ten thousand panes in his life, and tapped it to break it along the score.

It went to powder. All of it, the whole pane, in one breath, off the table and onto the floor, from the tap of a knuckle.

"The tail," said Ferra, in the silence after.

The prince nodded. "The score's a crack. Into the middle. Past the skin." He looked at the heap on the floor. "You can't cut it afterwards. Not ever. Not an edge ground, not a hole drilled, not a corner taken off. Anything that gets past the skin lets the whole of it go." He turned to Orrin. "Every pane cut to its template first. Ground on every edge first. Every hole in it, first. Everything that's ever going to be done to it, done, before it goes into the hot box. And after it comes out of the air, nobody touches it with anything but a cloth."

Bram, who had been sweeping up the powder, stopped sweeping.

"Everything right before it's hot," he said.

"Everything right before it's hot."

Bram looked at the heap of powder in his pan. Then he looked at the prince, and Ferra, who had worked beside him for longer than she had worked beside anybody but the prince, saw on his face something she had seen there perhaps three times in all those years. He was pleased. He was entirely and simply pleased.

"That's the first thing you've ever made," Bram said, "that I'd have made the same way."

Ferra wrote it down, the rule and not the remark. Then, because she wrote everything, she wrote the remark too, in the margin, small.

At the end of the fourth week she laid the book open on the long-room table, at the page where the glass began, and turned it round so that the table could see it.

It was eleven pages. Every pane that had gone into the hot box, three hundred and eighty of them, in a column down the left of each page, with its time and its colour and its air and what it had done. Crazed. Burst on the rack. Held. Held. Burst. Held. Crazed. Held. The column went down eleven pages, and at the top the word crazed and the word burst were on most of the lines, and at the bottom, on the last page, they were on almost none.

"Three hundred and eighty," she said. "Two hundred and twelve held. A hundred and sixty-eight broken, in one way or another, and every one of them glass we'd paid for twice." She put her finger on the last page. "The last forty, thirty-nine held."

Nobody said anything. The prince was looking at the column, at the words going down it, the crazed and the burst thinning out down the pages until the held were nearly all of it.

"It's the cost of the thing," Ferra said. "It's what it cost to find out. I want it in the book so nobody ever tells us it was easy."

"Nobody will," said Bram.

"Somebody will," said Ferra, "in twenty years. And they'll be wrong, and this will be here." She closed the book. "Orrin can do the dome's panes in a month. Cut to template, ground, into the box, into the air. He says he'll have the first forty by the end of the week." She looked at the prince. "And you'll want something else out of it. I saw you looking at the hammer pieces."

He did not answer at once.

"They don't cut," he said at last. "When it goes, it goes all at once, into pieces that don't cut." He looked at his hands on the table. "There's a thing I want to make that will be in front of a man's face, somewhere I can't get him out of quickly. And if it breaks, I'd rather it went all at once into pieces that don't cut, than into a knife."

Ferra looked at him for a moment. Then she opened the book again, to a clean page, and wrote at the top of it, in her small square hand, a heading she had never written before.

Helmets.
