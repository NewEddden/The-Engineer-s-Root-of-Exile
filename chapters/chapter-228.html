Chapter 228 - The Injury

Armand brought the sheets to the counting room on the twenty-second of June, in the box.

He carried it up the keep stair himself, which took him a long time, and set it down on Ferra's table without a word, and unlocked it with the key on his chain, and took them out: five years of them, in bundles tied with tape, one bundle for every spring since the autumn Mott had stood in the yard with clean skin and read inside knight range. Three readings to a person, on three days, in his small columns. The name and the age and the trade. Every person in the four baronies who had let him, and against each of them a man or a woman of the same age and the same street who had never in their lives handled a charged stone, matched, name for name. Something over six hundred people a year, by the last of them, read at tables in the school hall by Armand and the three students he had brought south.

"I'd like it run," he said, "on the machine."

Ottoline looked at the bundles, and then at the tabulator under its cloth against the back wall, and then at Armand; and said nothing for a breath, which was as near as she came to being moved by anything.

"Four years of change," she said. "Five of readings. Matched." She untied the nearest bundle and turned the top sheet to the window. "A card a person a year. Six thousand cards. More." She looked at the columns. "Your hand's very small."

"I was trained in an age when paper cost money."

"It still costs money," said Ferra, from her table, without looking up. "It costs less. Ottoline will want my two best clerks to read your hand onto the cards, and the clerks will want paying, and you'll have it in a week."

"A week," said Armand. He had been five years at it. He stood looking at the bundles on the table as if they were somebody else's. "I had it in my head that it would take a winter."

"It would take you a winter," said Ottoline. "It'll take the machine a week. Most of that's the clerks."

Emeric was in the counting room that morning because he was usually in the counting room in the morning, at the end of Ferra's table with the Wayford tape. He did not get up. He could have done the sums himself in a fortnight of evenings, by hand, with Ferra's best clerk to read the columns to him; he had thought about it every spring since the autumn of the fourth year and had never once asked to see a sheet. They were Armand's. He had said so in writing, at the long table, in Ferra's book. He sat at the end of the table with the tape running through his fingers and let Ottoline untie the bundles, and did not look at the columns as they went past.

Armand saw him not look. He did not say anything about it. But on his way out, slowly, with the empty box under his arm, he stopped by Emeric's chair and put his hand on Emeric's shoulder for a breath, the hand that was always going inside his coat, and took it away again, and went down the stair.



The tabulator ran for six days.

It ran in the day while the clerks punched, and at night while Ottoline sorted, with a clatter like a dropped tray that went on and on, and Ferra said she could hear it through two floors and in her sleep and possibly in her grave. The two best clerks sat at the long table under the window with Armand's sheets and a glass each, reading his small hand onto the cards a figure at a time, and Ottoline went down the cards behind them with her pencil, and put a dot in the corner of every tenth; and twice she found a figure read wrong and took the clerk's card out of the stack and gave it back to her without a word, and the clerk did not make that mistake a third time.

Ferra did the matching. Every man who handled stones against his twin who did not, every year, by age and street and trade, so that no cold spring or hungry winter could make the difference; she sat at the end of her own table with the two lists side by side and checked every pair, three thousand of them, and found nine she did not like, and took them out, and wrote down why.

On the night of the twenty-sixth the engine house's third engine went round for the first time under its own steam, the big one on the long bed at the north end, with Tess sitting on a stool beside the dynamo on the end of its shaft with her hand on the bearing cap and her eyes shut, feeling it come up to speed; and the needle on her board went over to a figure nobody in Kaldo had ever seen on a dial before, and stayed there, and the lamps along the engine house's walls went bright enough to read by at the far end of the floor. The east half of the town would have it by the end of the summer, lane by lane, behind the trench gangs. Bram stood in the engine house door with his arms folded and said it was a great deal of steam to light a great many people's suppers, and that in his day a man ate his supper in the dark and was grateful; and Tess opened her eyes and said that in his day a man's supper had killed him at forty, and Bram went away.

Emeric heard about it from Ferra, who heard about it from the tape. He was on the slip with Fletcher that night, looking at a keel that was going to be an ore lighter, and did not see it. He was sorry, and went down in the morning and looked at the needle, and it was still there.

On the twenty-eighth Ottoline put the last card through and took the sorted stacks off the machine's pockets and squared them, every stack, with the edge of her palm, and wrote a figure on a slip of paper and put it on top of each; and then she sat down at her own table and looked at the stacks for a long while without touching them.

"I'd like Master Armand," she said at last, to Ferra. "Before anybody else."



Armand read it out at the long table the next evening, himself.

He had asked for the table. Not the counting room, where the cards were; the long room, at the long table, with everybody who had ever been read or had read anybody, which was most of the people who mattered in Kaldo: Ferra and Imani, Ward at the door, Bram, Saban, Ottoline with her stacks in a box at her feet, Tess, and the four knights, Rylan and Osric and Declan and Ward himself; and Mott, from the still crew, who had been asked and had come in his working clothes and sat at the end of the bench with his cap in his hands looking as if he had been sent for by a magistrate. The windows were open. The light was going.

Armand did not stand. He sat at the head of the table, where Emeric usually sat, because Emeric had given him the chair without being asked; with the sheet Ottoline had written for him under his hand, and his stick against the table's edge, and his spectacles on, which he did not usually wear in company.

"The still crew," he said. "Mott's crew. Fifteen men who have carried charged fire stones in their bare hands every working day for between five years and seven. Against fifteen men from the same streets who have not." He looked at the sheet. "Every man of the fifteen reads higher than he read five years ago. Every one. The least of them by a third. The most by more than twice. Against their fifteen, who read what they read, and a little less, because they are five years older." He turned the sheet. "No tracery. None. Mistress Imani has looked at every one of them, at my asking, every spring, at their arms and their chests and their necks." He did not look at Imani. "Clean skin."

Nobody said anything.

"The foundry's stock handlers. The same. The men who work the stone shed. The same. The ship's crew, who have handled her stones since she was built. The same, and more." He turned another sheet. "The knights of this barony, who were made by the rite, and who have handled stones every day since they came here. Every one of them reads higher than when they came. Declan most of all. Declan has gained more than any person in the four baronies." He looked down the table at Declan, who had gone very still. "With no new tracery on him at all. Not a line."

Declan looked at his own forearms, at the old raised silver going down one side only, as if he had never seen them.

"The bands," said Armand. "The same. Against their matched, the same." He took his spectacles off. "I set out five years ago to prove a young man wrong in front of this table. I said capacity is set at birth and judged once, by eye and by feel, and that a man's reading at twenty is his reading at sixty, less what age takes. That is what the tower teaches. I taught it for fifty years." He put the spectacles down on the sheet, carefully, square to its edge, as Ferra put a pen. "It is not true."

He looked at the sheet a while longer.

"Capacity grows," he said. "Slowly, when the body is made to carry more than it is used to, and is given time. It grows the way a smith's arm grows. It breaks, when it is made to carry far more than it can, all at once; and if it does not break the man, it heals, larger, with a scar. The scar is the tracery." He looked up. "The tracery is no mark of the Light's choosing, and no badge. It is an injury. It is the scar the body lays down where it has been torn and has healed in a hurry because it had to."

Rylan, halfway down the table, had put his forearms flat on the wood in front of him, palms up. He was looking at them.

"The rite and the stones come to the same place," said Armand. "The same total. A man who has carried a stone for twenty years and a man who has lain on the floor of that room for fourteen hours end in the same place, near enough. What differs is the rate." He laid his hand flat on Ottoline's sheet. "The total decides where you land. The rate decides whether you live."

He stopped.

Emeric thought, for a breath, that he had finished. He had said it. It was out on the table in front of everybody who mattered, in his own voice, with his own five years of small columns in a box at Ottoline's feet behind it, and it would be in Ferra's book by morning in his words and nobody else's; and Emeric felt something go out of his own chest that had been held there since the autumn of the fourth year, and was ready, very nearly, to say so.

Then he saw Armand's hands.



They were shaking.

Not much. An old man's hands on a table, shaking a little. But they were flat on the sheet, and the sheet was moving under them, a small dry rustle against the wood that everybody at the table could hear in the quiet; and Armand looked down at them as if they were somebody else's hands, and took them off the sheet and put them in his lap, and they went on shaking there.

"Every man in my rotation," he said.

His voice had not changed. It was the teacher's voice, dry and level, the voice for a figure that had come out wrong.

"I ran the room for a season, at the top of the tower, before I came to the castle. Every top mage of the tower took a season in it. It was being done before me and would be done after, and I went in knowing it was wrong, and I told myself I could make it hurt less." He was looking at the table, not at anybody. "I didn't count. The tower doesn't. I have counted since, from what I remember and from the chapter's roll, which I copied out in my own hand the winter I left. Forty-one men in my season. Six got up. One of the thirty-five was nineteen, and asked me for water, and I could not give it to him because the doctrine said the room must be dry. I was never told his name. I have carried that boy for nearly thirty years. I told myself he died because the Light did not choose him, because if I did not tell myself that I would have had to tell myself something else."

His hands were shaking in his lap. He did not look at them.

"And Rylan's three," he said, and turned his head, and looked down the table at Rylan.

Rylan did not move. He was looking at his own upturned forearms.

"The men who lay beside you," said Armand. "Who gave you their messages and took yours. You said once, at a fire in Ward's yard, and it came to me, as everything said in that yard comes to me, that a man in vestments stood over a randomness and called it a verdict. I said, to anybody who would listen, that you were wrong. That it was not random, that the Light chose, that it was cruel but it was a choosing." The voice stayed exactly where it was. "You were not wrong. It was not a choosing. It was not even random. It was a method, run too fast, by men who had never once thought to run it slower, because nobody had ever measured anything." He put his hand flat on Ottoline's sheet again, and it shook there. "None of them had to die. Not your three. Not my thirty-five. Not one of them. They could have carried stones in a foundry for twenty years and come out where they were going, with clean skin, and grown old." He was quiet. "We killed them for want of patience. And then we read their names out over them and told their mothers it was God."

The light had nearly gone from the windows. Nobody had lit the lamps.

"I would like," said Armand, "a glass of water."

Imani was already getting up.

And Armand's face changed.



The colour went first, out of his face from the top down, as if somebody were drawing a blind; and then his breath, which caught at the top, and caught again, and did not come down; and his right hand came up off the sheet and went inside his coat, flat, high, over the breastbone, and pressed there, hard, so that the knuckles went white. He leaned forward a little over the table. His mouth opened. He did not say anything.

Imani was round the table before Emeric was out of his chair. She had his left wrist in her fingers and her other hand on the side of his neck under the jaw, and her face had gone into the shape it went into in the ward, which had nothing in it at all but attention.

"Lie him down," she said. "The floor. Coats. Now."

Ward and Bram had him out of the chair and down on the long room's boards before she had finished saying it, with Ward's coat under his head and Bram's under his shoulders, lifting him as if he weighed nothing, which he very nearly did. Ferra had the lamp lit. Somebody had opened the other window. Imani knelt beside him with her two fingers at his throat and her lips moving.

"Out of step," she said, to nobody. "Fast. Then nothing. Then fast." She counted. "It's dropping beats. It's..." She stopped counting and started again. "I can't keep it. It won't hold still long enough to count."

Armand's eyes were open. He was looking at the ceiling, breathing in short catches, with his hand still flat under his coat; and he moved his eyes, without moving his head, until they found Emeric kneeling on the other side of him.

"It does this," he said, on a breath. "It stops. Give it a count of a hundred."

"How long has it been doing this?" said Imani.

"Longer than you've been a healer."

Imani looked at him. Then she put her fingers back on his throat and counted, and her face did not change, and Emeric, across the old man's body from her, watched her get to forty, and lose it, and start again; and get to sixty; and look up at him, once, with no expression, which was the worst thing he had ever seen on her face.

It did not stop at a hundred.



Emeric had his sense out. He always had it out, a little, thin, the way a man has his eyes open; and he had known Armand's note across rooms and corridors and tables since the first year, the deep slow note of a High mage worn thin with age, a great still lake with very little water left in it. It was there now, on the floor in front of him, and it was wrong. It was going in and out, like a lamp guttering in a draught. He could feel it from where he knelt without trying. He could feel nothing else about it at all, nothing that would tell him why, because his sense came back from the outside of a man, as a hand comes back from the outside of a jar, and what was wrong was inside.

He had never been inside a man. Not this way. He had been into the room behind other people's notes, given, many times, with the six; and once forced, at Wayford, and he would never force that door again; and that was the delve, and the delve was memory and the room and the people in it, and it was no use to him here at all. This was not a room. This was a body, on a floor, with its heart doing something it should not.

A sense goes out thin. It touches and comes back. He thought: it does not have to come back.

"Armand," he said.

The eyes moved to him.

"I want to go in. Not the room. You." He did not know how to say it better. "I want to let my mana into you, slowly. To see. I don't know if it'll work. If it hurts, or you push, I'll stop."

Armand looked at him for a long breath, breathing in catches.

"I told you once not to look at the middle," he said. "You never did. You never used to ask, either."

"I'm asking."

"Then yes." The mouth moved, very slightly, at one corner. "Go on. Slowly. You know my rate."



He put his hand flat on Armand's chest, over the old man's hand, over the breastbone.

He did not push. That was the first thing, and he knew it before he had thought it: that if he pushed, the old man's own mana would push back, as every living body pushed back at a raw push, as a knight's tracery threw off a spell; that there would be a clash, small, inside a chest with a failing heart in it, and he would kill him. So he did not push. He let it out the way he let his sense out across a room, thin, with no shape and no glyph, the way breath goes out on a cold morning; and he let it out at Armand's own note. He had known it nine years. He matched it, as a man hums a note back to a man who has just hummed it, until there was no telling the two apart; and then he let it go down, under his hand, into the old man, so slowly that it was less like pouring than like warmth going into a stone in the sun.

Armand did not push back. There was nothing to push back at. It was his own note coming into him, a little more of it, from outside, as rain comes into a lake.

And it went in.

It went down into the chest under his hand and spread, and as it spread it came back to him; not as the sense came back off the outside of things, but from inside, from everywhere it touched, all at once, a little at a time, as a lamp lit in a dark room shows the room not all at once but corner by corner as the eye finds it.

He saw Armand.

Not with his eyes. With the mana he had sunk into him, which lay through the old man now like water through a sponge, and came back to him from every part of him it lay in. And the old man was not one note any more, but a shape made of notes, brighter where the blood ran thick and dimmer where it did not: the great vessels from the heart like two rivers of light going up into the neck and down into the body; the lungs, two dim branching trees full of breath, filling and emptying in short catches; the bones, dull, faintly there, as wood is faintly there to the sense. And in the middle of it, under his hand, a fist of light, beating.

It was beating wrong.

He had a word for it, from another life. He did not say it. The top of the heart, the two small chambers that should have gone first and set the beat for the rest, were not beating at all; they were shivering, fast, all over, like the skin on a horse's flank under a fly, so fast and so disordered that nothing in them kept time; and the big chambers below, which should have followed them, could not tell when to go, and went when they could, and stumbled, and went again, out of step.

And on the left side of the big fist of light, low, on the outside of its wall, there was a place that was not light at all.

It was small. The size of the end of his thumb. It was dead: it held no mana, none, as a knot in a plank holds none, as an old scar on a man's hand is dead to the touch; and the muscle round it was dim, and moved stiffly, as if it were working round a stone. Something had happened to this heart, years ago. Something had struck it from inside, in a night, and part of the wall had died, and the rest had healed round the dead part and gone on beating, with a patch in it that did nothing, for twenty years or forty, while an old man walked up keep stairs with his hand inside his coat.

Emeric held there, with his hand on the old man's chest and his mana lying all through him, and looked at it.

And while he looked, the shivering slowed.

He felt it before he understood it. Under his mana, where it lay thickest round the top of the heart, the disordered shiver went quieter; and quieter; as a crowd goes quieter when one voice in it starts to sing; and then, all at once, in one beat, it stopped shivering and went: once, cleanly, and the great chambers below heard it and followed, once, together. And again. And again.

The fist of light under his hand was beating in step.

He understood it then. The old man's body was using him. His mana, lying in the chest at the old man's own pitch, was not his any more; the body had taken it as its own, as a body takes food, and was spending it inward, on itself, where inward mana costs almost nothing, on the thing that most needed it. On the heart. It was holding the heart in step with Emeric's mana as a man holds a door shut against the wind with his shoulder.

He held still and let it.



It was a long time. He did not know how long. The lamp burned and the long room was perfectly silent round them and Imani's fingers were at Armand's throat, counting, and he could hear her counting now, under her breath, and not losing it.

"Sixty-two," said Imani. "Sixty-two. Even." Her voice was very steady. "He's in step. Emeric. He's in step."

He heard her from a long way off.

He was very deep in. He had not meant to go so deep; it had happened slowly, as the mana spread, as he followed it out into the old man's arms and legs and up into the slow dim branching of the lungs, and the shape of Armand all round him was very big and very quiet and beating, and his own breath, he found, had gone into the old man's rhythm, in and out, in short catches, and was smoothing out as the old man's smoothed. He could not, for a while, find which heart was his. There were two, and they were beating together now, in step, and one of them was under his hand and one of them was somewhere behind it, and he did not know which, and it did not seem to matter very much.

Somebody was hurting his wrist.

It hurt a great deal. Fingers, small and hard, dug into the inside of his left wrist between the bones, where nothing protected the nerve, and twisted; and the pain went up his arm like a hot wire and arrived in his head, in his own head, behind his own eyes, where Armand had told him once to come back to, and he came back to it.

He took his hand off the old man's chest.

He drew the mana back as slowly as he had let it in, because he did not dare do anything else, a little at a time, as a man backs out of a room where somebody is sleeping; and the shape of Armand faded round him corner by corner, the lungs and the bones and the rivers of light, until there was only the note again, the old deep note on the floor in front of him, outside him, steady. Steady. Not guttering.

Imani let go of his wrist. There were four white marks on it, going red.

"You went grey," she said. "You went the colour he was. Your breath went with his." She was looking at him with her ward face. "Armand said it once at this table. Something that hurts. You come back to the hurt." She sat back on her heels. "So I hurt you."

"Thank you," said Emeric.

His head was splitting. It had started behind his eyes and it was going out from there to the edges of his skull, the bill for going out, double, and his nose was not bleeding, which he noticed because he had expected it to be. He sat down on the boards beside the old man because he could not have stayed kneeling.

Armand was looking at him. His colour had come back, or some of it. His breath was going in and out, slow, without the catch. His hand had come out of his coat and lay on the floor beside him, open, and was not shaking.



"What," said Armand, after a while, "was that?"

"I don't know."

"You went in."

"I went in to look."

Armand lay on the floor of the long room with Ward's coat under his head and looked at the ceiling, at the old beams that had been black with soot when Emeric came and were not now.

"In seventy years," he said, "I have known men go into a man. The tower does it. It goes into a man to fill him, in a stone room with a drain, at a rate that kills nine in ten." A pause. "I have never known anybody go into a man to look at him."

"I went in at your rate," said Emeric.

Armand turned his head on the coat and looked at him. And then he laughed: once, weakly, on his back on the floor, the dry short laugh of a teacher who has been handed his own argument by a pupil and cannot think of an answer to it.

"At my rate," he said. "Yes. I suppose you did."

Imani was not laughing. She had her fingers back on his throat.

"Tell me what you saw," she said to Emeric. "All of it. Not the pretty parts."

He told her. The top of the heart, shivering, not beating; the big chambers below stumbling after it, out of step. The dead place in the wall, low, on the left, the size of the end of a thumb, holding no mana at all, and the muscle round it stiff. He told it plainly, as he would have told Bram a crack in a casting, and Imani listened with her eyes on Armand's face and her lips pressed together, and did not interrupt.

"An old strike," she said, when he had finished. "A heart attack. Years ago. He'd have had a night of it, once, with his arm and his jaw on fire, and thought it was something he ate, and lived." She looked at Armand. "When?"

"The winter I came down from the tower." Armand was looking at the ceiling again. "I thought it was grief."

"And the shivering since?"

"Ten years. Fifteen. It comes and goes." Then: "It comes more."

"And you never told me."

"You'd have written it down."

"I'd have written it down," said Imani. She took her fingers off his throat. "I've old men in the ward with that stumble. I give them foxglove and tell them not to climb stairs and they die in their chairs in two years or ten. I've never once known what it looked like inside." She looked at Emeric across the old man. "And it went in step. Under your hand."

"Under my mana. His body took it. Spent it inward." He rubbed his eyes. "Inward costs nothing. It held the heart in step with it."

"Then put it back in."

"I can't sit beside him with my hand on him for the rest of his life."

"Why not?" said Armand. "It was very pleasant."

Nobody laughed. Emeric sat on the boards with his head splitting and looked at the old man on the floor, his teacher, his mother's teacher, who had walked up the keep stair with his hand inside his coat since the day he came south and never said one word; and found that he was thinking, quite clearly, under the headache, about four miles straight up.

"There's a place," he said, "where the whole sky does what I just did."

Imani looked at him.

"Four miles up," said Emeric. "You've been in it. The mana's so thick there that it gets into everyone whether they want it or not. That's what the bleeding sickness is. Too much, too fast, into bodies that can't spend it." He looked at Armand. "But a body that needs it. That's got somewhere to spend it, inward, on a heart that's out of step." He stopped. "I don't know. I've never seen it. Nobody's ever seen it. But in an hour up there, he'd have more mana lying in him than I could give him in a week, and his body would do with it what it did with mine."

"He's eighty-five," said Imani, "with a heart that shivers and a hole in it. And you want to take him four miles up where the air's too thin to breathe and the cold kills."

"In the ship. Sealed. Warm. The pump going. Breath candles at the seat." He held her eyes. "You with him. Calling the time."

Imani held his eyes a long breath. Then down at Armand.

"Two hours," she said. "Not one more. And the first time, one. And if his colour goes or his breath goes or I don't like his face, we come down, and I don't care what it does to anybody's sums."

"Do I," said Armand, from the floor, "have a say?"

They both looked at him.

"Yes," said Emeric.

"Then yes." Armand closed his eyes. "I have never been. I have wanted to go since I was an apprentice and my master came down from the last climb with his nose bleeding and his eyes like lamps and told me what it was like, and the Church shut it the next spring." He was quiet. "I should like to see it before I die. I should like to see it a good while before."



He did not know then how far it could go.

That mana could mend a body, he had learned that evening on the floor of the long room with his hand on an old man's chest; that it could hold a heart in step while it lay there, and that the body took it and used it as it used bread. That it could only go so far, he would learn over the summer, sitting in a sealed saloon four miles up beside the same old man, watching his hands go still on the rail and then, a few days after, on the ground, watching them begin to shake again. And that a stone, taken inside, could go a great deal further than the whole sky ever would, he would not learn for years, and would learn it on a winter morning on a gallery bench, with the old man ice-cold in front of him; and that was a long way off yet, and he did not know it, and it was as well.



Ward and Bram carried him up to his room at ten in his chair, between them, up the keep stair, the stair that had been built by a man who hated old men; and Imani went up behind them with her bag, and stayed.

The long room emptied slowly after. Ottoline took her box of stacks back to the counting room. Mott, from the end of the bench, who had sat through all of it with his cap in his hands and his mouth a little open and had not moved, got up at last and came along the table to Emeric and stood in front of him, a big slow man in his working clothes, turning the cap round.

"Clean skin," he said. "That's me. That was me, the first one. In the yard."

"Yes."

"Twice what I was," said Mott. He looked at his own hands, big and grey from the stones, as Declan had looked at his forearms. "I never knew. I just carried the stones." He looked up. "Is it bad? What's in me?"

"No," said Emeric. "It's what was always going to be in you. You just got there slower than the men in the room."

Mott thought about that for a long while. Then he nodded, and put his cap on, and went out.

Rylan had gone already. Emeric had not seen him go. He went to the window to close it and saw him: down in the yard, in the last of the light, in his shirtsleeves, alone. He had the practice sword. He was going through the forms, the long forms Ward had made them learn in the first year, one after another, very slowly, every movement exact, as a man does them when he does not want to think about anything else; and the pale lines on the insides of his forearms caught the light from the long room's window each time his arms came up.

Emeric left the window open. He went up to his own room and lay down on his bed in the dark with his head splitting, and could not sleep; and at midnight, when he got up for water, he looked out, and Rylan was still in the yard, still going through the forms, in the dark now, by memory, with nobody watching but the night watch on the far gate, who had the sense to look the other way.
