Chapter 123 - Three Instruments

They hanged Sethe on the second of August, in the yard behind the court, at dawn, in the manner the law provided, and Emeric did not go.

Ward went. He said somebody from Kaldo ought to, so that it was known in the south that Kaldo had seen it done, and that it ought not to be the holder, so that it was not known in the south that the holder had wanted to. He went alone, at first light, in his plain coat, and came back to the lodging an hour after the bell and sat down at the table in the kitchen where the woman of the house had put out bread, and ate it, all of it, slowly, without saying anything. Then he said that it had been quick, and that there had been more people in the yard than there had been in the hall, and that Sethe had looked at the crowd the whole way up the steps as if he were looking for somebody in it, and had not found them.

That was all he said about it. Emeric did not ask him for more.

Ferra had not gone to bed. She was at the window of her room when Ward came back, with the barony's book open on the sill and the pen in her hand, and Emeric saw from the passage that she had written the date at the head of a fresh page and nothing under it.

She did not write anything under it until noon. Then it was one line, and he did not read it, and she closed the book.



The forfeits took nine days.

They took nine days because they were three, and each of them was a different kind of thing, and the chancery had a different room for each kind and a different clerk in each room who did not care for the clerks in the other two. Emeric spent the nine days going from room to room with Ferra and the boxes, and sitting on benches, and being asked the same questions in three different ways by three different men, and answering them. It was the most tedious week of his life. He found that he did not mind it at all.

Wayford went by judgment.

That was the simplest. A holder convicted of treason forfeited his holding to the crown on the day of his death, by the oldest law in the book, and the crown could do with it what it liked. The clerk of the judgment room, a fat cheerful man with ink on his chin, read the judgment, and the sentence, and the date of the hanging, and wrote three lines in a book and sealed them, and Wayford was the crown's.

Grayrock went by attainder.

That was harder, and took four days, and Emeric watched Ferra follow it line by line with her lips pressed together. Renner of Grayrock had died at Kaldo's South Gate in a private war he had started, which forfeited his holding, by the same law. But Grayrock had not been forfeited, in the two years since, because his widow had held it in trust for their daughter under a writ from this same chancery, and the writ was good, and nobody had challenged it. Now the attainder room struck Renner's name. It struck his line: not only Renner, but everything that came from him, as if it had never been, back to the day he took the field. The trust was in his daughter's name, and the daughter was his line, and so the trust was void. Bryda was not attainted. She had not taken the field. She was named, in the attainder room's clerk's careful hand, as resident in Grayrock in the crown's interest, which meant she could stay in her own house and nobody could put her out of it, and which meant nothing else.

"She'll hate that," Ferra said, on the bench outside the attainder room, very quietly.

"Yes."

"She held it two years. She held it well. She gave us harder terms than anybody's ever given us and kept every one." Ferra looked at the closed door. "Resident in the crown's interest. Like a tenant."

"She'll hold it as long as I hold it," said Emeric. "She'll run it. That won't change."

Ferra looked at him.

"You haven't got it yet," she said.

Milford went by presence.

That was the strangest, and the one Emeric had feared, because it was the one he had made. Paxton had taken the field in the war, at the back, in his colours, and the law did not care where on the field. He had stood his men off by understanding with Kaldo, and Kaldo had written to the crown to say so, and the crown had read it at last in July, out of the nine sacks, with the hanging judge's own reading of it in the hall to go with it. And the presence room, which dealt with men who had been present at things they should not have been, sat on it for two days and then ruled that Milford was forfeit, as the law required, because Paxton had been present; and that Paxton was not attainted, and kept his life, and his name, and the lands that had been his own before he was ever Milford's holder; and that he would be steward of Milford under whoever held it next, at a steward's wage, for as long as he cared to be, because the crown did not see the point of putting out of a barony the one man in it who knew where everything was.

"That's fair," said Ferra, reading it.

"It's the law and a kindness," said the clerk of the presence room, who was a tall thin grey woman, the only woman clerk Emeric had seen in the chancery. "We don't often get to do both in the same instrument. I enjoyed it."



Then the three instruments went up together to a fourth room, on the top floor, where a man in black sat at a table under a window with nothing on it but a pen, and decided who should have them.

Keller was there. He did not sit at the table. He sat on a chair by the wall with his hands folded, as a man sits who has been asked for an opinion and has given it and is now only waiting to see whether it is taken. The man in black did not introduce himself. He did not need to. He was the chancery, or the part of it that mattered, and he spoke to Emeric across the table for a quarter of an hour in a voice so dry and so reasonable that Emeric found it hard, afterward, to remember a single sentence of it whole.

The gist of it was this.

The crown now held three baronies in the south that it had not held in August. It did not want them. A barony held by the crown was a barony the crown had to send a steward to, and pay, and protect, and collect from, and the south was a long way off and full of people who had just watched a baron hang. The crown could grant them to new men. But new men in the south would need to be armed and paid and set up, and would spend ten years learning where everything was, and the crown had tried that in the south before. The crown could grant them to the old houses that already sat round the edges of the south, to men of this faction or that faction at court. But three baronies on the southern frontier in the hands of three faction men was a thing the crown had also tried before, and the crown was still paying for it.

Or.

"Or," said the man in black, and looked at Keller, by the wall, and then back at Emeric. "The cheapest place to put a thing is where it already is." He laid his hand on the three instruments. "Grayrock's ore goes to Kaldo already, under a contract the barony of Kaldo drew. Milford's steward warned Kaldo in a war and stood his levy off for Kaldo's sake, and has said so under oath. Wayford's post was robbed for four years for Kaldo's sake, and Kaldo proved it, and the man who robbed it tried to murder Kaldo's holder in the crown's own city. The magistrate who heard the matter recommends." He did not look at Keller again. "The crown has read four years of the barony of Kaldo's reports, in nine sacks, in a week. It has some idea now of what the barony has done with a plague town and a debt." He lifted his hand from the instruments. "The crown grants the three to the holder of Kaldo, to hold as Kaldo is held, under the same terms. Wayford by judgment. Grayrock by attainder. Milford by presence."

He signed. Three times. And sealed, three times, in red.

Emeric looked at the seals.

He looked at them for his father.

He had been looking for his father in every room of the chancery for nine days. In the judgment room, and the attainder room, and the presence room; in every clerk's face and every clerk's hand; in the turn of a sentence, the choice of a word, the speed of a seal. A king who had sent his son south with fifty Gold Rand and a joke and a plan for the ninth night that was not his, and who had said, at the end of the banquet, It is done, in a voice that could have meant two things. Emeric had wanted, he found, sitting on the benches, for one of these instruments to have his father's hand somewhere in it. A word in the chancery man's ear. A nudge. Anything.

There was nothing. He looked as hard as he knew how, and there was not a trace. Three baronies had come to Kaldo through four rooms of tired clerks and a magistrate who did not care for persons and a dry man in black who liked things where they already were, by law, in order, at the speed of the chancery's paper, and if Harrold had touched any of it, he had touched it so that nobody could ever see.

Which was, Emeric thought, either the answer, or exactly what his father would have wanted him to think.



Keller stood up from his chair by the wall as they gathered the instruments.

He did not say anything to Emeric. He looked at Ferra.

"Chancellor," he said.

"Magistrate."

"The instruments name the holder." Keller nodded at the three sealed sheets in Emeric's hands. "They do not name the officer of the barony who will receive the crown's correspondence touching them. The chancery requires a name for its book." He looked at her. "Not an office. A name. In May I recorded you by your office, because you gave me only your office, and it served. It will not serve the chancery." He was quiet a moment. "You have been in my room twice and in my hall once. I have read every line of a book you kept. I find I would like to know who kept it."

Ferra stood very still.

Emeric knew what it was. She had told him in the first summer, on her own terms, in her father's yard, before she took the books: paid properly, not a servant, and her name kept out of the castle's records. She had kept it out of them for four years. Wexler's books had never had it. The barony's had it only as a hand. On the door of her room in the keep it was one word in Hale's black paint, and no more.

"Ferra," she said.

The man in black, at his table, had picked up his pen again.

"Of?" he said.

Ferra looked at the pen. Then, very briefly, at Emeric.

"Daughter of Bram," she said. "Smith. Of Kaldo." She lifted her chin. "Chancellor of the barony of Kaldo, and of Wayford, and of Grayrock, and of Milford."

The man in black wrote it. Emeric watched the pen go across the page in the chancery's book, in the chancery's black ink, the first time in the history of the kingdom that her name had been written in a book of the crown's: Ferra, daughter of Bram, smith, of Kaldo. And her office after it, all four baronies' worth, which she had added herself, without asking him, because it was true now and somebody had to say so.

Keller nodded once, as if a figure had come out where he expected, and went out.

Ferra did not say anything on the stair. Halfway down she stopped, on the landing, by a window that looked out on the chancery's courtyard, and stood with the boxes' keys on their string in one hand, and he stopped beside her.

"I said four," she said, to the window.

"You did."

"I didn't ask you."

"You didn't have to." He looked out at the courtyard. "Somebody had to say it into a book. It might as well have been the person who'll have to keep all four."

Ferra did not answer. She stood at the window with the keys in her hand. And after a while, without looking at him, she reached out with her other hand and took hold of his sleeve, at the wrist, where the knife had gone along it in the passage and Ward's binding still was under the clerk's shirt, and held it, not hard, the way a woman holds a rail on a stair she does not quite trust, and stood there, until somebody came up the stair below them and she let go.



The man at the window across the courtyard was standing quite still.

Emeric had seen him as he turned from Ferra. A window on the other side of the courtyard, on the same floor, one of the attainder room's: a man standing in it, alone, in a coat of dark grey with a fur collar too warm for August, old, straight-backed, with white hair cut short. He was not doing anything. He was standing at the window looking down into the courtyard, as anybody might, waiting for a clerk.

And he was looking at Emeric.

Emeric knew him after a moment. Old blood, from a house said to be as old as the king's. A set of carved wooden soldiers, at the banquet, held out with no cost in it and no craft either. Aldane.

Aldane stood at the window across the courtyard of the chancery's attainder room, on the day Grayrock was attainted, and looked at him. He stood still longer than a man stands still by accident. Then he inclined his head. Not much. A nod. The nod of a man across a room at a dinner to another man he has done business with, or means to, or wishes to be seen to know.

Then he turned from the window and was gone.

Emeric stood on the landing with the three instruments in his hand. He did not know what it meant. He could not think of anything it could mean. Aldane had given him toys and gone back to his place the long way. Aldane had nothing to do with Grayrock, or with any of the south, that he had ever heard of.

He put it away, and did not forget it.



Ward went to the palace on the ninth, alone, while Emeric and Ferra were in the presence room.

He had told Emeric he would. He had told him on the road north, on the third night, with the boxes under the bed: that there was a thing he meant to ask the king for while they were in the city, and that he would go alone, because the holder of Kaldo was not to go anywhere in this city but the four places, and the palace was the fifth, and the worst. Emeric had asked him what thing. Ward had said, what your mother left. And then he had said nothing more, and Emeric had lain awake a long time.

He came back at dusk. He came up the stair of the lodging with a small box under his arm, plain dark wood, the size of a book, and put it on the table in Emeric's room, and stood back.

Emeric opened it.

Two stones. On a bed of old faded blue velvet with the shape of other stones still pressed into it, more than two, where they had lain beside these for twenty years and been taken away. Grey, dull, the size of a pigeon's egg. Hollow Crystals, with the same fine lattice over every face as Corbin's nine; but Corbin's had come sharp out of a case, and these had gone soft at the edges, the way a stone goes that somebody has handled for years. Like the one in his own pocket. Like his mother's.

He did not touch them.

"The king gave them," Ward said, "gladly. He said so. He said it twice." Ward stood with his hands behind his back. "He said the court has pressed him for years to be rid of them, and of everything of hers. He said the queen's house has pressed him hardest. He said he was glad they would go home to one of her sons." He paused. "There are more. In the treasury, under the queen's eye. He said these two for now. He said the rest would be harder."

Emeric looked at the two stones in their faded velvet, and at the shapes in the velvet beside them where the others had been.

Emeric did not ask the question that came into his mouth. He would ask it. Not tonight.

He closed the box. He took one of the stones out first and held it in his palm, briefly, and felt nothing from it, nothing at all, a dull cool weight; and then, at the very edge of his sense, where he kept it open all day now, thin, so thin he forgot it was there, something very faint. Not a note. The shape where a note had been. Like a room somebody has just walked out of, still warm.

He put it back in the velvet beside the other, and closed the lid, and kept the box inside his coat, against his chest, on the road all the way home.
