Chapter 123 - Grain

He made the offer that afternoon, in a room off the hall with a long table in it and the windows open on the mountain, and he made it well.

Rowena sat at the head of the table, in the blue coat again, with the grey heavy-shouldered woman who kept the harbour on her left and a spare, careful man on her right who was introduced as her Treasurer and who had plainly come down from the upper town on purpose. Emeric sat across from her with Ferra at his side and Saban at the end with his figures, and Vantic at the far end of the table with her book, taking nothing down.

It began with grain, because the draft in Ferra's strongbox began with grain.

He laid it out the way he laid out anything he had worked on for a month. Kaldo's harvests for five years, rising, and the reasons they were rising, which were drainage and the rotation and the mills. What Kaldo ate and what it did not. The surplus in tons, this year and what it would be next. What it cost to put a ton aboard a coaster at the lower water, and what the coasting men charged by the ton to bring it here, which Saban had from the coasting men themselves. And what, at those rates, a ton of Kaldo's grain would cost on Emberfell's quay, set beside what Emberfell's quay was paying now, which Saban had from the fish market and the chandlers. The sum came out in her favour. He had made sure it came out in her favour, fairly, by a margin that left Kaldo something and her more. It was a good offer. He had put a great deal of care into it.

Rowena listened to all of it without interrupting. She let him finish. When he had finished she looked at Saban's figures on the sheet in front of her for a moment, politely, the way a woman looks at a gift she already owns.

"I have a coast," she said.

That was all. It was one sentence, and it was the whole of the answer, and he understood it before she had finished saying it, and felt his face go hot in a way it had not gone hot in ten years.

She had a coast. Grain came to a coast on any ship from anywhere. It came from the south and from over the water and from Randillia's own granaries up the other end of the inlet, and it had been coming for a hundred years, and if Kaldo's was a little cheaper this year somebody else's would be a little cheaper next year, and it did not matter to her whose it was. Grain was grain. She could buy it off any boat that tied up at her quay. There was nothing in his offer that she could not have had tomorrow from somebody else, and nothing in it that she needed from him.

He had read the system. Emberfell's quay, what it paid, what a ton cost to land. He had read it very carefully and he had read it correctly. He had not read the person at the head of the table at all.

"Thank you," Rowena said, to Saban, and pushed the sheet back across the table to him. Then she stood, and the harbour-mistress stood, and the Treasurer stood, and the meeting was over.

Ferra did not say anything on the way back to the harbour house.

She walked beside him down the slope through the hot streets, with her book under her arm and her collar of small lights at her throat, and she did not say *I told you*, and she did not say anything like it. She did not need to. It was in the minute book in her strongbox, under the promise and the coal, in her own hand, dated two days before: *Offer what she lacks, not what we have too much of. We do not know what she lacks.* And his answer beside it, in her hand, in his words.

She had been right, and she had been right in writing, and she had let him go ahead anyway because he had the right to go ahead, and he had gone ahead and been wrong in front of a foreign table.

She did not get softer about it. She got harder. By the time they reached the harbour house there was nothing in her face at all, and when Saban asked her a question on the stair about the coasting rates she answered it in four words, correctly, and went up to her room, and shut the door.

Imani had watched her go up.

"She's angry with you," she said.

"She's right."

"That's why," said Imani, and went to find something to eat.

Saban went out after dark.

He did not say where. He did not need to; it was his work, and it was the work he was best at, and it was the work Emeric should have sent him to do a month ago, before anybody drew up any terms. He put on the plainest coat he had brought and took the chain of beads off his neck and left it on the table in the long room, and went down to the quay.

He came back after midnight smelling of the quay taverns, which smelled of sulphur and fish and spilled ale in about equal parts, and found Emeric still up in the long room with the lamp, as he had expected to, and sat down across from him, and poured himself water.

"I had it wrong," Saban said.

He said it first. He said it the way he had said it once before, in the room above Kaldo, when a building he had placed with great confidence turned out to have been pulled down six years since.

"The brief I gave you," he said. "The coasting men's. What they say about this place in the fish market at the lower water. It was all true. It was years old, all of it, and it was the version men tell about a place they sail into and out of and don't live in." He drank some of the water. "I've spent tonight with people who live in it."

He told it in order, the way Saban told everything he had gone and fetched.

Her brother had been meant to have the seat. The old duke had said so, and it was known. She had gone to the capital as a treaty and married the King, and her brother had held Emberfell, and been content with it, for years.

Then she came home. And her people loved her.

"That's the part they tell first," Saban said. "Down there. Before anything else. They loved her. She'd gone away young and come back with a war behind her, a real one, decorated, the kind the old men still talk about. And they loved her, and he knew it, and he couldn't bear it. He couldn't send her away and he couldn't have her made to vanish. Not with the whole town watching which way she went." He turned his cup. "So it had to be public. The old law. In the arena, in front of everybody, the land to the strongest. It was the only way he could be rid of her that they'd take."

"She told me," Emeric said. "Last night."

"She told you about the scar." Saban looked at him. "Did she tell you she never wanted the seat?"

Emeric said nothing.

"She beat him," said Saban, "and she let him up, and she gave it back to him. The whole of it. In front of the whole town. She told him it was his and she'd be gone in a month, because she didn't want it. She never had. Every man I drank with tonight told me that part before he told me the knife." He was quiet for a moment. "So she's sitting in a chair she never wanted, because the man who wanted it stuck a knife in her back after she'd handed it to him. And there was nobody else."

Emeric sat very still.

"And then she wrote a law," said Saban.

He had it close to word for word. He had made a man in a tavern say it three times, he said, because the man was a clerk from the harbour office and knew it by heart and was proud of it.

The sitting ruler of Emberfell named her successor in writing, at any time, and could name anyone at all. Blood was neither needed nor preferred. The naming took effect when it was confirmed by the standing table, which sat by office and not by birth: the head of the great works, the harbour, the knight-captains, the Treasurer, whoever held those offices at the time. That was the first part.

The second part was shorter.

"Arms are disqualification," Saban said. "Raise a weapon over the seat, or have one raised for you, and you and your line are out. For good. No duel gives a title. No victory's a claim." He put the cup down. "She wrote that the year after. The clerk said she wrote it herself, at her own table, in a night."

Emeric looked at the lamp.

He saw the table at dinner. The grey woman with the harbour's badge. The thin man who ran the works under the mountain. The knight-captain. The Treasurer, that afternoon, who had come down from the upper town on purpose. He had sat at dinner with the table that would one day choose who came after her, and talked to it about the weather, and not known what it was.

And he saw her brother. Under the law she had written, her brother would have lost the seat the moment the knife came out of his boot, and kept his life. It was his epitaph. She had written it so that nobody would ever again stand in an arena thinking a sword was an argument.

"Has it ever been used?" he said.

"No," said Saban. "Not once. She's never named anybody. There's never been a seat to take." He stood up, slowly, the way a man stands at the end of a long night. "It's a piece of paper, Your Highness. It's one woman's piece of paper, in a land that's settled every question it ever had with a blade. And the only reason anybody in this town respects it is that she's the one standing behind it." He went to the door. "I'd have had all this for you a month ago, if you'd sent me."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Saban, and went to bed.

He did not sleep.

He lay in the small room at the top of the harbour house with the window open and the mountain glowing red at its top in the dark, and the law went round in his head.

It was the same blindness twice. That was what kept him awake.

At sixteen, in the capital, he had heard that Emberfell's heir was dead and a queen of Randillia was sitting a foreign seat. The court had turned it over for a week and dropped it, and he had filed it with the shape and not the middle, and never once gone back for it. That was the first time, and he had been sixteen, and he had had a great deal to hide and very little room to wonder about anybody else's troubles.

The second time was last night. He had been a grown man, sitting across a table from her, and she had told him the story herself, flatly, in order, because he could not stop looking at her face. And he had heard it as a story about a scar. He had thought about mana, and the tracery, and the rate a body builds at, and how he might fix it. He had thought about his own brother. He had not once thought to ask what the seat had cost her, or what she was doing to keep it, or what she would want from anybody who came to her table offering things.

And then he had gone to her table in the afternoon and offered her grain.

He lay and looked at the red at the top of the mountain.

A woman in a chair she had never wanted. A law with nothing behind it but her. A table by office that could be bought. A ruler who had never named anybody, and who could die tomorrow on her own yard with nobody named, and leave a vacancy with no tiebreak and a town that had settled every question it ever had with a blade.

He knew what that was. He had spent ten years building its opposite: offices that did not need him, a table that sat by office, a Chancellor with a written stay he could not overrule, books in a form anybody could read, standards struck into rods so that an inch was an inch whoever measured it. Every one of those things he had built so that Kaldo would not stop the day he did. He had built it without once putting a name to what it was for.

She had a coast. She had sulphur and a mountain and a hundred years of grain on any boat. What she did not have was anything that would still be standing the day after she wasn't.

He lay awake with the law in his head until the red at the top of the mountain went grey, and then pale. One woman's piece of paper, and never once tested.
