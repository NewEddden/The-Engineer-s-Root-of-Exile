Chapter 301 - Grier

The K Discovery went out from the secret dock at a quarter to nine on the night of the twenty-seventh of May, with her lamps dark and her irises shut, and nobody on the quay to see her off but Ned Tallis, who had come down to say that he was not coming and stayed to watch her go.

Fletcher had the bow helm. Ward had the top one. Linus Vance sat at the chart table under the shaded lamp with his own maps pinned flat at the corners by four of Ferra's brass weights, because the maps were his and he would not have them rolled; and in the saloon aft, on the bench under the forward iris, sat the five black suits that had come out of bay three in March in a crate marked SAILCLOTH, and four of the people who would wear them, and Saban, who would not.

Emeric sat on the floor with his back to the bulkhead and the fifth suit across his knees.

It did not look like much, off a body. A close black under-suit, heavy for its size, with the iron mesh woven through it so fine that you could only feel it, not see it: a grain under the fingers like the back of a file. Plate at the knees and shins, matte, black, no more than a hand's breadth anywhere. The hood. The narrow-visored helm under it, with a pane of violet glass no wider than two fingers laid together. Ports at the collar and the chest and the hips and the knees for the held stones and the glow, each with its little shutter of blackened steel closed over it and pinned. A hip rack with six throwing daggers in it. The baton, in its loop on the right thigh, with its battery under a flap. A slim chute pack between the shoulders. And the boots and the gloves, which were the thing that had taken him most of April: soft soles of Isle rubber over a felt, and in the ball of each foot and the heel of each palm, sunk flush in a ring of brass, a small grey stone.

Bren had his gloves off and was turning one of them over in his hands and frowning at the palm stone, the way he had frowned at it every night for a week on the keep's underside.

"Stop looking at it," said Mira. "It doesn't get any better looked at."

"I'm not looking at it. I'm thinking about it."

"You're thinking about the ceiling."

"That ceiling," said Bren, "was already cracked."

"It was not already cracked," said Arthur, from the end of the bench, without opening his eyes.

Saban, in a dark coat and dark breeches with a wool cap over his ears, had a box on his knees the size of a bread crock: oak, brass-cornered, with a canvas strap and a coil of fine wire on a reel and an earpiece on a cord, Tess's last set before she went into the dome, built light enough for one man to carry a mile. He had been practising with it for a fortnight on the castle's roof, saying nothing that mattered to Linus at the other end in Ferra's cipher, and he had got good enough at it to be bored, which Ferra said was the only safe way to be with a thing like that.

"Four hours," said Linus, from the chart table. "Near enough. Less if the westerly's kind past Wayford, which it won't be."

"It's never kind past Wayford," said Fletcher, down the speaking tube.



They had argued about it at the long table for three nights, and the argument had been Ward's, and Ward had lost it.

Not because anybody had shouted him down. Nobody shouted at Ward. It was that he had been arguing against a thing he agreed with, and everybody at the table could hear it.

Grier had to be read. Keller could lay the vote beside the debts as fairly as a sum, and Vantic could give them the road the money had come by, through her brother's book; but a book could not go to a court, by her price, and a sum was not a man. Somebody had carried Grier the word that his debts were gone, and what he was to do in return. Grier knew who. Or Grier had seen who, which was not the same thing, and only a delver could find the difference.

And a delver had to be in the room. Not on a hill, not at the end of a wire. In the room, with two fingers on the man's wrist and the man's yes in the air between them.

"Then I'll bring him out," Ward had said, on the second night. "To you. To a field. I'll carry him out of his own house in a blanket and put him down in front of you."

"A frightened man in a blanket in a field won't say yes to anything," said Emeric. "And he'd be right not to."

"Then he comes out on his own feet. We ask him."

"How? A letter? A man at his door?" Saban had turned his hat round on the table. "His house has been watched since the tribunal. I know because one of the men watching it is mine, and he says the other four aren't."

So it was the house. And the house had four men on its walls at night, and a watch stone at its gate, and a lord in it who had sold the king for the price of his saddler's bill and had not been sober, by Saban's man, more than three nights together since.

"You'd go in," Ward said on the third night. He said it flatly, as he had said the king's line at this table in December, an order being passed down and not liked. "Over the wall. Into the house."

"Last. After them." Emeric nodded at the three, who sat along the table's foot and had not been asked anything and had heard all of it. "Hood up. I'll not cast a thing until I'm in his room with the hood down. Under the mesh I'm a cat in the dark to anybody feeling for me; Armand tried it and couldn't find me across the yard." He looked at Ward. "You can't come over the wall. You can't do it quiet, and you know you can't."

Ward had looked at him a long time.

He had tried the boots once. Emeric knew it and had never said so. On the fourth night of the training, at two in the morning, when the three had gone down and the inner ward was dark, a slow even note like a wall had gone out alone onto the keep's north face in a pair of grip boots and got four steps up the brick before something in it had gone heavy all at once, the way a knight's body went heavy when it did not trust a thing, and come down onto the flags on its feet with a sound like a dropped sack. And stood there a while. And gone in.

"The treeline," said Ward at last. "Saban and I hold the treeline. The ship holds high. If the house wakes, I come in, and I'll not be quiet about it."

"Yes."

"I hate it."

"I know."

Ward had put his hands flat on the oak. "Then we'll do it properly," he said, and that had been the end of the argument.



The westerly was not kind past Wayford. Fletcher said so twice, down the tube, with a satisfaction he did not trouble to hide.

At half past twelve they were over the dark country south of the capital, with the city's glow on the cloud ahead and to the left like a fire banked for the night, and Linus came aft with a chart and put it on the saloon bench between the three and Emeric, under the lamp's slot.

He had flown this country for the survey in the spring, in daylight, high, under the pretence of looking at roads, and he had drawn it the way he drew everything, as if somebody's life might one day hang on the width of a lane. It might. The house stood on a low rise two miles off the capital's south road, at the end of an avenue of limes: an old square house of two floors and attics, with a walled forecourt in front and a stable court behind, a gatehouse in the forecourt wall with a passage through it, a terrace and a balcony on the garden side, and the garden going down past a fishpond to a wood. Linus had marked the wood in green and the house in black and the wall in a broken red line, and at the gate passage he had drawn a small circle and written beside it, in his small slanting hand, stone, per S.

"Per Saban," said Saban. "My man's cousin delivers his coal. The watch stone's in the gate arch, on the inside, at a man's chest. It rings a bell in the porter's lodge. The cousin's never set it off with a sack on his back, and the porter swears at the cats every night of the week."

"Set at the human line," said Emeric.

"Whatever that means."

"It means it's been told to report anything with a note bigger than a dog's." He looked at the three. "Under the mesh, you haven't got one."

"We hope," said Bren.

"We know. Armand stood with his back to the yard door at the base for an hour last Thursday with his eyes shut and called out every time he felt one of you come through it. He called out four times out of thirty, and three of those were Imani."

"And the fourth?"

"Was you, when you sneezed."

Mira laughed, once, through her nose. Arthur was looking at the chart.

"Four on the walls," Arthur said. "Two on the forecourt, one at the gate, one in the stable court. And the porter."

"The porter sleeps," said Saban. "He's sixty. He sleeps in his chair with his boots on the fender from one till the change at five, and the bell's the only thing that wakes him."

"Then nobody rings it," said Arthur.



Fletcher brought her over the wood at three thousand feet a little before one, and held.

The air stone went quiet. The propeller ran down behind them to a slow tick and stopped, and the ship hung on her matrix in the dark with no more sound in her than a house makes at night: the pump breathing, the trim keys clicking under Fletcher's hand at the bow, somebody's boot on the stair. That was silent running. The stones that held her up pushed nothing but her own weight, and pushed it without moving any air at all; from the ground she was a patch of sky without stars in it, and nobody looked up at the sky for a patch without stars.

They suited in the saloon by the lamp's slot, in the order they had suited every night for a week, without talking. Under-suit, which went on like a second skin and pulled at the hair on Emeric's forearms. Shin plates. Boots, laced, the sole stones grey and dead. Gloves. The helm, which he set down over his locs, gathered at the back and bound flat by Imani at noon with a strip of cloth and a look; and the hood over the helm, drawn close at the throat.

And it was like stepping into a house with thick walls.

He had known it would be. He had worn the mesh on the castle roof every night for a week and on the keep's underside and in the mill shed's rafters, and every night it had been the same, and he had not got used to it. His sense did not stop. It never stopped; it had been up since he was twenty-three, as a man's hearing is up, so that nobody in Kaldo had come up behind him by ordinary means in ten years. Under the hood it went on, and came back to him through the iron dull and close and far off, like voices through a wool blanket. He could feel the four beside him in the saloon, faint. He could feel Fletcher at the bow helm and Linus at the table and Saban by the bulkhead, a little clearer, because they were not wearing iron. Past the hull, the night, and the wood, and the house: nothing much. A dim warm field where the wood was. A few small sparks that were sheep. That was all.

He would be going in half deaf. He had chosen it.

Ward, beside him, had stopped with his helm in his hands.

"You've gone," Ward said.

"I'm here."

"I know you're here. I can see you." Ward looked at him through the slot of the pane, and Emeric could not see his face, only the shape of the helm turning. "I've felt you since you were twenty-two. You've gone very small."

"That's the point."

"I know that's the point." Ward put his own helm on, and drew his own hood, and stood a moment quite still; and Emeric felt his slow even note go dim through the iron, the way a lamp dims when somebody puts a hand round it. "I don't like it," said Ward's voice, muffled. "That's all. I'm allowed not to like it."



The floor hatch came up on the night.

Three thousand feet. The cold came in and stood in the saloon with them. Below the hatch's lip there was nothing for a long way, and then the wood, a blacker black, and beyond it the faint grey bar of the lime avenue going up to a square of darker dark with one yellow light in it, low on the ground floor, on the garden side. Further off the capital's glow lay on the cloud, and the south road was a pale scratch with nothing on it.

Arthur went first, because he always did.

He sat on the hatch's lip with his legs over the dark and his hand on the coaming. He did not look back. He put two fingers to the hip port and turned the first shutter's pin, the lower one, so that the stone under it woke and the shutter stayed closed over its light; and then the other hip, and the collar; and Emeric, standing over him, felt nothing at all, because the stones were outside the mesh but they were small and cold and a long way under a lot of iron, and his own sense was in a blanket.

Arthur pushed himself off the lip.

He did not fall. That was the thing that still put the hair up on the back of Emeric's neck, after the castle roof and the base and a week of nights. A man going through a hatch three thousand feet up ought to drop like a stone down a well, as Ward had dropped off the Hod's rail above the Isle; and Arthur went out of the hatch and down below it slowly and steadily, upright, with his arms loose at his sides and his cape gone, nothing but the black suit, as if somebody were lowering him on a rope that nobody could see. No roar. No hiss of air plates opening. No wind at all, except a faint stir of the cold night going past the hood. He went down into the dark, getting smaller, and the dark took him.

Mira went. Then Bren, who sat on the lip a breath longer than either of them and then pushed off with his right arm a little across his body, over his left side, which he did not know he did and which nobody had ever told him.

Emeric went down the ladder's top three rungs and leaned out and watched them go: three small dark things against a darker wood, sinking, and only knew where they were because he knew where to look.

The suits made no wind because the stones in them pushed nothing but the plate. An air stone threw air; that was all an air stone was, a thing that threw air one way so that it, and whatever it was fixed to, went the other. The ship's matrix did not throw air, but a ship's matrix low over snow could still raise the snow, because a hull the size of a barn going down through still air shoves the air aside like a barge shoves water. A suit was the size of a man. A held stone in its housing pushed on the plate it was set in, and on nothing else in the world, and the plate carried the man. So there was nothing to hear but the body itself going through the air, and that was the faint stir he had heard as Arthur went, a sound like a sleeve brushing a doorframe. Come down fast and it was a wind. Come down slow and it was nothing.

So they came down slow. Ward's rule, every night of the training: slow, slow, slower than you think, there is nobody down there in a hurry but you.

"Your turn," said Ward, behind him.

"After you."

"I'm not going last." Ward's helm turned to Saban, who stood by the bulkhead with the box on its strap over his shoulder and his face very still, the way a merchant's face is still at a price he has decided to pay. "He goes down on the line, when Fletcher takes her to the wood's edge. You go now. I come behind you. I'm not having you in the dark under me where I can't see you."

Emeric went.



The air came up round him and was cold and quiet.

He had flown bare over the Mere and the falls and the Band's foot, with nothing round him but his shirt and his own mana; and he had fallen in plate above the Isle with the air roaring in his teeth. This was neither. The suit's stones held him, and he held nothing. He could not have cast if he had wanted to; the mesh stood between his hands and the night like a wall between a man and a field, and anything he tried to put through it would have come back on him or gone nowhere. He was a passenger in his own clothes. He hung under the ship and let the stones put him down through the dark at the pace he had told them, a man's height a breath, and watched the wood come up.

Above him, a little to his left, Ward came down. Emeric heard nothing.

That was what Ward said afterward, and he said it twice, at the treeline and in the ship going home, as if it were the strangest thing that had happened to him all night. Not that the others were quiet. That he was. He had come down out of the sky in the dark over a lord's house and listened, all the way down, with everything he had, for the sound a man in plate makes; and there had not been one. Not a creak of leather, not the chink of a buckle, not the wind on his own helm. He had heard his own breath inside the hood. Nothing else. A knight of the rite who had been listening for his own approach for thirty years, so as to make it less, and had never once not heard it.

"I thought something had gone wrong with my ears," he said. "I thought, I'm old."

The wood came up. The top of it was a rough dark floor of leaves, and then it was not a floor but separate trees, oak and some ash, and the stones put him down between two of them through a gap Arthur had found and marked for the rest with a strip of pale cloth tied to a twig at the height of a man's hand. He came down past the cloth. His feet touched leaf-mould and he let his knees go and the stones let go of him at the same breath, and he went down onto the wood floor the way a cloak goes when a man takes it off and drops it: soft, all at once, nothing in it.

The three were there, crouched among the hazel, black on black. Mira had her helm turned toward the house. Arthur lifted one hand, the fingers flat, and lowered it: down, and wait.

Ward came down beside him a breath later in the same way, a big man landing like laundry, and crouched, and was still.



Saban came down on the line ten minutes after, when Fletcher had brought the ship down the sky to three hundred feet over the wood's far edge, where the trees were thickest and the house could not have seen her if it had been looking, and held her there on the matrix with the trim keys going like a clock.

He came down in the bosun's chair with the box on his lap and his eyes shut, and Ward took him under the arms at the bottom and set him on his feet, and he opened his eyes and said in a perfectly ordinary voice, "I'll not be doing that again," and then knelt in the leaves and unreeled his wire up the nearest ash with a weighted cord that Bren threw for him over a branch, and put the earpiece to his ear.

After a while he tapped on the key with one finger. Then he listened, and nodded, and wrote nothing down, because he had not brought a pencil on purpose.

"Linus has us," he said, very low. "Ship goes up again to three thousand. He'll listen every quarter hour on the quarter. Fletcher says if we're longer than two hours he'll come and look for us himself and he won't be quiet either."

The ship went up. Emeric did not feel her go. He saw a few stars come back over the wood's far edge, one by one, where she had been.



The treeline at the garden's foot was a hedge of holly with the wood at its back. From it the garden went up toward the house in the dark: a long lawn, the pond with a little light lying in it from nowhere, a gravel walk, the terrace, and the house itself, square and black against the city's glow, with its one yellow window on the ground floor at the garden end.

Emeric put his visor to a gap in the holly and looked.

The pane did what every Kaldo pane did: it took what little light there was and made more of it. The lawn came up grey. The pond came up pewter. The house came up in dim lines, the windows darker squares in a dark face, the balcony on the first floor over the terrace with its iron rail, the roof and its leads and two chimneys. And on the forecourt wall, round the far side, he could see a lamp moving, small, yellow, steady, at a walking man's height. That was one.

His sense told him almost nothing. There were people in the house; he could feel that much through the wool, a few faint warm places, close together on the attic floor, one by itself on the first floor, one on the ground floor at the garden end where the light was. Out on the walls the watchmen should have been bright, waking men with lamps in their hands and their notes up. They came to him through the iron like coals under ash. He could not have told them from the horses in the stable court.

He had known that too. It was a different thing to know it with the house in front of him.

Ward was crouched at his shoulder. He had his helm off, which he had not been told to do and had not asked about, and he had his own sense out, plain, a knight's, which felt nothing past a few paces but heard a great deal; and he was listening to the house with his whole face.

"Four," Ward said. "Saban's right. Forecourt, two, walking opposite ways round. One sitting, at the gate; he's been sitting since we got here, on something that creaks. The stable court's walking slow. Dog in the stable court. Old, by the bark it isn't making."

"Dog," said Bren, under his breath.

"Old," said Ward. "And on a chain. I can hear the chain."

Arthur looked at Emeric. Emeric nodded.

The three went out of the holly one after another and were gone across the lawn, low and quick, black on grey, not running. On foot nothing slowed them; there was no plate at the hips or the shoulders to bind, only the soft suit and the knees, and the knights' own legs came through it whole, so that they went up the long lawn faster than a man could have gone in his shirt, and quieter than a cat. Emeric watched them through the pane. A dark shape going round the pond's far side. A second along the gravel's edge where it was soft. A third that he lost altogether until it was at the terrace wall, and then was not there either, because it had gone up it.

"There," said Ward. He had not seen it. He had heard it not happen.



The rule for the boots was Mira's, in the end, not his.

He had built them. He had sat in the drawing office in April with a stone in a brass ring in one hand and a scrap of the mesh in the other and worked out why his own planted step could never go through the iron, and how a stone could do the same job from outside it: a gravity stone held still against the world could push any way it was told, because whatever held it still was its pointer, its down. A stone bolted to a wall was held by the wall. The held stones in the suit's ports read their still from the small steady turning in its brass cage behind the breast disc, the K Amara's trick made small. And a stone sunk in the sole of a boot, pressed flat against a wall by a man's weight, was held still by the wall it was pressed against, as surely as if it had been bolted there. It needed no steady. It needed the wall. Lift the foot, and it had nothing to be still against, and it went quiet.

So the suit's held stones made a man light, and the boot stone under his sole made the wall his floor, and the glove stones did the same for his hands; and the knight fed them all by touch, the way a knight fed every stone a mage had set for him, from inside, by wanting. No casting. Nothing through the mesh. All of it in the feet and the gut.

"Too little and you peel off," he had said on the first night on the castle's inner wall, "and too much and the plaster cracks."

Bren had cracked a good deal of plaster. Arthur had peeled off twice, on the second night, very slowly, like a leaf off a window, and come down on his feet each time looking thoughtful. And Mira had walked up the inner wall on the first night, and over the coping, and across the underside of the keep's arch like a girl crossing a kitchen, and come down the other side and stood on the flags, and said, "It's not the boots. You don't think about the boots. You think about the floor. Wherever your foot is, that's the floor, and you're just walking on it." And then, when they had all looked at her: "It's what Ward says about the horse. You don't ride the horse. You ride the road."

Ward had said nothing at all to that, for some time.

Emeric was, by any count anybody had kept, the worst of the four at it. He had found the planted step in his own body a year ago at his own window, and his body knew it: lighten, plant, the wall comes round. In the boots it was not his to do. The stone did the plant, and he had to want it to from inside, as a knight wanted, and every time his feet touched a wall something in him reached to do it the old way and met the iron and stopped, like a man going up a stair in the dark and finding one step fewer than he had counted. He had got good enough. He had not got good.

He went across the lawn after them when Arthur's hand came up white for a breath over the terrace wall, the glove's palm turned to the wood, and went down again.



The house's garden face was old brick with stone at the corners, and it went up two floors to a stone cornice and the eaves of the leads above, and under the eaves, where the roof's timbers came out over the wall, there was a deep dark shelf of shadow a yard wide where the brick met the boards. That was the road. Linus had seen it from the air in March and written beside it, eaves deep, dry, the only line on the chart with no question mark.

Emeric put his glove on the brick at the terrace's corner and wanted it, and the stone in the palm went still against the wall and the wall pulled at his hand. Then his foot. Then the other. The held stones at his collar and hips took the weight off him as the suit's stones did, and the world turned through a right angle under his boots, the wall coming round to be the floor, the lawn going up behind his back to be a wall, the sky out in front of his visor like a ceiling with stars in it.

He did not look at the lawn. He looked at the brick, the next brick, the next. He walked up the house.

It was not quiet in his head. It was quiet everywhere else. He could hear the soft press of the rubber soles on the brick, and his own breath in the hood, and very faintly, in the stable court round the house's far side, the old dog's chain moving on the cobbles. Two floors. A window went by under his feet, dark, with a curtain behind it, a bedroom; the warm faint place on the first floor by itself, asleep. The cornice. Over the cornice was the hard part, from the wall onto the underside of the eaves, from one floor to another at a right angle; and he did it as they had done it every night on the keep, hand first, flat to the boards above, wanting it, then the foot, then the other foot, the world turning through another right angle so that the eaves were his floor and he was standing on them upside down with the house's wall a yard off at his left, the gravel walk two floors below his head, and his cape, if he had worn one, hanging down past his helm toward the terrace.

He had not worn one. Nothing hung. Nothing on the suit could swing or flap or catch. Abloh had said, when Emeric showed him the drawing, that it was the first garment he had ever made with nothing in it to be beautiful, and that he found he did not mind.

Bren was ahead of him on the eaves, a black shape upside down in a black shelf, crouched, still, with one glove up flat: stop.

A lamp was coming round the corner of the house.



It came along the gravel walk at a walking pace, yellow, swinging a little, at the end of a man's arm: one of the forecourt men gone round the garden way, which Saban's cousin had said they did, once an hour, on the half hour, when the porter was asleep and there was nobody to be seen being dutiful for. A short heavy man in a frieze coat with a crossbow slung over his back on a strap and his hat pulled down, walking with his head down, as a man walks the same round for the fortieth time in a month.

He came along under the eaves.

Emeric hung on the boards two floors over him with his head down toward the gravel and watched the hat come. He could see the crown of it. He could see the cord of the crossbow's sling across the frieze, and the bow's steel limbs, and the winder at the butt; a wind crossbow, palace guard pattern or a copy of one, the kind that had put a bolt through Bren's old breastplate at fifty paces the September before as if it were a barrel lid. Under this suit there was nothing at all between that and his ribs but cloth and a grain of iron fine enough to feel through. The plate was at his knees. A bolt would come through the rest of him the way it would come through a shirt.

He did not move. He did not breathe much. He found, oddly, that he was not afraid, only very interested, as if he were watching the lamp come down a long corridor from the far end of it.

The lamp's light came up the wall under him in a yellow fan, slow, as the man walked, over the brick and the window and the cornice, and up under the eaves' edge, a hand's breadth short of the shelf, and stopped there, because a hand-lamp's light goes up a wall only as far as the man holding it lifts it, and this man was not lifting it. It lit the stone of the cornice, and the bottom of the boards. The shelf over it stayed dark. Emeric hung in the dark a yard above the edge of the light, upside down, and Bren hung beside him, and the man walked along under both of them with his head down and his lamp swinging, and they could have dropped a coin into his hat.

He stopped.

He stopped right under Bren. He put the lamp down on the gravel, and fished in his coat, and brought out a short clay pipe and a twist of something, and stood with his back against the house wall, under the window, under the cornice, under the eaves, and filled the pipe with his thumb.

Bren's helm turned, very slowly, and looked at Emeric.

Emeric did nothing at all.

The man lit the pipe from the lamp's chimney with a spill, bending to it, and the smell came up, a cheap dark leaf, rank, very strong; and stood and smoked it, with his eyes on the black lawn and the holly at its foot where Ward and Saban were, and his crossbow's limbs against the brick an arm's length below Bren's head.

It took him about as long as a pipe takes. Emeric counted it, because there was nothing else to do: three hundred and some breaths, slow ones. His feet began to ache in the boots from wanting. Halfway through it he felt the stone under his left palm begin to slip, as a stone slipped when you stopped minding it; a faint loosening, a feeling like a hand going numb on a rail; and he put his want back into it from inside, gently, so as not to put too much and crack the old boards over him, and it held.

Below, the man knocked his pipe out against the brick, twice, and a few sparks went down onto the gravel and died. He picked up his lamp. He walked on round the far corner of the house toward the stable court, and his light went round the corner with him, and was gone.

Bren let out a breath that Emeric heard through two hoods.

"I'm going to smell of that," Bren said, very low, "for a week."



The eaves went round the corner of the house to the forecourt side. From the corner, hanging, Emeric could see the forecourt under him: a square of gravel inside its wall, the gatehouse in the middle of the far wall with its arch and passage through it, the porter's lodge built against it inside with one small window lit red by a fire, and the lime avenue going away beyond the gate into the dark.

Two of the four were already down.

He had not seen it happen, and he had not heard it, and he had not felt it, which was the worst of the three. In the shadow at the foot of the forecourt wall on the left a man lay on his side on the gravel, quite still, with his own coat folded over his head and his wrists and ankles bound behind him with the black cord out of Arthur's pouch. His lamp stood beside him, turned down to a bead, upright, set there carefully, so that from the house anybody who looked out would have seen a lamp on the gravel at the foot of the wall where a man might have put it down to piss. And at the stable court's arch on the right, which Emeric could just see the inside of from the corner, a second shape lay in the same way, and the old dog lay with its chin on the man's boot, untroubled, and thumped its tail once on the cobbles as Emeric watched.

The baton dropped a man and did not kill him. It had been the first thing Emeric had made sure of, before the steel or the stones or the boots: a storage battery and a coil in a black tube, mundane all the way down, as Earth's had been, with the lightning stone only there to make it sharper. It was not mana. A knight's tracery did nothing to it. Touch a man with its two brass teeth and the shock went through him and his own body did the rest, every muscle he had going at once and then letting go, so that he went down where he stood like a coat off a peg and lay for a minute or two not knowing anything; and by the time he knew anything his coat was over his head and his wrists were tied and nobody had said a word near him. He would wake hurting in the arm where the teeth had gone in, and stiff, and sick a little, and alive. Imani had put it on herself, in the tank room, with Ferra holding the watch, and gone down on the mats and come up again in a minute and a half and said, "I'll write it down," and then, "I'd like that not to happen to me again," and written that down too.

The gate was Mira's.

The gate's man sat in the passage through the gatehouse, as Ward had heard, on a stool against the wall, with his lamp on a hook beside his head and his crossbow across his knees, where he could see down the avenue and up the forecourt both; and the passage was stone, vaulted, a cart's width, with an arch at each end and the watch stone set in the inner arch at a man's chest, a small grey thing in an iron cage, like a lamp stone nobody had lit.

From the corner of the eaves Emeric could see straight along the forecourt wall's top to the gatehouse, and the gatehouse's roof, and nothing of the passage under it but the two arches, lit yellow from inside by the man's lamp. And a small black shape going along the forecourt wall's top toward the gatehouse, low, fast, on all fours. And going over the gatehouse roof's edge, head first, and down.

He did not see what happened in the passage. He saw a little of it, through the inner arch, against the lamplight, and that was all anybody saw.

A shape on the vault's ceiling, in the dark above the lamp's hook, where no lamp ever throws its light because a lamp on a hook lights everything under it and nothing over: a shape upside down, crouched, its boots on the stone of the vault and its knees up, very still. Mira. She had come down the gatehouse's outer face and in through the outer arch over the top of its keystone and along the vault's curve like a fly along a ceiling, a yard over the man's hat, and he had not looked up, because nobody in the world looked up in a gate passage at night; there was nothing up there to look at but the stone.

Under her the man yawned and shifted the bow on his knees.

She let go with her feet.

Not all at once. The boots let go, and the gloves held, and she swung down out of the vault on her two hands from the stone like a girl swinging from a branch, upside down, until her helm was beside the man's ear and her body hung down the vault's curve behind her; and the baton was in her right hand, and the brass teeth touched the side of his neck under the hat's brim, lightly, the way you touch a sleeper to wake him.

There was a very small sound, a crack like a twig, and a blue spark the size of a seed.

The man went straight. Every part of him at once. The bow jumped on his knees and Mira's left hand came off the vault and caught it by the stock before it hit the flags, and the stool went back, and she caught that too, with her foot, because her left glove had gone back to the stone and held her; and the man slid down the wall into the corner of the passage like a sack sliding off a cart, and was still.

She came down off the ceiling onto the flags beside him, the right way up, and set the stool back where it had been, and hung the bow on the lamp's hook, and had his coat over his head before Emeric had breathed again.

The watch stone in its cage at the inner arch, at a man's chest, two feet from her, said nothing at all.

Something did, a moment later. A grey cat came out of the porter's lodge's cat door, small and lean, and stopped in the forecourt with one forefoot up and looked at the passage; and went under the inner arch past the watch stone, which rang nothing, because a cat was a cat; and went up to Mira where she knelt over the gate's man and smelled her knee, and then her glove, very thoroughly. Mira held quite still. The cat considered her and went off up the passage toward the avenue with its tail up, unimpressed, to do whatever cats did on lime avenues at night.

In the lodge the porter slept on, in his chair, with his boots on the fender.



Arthur was under the balcony.

He had taken the forecourt's second man himself, on the far side of the house, quick, and come back along the terrace wall to the garden side and gone up under the balcony on the first floor, and he had stayed there, folded into the dark angle where the balcony's stone floor met the house wall, upside down on its underside, with his helm toward the room beyond its windows. He had been there, by the count Emeric kept afterward, about twenty minutes. He had not moved.

The room behind the balcony was lit.

It was not Grier's. Grier's was on the ground floor, at the garden end, where the light had been since they came; Saban's cousin knew the house's insides from the coal cellar up, and Grier slept downstairs now, in his library, in a chair, when he slept, and had not been up his own stair since the vote. The lit room over the balcony was a woman's: a lamp on a table by the window with a white curtain half drawn, and a shadow on the curtain that moved now and then, and sat, and got up again, and walked. Grier's wife. Saban had her name, and had said it at the long table, and Emeric had not wanted to know it.

She was awake. She had been awake every night for a month, Saban's cousin said, walking the floor over her husband's head while he sat under it in the library with the lamp and did not drink, which was new, and did not sleep either. And her windows opened onto the balcony, and the balcony looked down on the terrace, and the library's French windows opened onto the terrace right under it.

So Arthur waited for her lamp.

Emeric came along the eaves to the corner over the balcony, and stopped, and hung, and waited too. There was nothing else to do. The shadow on the curtain walked, and sat, and walked. Once it came to the window and moved the curtain aside a hand's breadth with two fingers and stood looking out at the dark garden; and Emeric, two yards above and to the right of her head on the underside of her own eaves, looked down on the top of a grey head with the hair coming down out of its pins and a shawl over a nightgown, and the white of a woman's hand on the curtain's edge; and under her, under her own balcony's floor, folded into its dark like a bat in a church porch, Arthur, two feet beneath her slippers.

She let the curtain go.

A long while after, the lamp went down to a bead, and then out.

Arthur waited a hundred breaths more after that, slow ones, while the room behind the curtain went from a woman walking to a woman lying down to a woman lying still; and then came down off the balcony's underside onto its rail, and off the rail onto the house wall, and down the wall head first to the terrace, and stood on the flags by the library's windows, the right way up, and put one glove flat on the glass, lightly.

That was the sign. Emeric came down the wall.



The French windows were not locked. That had been the one thing nobody could know until they tried them, and Saban had said he would put money on it and not his name; and Arthur turned the handle with his glove over it so that the latch would not click, and it did not, and the window opened inward a hand's breadth on a room that smelled of old books and cold ashes and a lamp burning low.

Mira and Bren came along the terrace out of the dark and stood one each side of the windows with their backs to the wall, and their batons in their hands, and did not look in.

Emeric went in.

It was a long room with books on three walls, a good many of them, more than any man reads, bought by the yard by some grandfather, with the spines matched. A fire long out. A desk with nothing on it but a lamp turned low and a jug of water and a glass. And a chair by the cold hearth, a big old leather chair gone shiny at the arms, with a man in it.

He was awake. He was looking at the window.

He had heard nothing; Emeric would have staked his life on it, and had. He had felt the night air come in, because a long room in May with a cold hearth feels it when a window opens, and he had turned his head to it without getting up, slowly, and was looking at the black shape that stood just inside his window on his own carpet with its hood over its head and a narrow slot of violet glass where its face should be. And he did not shout. He did not reach for the bell pull, which was a long tasselled cord beside the hearth an arm's length from his hand. He looked at it, and Emeric saw his face do a thing faces did not often do: go from fright to something like relief, all at once, like a man who has been waiting a month for a knock at the door and at last hears it.

"Well," said Lord Grier. "There you are."

Emeric drew the hood back, and took the helm off, and held it under his arm.

It was like coming out of deep water.

The room came at him all at once through his sense: the warm close bulk of the man in the chair, and the woman upstairs asleep at last, and the servants in the attic in a row, and the four bound men out in the dark, sore and coming round, and the dog, and the cat on the lime avenue, and Ward at the holly a hundred and fifty yards off like a wall in a wood, and Saban beside him bright with nerves, and three thousand feet up, very far and faint, the ship. He had not known how much he had been leaning on it, all his life, until he had spent an hour without it. He stood still on Grier's carpet and let it come back and did not show anything, because Grier was looking at him.

Grier knew his face. Everybody in the capital knew the face; it had been on the pulpits since December. The gold eyes. The locs bound back under Imani's strip of cloth.

"Your Grace," said Grier. He did not get up. "I'd ask you to sit, but I'd not know what to offer you. I don't keep wine in the house now." He looked at the jug. "I'd have thought you'd send somebody."

"I did. They're on your terrace."

"And my men?"

"On your gravel. Tied, with their coats over their heads. They're not hurt. They'll be stiff in the morning and they'll not know who did it or how many." Emeric put the helm down on the end of the desk. "Nobody in this house has seen anybody's face tonight but yours. Nobody will."

Grier looked at him a while longer. He was a man of about sixty, heavy, with a red face that had gone sallow and soft under the red in the last month the way a fruit goes under its skin; and he had a good coat on over his shirt, buttoned wrong, and no wig, and grey stubble, and his hands lay on the chair's shiny arms quite still, and shook a little anyway.

"I said aye before he'd finished my name," Grier said. "Did they tell you that? Your people? Before the clerk had finished saying it. I'd been sitting on it so long it came out of me by itself." He looked at the hearth. "Forty-three to twenty-one. Two parts in three, exactly. If I'd said no he'd be in his palace tonight."

"Yes."

"You've come about that."

"I've come about the man who told you your debts were paid."



He sat down on the hearth's fender, which was low, so that he was under Grier's eyes and not over them; and he told the man what he wanted, in the plainest words he had, the way he had told the deserter on Varek's wall.

That Grier remembered more than he could say. Everybody did. That he, Emeric, could see what Grier remembered, if Grier let him, as if he were standing behind him and looking over his shoulder at the same thing. That Grier would be awake, in this chair, in this room, the whole time. That it would go only where Grier took it: to the night of the note, and the man who brought it. Nothing else. If he saw anything else, he would look away from it and not keep it. If Grier wanted him out, he had only to say so, or not even say it, only push, the way a man pushes a hand off his arm; and he would be out.

"It's the Church's art," said Grier.

"It's mine. The Church has it too. That's not the same thing."

"Does it hurt?"

"It mustn't. If it does, I've done it wrong, and I stop."

Grier was quiet a long time. Upstairs the woman turned over in her bed; Emeric felt it, and felt Grier hear the boards and look up at the ceiling, and look down again.

"My father was knighted at Carrow," Grier said. "By the old king. In the field. He'd held a ford for a day with forty men and the old king came down to the water after and did it with his own sword with the mud still on it. My father had the sword's mark on his shoulder through his coat for a week, he said, from the flat of it." He looked at his hands. "I owed my saddler eleven gold for four years. Eleven. My father would have taken the whip to me." He breathed out. "And then it was all paid. All of it. In a week. By people I'd never met. The vintners. The two earls. My steward came in with the quittances on a tray like letters at breakfast and I thought somebody had died and left me something." He stopped. "And then the man came."

"Will you show me?"

Grier looked at him, at the gold eyes, for what seemed a long time; and then at the black suit, and at the helm on his desk with its narrow violet pane; and at the window, where a black shape stood on either side with its back to the wall.

"Will they know?" he said. "Up there. That I did."

"Not from us."

Grier put his right hand out on the chair's arm, palm up, as a man puts his hand out to have a splinter looked at, because he did not know what else he was supposed to put out.

"Do it," he said.



The room was where it always was.

The long table. The shelves. The slow clean light. He went in through the bead at his wrist with two fingers on the inside of Grier's wrist, where Imani put hers, and the pulse under them was quick and thin and not steady, an old drinker's that had not had its drink for a month; and he waited until it came down a little, and then reached along his own mana into the man's note, which was broad and soft and muddled, like a field after rain, and asked.

Grier gave clumsily. They all did, the first time. He did not know how and could not have said what he was doing, and he did what he had been asked: he thought about the night the man came. And because he was thinking about it, it was there.

A hall. This house's hall, at the foot of the stair, with the black and white flags and the long-case clock. Lamps lit. Late. A fire in the hall's hearth, which was not lit now, in May, and had been then. Snow on the window's sill outside, thick, blue in the lamplight.

"What do you see?" Emeric said, in the library and in the room at once, slow, the voice Imani used at the foot of a bed.

A man at the door. The porter had let him in; the porter was behind him with the snow on his shoulders. A man of forty or so in a long grey coat, plain, good cloth, no badge. A thin face, clean-shaven, careful. A narrow-brimmed hat that he did not take off, and gloves he did. He had a flat leather case under his arm.

"What does he say?"

He would not sit. He would not take wine. He had a voice like a clerk's, quiet, every word put down square; and he said that his lordship would have had the quittances from the vintners' company and the rest. That his lordship was to understand that friends of the crown had thought it a shame that a man whose father held the ford at Carrow should be troubled by such small things. That there would be a sitting of the council in the spring, on a matter touching the king's oath, and that his lordship's friends were confident his lordship would see where the crown's good lay. That was all. And then he had opened the case, and taken out a single folded sheet, and given it into Grier's hand, and gone.

Emeric laid it on the table. He did not hurry.

"His hands," he said. "When he opened the case. Look at his hands."

Grier looked. He had not looked, at the time; he had been looking at the sheet. But a man sees what is in front of him whether he looks or not, and what he has seen, he has.

The gloves off, in the left hand. The right hand on the case's buckle, long-fingered, clean, a little ink on the second finger at the side where a pen rests. And at the wrist, as he reached across himself to the case under his arm, the grey coat's cuff riding back a finger's width, and under it, on the shirt's cuff, not the coat's, where no man would see it who was not standing at his elbow with a lamp on that side: a band of embroidery the width of a ribbon, silver thread on grey. A tower, and a star over it.

Emeric knew it. He had grown up with it on the sleeves of half the men who stood behind his brother's chair at dinner. Corbin's household. Not the crown's badge; the heir's. The men who kept the heir's accounts and carried the heir's letters wore it on their shirts under their coats, as the household's upper servants did, so that they were known to each other and to nobody else.

"Did he give a name?"

Grier's face, in the library, twisted a little. He had not wanted to remember this. "He said it at the door. To the porter. I heard him from the stair." The hall again; the stair; the porter's voice, and under it the other, quiet, square. "Master Rudd. For his lordship. He's expected." And in the room, a small cold thing, clear, set down: the porter had said it back, wrongly, Master Rudge, and the man had corrected him without any change in his voice. Rudd. With two d's.

"And the sheet?"

The sheet. Grier had read it in the hall standing, by the lamp. Not a letter; a list. Every one of his creditors by name, the sum, the date paid, a tick against each; and at its foot, in the same careful hand, not signed: The above discharged in full. And under that, smaller: Burn this.

He had burned it. Emeric watched him burn it, in the hall's hearth, that night, holding the corner until the flame reached his fingers; and watched him go up his own stair afterward, slowly, with one hand on the rail, and stop halfway and stand there a long time looking at the dark at the top.

That was not his. He let it go.

"When was this?" he said. "What night?"

"The second of the new year," said Grier at once, in the library, and Emeric felt him reach for it and find it the way a man finds a date he has told himself many times. "The second night. The night after the feast. I'd been at the feast at Lord Ashe's and come home sick on the road."

And in the room the snow lay on the hall's window sill thick and blue, and through the window, past the snow, over the lime avenue's black tops, the moon stood full and high and white.

Emeric did not say anything about the moon. He laid it down on the table beside the man in the grey coat, and the clock in the hall with its hands at a quarter to eleven, and the snow, and left it there. A full moon on the second night of the year was a thing Linus could look up in his almanac in a minute, and so could Saban. If Grier had his night wrong, he had it wrong honestly; men did, with nights they had told themselves over and over until the telling had worn the night smooth. The name was the name. The badge was the badge. The night was Saban's to find, the slow way.

"Thank you," he said, in the library and in the room. "That's enough. I'm coming out now. Slowly."

He came out slowly. He let go of Grier's note from the fingertips, as you let go of a hand; and was on the fender in the long cold library with two fingers on a wrist, and the pulse under them was eighty and steadier than it had been, and Grier had his eyes shut and tears running out from under the lids into the grey stubble, quite quietly, as if they belonged to somebody else.

"It didn't hurt," Grier said.

"No."

"I thought it would. I thought I'd like it to." He opened his eyes and wiped them with the back of his free hand, roughly, like a boy. "Rudd. Master Rudd. I'd forgotten I knew it. I'd have said I never knew who." He looked at Emeric. "Is that enough? For whatever you mean to do with it?"

"It's a beginning." Emeric took his fingers off the wrist. "I'll not lie to you. There's nothing tonight that a court would hear. A man's memory isn't a book." He stood up. "But a man is. When it's time."

Grier understood him. Emeric watched him understand it: the long slow look at the cold hearth, and the hands flat on the chair's arms, and the breath let out.

"When it's time," said Grier, "you'll want me to stand up in the council and say so."

"I'll not ask you to. Not tonight. Not until it's safe for you to." He picked up the helm. "If you choose to, then. Not because I asked."

Grier looked at the ceiling, over which his wife was asleep for the first time in a month.

"Go out the way you came," he said. "I'll ring for my men at five when the porter changes. I'll say I heard nothing. That'll be true." He put his hand over his eyes. "I'll be very angry with them for being asleep on the gravel. That'll be true too."



Emeric put the helm on and drew the hood, and the world went into its blanket again.

It was worse, going back into it. He had had the room and the house and the night all open to him for half an hour, and now they shut, one after another, like doors down a passage. He stood on the terrace in the dark between Mira and Bren and was half blind and half deaf, and his feet in the boots felt like somebody else's, and he found he did not like the suit very much at all.

Arthur's glove came up white, once, over the terrace wall. Down the lawn. The pond. The holly.

Ward was standing behind it with his helm in one hand and his other on Saban's shoulder, and as Emeric came through the gap he felt Ward's note through the wool, very dim, go from a wall braced against a weight to a wall with nothing against it; and Ward said nothing at all, and took his hand off Saban's shoulder, and put his helm on.

"Well?" said Saban.

"A name. A badge. A night that may be wrong." Emeric crouched in the leaves and took his gloves off, because his hands had begun to shake now that they could. "Master Rudd. Two d's. The heir's household; the tower and star, on the shirt cuff, under the coat."

Saban let his breath go. "The under-purser," he said. "I know the name. I've never seen the face."

"You will. He has ink on his second finger and he corrects porters." Emeric looked at him. "And Grier says the second night of the year, and there's a full moon over his limes in the memory, and snow."

Saban was quiet. Then he took the earpiece out of his coat and put it to his ear, and waited for the quarter, and tapped, and listened.

"Linus has it," he said. "He says the full moon in January was the twelfth. He says he'll swear to it on Ferra's almanac and his own. He says Fletcher's coming down."



They took the pickup in the field beyond the wood, because the wood was too thick and the house too near: a long hay meadow running down to a brook, cut a week ago, with the cut hay lying in rows, a quarter of a mile from the nearest farm, which was dark.

The K Discovery came down out of the sky over it with her stones dimmed almost to nothing and her lamps out, a long shape blacker than the sky, and stopped at a man's height over the hay with her forward leg half down, holding, the trim keys clicking. Up close she was not silent. Nothing her size was. A ship's matrix holding a hull still a yard over a field shoves on the air under her the way a hand pressed flat on water shoves on the water, and the cut hay lifted under her keel and stirred and turned over in its rows, and the smell of it came up, sweet and green, so strong after the pipe that Bren laughed out loud inside his helm.

The hatch came down. Saban went up first, with his box, pushed from below by Ward. Then the three. Then Emeric, and Ward behind him, last, as Ward had said he would be.

And the light came.

It came from the north and above, white, and very sudden: not a lamp's light, which comes up and goes round a thing, but a mage's, thrown, the kind of hard white glare a casting mage makes over his hand and pushes out in front of him on his own mana, flat and wide like a sheet shaken out over a bed. It went over the hay meadow from the far end like a wave over sand, and lit every row and every stubble stalk and the brook and the hedges, white, with black shadows behind them, and came up the field and found the ship.

It lit her whole flank. The oiled wood and the brass straps and the four viewports' irises and the bronze leg standing down into the hay; every line of her, lit like a ship on a quay in noon sun, for anybody to see who was looking.

Somebody was. Emeric was on the hatch ladder's third rung with his head and shoulders inside the hull and his legs out under her belly in the glare, and he turned his helm and looked up through the narrow pane, and saw it: a small hull, no longer than a market barge, a few hundred feet up and half a mile off over the next field, coming slowly south over the outskirts the way the capital's sky-ships came home off their night runs to the landing tiers. A sky-ship. One of the crown's own mage-fed hulls, with a man in it pouring himself into the lift a breath at a time, and another man at her bow with his arm out and the white light standing off his open hand.

And over the light, as Emeric watched, at the bow, something else began to show. A ring. Faint, gold, drawing itself line by line in the air in front of the casting mage's chest, the way a glyph drew itself when a mage meant something by it.

He did not wait to read its core. Nobody did.

He had his mouth open to say it. He was on the ladder, half in, half out, with Ward's hand on his boot below him and the hatch's coaming against his chest, and he got as far as the first breath of the word Fletcher, up the speaking tube that hung by the hatch for the stone-keeper's station; and Fletcher was already doing it.

Afterward Fletcher said he had not thought about it. He said he had seen the light come over the field from the bow cockpit, and the hull in it, and the gold, and his hand had gone to the brass guard on the lever under the bow helm's panel the way it would have gone to a rope in a squall, and lifted it, and taken the lever, before he had decided anything at all; and that if anybody had asked him at that moment who had given the order, he would have said Emeric had, and believed it.

"Hatch," said Fletcher's voice, down the tube, quite calm. "Hold on to something."

Ward came up the ladder underneath Emeric like a wall coming up a stair, and put him through the hatch bodily with one hand in the small of his back, and came through after, and Saban got the hatch up on its chain and the dogs over it with both hands; and the floor tilted, and the ship lifted out of the hay with the leg still coming up into her belly and the hay going up off the field under her in a storm, and her nose came up, and Fletcher pulled the lever down through its stroke.



Emeric had heard it once. At sea, in January, far out where nobody but fishermen could hear it, with Ferra beside him at the cabin wall timing it on her watch. He had thought then that he knew what it was.

He had heard it from inside, out at sea, with nothing for the sound to come back off. Over a hay meadow two miles from the capital's south road, low, with a wood and a house and a farm and a village and another village beyond that, it came back off everything.

The gate went over behind them with a thud he felt in the soles of his boots. The propeller feathered, and stopped turning, and the main stone's blast went straight down the can, and the fire ring came up onto its seats in the can's wall, and the iris opened at the mouth; and the roar the stone always made at full went up through itself into something else, a scream, high and enormous and tearing, that came through the hull and the iron skin and the felt and the hood over his helm and into the middle of his head and stayed there. The whole ship shook with it. The lamp's slot rattled in its frame. Bren had both gloves over the sides of his helm and his mouth open, shouting something nobody could hear.

And she went.

Not the way she climbed on the matrix, slow and level, a lift going up a shaft. She went as if she had been kicked. The floor came up under them and then went back under them, all of it at once, hard, so that everybody who was standing sat down and everybody who was sitting went against the nearest wall, and Saban's box slid the length of the saloon floor on its brass corners and Arthur put a foot on it; and through the forward iris, which nobody had shut, Emeric saw the field and the hedge and the wood go under the bow and away, and the sky-ship's white light swinging after them over the stubble and falling behind, and behind, and losing them.

The gold ring at the sky-ship's bow finished itself. Whatever it was went out across the field in a white flash like a door opened on a lit room, and struck where the ship had been, in the hay, and the hay went up in a sheet of flame twenty yards long where she had stood a breath before; lightning, then, or fire, he never knew. It was half a mile behind them by then. A mile. Two.

Twenty breaths. That was what it took. Linus had his watch on it, at the chart table, holding it in both hands against the shaking, and he said twenty, afterward, and nobody argued.

Below them the country went by in the dark faster than any of them had ever seen country go: the south road, a pale line, there and gone; a village, a church tower, a few lit windows coming on as the scream went over them; another; fields; a river with the moon in it; a third village with dogs running out into its street. Somewhere in each of them, Emeric thought, a man was standing in his nightshirt in his door with his face turned up, and seeing nothing, and a star going the wrong way.

He got up and went aft, holding on to the bulkhead rail, to the after iris, and opened it a crack, and looked back.

Behind them the night was dark and full of stars, and low down over the fields, a long way off now, very small, there was a fire in a hay meadow, and over it a small white light hanging still in the air, the sky-ship's, where she had stopped dead to stare. And under the K Discovery's own stern, close, where the can stood out on her arm, he could see the can's lip, glowing. Dull red at first, then brighter: a ring of cherry in the dark, the colour of a horseshoe on an anvil, with the air behind it shimmering so that the stars over the capital wavered in it like stones under water.

The red eye. That was what the sky-ship's crew were looking at now, from their bow, if they were looking: a red eye going away from them very fast toward the south and the sea, and getting smaller, and smaller, until it was a spark, and then nothing.

Fletcher took her up through the cloud with the burn still on, at a slant, until they were over it at a mile with the stars all round; and then, at the fourth minute by Linus's watch, pulled the lever up through its stroke, and shut the gate, and unfeathered the propeller; and the scream came down through itself into the roar, and the roar into the quiet thrum of silent running, and the ship went on south over the white floor of the cloud under the moon as if nothing had happened at all.

Nobody said anything for some time. Bren took his gloves off his helm. Mira was sitting against the saloon wall with her knees up, and she was laughing, without any sound, with her helm off in her lap and her face red, and could not stop.

The dial at the stone-keeper's station, corded to the can's shell, came down out of the red, slowly, a needle's width at a time.



He went up to the bow cockpit when his hearing came back, which was somewhere south of Wayford.

Fletcher was at the helm with his hands on the wheel at a quarter to three and the panel's lamps turned down to beads, and the brass guard was down over the lever again, shut, as if nobody had ever touched it. He did not turn round. He had his hat on, which he did not usually wear at the helm, and he had pulled it down.

"They saw her," Fletcher said.

"They saw a shape. A light on a flank, for as long as it takes to draw a ring. And a noise, and a red light going away." Emeric sat down in the second seat. "They'll swear to a ship. Nobody'll believe them. Not this year."

"They'll believe the noise. Three villages heard the noise." Fletcher looked at the panel. "I didn't wait for you."

"No."

"I should have. It's your ship. I've flown her nine years and I've never once taken a thing on myself that was yours to say." He was quiet. "My hand was on it before I'd thought. I'll not say I'm sorry. I'd do it again."

"I'd opened my mouth to say it," said Emeric. "You were faster. That's all." He looked out at the cloud going by under the moon, white and soft and endless, with their shadow on it. "If you'd waited for me to say it, he'd have finished the ring with us still on the ground, and we'd be finding out now what was in it."

Fletcher said nothing to that for a while.

"There's a rule now," Emeric said. "I'll write it for Ferra's book. No burn near anybody's eyes. Not over the land, not near a town, not where a man on a road could hear it, until the war. Out at sea for the drill, and for this, and nothing else."

"Until the war," said Fletcher.

"Until the war."

Fletcher nodded slowly. He took one hand off the wheel and touched the brass guard over the lever with the backs of his fingers, lightly, the way a man touches the flank of a horse that has just carried him out of something.

"Nine years she's carried things," he said. "Ore and men and grain and a sick old man to the Band and back. Glass. Paper. You." He put the hand back on the wheel. "That's the first thing she's ever been for that wasn't carrying something."



Ward was in the saloon, in the corner by the after iris, with his helm on the bench beside him and his hood back and his hands on his knees, looking at nothing.

The three were asleep. They had gone down all at once, as soldiers do, where they sat: Arthur on the bench with his arms folded, Mira against the wall with her helm still in her lap and her mouth open, Bren on the floor with his head on Saban's box, which Saban had let him have. Saban was at the chart table beside Linus with the almanac open in front of them, and Linus's finger under a line, and Saban writing on the back of one of Linus's spare charts with Linus's pencil, very small.

Emeric sat down on the bench by Ward. He had taken the hood and helm off at last and the night came in at him, all of it, the ship and the five asleep and awake round him and the cloud and the dark country under it, and he sat with it and let it, as a man sits by a fire after a long cold road.

"You can come back now," said Ward. "You're back."

"I'm back."

"You were a cat on a wall for an hour and a half." Ward did not look at him. "I could not hear you. I could not feel you. I stood in a hedge with a merchant and listened to a house for an hour and a half, and for all I knew you were lying on its gravel with a bolt in you, and the first I'd know was the bell." His hands stayed on his knees. "I've done a great many things since you were eight. That's the worst."

"I know."

"You don't. You were inside." Ward turned his head then and looked at him, and his face was as it always was, and his note was steady, and Emeric could feel what it cost. "I'll do it again. When you ask. I'll stand in the hedge." He looked away. "I'll want to be told more next time. Through that wire. Every quarter. If it's only that you're on the roof."

"Every quarter."

"And I'll have a suit of those. My own. Cut for me." Ward looked down at his own black under-suit, which had been Bren's spare and was tight across the shoulders, and the boots, which he had come down in from three thousand feet and had not used for anything else. "And somebody's going to teach me the wall."

Emeric looked at him.

"Mira," said Ward. "Not you. You're bad at it."



Vantic heard it in the tithe house the next evening, at dusk, behind her own rail, with the shutters up and her clerk gone out by the side door with his coat over his arm, as before.

He had been at the Dust House at dawn, because Bram had been waiting on the shore with something on a cloth and a face like a man who has been up all night and is not sorry; and he had slept four hours in the afternoon with Ferra's hand on his back, and Ferra had read Saban's sheet, and Linus's line in the almanac, and drawn a box round the twelfth of January and written beside it, Not the second. Find out who was at Ashe's. And then he had come down the hill at dusk with Saban's sheet in his coat, because he had given Vantic his word in April and he meant her to see that he kept it.

He told her: a name, a badge, a night that was probably wrong. Not her brother's name; not the House's; not a word about any book, because there had been none in Grier's library, and no figure in his memory but his saddler's eleven gold. A man in a grey coat with ink on his second finger who corrected porters. A tower and a star on a shirt cuff. The heir's household. The under-purser.

She listened to all of it without moving, with her gloved hands flat on her desk, the way she listened to a borrower.

"And when it comes to a court," she said.

"Then Grier stands up and says it with his own mouth, if he chooses to. And Saban brings them the man. Your brother's book stays where it is. Nobody opens it. Nobody needs to."

She was quiet a long time. Outside, on the square, somebody was laughing, and a cart went by on the cobbles, and the bell at the church struck the half.

"You came to ask me," she said. "Whether that breaks it. My price."

"Yes."

"It doesn't." She looked at him over the rail, and for once, he thought, she let him see that she was pleased: not much, a little at the corner of the mouth, the same place it always went. "I said it was never to go to any court. The book. Tullio's paper. That's what the price was for." She took her hands off the desk. "You found a man. You didn't find a book."
