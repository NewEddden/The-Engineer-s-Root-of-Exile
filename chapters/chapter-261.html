Chapter 261 - Air in a Bottle

The first flask burst on Monday morning, the sixth of September, in a cattle trough at the back of Bram's yard, which was where Bram had said it would burst; it went at a little over five thousand pounds to the inch by the gauge on his hand pump, with a flat hard bang like a door kicked in, and put most of the trough over Bram.

He stood in the wet with the pump handle still in his fists and looked down at the split in its side.

"There," he said. "Now we know what they hold."

Ferra came forward from the forge door, where she had been told to stand and had stood, and read the gauge, and wrote it. Under it she wrote a third of it, the figure the others would be filled to and not a pound over, and ruled a line, and initialled the line, so that nobody at the plant could ever say they had not been told.

There had been nine. Now there were eight. Bram had made them on the pattern of the Hod's flasks, the same forged iron and the same neck, with Ivo's glass fused inside, only a third the length and narrower, so that a man could carry one slung along his ribs under his left arm and still swim. The glass had been in the Hod's flasks to hold the Band. It stayed in these because a flask of plain lake air, squeezed in by the plant's compressor, would take a drop of water at every fill, and the drop would lie in the bottom, and the iron would drink it.

"You'd be breathing rust by the spring," said Emeric.

"Not out of that one," said Bram, and went to change his shirt.



Tess had the valve.

She had had his two sheets since the middle of August, with nothing on them to say what they were for, and had not asked, which in Tess meant she had worked it out by the second evening. Three of what she had built lay in a box under her bench at the end of bay three with their covers off. The fourth sat in front of her: a brass can the size of a fist, flat across its face, with a rubber mouthpiece on a short neck at one side and a hose into its back from a flask in the vise.

"The flask's at seventeen hundred," she said. "A mouth wants what the water's pressing on it, and not a breath more. So it comes down twice. Here, on the flask's neck, to something a hose can carry. And here, to whatever the water says." She touched the can's face. "Isle rubber across the front, water on the outside, your mouth on the inside. You breathe in, the skin goes in, it pushes a lever, you get air. You stop, it stops. You breathe out, it goes out a flap."

"When you ask for it," said Emeric.

"When you ask. Not before." She did not look at the box. "The first one gave it before."

"It roared," said Osric, from the stool against the wall where he had been since seven. "Blew her hair off her face."

"It moved it."

"It blew it, Tess."

The third had stuck open once in four hundred breaths, which Tess had counted; and once in four hundred was a man under a platform at night with his air going up past a lantern in a roar. So there was a fourth, with a longer lever, and a button in the middle of the can's face.

"The purge. Mouthpiece comes out under water, it fills. Put it back, press that, and it blows the water out." She pressed it, and the can hissed. A thin rod ran down the flask's side to a ring at its foot. "And there's a spring valve in the neck that shuts when you're down to a fifth. Your breath goes stiff, and stiffer, and you think you're dying. You're not. You reach back and pull the ring, and there's your fifth." She let it go. "So you know you're on it. Nobody's going to read a gauge in the dark. They'll notice their breath."

Osric rolled the rain butt in from the yard door without being asked and filled it from the bay's tap, slopping half of it on the floor. Tess leaned out of her chair and put her face in it to the ears; and the bay heard her breathe, a catch and a hiss in the can and a burble at the butt's side, over and over, while Osric stood behind the chair with one hand held a little off its back, not touching, the way a man holds his hand behind a child on a wall.

She came up at sixty breaths by Merrin Sallis's count and wiped her face on her sleeve. "Stiff at the top. I'll take a turn off the spring."

He had seen her valve before, in another life, in a photograph of two men on a beach during a war; one had made his from a valve for running a motor car on gas. Tess had made hers in three weeks, in a chair, with her face in a rain butt.

"A scuba set," he said. "The flask and the valve and the harness. The whole of it."

"What's that short for?" said Osric.

"Something longer."

"Everything you say is short for something longer," said Osric; and Tess wrote demand valve on the can's face in pencil, because that was what she had been calling it since the second evening.

The sleeping mixture took nine days and most of a generation of Bettany's rats.

He made the stronger thing at the acid works on the Saturday, out of the bleach yard's powder and the spirit off the still: a heavy clear liquid with a sweet smell, that sank in water like a bead of glass. Chloroform, he called it, and Imani wrote it on the bottle.

It did not burn, with a lantern a yard off, and ether did. It was quicker and took less. And now and then it stopped a heart: not from too much, which would have been fair, but in the first breaths, in the young and the frightened, without warning. He said so at the bench. He did not say that on his other world it had put a queen to sleep for her eighth child and killed a girl of fifteen in a chair having a toenail drawn.

So they cut it with ether, and Imani wrote down every rat. By the ninth day the bottle on her bench was two parts ether to one of the heavy stuff, and it put a rat down in half the time of ether alone and had not stopped a heart in sixty.

"Two minutes," said Imani, with the mask in front of her: a wire cup the size of a hand with eight thicknesses of boiled gauze over it. "On a big man, three. On a man fighting it, longer. Minutes, not breaths. And on a starved man I'd not use it at all. They go under quick and they don't come back."

Rylan had come down with Ward, going through the raid's list item by item in his price-of-fish voice. He picked up the mask. "Could the eight not carry a bottle each? It's a small bottle."

"No," said Imani.

Rylan waited.

"A knight with a pad over a man's face holds it till the man stops moving," said Imani, "because that's what he's been taught to want from a man in the way. With this, stopping moving is the dose for dying. My medics hold it till he's under and take it off and watch his chest, and drip again when he stirs. You do the chokes. They do the bottle. Nobody carries both."

Rylan looked at Ward. Ward said nothing.

"That's fair," said Rylan, and drew a line under it on his list, which from Rylan was a great deal.

Behind her the incubator ticked on its shelf, the strip warming and letting go. Hester's broth was in it, four jars velvet on top.

"The dock man's back on the hooks," Imani said, while Rylan wrote. "All five fingers. The red line went back to the wrist and never came up again." She laid her hand flat on the incubator's top, as Hester had. "I'd take it on Rowena's ship if it would keep. Two days off its bed and it's soup."

So the broth stayed in Kaldo, and she began the list for the boxes on the back of a card. Pint bottles with their spoon of citrate, out of midwinter's oranges by way of chalk and the acid works. Ward had said it all on the shingle that afternoon, once, from the book, and would not say it again; so Emeric said it over in his own head. Out at three breaths apart, Rylan first and himself last. Fall dark, hearts asleep, for a count of ten. On ten, the hearts awake and the suit's air stones, which pushed a man along in flight, set to drive straight down beneath him: a flat hard plate of air that bled the fall out of him with no gravity stone lit. Arthur sat up. "I did it right, sir."

"You did. So do it again, so you know it wasn't luck. The first time's the one you'll think about there. I want it to be one you don't remember." Ward looked down the cove at the platforms. "The ship's going up for Mira and Bren's second. Go with them. All of it."

Arthur went, and did it right again. Ward watched him from the first platform with the borrowed lantern in his hand, and when Arthur came up under the bank, Ward hung the lantern back on its trestle and walked off up the shingle without a word.

Rylan borrowed something that week too: Emeric's old leg sheet, the one Imani had taped his thighs against for a year. He copied it in his own downhill hand, and his knights did heel drops off the armoury step every dawn now, and Maren's egg order went up four dozen a week.



On the Saturday at dusk, the second of October, waiting on the shingle for the ship, he tried the ice.

A man hurt in the water at the Isle would want somewhere to be laid, and there would be nothing in the cove to climb onto but the camp's own landing. And he could make a floor.

He knelt at the edge with one hand on the surface and the other in it a foot out, the way Armand had taught him on this shore in the winter, the cold coming up one arm and the heat going out of the other into the whole grey Mere. The water went stiff and white under his palm and the white spread across the surface the way frost spreads on a window, until there was a plate of ice lying at the shingle's edge as wide as a door and as long, and as thick as his forearm.

Osric stopped talking.

Emeric stood up and stepped onto it.

It held him for the step. Then, slowly and without any fuss at all, it tipped: the far edge went down and the near edge rose behind his heels, and he slid down its face into the Mere to the neck, and the plate came over on top of him flat and pushed him under into the black, with a door of ice over his head and the light gone.

He came up at its edge, spitting. The plate lay beside him right way up again, rocking gently.

Osric had sat down on the shingle, he was laughing so hard. Declan had his hand over his eyes. Ward stood with his arms folded and looked at the ice, and at Emeric in the water beside it, and said nothing at considerable length.

"It's floating," said Emeric. "There's nothing under it to push back." He put a hand on its edge, and it dipped. "It needs holding."

"You'll want a dry shirt," said Ward. "The ship's in an hour."

He wrote it in the K Discovery's cockpit on the way up, in a hand that shook with cold: A floating plate has nothing under it to push against. And under it, because Tess had said it about the suits and it was still true: Build it so it doesn't need holding. He looked at that a long time, and crossed out doesn't need, and wrote can be held, and did not know yet by what.



Bren flared late on Thursday the seventh of October.

They had made eleven drops and the Mere had begun to feel like a room they knew. The moon was past half, low in the west, and threw a long broken road of light across the water toward the bay, so that from above the whole Mere looked lit, and nearer than it was.

Emeric was last, with his sense on the seven, and felt it before anybody could have seen it. Six plates. Six notes slowing. Six flares. And the seventh note going on past where its flare should have been, still falling at the plate's speed, with nothing waking in it.

He knew where the red sat on that boy's wrist; he had set it himself on the first night. The note was below it already and he was a thousand feet above.

He did not decide anything. He cut his plate and went Heavy and dropped through the dark like a stone down a well, the air screaming at the edges of his mask, and saw Bren's flare come at last below him, a small frantic violet bloom at the height of a barn roof, for half a breath; and then the water.

He heard it hit. A flat hard crack, like a plank thrown down on a floor.

Bren's note went dim.

Emeric woke every stone he had a hundred feet up and far too fast, and the stop hit him in the chest and the legs like a wall, and his knees and his spine took it, and held, just; and he was in the water beside the place and under it, Heavy again, in the black. He did not need to see. The note was ten feet down and a little left, sinking slowly, a dim warm smudge in the cold with no rhythm in it he liked.

Ward was already there. Ward's note had come into the water forty yards off and turned toward Bren's without a pause; and Emeric opened the hair's width of the door, Ward's door, and said down it, Left. Down. I have him, and felt Ward hear it, and felt Ward's hand find the back of the boy's harness at the moment his own did.

Bren's mask was gone. His mouthpiece streamed somewhere on its hose. His body hung soft and heavy between them the way a living body does not. They went up with him with their legs going, and Emeric went Light, and they broke the surface in the moon's road with Bren's face up between them and the water pouring off his cap.

The boat was coming: two garrison men at the oars, and Imani in the stern with her bag between her feet, as she had sat every night of the eleven in the dark at the bay's edge with nothing to do. She was standing before it reached them, and had her hands on Bren before he was over the side.

They laid him on his face. She knelt astride his back in the bilge with her skirt in the wet and put her hands flat under his ribs and pressed, hard, and again, and the water came out of him, a great deal of it; and then he coughed, an ugly torn sound, and was sick over the side, and breathed.

Then he cried out, high, with his hand going to his left side.

"Don't touch it," said Imani. "Lie still. Breathe small. Small, I said. Like a mouse." She had the plate on that side unbuckled, and her hands went over him through the gap a rib at a time. "Two. Cracked, not through." She sat back on her heels. "The plate bent in and came back out. You'd be dead in a shirt."

Bren's teeth were going. Mira's head had come round the boat's bow in the water and stopped a yard off, with her mask pushed up and her eyes on him, saying nothing.



Ward asked him on the shingle, with a blanket round him and Imani's strapping round his ribs and his lips still blue, and the rest of them standing round him dripping.

"What did the needle say?"

Bren looked at the stones. "I didn't look, sir. I could see the water. The moon was on it. It was right there." His voice went. "I thought I'd flare when I could see the bottom."

"The moon's on the water at any height you like," said Ward. "It was on the water when you left the ship." He did not raise his voice. He never did. "You flare on the needle. Your eyes will lie to you every night of your life over water. The needle will lie to you about once a year, and when it does there's somebody behind you. Who's behind you?"

"His Grace."

"Last. With his sense on you. That's why he's last." Ward looked at him a moment longer. "Four days, if the healer says four."

"Four," said Imani. "Then I'll listen to him breathe and we'll see. No bread. No cold water. And no Mira telling you you were an idiot."

"I wasn't going to," said Mira.

"You were," said Imani.

Mira sat down on the shingle beside him in her wet plate anyway and said something too low to carry, and Bren laughed, and stopped, and held his side, and called her a word Imani pretended not to hear.

The Mere kept Bren's mask. Ivo had made spares.



Bren was back on Tuesday the twelfth. Imani listened to him breathe in the ward for a good deal longer than Bren thought necessary, front and back, and wrote on his card that he could swim and drop and was not to catch anybody heavier than Mira for a fortnight; which meant the block door.

"Then Bram empties the sack to Mira's weight," said Ward, "and Bren catches it. Or Bren tells me now he can't, and Arthur stands there."

"I can, sir."

He could. He caught Mira's weight in sand that night with his left arm held stiffly to his side and his teeth shut, and Mira told him she was heavier than that, and nobody else said anything about it.

Emeric took Arthur aside the next evening on the shingle, before the ship.

He had meant to for a week; Imani had said in the ward, without looking up from Bren's card, that there would never be a good place and that this was the kind of thing he put off. So he did it at the edge of the bay in the last of the light, with Ward a little way off, not watching.

Arthur stood with his hands behind his back like Ward. He knew what it was about. He had known since he first saw the map, with its fence and its long sheds, and counted the years on his fingers behind his back where he thought nobody would see.

"Don't hope," said Emeric.

Arthur said nothing.

"Nobody knows who's in those sheds. A man taken eighteen years ago can be dead seventeen of them. He can be dead a week. He can be in another camp on the other side of the island that we never heard of. If we told your mother and he wasn't there, we'd have killed him twice." He kept his voice level. "Ferra said that to me at Aster's counter in July, about her. It's as true about you. And men in your place break missions. Not because they're bad men. They look for one face in every shed, and they stop where they should go on, and go on where they should stop, and somebody else dies of it."

Down at the water Mira laughed at something Osric had said.

"You'll go into every shed as if every man in it were a stranger," said Emeric. "Because he will be. Until he isn't."

Arthur was quiet a long time. When he spoke he did not ask what if.

"Yes, sir," said Arthur.

He meant it. Emeric felt him mean it, with the sense that could prove nothing and told him everything he needed: the boy's note went level and stayed level, the way a needle settles when the swing has gone out of it. He meant it the way he did everything, once, carefully, and then it was done.

"Go and check your straps."

Arthur went. Ward, a little way off, had been looking at the falls the whole time, and went on looking at them.



The deep drill was the last, on Saturday the sixteenth: Ward's idea and Imani's rule. Nobody knew how deep the cove at the Isle was, so the eight went down in the Mere under the falls on a knotted line from the boat at one in the morning, to sixty feet, to learn their ears and the cold and what the dark did to a man's head.

It was colder at sixty feet than anything. The water pressed the mask's skirt into his face until he breathed out through his nose to ease it. He hung on the line at the sixth knot with the seven round him in the black, a ring of small warm points in a great cold nothing, and counted them, and counted them again, and went on counting them, because that was what he did with them; and it was only when the valve went stiff at the top of each breath, and stiffer, that he understood he was on his fifth.

He reached back and pulled the ring, and the air came easy. Not one of the seven had reached back yet. Not Osric, who breathed like a bellows on the shingle. Not Mira.

They came up a knot a minute and lay by the boat blowing. Ward, who had been a yard off on the line the whole way, was the last to take out his mouthpiece.

"You were on your ring before Mira," he said. "Before anybody."

"It's the cold."

"It's as cold for Mira," said Ward, and climbed into the boat.



They brought the kit back to the boat shed at a quarter to two, and the others hung theirs on the pegs to drip and went up the path to the dock house, to the soup and the stove and Ward's count. Emeric stayed, because his suit was always last, and because Imani was on the bench by the shed door with the stethoscope round her neck and had not gone.

"Shirt," she said, when he had hung the last piece.

He took it off. He stood on the boards by the tank's rim in the lamplight with the eight suits dripping round him and let her put the silver cup on his chest. In front. Under the left arm. The back, both sides, low and high. Breathe; stop. Then over his heart, for a long time, with her other hand flat on his back to hold him still, though he was not moving.

It went on long enough that he knew it was not about his lungs.

"Ward said you went on your ring first," said Imani, with the tubes still in her ears.

"Ward talks too much."

"Ward said nine words. He came to the boat to say them on purpose." She moved the cup a finger's width. "You count them. All seven, the whole way down and up. A man carrying seven people in his head breathes like a man carrying them up a stair. You come up blowing like Osric." She took the cup away at last. "And you'll do it there, because it's what you're for."

He had put it down to the cold, and his knees, and being thirty-two.

"Count them slower," said Imani. "They'll still be there."

"I'll try."

"You'll do it worse the first time. Then less worse." She put her hand flat on his chest where the cup had been, warm on the cold skin, and left it there. "I'm sailing on Rowena's flagship. Not Bettany. Me. My medics and the boxes and the white dish."

He had asked Rowena for room for a healer, and had let himself think it might be Bettany, without once saying so, because anything said would have been said back to him.

He opened his mouth.

"Don't," said Imani.

He shut it.

"You'll be in the water under a tower with two hundred and forty men in sheds, and some of them bleeding by morning. I'll be on a deck a mile off with a dish. You don't get to keep me home from that." She looked at him. "You were going to try. I watched you start."

She took her hand away and stood up and began to undo the front of her dress.

"Help me with these. The hooks are wet."

He looked at her.

"By the spring I'll be putting Tess's valves in patients' mouths with the plant's oxygen behind them, when their chests won't fill," said Imani. "I'm not putting a valve in a man's mouth that I haven't had in my own." She stepped out of the dress and laid it on the bench and stood on the boards in her shift with her arms bare in the lamplight and the old kettle scar pale on the back of her left hand. "For the patients."

"For the patients," said Emeric.

"Get a flask," said Imani, and pulled the cloth off her hair.



The water was cold enough to stop the breath, and she swore at it going down the ladder in a word she did not use in her ward; then she was in to the neck with her shift floating up round her and her braids floating out, and her chin up, and her teeth going.

He came down after her with a flask along his ribs. The lamps put two long yellow cones down into the water and turned the black iron walls gold where they touched, and her skin gold, and her shift no colour at all.

"Show me."

The mouthpiece behind the teeth, the lips round it. Breathe slowly in and let it give; breathe out and hear it go. She did a dozen breaths at the surface with her face down and her eyes open on the rivets, and came up and said it was stiff at the top, exactly as Tess had said; and took a breath through it and let her legs go, and sank.

He went down with her. They knelt on the floor facing each other in the gold, and she breathed, catch and hiss, with her eyes wide on him and a thin silver stream going up from the side of her mouth, and her hair moving round her face as if there were a wind down there. He had never seen her under water. He did not want to look at anything else.

She took the mouthpiece out and held it out to him. Two on one, two breaths each, as Ward had drilled it into eight people in this tank. He breathed twice and gave it back; she breathed twice, watching him, and gave it back; round and round, a hand each on the other's shoulder.

On the fourth pass she did not give it back. She took her two breaths and held the mouthpiece off to one side by the can, out of his reach, hissing a slow silver thread toward the lamps; and put her other hand behind his head and pulled him in and kissed him.

It was a short kiss, of necessity. It was the coldest and the strangest he had ever had, nine feet of water on them and her mouth warm in the middle of it and no air in either of them; and when she let go she was laughing, a great ragged burst of silver going up out of her, which was the last of her breath. She pushed the mouthpiece into his hand and went for the surface.

He came up after her into the noise of the shed. She was hanging on the rim with both arms, coughing and laughing at once, with water streaming off her braids.

"That," she said, "was stupid."

"It was your idea."

"It was a stupid idea." She turned round with her back to the iron and looked at him a hand's width off. "Your lips are blue."

"So are yours."

"Then do something about it."

He put a hand on the rim either side of her and kissed her properly, in the air, with her back to the cold iron and the cold water to their necks and her arms coming up out of it round him, wet and warm at once. She kissed him back slowly and then not slowly at all, with her fingers in the wet locs at the back of his head and her knee coming up against his hip under the water. He felt her heart against his chest through her shift and his wool, going fast, and felt her lips move once against his, counting it; and then felt her lose it, and laugh into his mouth about losing it, and not look for it again.

The cold had stopped mattering. She had her hands flat on his back under the wet wool, and the water slopped over the rim onto the boards every time either of them moved; and she said his name against his jaw in the voice he only heard from her in the dark, and he lifted her, easily in the water, so that her braids fell round him like a curtain and shut out the lamps.

"Somebody will come," she said into his hair, as if it did not worry her.

"Ward said half an hour."

"When did he say it?"

He tried to think when Ward had said it.

The crew bell rang.

It was the old ship's bell on its post outside the dock house, that Fletcher rang to call a crew aboard and Ward had rung every night for three weeks to call the eight in to be counted: three strokes, slow, flat, perfectly even. They came down the path through the reeds and in at the open shed door and rang round the iron of the tank like a hammer on a boiler.

Imani went still in his arms. Then she put her forehead down on the top of his head and began to laugh again, silently, her whole body shaking with it, and he held her up and waited for it to stop and it did not.

"A quarter past," she said at last. "He rings it at a quarter past."

"He's rung it at a quarter past every night for three weeks."

"Then you might have said. You're the one who counts things."

"I was counting other things."

"I know. I heard you through the cup." She went up the ladder in her soaked shift with the water pouring off her, and stood on the boards shaking with cold and laughter both, and held her dress against her front and looked down at him. "Go and be counted. I'll wear your coat home. And tell Ward his bell's very good."

He went up the path a few minutes after the quarter in a dry shirt off the peg with his wet hair tied back. The eight were round the stove with their soup. Ward stood by the door with the crew book open, and looked up when Emeric came in, and down at the book again, and made a mark.

"Eight," said Ward, to the book.

He said nothing else whatever, at greater length than Emeric had ever heard him say it.



Ferra knew by breakfast, because Imani told her over the bread, flat and in order: the tank, the valve, the fourth pass, the bell. Ferra listened with her cup in both hands and did not put it down once, and at the end she looked across the little table at the coat over the back of Imani's chair.

"Ward rings that bell at a quarter past," she said. "It's on the rota. You both read the rota."

"We were busy," said Imani.

"You were under water in October in a boiler with one breath between you." Ferra opened her book at the rota, found the sixteenth, and wrote beside Ward's quarter past two, in square small capitals, HEARD; and turned the book round so that they could both read it, which she did not do.

Then she turned it back and went to work.



Imani bled for the bottles on Friday the twenty-second, and not a day sooner.

The donors came from seven in a line down the passage, off the register, the dock quarter's among them now and not few, passed by the blue vitriol drop that morning. Bettany's needle went in, and the blood ran down the tube into a pint bottle hanging below the bed with its spoon of citrate in the bottom, and a nurse rocked it as it filled so that it would not set. Corked, labeled with its letter and the date, into the straw in a cold-stone box. The donor went to the end room for his dinner, where people could see him eat it.

By noon there were thirty-two, in four iron-cornered boxes along the ward's end wall, each with two charged stones in the lid set to pull and a thermometer through the side that Imani read every hour and wrote down; and the leveller in a fifth, with its dials and its coil and its cuff; and the two donors' names on its lid so that nobody would have to go looking. They would go out to Rowena's deck in the K Discovery's hold, with Imani and her medics, when the eight went.

The kit had gone into crates the evening before: eight suits in straw, belts, masks and spares, eight altimeters each in a small box Tess had made, eight flasks filled to Ferra's figure, fifty hoods, forty black sets of plate. Bram stencilled every lid himself, as he had in the week the Visitor walked the town.

STOVE PARTS.

Tess had come down the armoury ramp before the lids went on, with Osric behind her carrying her bag, and gone along the open crates, and picked up each altimeter and tapped its glass once with her finger and watched the needle and put it back. Eight times. She did not say why. Osric stood behind her chair and did not say anything either.

That night Ferra sat at the end of the long table with her book open at the page with nothing at its head, Rowena's four ships, and wrote under them the date of the new moon, the twenty-seventh, in her square small hand.

Then she left the space after it empty, and closed the book.
