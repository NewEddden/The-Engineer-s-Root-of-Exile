Chapter 131 - Lifted

On the first of December Vantic took the House's own book of the covenant out of the locked case in the east tower where it lived, and brought it down to the hall at noon, and opened it on the long table in front of the Standing Order, at the clause.

It was the House's book, not the barony's. Emeric had seen it twice in two years: a tall narrow volume bound in grey, the House's colour, with the covenant written into it in the House's chief clerk's hand on the day the loan was made, and every amendment after, and the factor's initials beside each. The cap was the ninth clause. Four lines.

Vantic took a rule out of her coat, the folding brass one she had measured the hull with, and opened it, and laid it across the four lines. She dipped her own pen. She drew one line through the clause, straight, from the left margin to the right, in the House's grey ink. Not through the words. Under them, through the middle, so that every word of the clause could still be read beneath the line, as the House required. Then she wrote beside it in the margin, small: struck, by the House's letter of November, effective this day. And the date. And her initials.

She blotted it, and closed the book, and stood with her gloved hand on the cover.

Nobody at the table said anything.

"It's done," said Vantic.

"Thank you," said Emeric.

"Don't thank me." She picked up the book. "Build something. The House would like to see what it lent against." She went to the door with the book under her arm, and stopped, and looked back down the table at Imani, at the end of it, by the door, with her sleeves pinned. "I believe," said Vantic, "that the first thing is already decided."

It was.



Imani had asked for the hospital in the third summer, at the table, in front of everyone. She had asked for it properly, with a drawing of her own and a list, and Ferra had read the cap out, line by line, and the hospital had not fitted under it by a long way, and Imani had sat very still at the end of the table and said that she understood, in a voice that had told everyone she did, and that she would ask again.

She did not have to ask again. Emeric broke the ground that same afternoon, the first of December, with a spade, in front of her, and took the boat upriver to the shaft the next morning.

The site was the long slope below the inner ward on its south-east side, where the plague rows had been. The rows had been moved out to the hollow below the wall in the first year, every one of them, and the ground had been limed and turned and left, and nothing had been put on it since, because nobody in Kaldo wanted to live where the rows had been and nobody had needed to. It was high enough to drain. It was below the tower, so the tank would push clean water down to it without a pump. It was the right place. Imani had said so in the third summer. She had also said that it was the right place because it was the one piece of ground in Kaldo that nobody would fight her for, and that she would rather the dead had the hospital than the living had nothing.

So he broke the ground there. One spadeful, in the cold, at noon, with the whole Standing Order standing round in their coats and half the lower ward come up the slope behind them to watch. He turned the turf, and turned it over, and stepped back, and gave the spade to Imani.

She took it. She looked at it as if somebody had handed her a thing she had wanted for a long time and had stopped believing she would get.

Then she put it into the ground and turned a spadeful of her own, harder than his, and deeper, and stood leaning on the spade over the dark earth with her breath going up white in the cold, and did not say anything.

The lower ward cheered. It was a thin cheer, at first, because the lower ward did not know what a hospital was, exactly, except that it was the healer's and that it was where the rows had been. Then somebody at the back, a woman, called out Imani's name. And then the cheer was not thin any more.

Imani did not look round at it. She stood with her hands on the spade and her face down, and Emeric, beside her, saw her jaw set, and stay set, and saw her not cry, which was a thing he had not known she could do so hard.



The hospital had its own water. That was the thing Imani had asked for first in the third summer, before beds, before rooms, before anything, and the thing she had been most certain would be refused: clean water, its own, not carried, not shared with a standpipe that half the upper town used. A pipe off the tower tank. The tower stood above the slope, on the highest point of the inner ward, and the water in it had been sand-filtered and boiled and stood cooling in its cement skin, and a pipe from the bottom of the tank down the slope to the hospital would bring it to every room with the whole weight of the tower behind it.

Bram laid the main himself, in the second week of December, in a trench Ward's levy dug through the frost. Clay pipe, glazed inside, the joints packed with the new cement. It went from the foot of the tower down the slope to where the hospital would be, and ended in a brass tap on a stone post in the middle of an empty field of turned earth, which was the strangest-looking thing in Kaldo, and Imani went up to it on the day it was finished and turned it on and watched the water come out, clear and hard and cold, onto the frozen ground, until her hand on the brass had gone numb.



The second thing built without a cap went up the hill to the mines.

It was Bram's and Tess's second water-tube boiler, the twin of Somebody Came's, with a small upright engine on it of the same pattern, and it went up to Kaldo's own lead and zinc workings on the hill on the barony's biggest cart behind six of Hollis's oxen, in three days, with Bram walking beside the cart the whole way and Hollis walking at the oxen's heads complaining about his oxen.

Kaldo's mines flooded every winter. They had flooded every winter since the plague emptied them, and years ago Emeric had stood at the head of the lower working and watched the water come up the shaft a hand's breadth a day, and built the treadle pump, and when the treadle pump could only hold April's water he had taken the four gravity stones out of the springs of his own carriage and put them in tipping cradles on the ends of a great timber beam, and made the beam rock, and pump. It had run while he kept coming back to fill the stones. It had stopped on the fourth day after he left. The lower working had gone under to the turn.

He had come home from that and looked at the beam on the hill, and seen what he had built: the capital's sky-ships, which hung over the palace on festival days and came down the moment the mage who kept them filled walked away.

The beam was still there. The tumbling bob was still there, the peg and lever that had turned the stones over at the end of each stroke, which the hill's carpenter had made and named, and which nobody had used since. The treadles were still under it.

Bram did not take any of it down. He put the engine beside the beam on a footing of the new cement, and coupled the engine's crank to the beam's end with an iron rod, and took the tumbling bob's lever and used it, exactly, as the model for the engine's valve trip: the same peg, the same throw, the same tip-and-turn at the end of every stroke, now opening and shutting a steam valve instead of turning a stone over in a cradle. He showed Emeric. He did not explain it. He stood beside the engine with his arms folded and let Emeric see it for himself.

Emeric saw it. The beam his stones had rocked, rocked by steam. On a lever a carpenter had made for a magic that had failed.

They lit her on the twentieth. The beam went up, and down, and up, slow and heavy, and the pump rods went down the shaft with it, and a quarter of an hour later the water came up out of the pipe at the shaft head in a steady brown gush into the drain, and went on coming, and did not stop when Emeric walked away down the hill, or when he came back the next morning, or the morning after that.

Tess was at the shaft head on the third morning, in her chair, wrapped to the chin, because Bram had brought her up on the cart to see it. She watched the beam for an hour. She did not write anything. Then she asked to be taken home, and Bram took her, and Emeric, coming down the hill behind them at dusk, found out why when he went into the dyehouse.

The wall was still there. The doctrine at the top of it in chalk, years old now and smudged, and the list under it: every job in Kaldo that only the prince could do, and beside each one, when something else had learned to do it, a name and a date and a line through it. Harl's belt. Bram's pipe. The cold store, with Armand's name in Armand's own hand. And the beam on the hill, which had never had a line. Beside it, where a name should have gone, Tess had written in the second summer, in her careful hand: the treadles. And under that, smaller: not a fix.

She was at the bench in her chair with the chalk in her hand. She had drawn a line through the beam. Under not a fix she had written one word.

Steam.

She did not look round when he came in. She looked at the word a while longer. Then she put the chalk down on the bench and wheeled herself out past him into the yard.



The third thing was the castle.

That one was in the open, and Emeric made sure it was. It was the keep, and the keep had not had water above its ground floor since it was built, or drains anywhere but a stone chute on the river side that emptied into the moat ring, and Ferra had been saying for a year that the holder of four baronies could not go on living in a building where the Chancellor carried her own wash water up two flights of stairs. So the keep would be replumbed. Pipes from the tower tank up through every floor. Drains down through every floor to the new trunk. Taps on the stairs. A boiler in the cellar for hot water, which nobody in Kaldo had ever had out of a tap, and which Maren said she would believe when she put her hands in it.

It was a job that would take years. It would need a great deal of iron and copper and lead and brass, and crews of Bram's men going up and down the keep stair with tools and pipe for years, and nobody in Kaldo would think twice about any of it. Ferra put it in the book on its own line, with an allowance against it that was generous and real, and Vantic read the line and initialled it and said that it was the most reasonable thing the barony had spent money on since she came, and that she would like a tap on the east tower stair.

She got one. It was the first one in.

And behind it, at night, in the cellar, the digging began.



The castle cellar ran under the whole of the keep: a long low vaulted place of old brick, cold, smelling of the river, that had been the old baron's wine cellar and still had his racks along one wall with a few dusty bottles in them that nobody had dared to open. Bram had set up a bench at the front of it in November for the renovation, for locks and hinges and pipe collars and lamp mounts, and in the day there were men in it, and the sound of filing, and nobody thought anything of it.

At the back of it there was a wall.

Old brick, like the rest, with the racks against it. And behind the wall, Emeric knew from his sense, from standing in front of it with his hand on the brick and letting himself go out thin into the stone, there was not more cellar. There was rock. The keep stood on the rock of the inner ward, and the cellar had been cut down into the top of it, and behind the back wall the rock went on, solid, grey, with a seam of softer stuff running through it where water had got in once and gone out again a long time ago.

He meant to have a room there. Nobody but four people knew why.

They dug it themselves. There was nobody else who could be trusted to, yet, and he would not have asked anybody else. Emeric and Ward and Bram, three nights a week, after the keep was asleep, with picks and bars and shovels and a lamp stone in a cage, behind a door Bram had hung across the back of the cellar that looked like a door to a coal hole and had a lock nobody else had a key to.

The first night, Bram swung a pick at the rock for an hour and made a hole the size of his head, and sat down on the cellar floor with his back against the wall and said that he was a smith, not a miner, and that at this rate the room would be ready for his grandchildren.

Ward did not sit down. Ward took the pick from him.

Emeric felt him do it. Ward drew on himself the way a knight did, inward, into his arms and his back and his shoulders, but not the way Emeric had felt him draw in the passage, where the bracer had gone bright and hot and Ward had moved too fast to follow. This was low. Very low. A thin steady draw, like a lamp turned down to its smallest flame, so low that a man could hold it for hours instead of minutes. And Ward swung the pick, and the rock came away in a slab the size of a door step, and he swung it again.

He did not stop for an hour. When he did, there was a heap of broken rock behind him the height of his knee, and he was sweating, and breathing hard, and not tired.

"Low," said Ward, when he saw Emeric looking. "A knight learns to hold it low on campaign. You cannot fight at full draw for a day. You can march at low draw for a week." He leaned on the pick. "Most knights forget it, after the war. It is dull."

Bram was looking at the heap of rock with his mouth open.

Emeric picked up the second pick.

He did it the way he had done the step in his room: he did not push. He saw himself swing the pick and saw the rock come away, the way Rylan had said, and he let the mana go into his arms and his back and his shoulders, and then, because Ward had just shown him how, he turned it down. Down and down, until it was a thread, until it was nearly nothing, a warmth in the muscle that he could feel was there and could not have said was doing anything.

He swung. The pick went into the rock and the rock came away. Not a slab like Ward's. A good piece, a big piece, more than Bram's.

He swung again.

He swung for an hour, side by side with Ward, at the back of the cellar, at low draw, with the lamp stone in its cage throwing their shadows huge on the vault, and when he stopped there were two heaps behind them, Ward's and his, and his was smaller than Ward's by about a third, and his arms and back burned with the deep dull ache he knew from the shutters, and he was not tired either.

Bram had not moved from the floor.

He sat with his back against the wall and looked at Emeric's heap, and then at Emeric, and then at Ward. Ward looked back at him, and did not say anything, and did not need to.

"You dig like Ward," said Bram, at last.

"I've been drilling with him for four years."

"Drilling," said Bram. He looked at the heap. He looked at it for a while, the way he looked at a casting that had come out of the sand better than it should have. Then he got up, slowly, with his hand on the wall, and picked up a shovel, and began to move the broken rock into a barrow, and did not say anything else about it, then or afterward. But the next night when they came down to the cellar he had brought a fourth pick, and a whetstone, and he sharpened all four before anybody swung one, and handed Emeric his without looking at him, the way he handed a tool to a man he had decided to stop asking questions about.

The spoil went out of the cellar by day, in the renovation's barrows, up the cellar stair and out through the yard to the heap where the keep's old plaster and broken brick were piling up for the new footings, and nobody counted it, because it was building rubble, and the keep was being rebuilt.



Imani came to the hospital ground on the evening of the eighteenth with a jar of water.

The ground was dark and empty, turned earth and frost and the brass tap on its stone post, and a shed Bram's men had put up at the top corner for the tools and the plans, with a lamp in it. Emeric was in the shed, at the plans. He had been there most evenings, because it was the one place in Kaldo that week where nobody would come to him with anything that was not the hospital.

She came in without knocking and put the jar on the plans.

"The canal ring," she said. "Under the third bridge. Where the children skate."

She had her brass hinge with her, the bead in its two plates, in a fold of linen in her pocket. She had carried it, Emeric had learned from Ferra, every day since April. She took it out now and unwrapped it, and took the pin, and put a drop of the canal water on the end of the pin with the tip of a straw, the way he had shown her in April, and sat down on the stool at the end of the plan bench under the lamp, and held the bead to her eye.

He did not say anything. He did not know why she had come to show him this, of all the things she could have shown him, and he did not ask.

She looked a long while. Then she held the brass out to him without taking her eye off the jar.

He took it. He looked.

The drop was alive. It was more alive than the well water had been, more alive than anything he had seen through the bead in April. Long ones and round ones and the ones that turned, and a great many of a kind he had not seen before, small and quick and very many, swarming, so thick that the drop was grey with them, moving all together, like a crowd in a market.

"The children skate on that," said Imani. "In the winter. And swim in it in the summer. And their mothers draw water from it when the pump's frozen." She took the brass back from him. "I want to know which ones. Which of them make a child sick and which don't. I can't tell. I can see them, and I can't tell." She put a fresh drop on the pin. "So I'm going to draw every one of them. Every kind. And then I'm going to draw what's in the water at the ward from every child who comes in with the flux, and see which kinds are in both."

She held the bead to her eye again.

"It'll take all winter," she said.

"Yes."

"You don't have to stay."

He pulled the other stool up to the bench, and sat down on it, and took a sheet of the hospital's plan paper off the pile, and turned it over to the blank side, and took a stick of charcoal.

"Tell me what you see," he said. "I'll draw it. You look."

Imani took the bead away from her eye and looked at him across the bench under the lamp.

She did not say anything. She put the bead back to her eye.

"Long," she said. "Thin. With a tail at one end. Like a tadpole, but the tail's at the front. It's going round the edge of the drop as if it's looking for a way out."

He drew it.

They did it until the first candle had burned down and Imani lit the second from it without looking up. Then until the second had burned down past its middle. She told him and he drew. Sometimes she said no, wrong, the tail's longer, and he drew it again. Sometimes she took the charcoal out of his hand and drew it herself on the corner of the sheet, quickly, two lines, and gave the charcoal back. Neither of them said anything that was not about the drop. Her knee was against his under the bench, because the bench was narrow and the stools were close, and neither of them moved it.

When the second candle was down to the stub she put the bead on the linen and rubbed her eyes with the heels of both hands.

There were nineteen drawings on the back of the hospital's plan. Nineteen kinds.

She looked at them. Then she gathered them up, carefully, all of them, and folded them once, and put them inside her coat.

"Again tomorrow," she said. Not a question.

"Again tomorrow."

She wrapped the bead in its linen and put it in her pocket. She stood up from the stool, and stood there a moment in the lamplight with her hand on the bench.

"Goodnight, Emeric," she said.

It was the voice from the ward in February. Not quite. Something had gone out of it, or something had come into it; he could not tell which. She went out of the shed into the frost and the dark, and he sat at the bench with the charcoal in his hand and the stub of the second candle guttering, and did not get up for a long while.
