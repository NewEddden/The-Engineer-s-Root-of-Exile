Chapter 131 - What Was Promised

The thaw came to Kaldo from underneath.

It came up out of the lake first, in the last weeks of the winter, when the ice along the lower edges of the island went dark and soft, and then grey, and then one morning was water again with a skin of slush riding on it that knocked against the piles of the Left Bridge all day with a sound like somebody tapping a spoon. The snow held on the roofs a fortnight longer. It came off them all at once, in the night, in long slides that woke half the town, and in the morning the gutters of the long street were running and the children were out in them to the ankle, and every mother on the island was shouting at them to come out.

Rylan was on the western wall.

He was on it most mornings now, at the first watch, in the black plate with the cloak drawn over it and the hood up, walking the stretch from the gate tower to the corner and back at the pace a man walks a wall when he means to be seen walking it. Emeric saw him from the bridge on his way down to the quay, a dark shape against the grey where the wall met the sky, and a carter coming the other way over the bridge saw him too and did not look twice, and neither did the woman behind the carter with a basket of eggs, and neither did the boy behind her.

Nobody had said anything about it. Not in the long room, not on the wall, not in the square. Declan had not said anything, which was a thing the whole garrison had noticed and nobody had mentioned either. It was how the barony said it had accepted a thing. It stopped talking about it.

Emeric went on down to the quay.

The sulphur came in on the ninth day of the thaw.

Two coasting boats, hired, Saban's hire under the lane terms, came in over the bar on the morning tide with their holds full and the canvas lashed down over the hatches, and lay at the quay at the lower water while the lightermen took it off them in baskets. It was yellow. Emeric had known it would be yellow, and it was still a surprise to see it, flat pale yellow in the grey morning, in lumps the size of a fist and in dust, coming up out of a black hold into the thaw light like something that had been kept too long in a cellar and did not approve of the day. It smelled faintly of a struck match. By the second hour everybody on the quay smelled faintly of a struck match.

Ferra had come down on the steamboat with the harbour scale's book under her arm and two of her clerks, and she stood at the beam scale for the whole of the morning and had every basket weighed. The children of the lower water came to look, because children came to look at anything that came off a boat, and the lightermen sent them off, and they came back, and Emeric told the nearest of them, a girl of about eight with a baby on her hip, that the yellow dust would sting her eyes worse than smoke and that if the baby put any of it in its mouth she would hear about it from her mother and from him. She considered this, and went back up the quay to the others, and he heard her tell them, and heard them not believe her, and saw them stay off it anyway.

He told the lightermen there was to be no pipe lit on the quay until the last basket was under cover. They looked at each other. One of them, an old man with a face like a walnut, said he had carried pitch and lamp oil and powdered lime off boats for forty years and never been told not to smoke by a baron. Emeric said that was because none of those had ever been this, and the old man looked at the yellow in the basket at his feet for a while and put his pipe away.

Ferra had it at the end of the morning.

"Forty-one tons and six hundredweight," she said, "between the two holds. At Saban's figure at her quay, and the freight, and the dues, which the terms make a Copper the ton." She turned the book so he could see it, as she always did, though he could read it upside down and she knew it. "Twenty Gold Rand, seven Silver, and four Copper. Landed."

"For forty tons."

"For forty-one tons and six hundredweight." She closed the book. "Saban says the second pair of boats will be cheaper because the masters have now been paid once and believe it."

He looked at the heap under the canvas at the head of the quay, where the lightermen had built it. He had never in either of his lives stood beside forty tons of sulphur. It was a heap about the size of a cottage. It did not look like very much, for a thing he had flown five hundred miles for.

"It'll look like less by the autumn," Ferra said, and went up the gangway onto the steamboat to be out of the smell.

They sat that evening in the long room, the six of them and himself, at the end of the table nearest the fire, with the garrison's supper cleared off the other end and the boards scrubbed.

Ferra had copied the terms onto a single sheet. Not the terms as written, which ran to four pages in her hand and the Treasurer's, but the terms read backwards, as what Kaldo owed. She had pinned the sheet to the wall above the table with two tacks, so that it hung where everybody could see it, and she read it out, down the left side of the page, in her flat voice, as a column.

Presses. Paper. Books. Men to start the school, and the men to teach the men. The well standard, the measures, the drains. All of it in Rowena's name, not Kaldo's.

Down the right side of the sheet, against each line, she had written what Kaldo had.

The standards were written. The drains were Imani's, and Imani had men who had dug Kaldo's and could dig anybody's, if they could be spared, which was a separate line. Orrin had said he would give up one teacher, and had said it with his face set in a way that said he would give up one and not two. The books could be printed. Those lines Ferra read quickly.

Then she came to the paper, and stopped.

"We make forty quires a week in a good week," she said. "Neta's vat and the finishing shop. Nearer thirty in a bad one. The keep's clerks use eighteen. The four baronies' writs use nine. Orrin's primers use the rest and then ask for more, and the printer can print faster than we can feed it, and has been standing idle two days in six since the autumn for want of sheets." She put her finger on the line. "That's before a single ream goes west. A duchy's clerks. A duchy's school. That's a harbour's worth of warrants and dues, every day, on paper, because we told her that's what makes paper outlive the person who wrote it."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"We don't make enough paper for Kaldo," Ferra said. "We promised it to a duchy."

"We knew that at the table," said Saban.

"We knew it as a figure," she said. "I'm reading it to you as a line."

Armand said the thing about the man at the gate while Ferra was pinning the sheet back where it had lifted at the corner.

He said it the way he said the weather, looking at nobody in particular, with both hands folded on the head of his stick.

"While you were away," he said, "a man came to the castle gate and asked for Ward by name. When he was told Ward was not there, he asked for the grey robe." He paused, very slightly. "I have not been called the grey robe since the capital. I had supposed there was nobody in the south who knew to call me it."

"What did he want?" said Saban.

"To leave a box. He would not leave a name. He would not come in." Armand turned his stick a quarter turn on the flags. "I gave it to Ward the night you came home. I mention it only because nobody has mentioned it since."

He did not look at Ward. Nobody else looked at Ward either, because Armand had not, and Ward sat where he always sat, at the corner of the table nearest the door, with his hands on the boards, and said nothing at all.

Emeric noticed it. He noticed that the box had come on the night they came home, and that it was now the end of the winter, and that Armand, who never said anything by accident, had chosen to say it here, at the table, in front of five people, as weather. He did not ask. He put it away in the place where he kept the things he would come back to, and saw Ward see him do it.

Ferra finished with the tack and sat down.

"The press can't go," Emeric said.

He said it because it had to be said by somebody and it was his to say. They looked at him.

"The printer runs on stones I write. Every page it's ever printed, I made first, in a place none of you can build in yet and nobody in Emberfell ever will." He put his hand flat on the table. "I told her none of it needs Kaldo. That's what she was buying. A printer that stops the day I stop isn't that. It's a leash with pages on it."

"So what does she get?" said Imani.

"A press. The old kind. A screw and a platen, a bed, a frame of type set by hand and inked with a ball and pulled one sheet at a time. Any smith who can mend a cider press can mend it. Any boy who can read can learn to set it." He looked at Bram. "It's slower than ours by a hundred times, and it's hers."

"Type," Bram said.

"Cast type. Every letter, every size, in its own small block, a few thousand of them to a case." He had drawn it in his head on the way home over the sea and had not liked what he drew. "You cut a punch for each letter, in steel. You strike the punch into a bar of copper, and that's the mould. You pour the type into the mould and dress it. Nevin cuts the punches. He'll be cutting them for a year."

"Nevin cuts stones," said Ferra.

"Nevin cuts anything small that has to be exactly one shape," Emeric said. "He'll hate it. He'll be the best in the south at it inside a season."

Bram was looking at the tabletop.

"Poured out of what?" he said.

It was the right question. Emeric had hoped for about another quarter of an hour before somebody asked it.

"Lead," said Bram, answering it himself. "It's lead, the letters. Lead and a little something to harden it. It has to run into a mould that fine and not shrink off the sides." He looked up. "We've no lead. Not a cart of it. There's what's on the church roof and what the tinker has in his box and that's the barony."

"No," Emeric said. "We haven't."

Bram nodded, as though that were an answer, and it was the only one there was, and the table let it lie on the boards between them where he had put it.

"Then what's the sulphur for," said Saban, "if not the press?"

"Ink, first."

He told them plainly, because it was plain. The iron salt that made the ink go black came out of the old spoil heaps behind the foundry, where the rain had drawn it out of the ore over nine years. A boy with a spade and a barrel, digging it out of the cracks. It had been enough for a trial. It would not be enough for a works.

"You burn sulphur over the heap," he said, "and let the fumes and the rain work through the old spoil, and leach the ground afterwards, and you get it in barrels instead of by the spadeful. Green crystals. The same salt. Without a boy on his knees in the cold picking it out with his fingers."

Ferra wrote it down.

"And the paper," he said. "Every sheet we've ever made is rag. Somebody's old shirt. Every rag in the south goes through a handcart and a scale and a boy paid by the pound, and there are only so many shirts." He turned his hand over. "Cook straw in water with sulphur burned into it, and a little lime, long enough, at the right strength, and the straw comes apart into fibre. Pulp. It won't make a sheet as good as rag. It'll make a great many more of them."

"Straw," said Bram.

"We grow a mountain of it every autumn," Emeric said. "We stack it and thatch with it and feed it to cattle and let the rest rot in the yard."

Bram sat back and looked at the ceiling, and Emeric watched him see it: the ricks behind every farm between the lake and the Milford road, the carts that hauled it in and the carts that hauled it out again as dung, all of it, every year, a mountain. Bram did not say anything else. He did not have to.

There was a third thing sulphur was for, and it was the one sulphur was famous for, in the world he had come from. Emeric thought of it, sitting there at his own table with the fire going. He thought of it clearly, the whole shape of it, and then he put it back where he had got it from, and did not say it.

He wrote to Rowena before the table broke.

He had meant to do it for a week and had put it off because he did not know what to say, and found when he sat down with the pen that he did know, and that it was short.

The sulphur came. Forty-one tons, weighed, and none of it lost. The heap is the size of a cottage on the quay at the lower water and the whole town smells of it.

I will come, as I said I would, in a boat, slowly, so that you can see me coming up the inlet. But it will be a Kaldo boat. I will not come in anybody else's. So it will be some time.

He read it over, and thought about adding something, and did not. He gave it to Ferra to copy fair, because his hand was a clerk's hand and a sovereign ought to get a Chancellor's, and she drew the lamp closer and began.

Bram came round the table to read it over her shoulder.

He did it the way he read everything of hers, standing close enough that she knew he was there and far enough that she could not reasonably object, and he read the whole of it, slowly, moving his lips a little at a Kaldo boat. Then he straightened up.

"A Kaldo boat," he said.

"Yes."

"The one we've got was Fletcher's. You bought it off the stands and put the rest in after." He did not look toward the lower water, where she lay behind the bar. "A Kaldo boat's a Kaldo hull. A hull's plate. That's a hull's worth of plate, and more if she's to carry what Emberfell sends back." He put one finger on the table. "The mill rolls sheet a few lengths a week. Most of it's promised to the rail, and the rail's promised to Grayrock, and Grayrock's promised to everything."

"I know."

"You don't," Bram said, without heat. "You know the figure. I stand at the rolls." He took a folded sheaf out of his apron. It was rail tallies, the long narrow sheets Jorrin kept for the rolled lengths, and he turned them over on the table in front of Emeric so that the backs showed.

The backs were drawn on. Every one of them.

It was a vessel. A great pot of iron plate, riveted, the shape of a pear lying on its side, lined thick inside with what Bram had written as Colm's brick and underlined twice. It stood on two trunnions so it could tip. There was a pipe into the bottom of it, and a row of holes in the bottom where the pipe came in, and a note in Bram's square hand beside the holes that said air through the iron, not over it. There were six drawings of the same thing, each a little different from the one before, the way a man draws a thing when he has been drawing it in the evenings for months and does not trust any of them.

"Since the autumn," Bram said. "Since you told me why the rail's slow. It's not the rolls. It's the iron. It's the time it takes to make iron into steel a bar at a time in the forge. You said you could blow the stuff out of it all at once, with air, if the air went through the melt and not over it. You said it once, at the foundry door, to Jorrin, and you went off to build a ship." He tapped the top drawing. "That's what it'd look like. I think. Tell me where I'm wrong."

Emeric looked at the six drawings for a long time.

Bram was not wrong. Not in the shape. He was wrong in two places in the lining and one in the tuyeres, and he had put the trunnions where they would make the thing impossible to pour, and he had drawn it three times the size a first one ought to be. None of that mattered. The man had taken one sentence said in a doorway nearly a year ago and built it on the backs of his own tallies in the evenings, alone, without asking anybody where it came from, because he had stopped asking that years ago.

"Yes," Emeric said.

Bram waited.

"Yes. Build it. Smaller than that. I'll show you where." He put his hand on the tallies. "Build it."

"You've not priced it," said Ferra, without looking up from the fair copy.

"No."

"I'll price it." She finished the line she was on, and blotted it, and pulled the tallies across the table to her and turned the top one to the lamp and read Bram's drawing the way she read everything, as things that cost money. It took her about as long as it had taken Emeric to look at it. She asked Bram three questions: how much plate in the pot, how much brick, and what blew the air. Bram said a shaft off the works' own wheel, a bellows the size of a cart, and she wrote that down.

"The pot, riveted, eleven Gold," she said. "Brick for it and for the spare lining, because there will be a spare lining, four. The bellows and the shaft gear, nine. A coke oven, since it can't be charcoal, and you'd want Grayrock's coal and not the woods', six. Bram's time and three men's for a season, and the loss of what the mill doesn't roll while they're at it." She wrote the figure at the foot. "Thirty-eight Gold Rand, and something over. Out of the lane money, if the lane runs. Out of the stone money if it doesn't, and there's none of that left, so the lane had better run."

She turned the tally round so they could both see the figure.

"Thirty-eight," Bram said.

"And something over."

He looked at it, and then at Emeric, and then took the tallies back off the table and folded them and put them into his apron again, all but the top one, which Ferra kept.

It was later, with the fair copy sanded and sealed and the lamp burned low, that Emeric asked Saban.

He asked it idly, the way a man asks a thing he already knows the answer to, because he was thinking about forty quires a week against a duchy and wanted the scale of the other side.

"Where does the south's paper come from now? All of it. Not ours."

"Wayford," said Saban. "Through the junction. There's nowhere else south of the capital that makes any to speak of."

"Then it's ours already," Emeric said. "We own Wayford's vats."

Saban's face did something small.

It was not much. It was the kind of thing Emeric had spent ten years learning to see on Ferra, a very slight stillness, the look of a man who has been handed a sum and has seen that the sum is right and the question is wrong. Saban did not say anything. He picked up his cup and drank from it, and put it down, and said that the second pair of boats would be in within the month.

Emeric looked at him a moment longer, and let it go, because it was late.

The others went to bed. Ward did not.

He sat at his corner of the table with his hands on the boards until the last of them had gone out, Imani last, with her shawl, and the door had shut behind her, and the fire had gone down to a bed of red. Then he got up and went out of the long room by the far door, and was gone about as long as it takes to climb a stair and come down it, and came back with a box under his arm, and put it on the table in front of Emeric.

It was plain wood. Pale, fine-grained, the joints dovetailed and not nailed, the lid fitted so close that it had to be lifted straight up. There was no seal on it that Emeric knew. There was no seal on it at all. There was a knot of cord round it, cut.

"Six weeks," Emeric said.

"Six weeks." Ward sat down across from him. "Armand was right to say it. I'd have sat on it another six."

He lifted the lid straight up and set it aside.

Inside, folded round what it held, was a piece of cloth. It had been a lining of something once, dark green silk gone soft and pale along its creases, with a thread of silver worked in a border that had mostly come away. Emeric did not know it. He looked at it and felt nothing, and then felt the nothing, which was worse, a smooth place where there should have been a thing.

"It was hers," said Ward. "She kept her combs in it."

Emeric unfolded it.

There were two stones.

Grey, dull, smaller than the first joint of his thumb, and carved over every face in a fine repeating lattice that had not gone soft at the edges, because nobody had ever handled them. He knew the lattice before he knew anything else about them. He had carried it in his pocket for ten years. He had seen it on seven more, set down on a banquet table with both hands in front of two hundred people by a brother who had called them unchanging.

One of them had lost a corner. It was an old break, clean, the size of a grain of barley, and the lattice ran up to the edge of it on every face and stopped, and the broken face was bare grey stone with nothing on it at all, as if somebody had dropped it on a hard floor a long time ago and picked it up again and put it away with the other.

Under the stones, folded small, was a slip of paper. It was a treasury inventory slip, the printed heads filled in by a clerk in a clean small hand he did not know.

Grey stones, carved. Two. Of no value.

There was no letter.

He sat and looked at them. He did not pick them up. After a while he found that his hand had gone to his coat, to his own, and he took it away.

"Tell me," he said.

Ward told it the way he reported a road. Flat, in order, from the start, and with nothing on it that did not have to be there.

"There's a road for letters," he said, "between me and your father. Nobody else uses it. One carrier. He carries my letters to the king and the king's to me, and nothing else, for nobody else, and he never goes near the post. He never has." He looked at the stones. "Sethe never touched it. He broke every road into Kaldo that he could find, and he never found that one, because it doesn't come into Kaldo. It comes to me."

"How long?"

"Since before you were born."

Emeric did not say anything.

"It goes in a cipher," Ward said. "Two men know it. Me. And a man called Kassim, who I went through the chamber with." He did not explain Kassim. He said the name and went on past it, the way a man on a road goes past a turning he knows and has no business down today. "Nobody else. Not your father's secretary. Not your father, if it comes to it. He has Kassim read them to him."

"And you've been writing to him."

"And he to me."

"About me."

"About you," said Ward. "Among other things. Not many other things."

The fire shifted. Neither of them looked at it.

"He barred me," Ward said. "When you were sent south. He told me I was not to carry your troubles to him. Not your debts, not your enemies, not your mistakes. Nothing that would not kill you. He wanted you strong, and clever, and he said neither comes out of a boy with the king in his pocket." He said it without any weight, as a thing written down. "Unless you asked me yourself. If you ever came to me and asked, then I could carry it, whatever it was. That was the one door he left in it."

"I didn't know there was a door."

"No."

"You knew I didn't know."

"Yes," said Ward. "I knew you'd never ask for a thing you didn't know was there. I knew that was what he was counting on. And I kept quiet anyway." He met Emeric's eyes. "That's the part I'm sorry for. Not the rest. The rest was his to decide. That part was mine."

"He told me," Ward said, "that I could ask for anything by that road. Anything at all. And that if I didn't want to be asked why, I was to write no question at the foot of it, and he wouldn't ask. He's never asked, the times I've written it. There haven't been many."

"You wrote it on this."

"At the foot. Yes."

"When?"

Ward looked at the stones.

"The autumn of the survey," he said. "The fourth night, at the guest house under the hill. Bram had gone up. You were sitting at the fire with your mother's stone out, turning it over in your hand, the way you do when you're working at a thing you can't get at." He said it without any tenderness at all, which was how Emeric knew there was some. "I served under her. I watched her buy these. One at a time, over years, from dealers who thought she was a fool to pay for them. For you. When she died they went into the treasury with her jewels and her gowns, because nobody knew what else to do with them, and a clerk wrote that on a slip." He nodded at it. "I thought about that, watching you with yours. I had a sheet of our own paper in the back of my book. I wrote to him that night."

Emeric remembered it. Not the letter. He remembered Ward across the hearth, writing on a loose sheet from the back of his book, for a long time, and folding it in four and in four again, and putting it inside his coat on the left side. He had seen it and thought nothing of it, because Ward wrote things down and did not show anybody, and he had not asked, because Ward's book was Ward's.

"You didn't send it."

"I carried it a year." Ward's voice did not change. "It was your business. It wasn't mine to settle for you. Every time I went to send it I thought, if he wanted them he'd have asked, and then I thought, he can't ask, he doesn't know they're there, and round again." He moved one hand on the table. "Then you built the jewellery for Emberfell. You sat up three nights over it, and there wasn't a control stone in any of it, because there were six in the world and none to spare, and I watched you decide that at the forge. I sent it the week after. The week before we went." He looked at the box. "The answer came while we were in the air. He'd told the carrier to find me, and if he couldn't find me, to find the grey robe at the castle. Armand's the only other man in Kaldo he'd trust to put a thing into my hand unopened."

"He must have ridden."

"Both ways. He'll have killed a horse."

"Why you," Emeric said. "Why does he write to you."

Ward was quiet for a moment. Then he told that too, in two sentences, the way he would have given the length of a road he did not like.

"He found me on a road when I was a boy, and got me a posting at the capital, and trained me with his own hands, and didn't want me in the chamber, and I went anyway, because I wasn't going to be weaker than the men next to me, and I wanted the strength to keep the man who'd picked me up and everybody under him. That's why he gave me to your mother, and that's why Rylan and Declan and Osric stand behind me without being told, because I went in years before any of them and came out."

It was the most Emeric had ever heard him say about himself, and it had taken about ten breaths.

He had known since the exile that Ward was his father's. He had known it on the first day, in the gallery, with the cold coming up through the boards. It had never troubled him. A king sends a son south with a guard, and the guard is the king's man; that was simply what a guard was.

He had not known his father was still writing.

To Ward. All these years. Every year of the ten. Letters going north and letters coming south on a road that went round the whole barony like a river round a stone, carrying his name, and not one of them to him. Not one. He had sat in the keep through the first winter with a garrison of four and a stipend spent down to the last few Gold, and his father had known, and had written to Ward about it, and had not written to him.

It stung. He let it sting. There did not seem to be anything else to do with it.

And then, because it was the next thing, he thought of Jory, and the other couriers, the ones who had died on the roads when Sethe was breaking the post, and of the letters to the Crown that had never arrived, and the silence out of the capital that he had read as his father's.

Ward said it before he could.

"The road carried his letters and mine," Ward said. "Nothing else. One man, one bag, two hands. It couldn't have carried theirs." He stopped. "That's true, and it doesn't make it better, and I'm not going to tell you it does."

"No," Emeric said.

They sat.

"If I'd known," Emeric said at last, "I wouldn't have used it."

Ward did not say anything.

"Not for the post. Not for stones. Not for any of it." He heard himself and it was true, and he went on because it was true. "You don't spend a thing like that on a road that's broken or a box of rocks. You keep it for the day there's nothing else. The day there's no other road at all. If I'd known it was there I'd have spent ten years not using it, and every one of those years I'd have been leaning on it without meaning to, knowing it was there." He looked at the box. "I'd have been weaker for it. He was right."

"He said you'd say that," said Ward.

Emeric looked at him.

"In the answer," Ward said. "There's no letter in the box because there was one to me. Two lines. The second was that. He will tell you he would not have used it." He did not say what the first line was, and Emeric did not ask.

There was one question he did ask.

"What have you told him? All these years."

Ward considered it with the same care he gave everything, and answered it the same way.

"What a father asks after a son."

Emeric turned that over. It could mean anything. It could mean that his father knew the price of every brick in the keep and the name of every man on the wall. It could mean that his father knew he was alive and eating. He thought of the forge, and the ship, and the band, and the suits in the pattern store, and a room inside a stone that nobody in the world was supposed to know existed, and he did not know, sitting there, which of those Ward had counted as a thing a father asks.

He did not press it. Not tonight. He could see that Ward knew he had not pressed it, and that Ward would answer it whenever he was asked properly, and that it would be a longer answer than this one. It was enough, tonight, to know there was a question.

What the stones meant came last, and fast.

He had been sitting with them in front of him for most of an hour, and he had looked at them as his mother's, and as his father's silence, and as Ward's apology, and he had not once looked at them as what they were. Then he did, all at once, the way a sum comes out.

Two Hollow Crystals. Unbound. Nobody's.

There had been six in the world, and none to spare, and he had built a whole winter's work around that, the jewellery with no control stone in it, the watchdog on the suits built round a stone he could not risk, the forge's rule that a stone which held anything could not be shaped, and the question under it that he had written at the back of the notebook and not been able to answer: what did the forge do to a control stone, and what did it do to the man it was bound to.

Now there were two he could risk.

He put his hand out and touched the nearer of them with one finger, and felt nothing from it, nothing at all, the plain dead weight of a stone that had never been anybody's. Then he took his hand back.

"They go to Ferra's strongbox," he said. "Tonight."

"She's up," said Ward. "Her lamp's lit. I'll fetch her."

He went out.

Emeric sat alone in the long room with the fire down to its last red and the lamp guttering, and did not move. The two grey stones lay in their lining on the scrubbed boards in front of him, on green silk that his mother had kept her combs in, beside a slip that said they were worth nothing. Above them on the wall, pinned at the corners with two tacks, Ferra's sheet hung in the last of the light: presses, paper, books, men, standards, drains, and down the right-hand side, in her hand, against every line, what Kaldo had.
