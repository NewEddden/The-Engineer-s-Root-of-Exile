Chapter 157 - Up the Inlet

She went down the lower water on the morning tide with her fires lit before light and the whole of the lower water on the quay to see her off, and she went slowly, because that was what she was.

Emeric stood at her rail with Ward and watched the quay go by. Fletcher at the foot of the slip with his hands in his coat, not waving. Abloh beside him, who did wave. The lightermen's wives. A great many children who had been told the boat was going to a mountain that burned. And the empty slip where she had stood since the autumn, with the tarred cloth that had covered her frames all winter lying folded on the stocks, and beside it, already, the chalk lines on the ground where Fletcher meant to lay whatever he laid next.

Then the marsh, and the channel through it, and the coast.

She was the first hull Kaldo had ever built.

He walked her on the first morning at sea from her stem to her stern, the way he had walked her on the quay, and she was the same. Fletcher's lines, long and lean and deep for the swell, a hundred and ten feet on the deck. Kaldo's plate, riveted on Fletcher's frames, painted black. Bram's boiler amidships, in its own compartment behind a bulkhead of the same plate, with a copy of the steamboat's engine abaft it and a shaft running back to a screw that Tobe had filed. A mast forward with Abloh's gaff sail furled on it, for when the coal ran low and the wind was fair. Coal in the bunkers either side of the boiler, enough for the run and a third again, because he had amended his own rule on the ship and meant to keep the amended one on this too. A hold forward of the boiler with the press crates and the reams and the type case and a ballast of blue-grey stone from the north end of the ridge.

No stone in her. Not one. He had checked twice.

Her master was Hamo Kerrow, who had sailed the coast for twenty years in other men's boats before he came home to the lower water and took the steamboat up and down the river for two, and who stood at her wheel on the first morning with his feet apart and his hands easy and an expression on his face that Emeric had not seen on anybody since Fletcher at the helm of the ship on the day she left the water. Kerrow had never had a boat that went where he pointed it without asking the wind first.

"She rolls," said Kerrow, at noon.

"She does."

"She rolls like a pig in a ditch. It's the weight low down. The boiler." He did not sound displeased. "Fletcher'll want to hear it. He'll want to hear all of it."

"Tell him all of it."

"I'm writing it," said Kerrow, and touched the book in his coat, which Ferra had given him on the quay with instructions.

It was six days down the coast at a coaster's pace.

Six days. He could have put the ship into the band off the lower water after supper on the first night and been over Emberfell's inlet before the moon was down. He knew exactly how long it would have taken, to the quarter glass, because he had done it. He stood at the coaster's rail on the second evening with the coast going by at the pace of a man walking fast, a low grey line of dunes and marsh and headland, and thought about it, once.

The ship would have been there by supper. Rowena had not asked for supper. She had asked, at the foot of a ladder on her own quay, with her knights between her and the slope, to watch a Kaldo boat come up the inlet slowly, like anybody, for a day and a half, so that she would have time to decide what to say.

So he stood at the rail and watched the coast go by at the pace of a man walking fast, and found after a day that he did not mind it.

Nobody aboard had anything to do. That was the strangest part of it. On the ship there was always something: the racks, the levers, the air, the next climb. On the coaster there was Kerrow's crew, who knew their work and did it, and the engine, which Bram's man minded and which did not need minding, and the sea. Saban played dice with the lightermen and lost on purpose, twice, and then stopped losing. Nevin sat in the lee of the deckhouse with a lens and a punch and a file and cut a small g, because he said it was the letter that would go wrong first and a punch-cutter who could not cut a g on a moving deck could not cut one on a still bench either. Merrit, the teacher, read the primer backwards to the ship's boy, who could not read it forwards, and by the fourth day he could read it both ways. Ansel the drains man was sick from the first swell to the last and said, whenever anybody asked, that he was very well.

Ward stood at the rail.

He stood where he could see the whole deck and the sea on both sides, and he did it all day, every day, the way he stood everywhere. But on the third evening he said, without turning his head, "This is the first time I've been on the sea since the war."

"On the sea," said Emeric. "Not over it."

"On it." Ward watched a wave go under the bow. "We came down this coast in a hired boat in the war, with Rowena's column. It was a boat like this. Smaller. It rolled like this."

He did not say anything else about it.

Rylan said nothing about anything. He stood his watches with the crew because he had asked to, and he slept on the deck in the lee of the boiler house because it was warm there, and once, on the fifth night, Emeric came up for air in the small hours and found him sitting against the bulkhead with his knees up, looking at the stars, and did not disturb him.

The inlet opened on the seventh morning.

He knew it from the ship, from above: a long narrow arm of the sea running inland from the coast between steep dark hills, twisting, deepening, the water green and then black, with the mountain at the head of it, and the town on the mountain's lower slope, and the harbour at the town's foot. From above it had been a line on a map. From the deck of a coaster making six knots against the ebb it was a day and a half.

The hills came down to the water on both sides, black rock and scrub, and in places pale grey ash in long slopes where the mountain had put it down in some year nobody remembered. Fishing villages in the coves, a dozen houses each, with their boats drawn up. Men in the boats stopped and stood up to watch the coaster go by, black, smoking, with no sail set and no oars, and a white name on her stern that most of them could not read. Some of them could. Emeric saw a man in a skiff read it, and turn, and shout something to the next boat.

By the evening of the first day in the inlet the mountain was in front of them, filling the sky at the head of the water, with its long grey smoke going up off the top of it and bending east.

They anchored that night in a cove under the hills because Kerrow would not take an iron boat up strange water in the dark. And in the morning, very early, a boat came out from the town to meet them, with four oars and a man in the stern in Emberfell's grey, who called up to know who they were, and when Kerrow told him, looked up at the white letters on the stern for a long moment and then turned his boat round without a word and rowed back up the inlet as fast as four oars would go.

"He's gone to tell her," said Saban.

"She knows," said Emeric.

"She knows there's a boat. She doesn't know it's got her name on it."

She was on the quay.

He saw her from a mile off, as the coaster came round the last bend of the inlet into the harbour with the town going up the slope above it in its terraces and the smoke from the great works drifting across the water. The quay was full. It had been full the last time, when he came in the ship, with her guard drawn up and the harbour-mistress's men and half the town come down to see what the thing with no sail was. It was full this time too. But it was full differently. The first time the crowd had stood back from the water's edge. This time they were at it, all along the quay, packed, and the harbour boats were out on the water in a loose ring, and there was no guard drawn up at all.

There was a woman at the end of the quay in a blue coat, standing by herself.

She had her arms folded. She did not move. The coaster came up the harbour at half speed and then at slow, with Kerrow at the wheel and his crew on the deck with the lines, and she did not move, and she did not unfold her arms, and she did not stop watching it. She watched it the whole way in. Emeric at the rail watched her watching, and understood that she had been standing there since the boat with four oars came back, which was some hours, and that she would have stood there the rest of the day.

The coaster came alongside with a bump that Kerrow would write down, and the lines went ashore, and the harbour-mistress's men made her fast.

Rowena looked at the boat.

She did not look at him. She looked at the hull, the black plate with the rivets in their rows, and the boiler house, and the stack, and the mast with Abloh's sail furled on it, and the stern, where she could not see the name from where she stood and did not need to. She looked for a long time. Then she unfolded her arms and walked down the quay to the foot of the gangway as it came down, and stood at the foot of it, and put her hand flat on the black plate beside it, the way Emeric had put his on the upright of the press.

"She's iron," she said.

"She's Kaldo's," said Emeric, from the top of the gangway. "All of her. The plate's the first we ever rolled. The frames, the boiler, the engine, all of it. There's nothing in her that came from anywhere else."

"And nothing in her that needs you." She said it to the plate, not to him.

"No."

She took her hand off the hull.

"It was worth the wait," said Rowena.

She said it plainly. She looked up at him when she said it, and then past him, at the stack and the boiler house and the rivets, and he understood that she meant the boat. Not the prince. He did not mind that at all.

"Come up," she said. "You've brought me something heavy, I'm told. My harbour-mistress says your master sent word by the pilot that he won't have it lifted by anybody but his own men, and she's offended. Come up and tell her he's right."

The press went into a room off the harbour house.

It had been a sail loft once, and then a store for net, and it had a long floor of heavy boards and windows along the harbour side that let in the light off the water, and Rowena had had it cleared and limed in the spring after the letter came, and had not told anybody what for. Nevin walked into it and stood in the middle of the floor and looked at the windows and said, "Yes," and nobody had to ask what he meant.

It took four days to set up. Nevin did it with the lightermen and two of Rowena's carpenters and a man she sent him on the first morning, a big quiet journeyman smith named Tavy from the great works, who was to be her printer. Tavy did not know what a printer was. He had been told he was to be one by the head of the great works, who had been told by Rowena, and he had come down to the loft with his tools and his dinner and stood in the doorway while Nevin unpacked the type case, and when Nevin lifted the lid and he saw the four thousand little grey letters in their boxes he took his cap off.

Nevin taught him for four days.

Emeric came in on the second day and watched, standing by the door. Nevin did not talk much when he taught, and neither did Tavy, and most of it was done with hands: Nevin setting a line in the stick and holding it out, and Tavy looking at it, and taking it, and setting the next line himself, slowly, with his big blunt smith's fingers picking the letters out of their boxes one at a time as if they might bite. By the end of the second day he could set a line in the time it took to say two short prayers. By the end of the third he had stopped taking his cap off when he came in.

On the fourth day Rowena came down with her Treasurer and her harbour-mistress and the head of the great works and the square grey knight-captain, the whole of her table, and a sheet of paper in her own hand, which she gave to Tavy.

Tavy read it. He read it standing at the case, twice, with his lips moving. Then he looked at Nevin, and Nevin nodded, and he began to set it.

It took him all morning. Rowena did not leave. She sat on a stool by the window with her table standing round her and watched him do it, the whole time, while the harbour went on outside and the gulls came and went. Nevin stood at Tavy's shoulder and did not touch anything.

At noon Tavy locked the forme and inked it, slapping the balls together first the way he had been shown, and laid a damp sheet on the frame and folded it down, and slid the bed in under the platen.

He put both his hands on the bar and pulled.

The screw went down with its long wooden groan, the same groan it had made in the corner of the pattern store, and Emeric, by the door, heard it and felt something in his chest go tight, and did not let it show on his face.

Tavy lifted the sheet off the type by its corners and turned it over.

It was her law.

It was the law she had written years ago, after the arena, in her own hand, and kept in her own hand ever since, in a drawer, the only copy that had ever been made, because there had been nobody in Emberfell who could make another that she trusted. Anybody may be named. Blood is no claim. The table that confirms sits by office, and whoever holds the office sits. A blade raised over the chair, or raised on another's behalf, bars the man who raised it and his line from any claim, forever. No duel confers the seat. No victory is a claim.

It was there on the sheet, in black type, in Nevin's letters cut in a year of mornings in Kaldo, set by an Emberfell smith's hands and pressed on an Emberfell floor, and every letter of it was the same as every other letter of its kind, and nobody's hand had made any of it.

Rowena took the sheet from Tavy. She held it for a long time.

"How many?" she said.

"As many as you have paper for," said Nevin. "There's two hundred reams in the hold."

"Every hall in the duchy," said Rowena. "Every one. On the door." She did not look up from the sheet. "Every village with a headman. Every quay. Every chapel. I want there to be nowhere in Emberfell a man can stand and say he never saw it." She handed the sheet to her Treasurer, who took it the way a man takes a thing he has been told is valuable and has not yet understood. "And one in a box, sealed, in the harbour house strongroom, and one in the Treasury, and one in the knight-captains' hall."

"And one for Kaldo," said Emeric.

She looked at him, then.

"And one for Kaldo," said Rowena.

The knight-captain, the square grey man in the plain coat, had been standing at the back of the table the whole morning without speaking. He was the one who went to the case, afterward, when the others had gone up, and stood looking down at the letters in their boxes for a while. Then he put out one thick finger and touched the capital A, very lightly, in its box, as if it were hot.

The head of the great works took him up the mountain on the fifth day.

It was the same thin grey man with soot on his cuffs who had sat at Rowena's table in the autumn and said that his master would have hated a book of cracked furnaces. He did not say much on the way up either. They went up the works road on mules, past the great works itself with its smoke and its sheds and its heaps of yellow sulphur steaming in the sun, and on up above it, on a track Emeric had not walked before, through ground that was grey ash and black rock and nothing growing, to a place high on the mountain's side where the rock was warm under the mules' feet and the air smelled of sulphur and hot stone.

There was a crack in the mountain there. A long black lip of rock with a breath coming out of it, hot enough to shimmer, and round it the stone was stained yellow and white and red.

And beside it, built against the rock, there was a kiln.

It was not a big kiln. It was square, of Emberfell's black stone, with a draught cut through the mountain's own rock from the vent into its foot, so that the mountain's breath went up through it. There was no fuel in it. There was no fire. There was a charge of broken blue-grey stone in it, glowing a dull orange from the heat coming up from underneath, and a man with a long iron bar raking it.

"Your letter," said the head of the great works.

Emeric had written it in the thaw, the week the kiln-master on the flat had failed to get the blue stone hot enough. He had written to Rowena that the throat of the mountain she had promised him in the autumn for Kaldo's heat work was needed now, for one thing, and he had drawn a kiln on the back of the letter, and a draught, and sent them with the next of the sulphur boats going home in ballast.

"Your stone," said the head of the works, and pointed at the charge. "It comes over in the boats. They go home empty after the sulphur and they want weight in them or they roll, and now the weight's your stone off your ridge, and it's worth more than sand." He did not smile. "Nobody in the harbour can work out why a prince sends rocks across the sea to be cooked. They think it's a charm."

"It burns hot enough here."

"It burns hotter than anything I've ever burned. The mountain doesn't care how much you put in it." The thin man looked at the kiln. "And I've added a thing you didn't ask for. You'll want to know."

He led Emeric round the side of the kiln to a heap of pale grey powder under a lean-to, fine as flour, and put his hand into it and let it run out through his fingers.

"Ash," he said. "The mountain's. The old men who built the harbour wall, in my great-grandfather's time, mixed it with their lime, and the wall's still there, and it's under water twice a day, and nobody's ever pointed it since." He wiped his hand on his coat. "Your letter wanted lime that sets under water. I thought you'd better have both."

Emeric looked at the ash.

He knew what it was. He had known the moment the man put his hand in it. There had been a people in his old world who built harbours out of lime and the ash of a volcano, and their harbours were still standing under the sea two thousand years later, and he had read about it once, in another life, on a winter evening, and forgotten it until now.

He had not thought of the ash. He had thought of the stone, and the heat, and the kiln. He had not once thought that the mountain might have already answered the question before he asked it.

"Your great-grandfather's men knew," he said.

"They knew it worked," said the head of the great works. "Nobody knew why. Nobody wrote it down." He looked at the kiln, and then back at Emeric, with the same honest look he had given him across Rowena's table in the autumn. "I'm writing it down."

The lime came down the mountain on mules that afternoon, the first twenty sacks of it, and the ash beside it, and went into the coaster's hold where the stone had been. It was the first cargo Emberfell had ever sent to Kaldo that was not sulphur. It was for the footings of a city that did not exist yet, on a flat that was still mostly trenches, six days up the coast.

Rowena gave a dinner on the sixth night, in her hall.

It was the long room off the hall with the windows open on the mountain, with the table laid for twenty, and the lamps lit, and the smoke of the mountain going up red at the top against the dark. Her table sat by office, as it always sat. Her Treasurer on her right. The harbour-mistress on her left. The head of the great works, with his cuffs clean for once. The knight-captain. Emeric across from her, and Ward, and Saban, and Rylan at the far end beside a young knight of Rowena's who had asked him three questions about the war and got three answers of one word each and seemed pleased with all three. Nevin and Tavy side by side, not speaking, both of them with ink under their nails. Merrit, who had spent the week in Rowena's school with Kaldo's first teacher and a room of forty children, and who ate as if she had not eaten in a week.

It was a long dinner and a loud one.

Rowena and Ward talked across Emeric about the war for most of it. Not the battles. The marches, and the weather, and a quartermaster they had both hated, and a night on this coast when the column's boats had gone aground on a sandbar in the dark and spent till dawn getting off it, and a horse Ward had had which had bitten everyone except Rowena. Emeric had never heard Ward talk about the war for as long as a quarter glass. He talked about it now for most of the evening, in his flat plain voice, and Rowena laughed twice, which Emeric had seen her do once before.

Saban and the Treasurer talked tonnage.

They talked it at the other side of the table, heads together, with Saban's figures on a sheet between them and the Treasurer's in his head. Two boats a month through the summer. How much the lane cost a ton, and how much it would cost with the coaster on it, and how much with a second coaster. What the harbour dues were, and whether the harbour-mistress would drop them for a Kaldo hull that came every month, and the harbour-mistress, overhearing, said that she would drop them when Kaldo's master stopped bumping her quay. Saban lost that exchange. He looked delighted to have lost it.

Late in the evening, when the plates were gone and the wine was going round the second time, and the table had broken into three and four conversations, Rowena turned to Emeric across the table.

"Your Chancellor," she said.

Emeric looked at her.

"The one with the book," said Rowena. "Who came in the autumn and wouldn't let my harbour-mistress's men lift a cask off your ship till she'd had it on a scale." She turned her glass on the tablecloth. "I asked my Treasurer to write to her in the spring about the lane. He tells me she answered him in four days, and corrected two of his figures, and was right about both, and he's been in a temper since." She looked at him with the half of her face that was not scarred. "Where is she?"

"In Kaldo," said Emeric. "Holding the book."

"She didn't want to come?"

"Somebody had to hold it."

"Somebody always has to hold it." Rowena drank. "I've been holding it for seventeen years. I know exactly how it's done. You find the one person in the room who'll do it properly and you make sure you never need to ask them." She set the glass down. "And then one day you look up and they've been doing it so long that nobody remembers asking."

She said it lightly. She was not looking at him when she finished it. She was looking down the table at Saban, who had stopped talking to the Treasurer at the word Chancellor and was listening with an expression of the most perfect contentment Emeric had ever seen on a merchant's face.

"Your factor," said Rowena, "is enjoying this."

"He enjoys most things," said Emeric.

"He's enjoying this one more." She lifted her glass an inch in Saban's direction, and Saban, caught, lifted his an inch back, and did not stop smiling. "Tell your Chancellor," said Rowena to Emeric, "that my Treasurer would like his two figures back. And that I'd like to meet her again when she isn't counting my casks."

"I'll tell her."

"You won't," said Rowena. "But your factor will."

He walked out onto the terrace below her hall at dusk the next evening, with Ward, to look at the harbour.

It was the last night. The coaster was to go down the inlet on the morning ebb with the lime in her hold and the reams gone out of it, and the press in the loft, and Nevin staying for the season. From the terrace he could see the whole harbour below him, the quay and the boats and the loft with a light in its windows where Tavy was still setting type by lamplight, which he had been told not to do. And the coaster at the quay, black, with her stack cold and her name on her stern in white, and Rowena's grey harbour boats moored round her.

And the mouth of the harbour, and the inlet beyond it, going away into the dusk between the black hills toward the sea.

A boat was coming round the headland at the harbour mouth.

He saw her the moment she came round, because she was the wrong shape. She was a coaster, a sailing coaster, one of the hired boats that had carried the lane's sulphur since the first spring, two-masted and deep, the kind Saban hired by the season from the coast. He knew her by her masts. She was coming into the harbour, which was wrong in itself, because the sulphur boats did not come in, they loaded at the works quay below the mountain and went, and they went laden, and they did not come back up the inlet at dusk.

She was low in the water. Much too low. She was down by the head, with her bow nearly under and her stern up, and she was coming in under one sail with the other mast bare, and the sail was not drawing, and she was not steering, she was drifting in on the last of the flood with her head falling off towards the rocks under the headland. There was no one on her deck that he could see.

"Ward," he said.

"I see her."

Below them on the quay a bell began to ring, and a woman was running along the waterfront from the harbour house, shouting for boats, and the harbour boats round the coaster were already casting off. It was the harbour-mistress. Emeric had never seen her run. She ran with her skirts in her fists and her grey coat open, down the quay, towards the harbour mouth, towards the boat that was coming in wrong in the last of the light.
