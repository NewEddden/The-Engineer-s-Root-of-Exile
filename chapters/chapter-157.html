Chapter 157 - From the Air

Ivo Thorne had wanted, his whole life, a piece of glass that was flat, and when he got two of them he sat down on the floor of the glassworks and did not say anything for a quarter of an hour.

The forge had made them on the fourteenth of May, at night, in the cellar, with Tess at the dial and the stone door shut. Two discs of the glassworks' best clear glass, each the width of a man's spread hand and as thick as a finger, and each with one face ground and polished flat. Not flat as a mirror is flat, or a still pond. Flat as the forge made things: flat to a fraction of the width of a wave of light, which was a thing Emeric could say and could not show anybody, because there was nothing in Kaldo that could see that small.

Except, it turned out, the glass itself.

He showed Ivo in the glassworks the next morning. He took the shutters off a lamp that burned spirit with a pinch of salt stirred into it, so that its flame came up a clear hard yellow, one colour and no other; and he laid one of the forge's flats face down on a lens Ivo had ground the month before for Linus's theodolite, and held the lamp over it.

Bands. Under the flat, across the face of the lens, the yellow light lay in stripes, dark and bright and dark and bright, like the grain in a board; and the stripes were not straight. They curved, and crowded together on one side, and drew apart on the other, and in one corner they made a little closed ring like a knot in the wood.

"Every dark band," said Emeric, "is a place where the gap between the two pieces of glass is the same. Like the lines on Linus's maps that join the places of one height. Where the bands are close, the glass is climbing fast. Where they make a ring," he put his finger beside the knot, "there's a hill, or a hollow. On your lens."

Ivo looked at the knot.

He was a long thin man with a long thin face and very clean hands, who had come south from the coast in a glassblower's cart for the wage and had said, every week since, that he did not like it here. He had spent a year once, on the coast, grinding one lens, and been called mad for it. He bent over the bench until his nose was a finger from the flat, and looked at the bands, and moved the lamp, and looked again, and then he took the flat off his lens and laid it on another of his, and another, and on the third the bands lay across the glass in long straight even lines from edge to edge, like a ruled page.

"That one," said Emeric, "is flat."

That was when Ivo sat down on the floor.

When he got up he went through every lens in the glassworks with the flats and the yellow lamp. He found the hill in Linus's objective and the hollow in Linus's eyepiece, and reground both against the forge's other gift, the laps: a set of iron dishes, one for each curve a lens might want, each one turned by the forge to its curve as true as the flats were flat, so that a lens ground in one of them with fine emery came out the curve of the dish and no other curve. He reground the microscope's glass. He reground the telescope's. He spent three days at it and went to bed on none of them, and on the fourth day he came up to the keep with Linus's theodolite in its box and set it on the long table in front of Emeric and said that it was the first instrument he had ever made that he did not want to apologise for.

"And the circle," said Ivo. "The circle's wrong."

The forge had made a third thing that night. A disc of brass the size of a dinner plate, with a ring of fine lines cut round its rim, one for every degree and every part of a degree, as many as the forge could cut and a man could see with a glass. A master divided circle. Every scale in Kaldo that measured an angle would be checked against it from now on, and a machine built from it, later, a dividing engine, that would cut the same lines on other circles, as many as anybody wanted, every one as true as the master.

Ivo had laid Linus's theodolite's own circle against it. It was out. Not by much. By a quarter of a degree, at one place on its rim, where the old instrument maker on the coast who had cut it forty years ago had sneezed, Ivo said, or had been drinking, or had had a bad day.

"A quarter of a degree," said Emeric, "on a sight across a valley a mile wide."

"Is twenty feet," said Ivo, who had worked it out on the way up the hill and did not like it. "Twenty feet out at a mile, on every sight that crossed that place on the circle." He looked at the theodolite. "Every map Linus has ever made with it."

Linus Vance, when he was told, was quiet for some while. Then he said that he had always thought the far end of the Milford survey came out a little short, and that he had blamed the chain.



Linus had been sworn to the ship on the eighth, in the long room, with Ward at the door and Ferra writing it down, the way Maren had been sworn a year before.

He had listened to it all without a word: what the long shed under the north wall held, and what she was, and what she could do, and what would happen to a man who told anybody. He was past sixty, thin and brown and weathered, with a surveyor's squint and a surveyor's patience, and when Ferra had finished reading the oath to him he asked one question.

"How high?"

"Tomorrow, about a mile," said Emeric. "Later, higher."

Linus thought about it. He looked out of the long room's window, west, toward Milford, which he could not see.

"Every map I've ever drawn," he said, "I've drawn from the ground. Walking. With a chain and a staff and a boy. Thirty years of it. You stand on a hill and look at the next hill and take your angle, and walk to the next hill and look back, and you put the country together a piece at a time like a broken pot." He turned back to them. "Nobody's ever seen a country whole. Not the way it is. Not all at once from above, like a bird." He put his hand flat on the oath, on the table, where Ferra had laid it. "Where do I sign?"



They took her out on the twenty-second at two in the morning, under oars.

Down the slip in the dark with her legs up in their slots and her foils folded and the sweeps out on both sides, the way she had gone out forty times in the low flights of the year before, with no lamp on her anywhere; out of the shed's river doors onto the black water, and down the river past the sleeping dock quarter on the current, a hundred feet of brown oak and brass sliding through the dark with nobody on either bank to see her, and round the bend below the brickfield, and out into the reach above the marsh where there was nothing but reeds and water and sky.

Fletcher was in the bow cockpit, with Bram beside him, at the water helm. Linus was there too, on a stool Fletcher had screwed to the floor for him beside the glass bulb under the cutwater, with his theodolite on its stand in front of him and his drawing boards in a rack and his pencils in a cup, and his face, in the dim green glow of the cockpit's one shaded dial, set like a man's who has been told he is about to see God and means to take notes. Tess was amidships in her chair, clamped to the floor at the stone-keeper's station, with her key in the first case and her data book open and the twelve levers of the matrix in front of her under their brass covers. Ward was at the side hatch. Emeric was up the ladder in the glass house on the dome, at the low helm, with the whole of the dark world round him in every direction through the glass.

They got the sweeps in. Fletcher brought her up onto her foils on the stones, the hull rising out of the water as she gathered way, until she was running down the reach on her three legs with only the foils under the surface and the spray coming off them in two long white wings, faster than any boat on the river had ever gone; and then Emeric took her off the water.

He had done it forty times. He did it now the way he had done it every time, gently, the matrix's first group coming up under Tess's hand, the lift coming into her through the oak like a held breath let out, the foils coming up out of the river with a last long hiss and the water falling off them; and she was in the air over the marsh at the height of the reeds, her propeller turning on its arm behind, going west.

And then, for the first time, he did not stop there.

"Tess," he said, down the speaking tube.

"Second group," said Tess's voice, small and tinny out of the brass mouth beside his knee. "Coming up."

He felt it. The second group of stones waking in their cases along the deck line, one note after another, bright, the way they woke; and the lift under him doubled; and the marsh went down.

It went down slowly. The reeds first, falling away below the glass, so that he could see over them, and then the trees on the edge of the marsh, which she had never once been higher than in all the forty nights of the low flights, went down below the level of her keel, and he could see over them too. The river behind them, a long pale grey line in the dark going back to Kaldo. Kaldo itself, the arc lamps on its wall a small ring of light like a dropped bracelet, going down, going small. The land to the west opening out ahead of them, dark, and darker, flat and then not flat, with here and there a single yellow point that was a farm where somebody was up early, and then, very far off, the line of the downs against a sky that was just beginning to be a different black from the land.

The air in the glass house grew cold. He felt it come in through the oak and the glass, the way a cellar's cold comes up through a floor.

"Bow," said Fletcher's voice, from the tube. It was very calm. "I have her."

That had been settled on the long table. Low down, the glass house on the dome; a man up there could see all round her, and down past her sides, and that was what mattered near the ground. Higher, the bow cockpit, the glass bulb under her cutwater, where a man looked down and forward at the land coming at him and not at the sky. Emeric let go of the low helm. Below him, through the deck, he felt Fletcher take her.

He went down the ladder from the glass house and forward, down the sealed stair, into the bow cockpit, where Linus was sitting on his stool with both hands on the edge of the glass bulb and his face pressed nearly to it, looking down.

Under the glass, under her keel, a mile under, lay Milford.



Linus did not say anything. He did not take a pencil out of the cup. He sat with his hands on the glass and looked.

It was not light yet. It was the hour before, the grey hour, when the land has come back up out of the dark but has not yet got any colour, and lies under the sky like a drawing in pencil. The river came in from the right, wide and slow and pale, and bent, and went away to the left in long loops, and on either side of it the land went up in steps. The bottoms first, flat and low beside the river, laced all over with water, every ditch and pool and slough of them catching the grey sky like broken glass, with the mist lying on them in long white rags. Then a step up, a bench, a long low shelf of land above the bottoms, darker, with the lines of hedges on it and the small dark squares of fields and the smaller squares of roofs. Then another step up, steeper, and the high flats above that, going away west to the downs, open and pale and empty.

It lay there like a map. It was a map. Every map Linus had ever drawn of it, with his chain and his staff and his boy, walking, a piece at a time, was down there under the glass whole, at once, and Emeric watched him see where every one of them was wrong.

"The bench," said Linus at last, very low. "The bench isn't straight. I drew it straight. It goes in. There." He put his finger on the glass. "Like a bay. A whole bay of it, half a mile deep, going back into the high ground, and the bottoms going in under it." He was silent. "I walked past the mouth of that. Three times. And never saw it was a bay."

"Can you map it?"

"Not yet." Linus took his face off the glass. "I need a line. A known line on the ground, that I can see from up here. Two marks, and the distance between them, measured. Then I've a scale, and I can take my angles off it to everything else." He looked at the theodolite. "I chained a baseline across the bench last autumn, for the telegraph. Oak stakes, a furlong apart exactly, at the top of the bench above the bay." He looked down through the glass. "I can't see an oak stake from a mile up. Nobody could. I need something white on each stake. A cloth. A sheet." He turned his head. "I should have said so on the long table. I didn't know till I looked how small it'd all be."

Ward was in the door of the cockpit behind them. Emeric did not turn round.

"There's light in half an hour," said Fletcher, from the water helm, not looking away from the glass. "Milking in two. Then there's folk on every road down there, and every one of them looking up."

Half an hour. Emeric looked down through the glass at the bench, at the place above the bay where Linus's finger had been. He could not see a stake either. He could see the bench, and the dark line of a hedge along its brow, and the pale ground of a field below the hedge, and beyond the field, on the bottoms, the wet. He could feel nothing down there at all, at this height; his sense did not reach a mile.

"Take her down over the first stake," he said to Fletcher. "Low. As low as you can over the hedge. And hold her."



She came down out of the grey with her propeller feathered, slowly, silent, a great dark shape sinking over the Milford bench in the hour before dawn, and stopped a hundred feet above the brow of the bench, over Linus's first stake, and hung there.

Emeric went out of the side hatch with two of the shed's dust sheets rolled under his arm and a bag of pegs and a mallet on his belt, and the cape on his back.

It was a knight's cape, the spare from the rack in the shed. Black, hooded, heavy with its folded battens, the cords to the wrists and the heels hanging loose; furled, it was only a cloak. He had put it on in the passage by the hatch while Ward held the sheets, and Ward had watched him put it on and said nothing, because Ward knew exactly what it was for, and it was not for falling.

The ladder went down from the hatch on its two ropes, sixty feet of it, swaying. He went down it. At the bottom of it there were still forty feet of grey air between his boots and the hedge.

He pulled the cords at his wrists.

The cape opened behind him with a crack like a sail taking wind, the battens snapping out stiff, and he let go of the ladder.

He did not fall. He had the weight off before his hands had left the rung, all of it, and the black wing spread behind him took the air the way it had taken Sorrel's in the works yard, and for the length of three breaths he went down out of the sky over the Milford bench in a long slow slant under a black wing like a hawk coming down on a field, which was what Linus saw from the bow glass, and what Fletcher saw, and what anybody on the ground would have seen if anybody on the ground had been up; and under the wing, where nobody could see it, the violet flicker at his heels held his weight off and set him down as gently as a leaf.

A man's height above the field he let a little weight back to brake, and felt Ward's wave go down through him before his boots touched, all of him at once, ankles and knees and hips and back, ready; and he landed in the wet grass at the brow of the bench a yard from Linus's oak stake, on both feet, with his knees going down and coming up again, soft, and was standing.

He did not let himself think about it. He knelt and spread the first sheet over the stake and pegged it at the corners, and the mallet's blows sounded very loud in the grey quiet; and then he stood up and looked east along the brow of the bench for the second stake.

A furlong. Two hundred and twenty yards, Linus had said, exactly, chained. Along the top of the bench, beside the hedge, over grass.

He ran it.

Heavy, light. Down, up. The grass was wet and the ground under it was soft and it did not matter, because a foot that is heavy when it pushes pushes on soft ground as well as hard, and a foot that weighs nothing between pushes does not sink. He went along the brow of the bench in the grey light in long low silent strides, with the hedge going by on his left in a dark blur and the cape furled again behind him, his own breath the only sound; and the oak stake came up out of the grass at the end of the furlong as if it had been thrown at him. He had not counted the strides. He thought, afterward, that there could not have been more than forty.

He pegged the second sheet. When he stood up and looked back, the first lay on the brow of the bench a furlong off, a small pale square in the dark grass, very clear, and above it the K Discovery hung in the grey sky like a cloud that had forgotten to move.

The light was coming. He could see the hedges now as hedges, and the field below him as a field, and at the edge of the bottoms, a cow.

The ship came along the bench toward him, slowly, her propeller turning just enough to move her, and Fletcher brought her down over the field below the brow, beyond the hedge, where the ground fell away toward the bottoms, as low as he dared, and held her. The ladder hung down from her side over the field. Its foot swung, a man's height above the grass, forty yards from where Emeric stood.

Forty yards, downhill, across the hedge.

He could run it. He could run it in a breath, heavy and light, and jump the hedge, and run the rest. Or he could do the thing he had done on the grade three weeks ago, sitting on a sleeper next to Paskin, the thing Paskin had asked him what it was for.

He knelt and put his hand flat on the ground behind his right boot, and leaned. The earth did not want to be told; he did not tell it. It gave. A ridge came up out of the wet grass under his palm, the length of his forearm, with a steep face toward him, and the small amber glyph went out off his hand, smaller than it had been on the grade, the size of a ring. He put his back foot against the face of it. He looked at the ladder.

He saw himself at it. Rylan's way. The whole line of it, from the ridge to the ladder's foot, over the hedge, straight.

And he went.

Everything at once. The push into his legs, all of it, the knight's push, against the ridge's face and not against the wet grass, so that nothing slipped; and as his foot left the ridge, the weight off, all of it, so that he did not rise and fall, he went out flat, low, fast, like a stone skipped off a pond. The hedge went under him. The field went under him in a blur. He had never gone so fast in his life on his own legs or off them, not in the stable yard, not on the roofs, and the ladder's foot was coming at him out of the grey light as straight as a rail.

The ship moved.

Not much. A breath of the morning wind came up out of the bottoms, the first of the day, the way it always did as the light came, and it took her a little, and she swung, a few yards, sideways, on her height; and the ladder swung with her, to his left.

He could not turn.

He saw it at once and could do nothing about it. He had pushed off the ridge in a line and he was going along the line, and nothing in him could bend it. Not his legs; they were off the ground. Not the gravity; the gravity only took his weight off him, or put it back, up and down, and he was going sideways. Air could have bent it, a burst of it, if he had had the time to build the glyph and the breath to pay three times what it cost Sorrel, and he had neither. He went past the ladder's foot a yard to its right. He felt the wind of it on his face. He went on, over the bottom of the field and the ditch at its foot, and the weight came back on him because he had nothing left to hold it off with, and he came down, at the end of his line, exactly where the line had always been going.

Into the wet.

The bottoms of Milford, in May, at the foot of the bench above the bay. Black water under a skin of duckweed, and sedge, and under the water mud with no bottom to it that he could find. He went in up to his chest. The cold of it stopped his breath. He stood in it with his arms up and duckweed on his face and the cape spread out round him on the water like a dead crow, and a heron, which had been standing in the sedge ten yards off minding its own business, got up with a great clatter and flew away, and said what it thought of him.

The ladder came down beside him. Ward was on it.

Ward came down the last rungs to the ladder's foot and stood on the bottom rung with the ladder swaying a foot over the water, and held out his hand, and looked at Emeric in the black water with the duckweed on him, and his face did not do anything at all for a breath. Then something went across it, very quick, and was gone, and Emeric would have sworn on Armand's book that it was the nearest he had ever seen Ward come to laughing.

"You can't turn," said Ward.

"No."

"Once you're going."

"No." Emeric took his hand and came up out of the mud with a long sucking sound, onto the ladder. "Once it's going, it can only go where the line goes."

Ward looked at him a little longer.

"Like the train," said Ward.

"Like the train," said Emeric, and began to climb.



Linus had seen the cape. He said so, in the bow cockpit, when Emeric came down the sealed stair from the hatch in a borrowed shirt with the Milford bottoms still running out of his hair. He had watched the whole of it through the glass, bent so close to it his breath had fogged it: the black wing going down off the ladder, and the man running along the bench, and the second sheet, and then the jump, the long flat leap off the brow of the bench under the furled cape, too fast and too far, over the hedge and the field and into the wet.

"The cape," he said. "Sorrel's cape. I'd heard of it. I'd not seen it." He shook his head slowly. "You went the whole furlong in no time at all, and then you went off that brow like a hare off a bank. I'd not have believed it." He looked at Emeric's wet hair. "You'll want to tell her it doesn't steer."

"I'll tell her," said Emeric.

Behind Linus, at the water helm, Fletcher did not turn round. He had his hands on the helm and his eyes on the glass and the light coming up over the Milford bench under them, and he did not say anything, and Emeric thought of the night of the first lift, a year ago, and Fletcher standing in the door of the glass house in the dark with his hand on the frame. What else have I been told.

Fletcher took her up.



The sun came up while they were at a mile, and Linus drew Milford.

He drew it the way he had drawn every map of his life, with a pencil and a board and the theodolite, except that he did not walk. He sat on his stool with his eye to the reground glass, and found the two white sheets on the brow of the bench, small and very clear now in the low gold light, a furlong apart exactly, and took his angle between them; and from them, a sight at a time, he took everything else. The river's bends. The bay in the bench. The hedges and the fields and the roofs on the bench, and the long white rags of mist lifting off the bottoms as the sun got into them, and under the mist, coming up out of it like a country coming up out of the sea, the wet: every slough and pool and ditch and spring-line of it, laced across the bottoms in a pattern nobody on the ground had ever seen whole, because on the ground it was only mud.

He drew for an hour and a half, without speaking, with Fletcher holding her on her height and Tess calling the cases' readings down the tube in her flat small voice every quarter of an hour. His pencil did not stop. When a board was full he put it in the rack and took the next. Emeric sat on the cockpit floor with his back to the bulkhead in his borrowed shirt and watched the boards fill.

He did not watch the map. He watched the land under it.

The bottoms, which were wet because the water came down off the high flats and the bench and had nowhere to go, and would be dry, in two years, with drains under them, and grow hay. The bench, which was where people lived and always had, in a line along its brow above the wet. And the bay in the bench, which Linus had walked past three times, half a mile deep and sheltered on three sides by the high ground: flat, and dry, and empty, and close to the river.

He saw them on it before a brick was laid. Steadings. Not one great barn, where one fire or one bad roof could take the whole of Milford's harvest in a night; four or five, spread along the floor of the bay, each with its yard and its granary and its hay barns. And between them, coming in along the floor of the bay from the line that would come out to Milford from Kaldo next year, sidings. A siding into each steading, so that the grain went out of the granary door straight into a wagon and down the line to whoever needed it, without ever going onto a cart.

And one of them, he thought, looking down at the bay under the glass, should not sell. One granary in each barony that was filled in a good year and kept, and not touched, and only opened in a bad one. So that a wet year at Milford, a year when the bottoms flooded and the hay rotted in the swaths, did not mean a hungry winter at Kaldo and Grayrock and Wayford too.

He did not say it. He would say it at the long table, where Ferra could put a number on it and Imani could tell him who would go hungry first, and they would both be right. He sat on the cockpit floor and looked at the bay with the sun on it and saw it full.



They were home on the river at Kaldo by half past six, down out of the sky over the marsh with the sun behind them, onto the water, onto the foils, off the foils, sweeps out; and in the shed with the doors shut before the first cart came over the east bridge. In Milford, Fletcher said, they would be milking now. And looking up, every one of them, at nothing.

The sheets stayed on the stakes on the bench above the bay. A Milford carter found them at noon and took them home to his wife, who was glad of them.

Paxton got the map a fortnight later, by the line, in a tube, with a letter from Ferra that said it was Linus's survey of the Milford bottoms and the bench and the high flats, for the steward's use in planning the drains. It was a great deal better than any map of Milford anybody had ever made. It was so much better that a steward who had been a baron, and had walked his own land since he could walk, would have known at a glance that no man with a chain and a staff and a boy could have made it in a year, let alone in the fortnight since Linus had last been seen in Milford.

Paxton's answer came up the wire the next morning. Ferra read it standing at the back of the telegraph room, and then read it to the table, without expression.

"Survey received. Very fine. Bay noted. Steward will not ask how. Respects to the holder, compliments to the chancellor. Will report Monday."

The fair copy went on the wall of the long room, at the end, where the four columns' sheet had hung in March. Linus drew it. He drew it at his table in the tally room over nine days, from his boards, in ink, with the river in blue and the wet in grey and the bench and the high flats in their contours, every one true to the reground circle; and when he had finished he lettered it across the top, Milford, Bottoms and Bench, from the Air, and at the bottom, in the corner, he put his name and the date, as a surveyor does.

And in the bottom corner, too, at the foot of the bench above the bay, beside a small black pool in the grey of the wet where a careful eye could just make out a heron, in his smallest and neatest surveyor's hand, a note.

Wet. Deep. Proved.
