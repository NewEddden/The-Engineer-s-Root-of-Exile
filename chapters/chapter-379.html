Chapter 379 - Every Steward's Desk

The switchboard had been built in the line room's north end, where the battery racks had stood until the autumn, and it looked like nothing anybody in Kaldo had seen before, which was the trouble with it.

It was a board of polished walnut the length of a bench and as high as a man's chest, with a row of small round shutters along the top like the eyes of a very patient animal, and under them a row of brass sockets, and under those, on the shelf, rows of cords with brass plugs on their ends that came up out of holes in the shelf and went back down into them on weights. Three stools in front of it. Three headsets on hooks. A clock. Ambrose had wanted a fourth stool and Ferra had said three was what they'd trained, and the fourth stool had gone back to the Works.

On the morning of the twenty-second of January, a Thursday, at a quarter to nine, the three operators sat on the three stools with the headsets on, two women from the counting house who had been the fastest on the Board's keys in the autumn and a boy of seventeen from Cade's yard called Pim Rusk who could hear a dropped pin through a closed door, and looked at the board, and the board looked back.

"It's only telephones," said Ambrose, behind them. He said it to himself as much as to them.

Nobody believed him. It wasn't only telephones. Above the line room's roof, above the town, above the Band's foot, eight drums hung at five miles in a great uneven ring over Randillia and the near half of Duvrenne, each one pinned where Bren's relay team had ridden it up over the first three weeks of the month, one every two or three days, in the clear weather between the gales. Eight drums and BR-01 over the bay. Each of them a decider and a radio and a sense stone and a mirror, beating to its neighbours once a minute, so that the line room's own little speaker on the wall went tok every few seconds with somebody's heartbeat, all morning, all night, like a clock with too many hands.

And on the far end of each of them, a desk.

The sets had gone out with the drums. Merrin's valves, a handset on a hook, a crank to call the line room, a mast on the roof that the steward's people had been told not to hang washing from and in at least one case already had. Bryda's, at the Grayrock counting house, in a corner of her own office, because she'd said she wasn't having a machine she didn't understand in a room she didn't sit in. Paxton's, at Milford, in the steward's office beside the seed room, on the desk his father had sat at. Aster's at Wayford. Rowena's at Emberfell, which had gone up the coast on the K Endeavor's run with a fitter who had come back saying the duchess had made him explain the crank three times and then done it better than him. Waylon's on the Isle, on a table in the cook tent, because the families had wanted to see it. Keller's in the capital, under protest. And one at Kesrath, by Duvrenne's leave and its own, in a room in the tower that nobody from Kaldo had been allowed into.

At nine by the clock the first shutter dropped.

It was a small sound. A click, and a little brass disc falling open behind its glass with a number painted on it, and the operator on the left stool, a broad calm woman called Joss Teller, put a plug into the socket under it without looking, as if she'd done it all her life, and said, "Line room," into her mouthpiece.

She listened. She took up a second cord and put its plug into a second socket, and turned a little key on the shelf, and said, "Putting you through, steward," and sat back.

"Milford," she said, over her shoulder. "For the counting house. For Ferra."

Ferra was at the long desk under the window, with Corbin beside her and Emeric beside him, and a handset of her own on the desk on a cord to the board. She picked it up.

"Paxton," she said.

His voice came out of it. Thin, with a hiss behind it like a kettle three rooms away, and a slight lag on the ends of words, as if every word had to go up five miles and across a hundred and come down again, which it had. But it was Paxton's voice. It was so exactly Paxton's voice that Corbin, who had met him twice, made a small involuntary movement in his chair.

"Is it working?" said Paxton. "I've no way of telling. I'm talking to a box."

"It's working. I can hear you."

"Then I'll say it once. I've got a list." There was the sound of a paper. "You'll want it in order."

"I always want it in order."

"Six things," said Paxton. "One man on each, written, and I've told them that if they're not doing it in the spring I'll know, because I'll walk out and look." The paper again. "Grafting's Hollis. Hardy roots under the good fruit, the western apples and the plums Zale brought, on our own stock, so they bear in four years instead of nine. And he says to tell the king he knows it's not breeding. He knows a graft joins them and doesn't make a new one. He says he's known that since he was six and the king needn't write it in the book again."

"Noted," said Ferra, and wrote it.

"Grain and storage is Farris. Six new silos at the railhead and two at the bench end, with the moisture rods in them, and a dryer, because a silo full of wet grain is a midden with a roof on it and I'll not have Milford's first silo be the one that rots. Machinery is Hollis again, God help him. The ploughs and drills and threshers go out to the holdings from March, a set to each, with the parts written so a smith in a village can make a new one. Not one cleverer machine. I said that to him. One stupider machine that a man in Dunmere can mend with a file." Paxton's breath, a hiss on the line. "Wind's me. The first line of the high towers on the open plain beyond the south road, twelve of them, and Tess's people to put up the masts. I'll have the first three turning by Lantern Night."

"You said that last year."

"I said it about the low ones last year, and they turned." He didn't sound offended. "Fields and watering's Farris and Linus's surveyors. New plots on the east side in squares with drains and paths, and the valves on timers. Not circles. I know you want circles. The ground doesn't want circles, the ground wants squares with a ditch at the bottom." The paper rustled. "Animals is Hester. Clean side and dirty side in every byre, the sick ones apart, the muck stacked a season before it goes on anything. She says if anybody from Kaldo comes to tell her how to keep a cow she'll show them out personally."

"And the glass?" said Emeric.

There was a pause on the line, a long one, long enough for the hiss to seem to get louder.

"That's the king," said Paxton.

"That's the king."

"Majesty." Not much warmer. Paxton had been steward of Milford since before Emeric was anything at all, and had never once in seventeen years pretended to enjoy being told what to grow. "The glasshouses are going up. Three. With the racks in them and the beds you drew, the water ones with no soil. For the western crops. Hollis has the seed out of the cold store and the first trays in on Monday." He paused again. "I don't hold with growing things in water. I'll tell you that now. But I've seen what that pepper of Zale's did in the tropical house and I'll not be the steward who said no to it. So it's going up."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank me in September if anything's grown." The paper again. "That's Milford. It goes in the Milford Book and on the monthly ledger, what we tried and what it cost and what fell over. Ilsa will have it on the first of every month. Is that what you wanted?"

"That's what I wanted."

"Good," said Paxton, and rang off, without saying goodbye, the way he left every room.

Ferra put the handset down and looked at it.

"He's been practising that," she said.

"For a week, I should think," said Corbin, quietly, and then looked surprised that he'd spoken.

The test had been Tess's, and like all of Tess's tests it had been written down in advance on a single sheet in an order that would find out what was wrong with the thing as fast as possible and in front of as many people as necessary. She was at the far end of the line room on her stool by the heartbeat board, a second board on the wall beside the switchboard with a little lamp for every drum and a needle for every link, with her stick across her knees and the sheet on her lap. She'd said nothing yet. She was watching the needles.

Line one was Paxton. While Paxton was still talking, the second shutter had dropped, and the woman on the middle stool had put Bryda through to the long desk's second handset, and Bryda had waited, which Bryda never did, until Paxton had finished.

That was the first thing on Tess's sheet. Two calls at once on different channels through different drums, Milford through the south-west relay and Grayrock through the northern one, both coming down through BR-01, and neither of them hearing the other.

"Nobody else on the line?" said Bryda. "I was told there'd be nobody else on the line."

"Nobody else, steward."

"Then listen, because I've got Hask stood next to me and he'll want to say his piece and I'll want to stop him." Bryda's voice was harder than Paxton's and came through better, as if the drums liked it. "The fans are in. Four of them in the main gallery and the two in the new cut, on the wire from the steam plant, and the air in the low level's sweet for the first time since my father's day. Hask had a man go down with a candle on Monday to prove it and the candle burned straight up. The lamps are in as far as the third gallery. Electric, the Works' bulbs, on a cable Hask won't let anybody touch with a pick. By March they're in all of it. Grayrock'll be lit underground before it's lit on the high street, and I'll hear about that from the high street, I dare say."

"The growhouses," said Emeric.

"That's the king, is it?"

"It's the king."

"The growhouses are in the old north workings. Where the seam gave out in my grandfather's time. Same warmth summer and winter, you said, and you were right, it never changes down there, it's like a cellar. The lamps go in next month and the first beds after. Mushrooms first, Hask says, because a mushroom doesn't want the lamps at all. Then greens." She paused. "And the grubs."

"The grubs," said Ferra, writing.

"The grubs. The mealworms in the trays, on the bran and the kitchen scraps, and the black flies' grubs on the rest of it, and the hens eat the grubs. I'll be honest with you. I didn't believe it. I had a tray in my own kitchen to see if it stank. It doesn't stink. The hens go mad for them and the eggs are up a fifth." Bryda's breath. "The tractors are on the hill farms. Two. Garrow's to run the haul road till the siding's in, which won't be before the summer. And Hask's got his mine frame on its knees in the low level setting props, and if any one of you comes up here and tells him to take it out he'll go for you with it."

"I'll not be telling him that," said Emeric.

"No. You won't. You'll be at sea." It wasn't said unkindly. "That's Grayrock. Ilsa has the books."

"Ilsa always has the books."

"Ilsa," said Bryda, "is the only person in the kingdom I'd let have them," and rang off.

At the heartbeat board Tess made a mark on her sheet.

Waylon came in on the third line from the cook tent on the Isle, and the whole of the Island families appeared to have come in with him, because behind his voice there was a noise like a market and somebody laughing very close to the mouthpiece and an old woman's voice, saying something sharp.

"Mother Sabbe wants to know where you are," said Waylon.

"Tell her Kaldo."

"I told her Kaldo. She doesn't believe there's a Kaldo. She thinks it's a man in a box on the table." The laughter again. "She says the man in the box has a thin voice and should eat more goat."

"Tell her I'm working on it."

"I'll tell her." A pause. "The first head went on the Kesrath bore on Monday. Oskel turned the wheels himself and then made his men do it. The gas is going down the line to the still. Nobody's died." Another pause. "Alina's got her mirrors marked out on the flat behind the tank farm with string. A thousand pegs. You can see it from the Kesrath tower and their mage came down to ask what it was and she told him it was a garden."

"Is it?"

"It'll be a garden that can set fire to a ship," said Waylon, "so I'm calling it a garden as well," and somebody near him said something in the Island speech, and the line went to laughter, and then to a hiss, and then, quite cleanly, to Waylon again, saying, "Did something happen there?"

"The Hod," said Tess. It was the first thing she'd said. She didn't look up. "Handoff."

The Hod was on her weekly charging run, high in the Band over the coast north of the bay, going east with the wind. She'd been talking to the line room through the eastern drum since she'd lifted at seven. Now she'd crossed the line where the northern drum heard her better, and the decider in the eastern drum had looked at the two beats and seen which was stronger and passed her across without being asked, the way a man passes a bucket down a line. And Waylon's call, which had been sharing the eastern drum with her for a few minutes, had gone round by the northern one with her, because it was the shortest road left.

On the heartbeat board two needles had moved and settled. That was all.

"It went quiet for a second," said Waylon. "Like a door shutting."

"That was a ship going past," said Emeric.

"Was it." Waylon thought about it. "Well. I'll not complain about a ship."

The last of the morning's four was Rowena, and Rowena didn't call the line room at all.

She called Maeve.

It had been arranged, which was how Maeve came to be sitting on the end of the long desk with her heels kicking against the drawers like a girl, in a borrowed knitted jacket of Mira's that she'd had on for three days, holding a fourth handset Ambrose had wired up for the purpose, with a face like a person about to be told off.

The shutter dropped. Joss Teller put the plug in and listened and said, "Emberfell, for the princess," and turned the key.

"Maeve," said Rowena.

Maeve held the handset an inch away from her ear, as if it might be hot.

"Mother."

"You've had your hair cut."

Maeve looked wildly round the line room. "How can you possibly know that?"

"Because you've written to me three times since the Long Table and not once mentioned your hair, and you always mention your hair." The thin voice, five miles up and two hundred along and down again, had the dry snap in it that Emeric remembered from the duchess's hall. "Is it short?"

"It's practical."

"It's short." A pause. "Good. You never could manage it long." Another. "Are you eating?"

"Yes."

"Are you eating properly, or are you eating the knights' food?"

"The knights' food is proper."

"The knights' food is porridge with a fight in it. I've seen it." The line hissed. "You're going on the ships."

It wasn't a question, and Maeve didn't answer it as one. She sat on the desk with the handset at her ear and her other hand flat on the wood beside her, and her heels stopped kicking.

"I haven't asked yet," she said. "Not properly. I'm going to ask in February."

"You've asked. You asked the day you got off the train." Rowena didn't sound angry. "I'm not going to tell you not to. I told you not to do things for thirty years and you did them anyway. I'm going to tell you that it's a very nice day here, and the sulfur works have gone up a level, and the port's got a new quay, and I have a telephone on my desk that I can call my daughter on from the other end of the kingdom, and I find I don't like it nearly as much as I expected to." A breath. "It's very nice to hear your voice."

"Mother."

"That's all. Go back to your knight with the basket." The line clicked.

Maeve sat on the desk and held the dead handset for a while.

"She knows about the basket," she said, to nobody.

"Everybody knows about the basket," said Ferra, not unkindly, and took the handset away from her.

It was half past nine. Tess got up off her stool, slowly, with the stick, and walked the length of the line room to the switchboard, and stood behind Pim Rusk with her hand on the back of his stool.

"Ring the Well," she said. "And Waylon back. And Bryda. All three. Hold them."

Pim rang. The shutters dropped, three of them, one after another. Three cords up out of the shelf, three plugs in. In the speaker at the long desk Orla Garran's voice from the Well, and Waylon from the cook tent, and Bryda from Grayrock, all saying some version of What? at once.

"Ambrose," said Tess. "South-west."

Ambrose went to the heartbeat board and put his hand on a single brass key under the lamp for the south-west drum. It was the one that carried Waylon. It was the one that carried Paxton too, when Paxton was talking, and half the line from the Isle, and in a month's time it would carry the railway's freight lists from Wayford.

"Now?" said Ambrose.

"Now."

He turned the key.

Somewhere a hundred and fifty miles west of Kaldo and five miles up, in the dark cold, a decider received one word from the line room in Tess's cipher, the word for be quiet, and did what it was told. Its radio stopped. Its beat stopped. Its pin held.

On the board, the lamp for the south-west drum went out.

And in the speaker, Waylon said, "...hello?" and then, a beat later, quite clearly, "Oh. There you are. Did you do something?"

Nobody in the line room answered him. They were watching the board. Two needles had swung, and two more, and then settled; and the little lamps beside the drums on either side of the dark one, the western and the southern, had brightened slightly, the way a room gets brighter when someone opens a shutter at the other end. The heartbeats in the speaker on the wall had gone from tok to tok-tok on two of the drums, which were now carrying their dead neighbour's traffic as well as their own, and had worked it out between them without anybody in the line room telling them how.

Waylon's call had gone round. Through the western drum, which heard the Isle from the far side, and back east by the southern one, a hundred miles further than it needed to go and with a hiss twice as loud; but it had gone round. It had never stopped. There had been a quarter of a second of nothing, which Waylon had heard as a cough.

"That's the hole," said Tess. "And that's it going round the hole." She looked at her sheet and made a mark. "Ambrose. What's the board say?"

Ambrose read it. "South-west not heard by anybody. No last word before it went. So it's not hit and it's not a broken link. It's down." He looked at her. "It's down because we told it to be down."

"But if we hadn't."

"If we hadn't, the board would say the same thing, and I'd ring Osric for a knight to go up with a spare." He thought. "And if it had sent a last word first, it'd say hit, and I'd ring Saban."

"And if it's only one of them can't hear it?"

"Then it's the weather, or the angle, and I'd wait an hour."

"Good," said Tess. "Wake it up."

Ambrose turned the key back. A minute later, which was the longest minute of the morning, the south-west drum's lamp came on again, and its beat came back into the speaker, tok, and the two drums either side of it went back to tok on their own, and Waylon said, in a slightly aggrieved voice, "It's gone loud again. Is it meant to keep doing that?"

"Only on Thursdays," said Tess, and nodded to Pim Rusk to let them all go.

She walked back to her stool and sat down on it and put the stick across her knees and her hands on the stick, and Emeric saw that the hands weren't quite steady, and didn't say anything. She'd been building the heartbeat since the summer. She had never, until a minute ago, seen it do what she'd built it to do with a whole kingdom's voices in it.

"Well," said Corbin, at the long desk. He'd been very quiet all morning. "That's every steward's desk."

"Every steward's desk," said Ferra.

"And they all report to me, while you're gone." Corbin looked at the switchboard, the shutters and the cords and the three stools. "Every week. On that."

"Every week on that. And a ledger every month. And Ilsa has the books."

"Ilsa always has the books," said Corbin, and it took Emeric a moment to realise his brother had made a joke, and another moment to realise he'd made it to Ferra, who almost smiled.

The knights' net was the afternoon, and it was simpler, because it had to be.

Osric did it in the covered yard at the barracks with forty knights and a chalk board, and Ward watching from the door with his arms folded. Every knight on the net. Every helm with a set, the MK3s with theirs in the helm behind the two brass antennas Abloh had drawn because they looked right, the older suits with a box on the chest. Four plates on a click at the chin: squad, company, command, emergency. Four, because four was what Merrin's valves could carry reliably and what a frightened man's thumb could find in the dark.

"Emergency stays empty," said Osric, and wrote it on the board. "Nobody talks on it. Nobody. You go to it to say one word, and the word's the word, and every operator in the kingdom who hears it clears his line for you." He put the chalk down. "If I hear anybody on it asking where the tea's got to, they'll be cleaning the yard's drains till the fleet comes home."

Somebody at the back laughed.

"Tolliver," said Osric, without looking round. "That was a joke. I meant it anyway."

And the check-in board. That was Ferra's, and Saban's before it, and Osric had had it from both of them. A board in every radio room, on the hulls and in the line room, with a peg on it for every knight by name, and a knight on duty reported in on the hour and the operator moved his peg. That was how you found a man. Not a beacon. Nothing on his body that told the net where he was by itself.

"Why not?" said a young Crestless near the front, one of the three who rode the guard frames. "If I'm down in a ditch with a leg broke, I'd want you to know where."

"So would anybody who wanted to put you in the ditch," said Ward, from the door. "A thing that tells us where you are tells whoever's listening. Saban said it about the relays in the summer. It's truer about you." He came in a step. "You're in trouble, you key the word, and you take the chance of being heard. That's your choice. Not the set's."

The young knight thought about it, and nodded.

"And Linus," said Osric, picking up the chalk again, "wants every one of you who goes up on the Hod's run in February to know that the Hod's carrying his camera now, pointed down, and if you lean on the hatch over it he'll have your names." He wrote Do not lean on the survey hatch on the board and underlined it. "He's photographing the whole kingdom from the Band. Field by field. He says it'll take him two years."

"What for?" said the young knight.

"So he knows where everything is," said Osric. "He's Linus. That's what for."

Amara went up to the Well on the Hod's tender at three, with Emeric and Harrold, and Ferra, because Ferra had said that if this was going to be done she was going to see it done.

The lock was why it had to be up there. Tess had been plain about it in the cellar on the fourteenth, with the Sand Room lit in front of them: both ends of a ride in the Band, always. The made body was on the ground every day of its life. It couldn't put a hand into a decider at the castle and go anywhere at all, any more than Emeric could, because between the castle and the nearest drum there were five miles of thin air with nothing in them for a mind to walk on.

So Amara went to the Well in her own made body, in a coat, with her hood back, and stood on the deck in the wind beside the drum as if she'd done it all her life, and looked out at the Band's long grey shine, and said, "Oh, it's beautiful up here. Why didn't anybody tell me?"

"Everybody told you," said Harrold.

"Nobody told me properly."

Orla Garran had put a stand in the lab for the body. It was the stand from the Motion Shop, the one Amara's made body had stood on through most of a year with its skin off while Ottoline worked on it, carried up on the tender that morning in its crate; and Amara looked at it and laughed, and stepped up onto it, backwards, and settled her heels into the old cups as if she were sitting down in a chair she'd had for years.

"It fits," she said. "Of course it fits." She put her made hand flat on the plate of the Well's drum, which Orla had wheeled in from the deck on its trolley so she wouldn't have to stand outside. "Show me, Emmy."

He showed her. He sat down on the deck by the stand with his back to the drum and his hand on the plate beside hers, and went in, and waited in the Well's decider for her, in the hall of it, with Tess's little sentences round them on the walls.

She came in after him.

It was like having someone come into a room behind you that you hadn't heard on the stair; except that he'd been waiting, and he knew the step. She stood in the Well's stone beside him. Her note was the same as it had been on the plate in the dome on the second, the same in made hand and sleeping one. The stone let her in. It knew her.

This is the house, he said, and it wasn't quite words.

It's very tidy.

Tess built it.

I can tell. Something that was a smile. Where's the door?

He showed her that too. Out of the stone, out into the Band, the road of it going off in every direction under them like a frozen river with the light coming up through it; and he put a thread out, the smallest one, and felt it go and touch BR-01, three miles off over the bay, and went.

She came after him. No between. She was in BR-01 beside him, and he felt her feel it, the sudden no-distance of it, and flinch, and then not flinch.

Oh, said his mother. Oh, that's clever. That's the glass.

It's the glass.

And the next one?

Wherever you like. They're all open to you. Only you and me.

She went before he'd finished. He felt her go out of BR-01 toward the northern drum like a girl going out of a door into a garden, and he went after her because he couldn't help it, and found her there, a hundred and fifty miles north and five miles up, in the northern drum's decider, looking for its eye.

He showed her where the mirror's stone came in. She opened it.

They looked down at Grayrock together.

It was the middle of the afternoon on a clear day, and from five miles up the mine town lay on its hill like a toy somebody had left on a table: the grey roofs in their rows, the winding gear over the main shaft, the long dark scar of the spoil, the new steam plant with its white plume going off sideways in the wind. The mirror was very sharp. It was the best piece of glass Ivo and Orrin had ever made between them, ground on the Works' new machine for six weeks; and Emeric, who'd seen through BR-01's in the dark, had never seen through one by day.

A cart on the haul road. A man walking beside the horse. Two dogs. Smoke from every chimney. In the yard behind the counting house, a woman with grey hair pinning washing to a line.

Bryda, said his mother. Isn't it? That's her walk.

He didn't answer. She went on looking. He felt her look move, and narrow, and the field close in as she did it, so that the whole town went out of the edges and there was only the yard, the line, the washing, the top of a grey head bent over a basket; and then she tried to see the face, and couldn't, because the mirror was straight above and all it could see of a woman pinning washing was her hair and her shoulders and her hands.

She tried the window of the counting house. It was a dark square. Glass, with light on it, and nothing behind it.

I can't see in, she said.

No.

Not at all.

Not at all. Not indoors. Not at night, not much, not under trees, not through cloud. Not faces, unless the drum's far off to one side of them and the light's good, and then not well. He was being careful. He wanted her to see all of it, and all of what it wasn't. A courtyard at a time, if you look close. The whole town, if you don't, and then you can't see the washing. And one place at a time. You can only look where you're looking.

She was quiet for a while in the decider beside him. Down on the hill Bryda finished the line and picked up the basket and went indoors, into the dark square, and was gone.

It's a very good eye, said his mother, for a thing that can't see anything that matters.

It sees armies.

Does it.

It sees ships. A camp. A column on a road. Hulls in the Band, if they're not in cloud. He showed her, from the northern drum, the long bare coast running off toward Duvrenne, and the sea, and two small fishing boats off the headland with the white of their wakes behind them, sharp as pencil lines. Anybody coming at Kaldo with more than a cart, it'll see two days before they get there. That's what it's for.

And the woman with the washing?

Isn't what it's for.

They came home. No between. The northern drum and then BR-01 and then the Well, and he was on the deck with his back against the drum and his palm on the plate and his legs not his, and above him on the stand the made body opened its eyes, and his mother looked down at him out of them, and then at Harrold, who was standing in the doorway of the lab with his hands in his coat pockets and a face Emeric didn't like.

"Two minutes and forty seconds," said Orla Garran.

"It felt longer," said Amara.

"It always does. He said that too."

"What did you see?" said Harrold.

"Grayrock," said Amara. "Bryda pinning out her washing." She got down off the stand, carefully, one foot and then the other, as if she weren't sure for a moment which body was standing. "Harrold. It's all right. I couldn't see her face."

"That's not the point."

"I know it's not."

"It's not the point," said Harrold again, and came into the lab, and stood by the drum and looked at it the way he'd looked at the Answer Tower on the point the first time it lit. "That's my kingdom. It was my kingdom for thirty years, and a good part of it still thinks it is, and I'm going to be the one minding it for most of the year while you're off at the bottom of the sea with your mermaid." He wasn't shouting. He never did. "And there's a thing five miles over Bryda's yard that can see Bryda's yard. And over every yard. Every road. Every lord's house and every village between here and Kesrath. And nobody down there knows it's there, and nobody down there said yes."

"Nobody down there said yes to the towers either," said Emeric. "Or the Board. Or the Sand Room."

"The Sand Room's in Kaldo. Kaldo took the cards. Kaldo knows what the cards are for, near enough." Harrold looked at him. "Bryda never took a card from you. The lord of Dunmere never took a card from you. They're just under it." He turned to Ferra. "You were against the Sand Room. You told me so in the summer."

"I was," said Ferra.

"And this?"

Ferra had her book open on the bench. She hadn't written anything in it yet. She looked at the drum, and at Amara, and at Emeric still sitting on the deck with his back to the drum, and then at Harrold.

"I'm against it too," she said. "I'm against it the way I'm against the knife in Imani's bag. It's a terrible thing to carry. It'll save somebody's life the day she needs it." She picked up her pen. "So it gets a rule, and the rule gets written down, and the day somebody breaks it I'll know, because I'll have written it."

"What rule?"

Emeric got up off the deck.

"Armies, not people," he said. "The eyes are for things that move against the kingdom in numbers. Hulls. Columns. Camps. Fleets. Not for any one person, not for any house, not for any yard. Not to see who's at the market, not to see who a lord's having to dinner, not to see a woman with her washing." He looked at his mother. "Even by accident. If you're looking for a column on the Wayford road and you see a man you know going into an inn, you look away and you don't say."

"And who decides what's an army?" said Harrold.

"Two of us. Never one. Only two notes go up there, and they're both in this room." He looked at Amara, who was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read. "And Ferra's book. Every time either of us goes out, it goes in Ferra's book, and where, and why, and what we looked at. Like the Sand Room. And she reads it."

"She'll be at sea."

"Then Corbin reads it."

Harrold was quiet.

"And who knows?" he said at last.

"This table. The six. You two. Corbin, because he has to. Tess, because she built it. Orla, because she sits by the drum." Emeric counted them. "Nobody else. Not the stewards, not the Assembly, not Waylon, not Duvrenne. Not yet."

"That's a great many people for a secret."

"It's the fewest I could make it."

Ferra was writing. They waited for her. When she'd finished she turned the book round on the bench so they could all see.

22 Jan. The eyes on the home net. Armies, not people. Two to decide, never one: E., A. Every ride in this book: who, where, why, what was looked at, how long. Read by F.; by C. when F. is away. Not told past the names on this page. Against: H. Against, and wrote it anyway: F.

Harrold read it. He read it twice. Then he took the pen out of Ferra's hand, which no one in seventeen years had ever done, and wrote under her line in his big, square, old-fashioned hand, And watched. H.

"What does that mean?" said Amara.

"It means I'll be watching him," said Harrold, and gave Ferra back her pen.

The petition came down the line at a quarter to six, when the line room's day shift was going off and the night shift coming on, which was why, afterwards, nobody could remember whose shutter had dropped.

It came in from Wayford, held. Not a call: a held message, from Aster's desk, the steward's office passing on a paper that had come to it by hand that morning from the west, as stewards' offices were supposed to now, so that the chair in Kaldo saw what the holdings saw. It came in Ferra's cipher off the line and was written out in clear on the duty clerk's pad, and the duty clerk, who was new, took it upstairs to the chair, because the chair was where held messages for the chair went.

Corbin was at his desk on the keep's second floor with the lamp lit and the day's sheets in front of him and his watcher in the chair by the door. He read it there.

It was a petition from the barony of Cinderhale, on the far side of Wayford, where the river mills were. Two hundred and eleven names and marks. It said, in a plain clear hand that had clearly written a good many petitions before and knew exactly how long a lord's attention lasted, that the king had made a law at his crowning that no man or woman in a mill should be made to work more than thirty-two hours in the week without the half again over; and that in Cinderhale the law had been read out in the market and nailed on the mill doors and the mill lords had taken it down again the next morning; and that the mills of Cinderhale were working sixty hours, as they always had, at the old wage, and that the king's law was a very fine piece of paper and nobody in Cinderhale had seen it since.

It asked, very courteously, who in the kingdom was supposed to make the mill lords keep it.

At the bottom, under the two hundred and eleven names, in the same plain clear hand: For the petitioners, Lewin Corrick.

Corbin read it twice. Then he put it on the pile for the morning, and then he took it off the pile again, and read it a third time, and put it on its own beside his elbow, and sat looking at it for a while with the lamp on it.

He didn't know the name. Nobody in Kaldo did, yet. He read the question again, the last line, the courteous one. Who was supposed to make them keep it.

He knew the mill lords, though. Not their faces; their paper. Every one of them was in Tullio's book, because six years ago, the summer Tullio Ducatis took the crown book out of his sister's House, he had brought it up to the Lieutenant's rooms himself and turned the pages, and said the name of every lord in it who would ride if his paper was called. Corbin had been the last thing that book paid for. He knew to the copper what it cost to keep. He wrote one word in the petition's margin, small, Tullio?, and folded the sheet for the bag to Ferra's office in the morning, and put it back beside his elbow.

He supposed, after a while, that the answer was him.
