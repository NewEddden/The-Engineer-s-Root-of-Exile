Chapter 202 - Courses

Hewitt had eleven setters and the prince wanted forty.

He did not want them all at once, which was the only mercy in it. He wanted them the way the flat wanted everything: a gang on the gate towers, because the south gate's towers were up to the string course and the north gate's were not; a gang on the palace, where the ground floor stood roofed and the drum over the middle of it was going up a course a week and would go up faster if anybody gave Weekes the men; a gang on the church footings on the second ring, where the priest came by every morning on his stick and looked at the trench and went away without saying anything, which was worse than if he had. And the second quarter, where two streets stood roofed in new slate and the rest was pegs and lime and a carter's track through the grass, and the fifth draw had come a month early because the woman who kept the island's count had said it would come early, and it had.

And the dock. The dock had taken his best.

He did not grudge it. The dock was the hardest stone job he had ever seen and he was not too proud to say so: great blocks off the ridge laid in the grey mortar that set under water, in a hole cut in the rock at the ridge's foot where the tide came in, by day and then by night too, under the white lights on their masts, because the tide did not wait for anybody's day. Bram had asked him for his six best setters in the spring and he had given them, and they had not come back. They were down there now, behind the yard wall, and at night he could see the glow of the lights over the fold of land from his own door.

So he had eleven, and a gang of wallers who could lay rubble fit to keep sheep in and not much else, and a yard full of stone waiting to be dressed, and a prince who had come to him three weeks ago with a drawing of a house and asked him how many of those his gangs could raise before the frost.

Hewitt had told him. The prince had written it down and thanked him and gone away, and Hewitt had known from the thanking that something was coming, and had decided, walking home that night past the dock's glow, that whatever it was he was going to hate it.

It was at the rail works, and it was the size of a barn.

That was the first thing. He had expected a tool. He had heard the talk on the flat, as everybody had, that the works was building something for the prince out of rail iron that was not a wagon and not an engine, and he had pictured a hoist, or a crane, or one of the mortar mills they had been promising the flat since the first quarter. He had not pictured this.

It stood in the rail works yard across the water, over a trial footing the works men had laid for it in the cinders: two lines of their own rail, laid either side of the footing a good twenty feet apart, and on the rails a frame of riveted iron on four legs, and the legs on little wheeled trucks like the ones that ran under the ore wagons. The legs went up higher than a house. At their heads was a beam, right across, from one pair of legs to the other, and the beam sat on four great screws, one at each leg's head, as thick as a man's arm, with iron wheels on them that a man could turn. On the beam ran a carriage. Under the carriage hung a thing like a funnel with three throats, three hoppers side by side feeding down into one, and under that a nozzle.

The nozzle was square. It was an iron die the size of a man's two fists, with a square mouth about four fingers across and a finger and a half deep, and round it, fast to it, a collar of heavy iron, riveted shut, with nothing on it at all, not a bolt head or a port or a mark.

Jorrin was on the beam. He was the prince's lathe man, Bram's old apprentice, and he had the run of the rail works while Bram was at the dock and the new engine house by the yard, and he was up on the beam in his shirt with a spanner, doing something to the carriage.

"What's in the collar?" Hewitt said.

"A die," said Jorrin, not looking down.

"I can see the die. What's round it?"

"Iron."

Hewitt looked at him a long time. Jorrin went on with his spanner. After a while Hewitt went round the thing and looked at it from the other side, and it was the same from the other side, and he did not ask again.

Weekes owned the mixes, and Weekes did it with a cone.

Hewitt had worked under Weekes since the first quarter's footings, and had heard him say perhaps two hundred words in all of them, and could have told you which two hundred. The master builder came across the water on the second morning with a works boy behind him carrying three sheet-iron cones, each the height of a man's forearm, open at both ends, wide at the bottom and narrow at the top, and set them down on a board in the yard, and nodded at the mortar mill.

There were three mixes, and three bins, and the bins were marked in chalk.

The first was the grey. It was the dock's mortar, near enough: lime burnt from the ridge's blue stone, over the sea at Emberfell where the mountain breathed hot enough to burn it, and brought back in the sulphur boats; and the grey ash off the mountain that went in with it. It set under water and it set hard, and the men on the flat had started to call it cement, which was a word Hewitt did not know the origin of and suspected the prince did. The second was the rubbish. That was what the chalk said, in Weekes's hand: RUBBISH. Common lime off the ridge kilns, with crushed brick in it, and slag off the foundry heap ground to grit, and the sweepings of the kilns. The third was dirt. It was the earth that came out of a cellar when you dug one, with chopped straw in it and a shovel of lime to a barrow.

Weekes filled the first cone with the grey, packed it with a rod, struck it level, and lifted the cone straight up off the board.

The grey stood there in the cone's shape. It sagged a hair at the top and stood.

He did the same with the rubbish, and it stood, a little lower. Then the dirt, which went down by a thumb and then stood. Weekes looked at the three of them standing on the board for a while. Then he took the first one, the grey, back to the mill and had it made again with less water, and did it again, and that time it did not sag at all, and he nodded.

After that nothing went into the hoppers that had not stood in a cone first. If it slumped, it went back. Hewitt watched Weekes send back his own mill's batch twice that week without a word and thought that it was the first thing about the whole business he understood.

The grey was counted by the barrow. Weekes had a board nailed up by its bin and a boy with a slate, and every barrow of grey that went up to the hopper was a stroke on the slate, and the slate went to Ferra's book at the end of the day. Hewitt asked why that one and not the others.

"Boats," said Weekes.

The prince came on the fourth morning and did the thing with the stone.

Hewitt had not known there was going to be a thing with a stone. He was there because he had been asked to be there, and because he was not going to let the works men build a wall in his hearing without him. The prince came over the water with nobody behind him but Ward, his knight, who stood by the yard gate with his arms folded the whole time and looked at nothing, and he went up the ladder onto the beam, and Jorrin handed him a crystal out of a box.

It was nothing much. It was pale and plain, the size of a hen's egg, with one flat face, the kind of crystal Hewitt had seen in mine lamps. The prince sat down on the beam, twenty feet up, with his legs over the side and his back against the carriage, and held it in his hand, and shut his eyes.

That was all. He sat there.

Hewitt waited. The works men, who seemed to know about this, went on with what they were doing, quietly, and did not look up. Jorrin sat on the other end of the beam and did not do anything either. The sun went up the sky. A gull came and sat on the far leg and looked at the prince and went away again.

It was most of an hour. When it was done the prince opened his eyes and looked at the stone, and fitted it into a socket on the carriage that Hewitt had taken for a grease cup, and put his hand over it for a moment, and took his hand away.

"What was that?" said Hewitt, when he came down.

The prince looked at him. He had a way of looking at a man when he was asked a question as if the question were a good one, which Hewitt had noticed on the wall walk in the spring when the prince had gone along the coping with a level telling the setters it was a quarter inch high on the last three, and been right.

"A house," the prince said. "One house."

"In that."

"In that. I did most of it before. Nights. That was only the putting in." He wiped his hands on his coat, though there was nothing on them. "It was shorter than I thought. A wall's only a line."

Hewitt did not know what that meant, and said so. The prince seemed to like that better than if he had pretended.

They ran it that afternoon, and it laid the wall in spots.

The carriage ran along the beam with a small clicking, like a clock with the case off, a click to every finger's width, and the frame's trucks clicked along the rails the same way, and a boy on a stool by a board of brass levers at the foot of one leg did nothing whatsoever except sit there with his hand near the one marked STOP. The engine that turned it all was the size of a cask, humming on a bed at the side of the yard, fed by a wire off a pole, and it was the first time Hewitt had seen one of those, too.

The grey came down out of its hopper, and down the throat, and out of the square die onto the footing: a dab, square and dense and sharp-edged, the size of a fist. Then the carriage clicked on, a hand's width, and stopped, and the die put down another dab. Then another.

It was like watching a hen peck. It went along the footing dab by dab, square and neat, each one standing up on the cinder like a little dressed block, and came to the end of its run and went back the other way on top of the first, dab on dab, and Hewitt stood and watched it lay four courses of little square spots of mortar one on top of the other in the time it would have taken one of his men to mix a board.

Weekes went and got a bucket of water and threw it at the wall.

It came through. It came through every seam, between every dab and the next, in forty little spouts on the far side, and ran down the cinders.

The prince looked at it. Then he laughed, once, not happily, the way a man laughs at something he has done to himself, and said to Jorrin: "It's blotting."

"It's what you told it."

"I know it's what I told it." He went up the ladder again. "It's what the other one does. I didn't think." He sat down on the beam and shut his eyes, not for an hour this time, and not with the same stone; Jorrin gave him a new one out of the box. When he came down he said: "Line. Not spots. It's to put it down the way you'd pour it from a jug, and not stop."

The next run went down the footing in one bead, square, dense, a finger and a half deep, like a length of dressed stone the size of a man's two hands laid end to end with no joint in it at all. It went to the end and came back on top of itself. Weekes waited till there were six courses and threw his bucket, and the water ran down the face and off and did not come through anywhere.

The prince put his hand flat on the top course, where it was still wet, and pressed. It gave a little under his palm and pressed back.

"It pushes back," he said, quietly, as if to somebody who was not there. Hewitt did not know what he meant by that either, and this time did not ask.

It tore at the corner.

The trial footing had a corner in it, a square one, where the wall turned to make the end of a house, and the carriage came along the beam in a straight line laying its bead and got to the corner and turned, at speed, the way it went everywhere, and the bead did not turn with it. It tore. The outside edge of it stretched and broke and dragged a tail of grey round the corner like a rag caught on a nail, and the inside edge heaped up, and the corner came out ragged and weak and open on the outside. They ran it again slower and it tore less and still tore.

And at the ninth course, on the straight, the wall bellied.

Hewitt saw it before anybody. He had seen walls do it all his life, walls some fool had run up too high in a day. The bottom courses swelled out a little, like a man's belly over his belt, and then the top courses above them sagged in, and the whole length of it, where it had stood so square and sharp, took on a slow tired lean like a drunk against a door. The grey was standing up; the die made it stand up. But the courses under it had not taken yet, and they were carrying everything above them wet.

They stopped it. The prince came down and looked at the belly for some time.

Hewitt had meant to hate it. He had meant to stand at the side with his arms folded, like the knight at the gate, and hate it quietly and go home. He found, looking at that bellied wall, that he was not able to, because it was a wall, and it had done the thing walls did, and he knew what to do about it.

"You've no quoin," he said.

The prince turned round.

"No mason turns a corner in rubble." Hewitt went and put his hand on the torn corner. "You set a stone there first. Dressed, square, bonded both ways. And at every door and every window, a head and a sill and the jambs. Then you fill between. The corner's the stone, not the mortar. Your machine doesn't have to turn anything. It runs up to my stone and stops, and goes back."

The prince looked at the corner, and at him.

"And that," said Hewitt, and pointed at the belly, "is you've run it up in an afternoon. You don't. Not in lime. Not in anything. You lay a lift, knee high, and you leave it, and you go and do the next wall and the next, and by the time you come back round the first one's taken. Lay the whole row a lift high and walk back to the start. That's how we do a long wall. You've got a long wall. You've got a street of them."

"And?" said the prince. Hewitt had not said there was an and.

"And you're laying a wall, not a pie crust." He tapped the top course with one finger, and it gave. "Every few courses you want iron in it, across. A tie. Flat bar, bent at the ends, laid in the wet across the bead and the next one. Else the two faces go their own way in ten years and you've got a house with a crack up the middle of it from the roof to the step. Your machine won't lay a tie. So a man walks behind it with a bundle of ties and lays them."

He stopped. He had said a great deal more than he meant to.

The prince took out the small book he carried, and wrote in it, and Hewitt, who was standing close enough, saw that on the page there was already a line in the prince's hand, older, a little smudged, that said lifts and had been underlined. The prince wrote under it. He did not say he had had the one already. He wrote quoins, H. and ties, H. and closed the book.

"Will you set the quoins?" he said.

"Who else is going to set them?"

The second house of the trial pair was the one that made Jorrin laugh, and Hewitt never did understand why.

They had set the rails long enough for two, and the prince had put in one house, one stone. On the second morning Jorrin ran the frame on its trucks down to the far end of the rails, past the first house, and turned the brass lever that told the carriage to read its stone from the other end, and ran it. And the second house came out of the die backwards. Every wall of the first, laid the other way about: the door on the left instead of the right, the chimney breast on the other gable, the whole house the first house seen in a looking glass, so that the two of them stood on the cinders back to back and made a pair, with one wall between them that was both of theirs.

Jorrin laughed so hard at that he had to sit down on the beam. He said something to the prince about lead. The prince laughed too, which Hewitt had not seen him do before, and said: "Leave it. It's better." And then to Hewitt, as if that explained it: "One stone does both."

Hewitt had seen enough by then not to ask about the lead.

On the fifth day they let the box run down on purpose, and that was the day Hewitt decided he did not hate it.

There was a box on the carriage, an iron box the size of a loaf, riveted shut like the collar, with a pipe of iron from it to the collar, and once a week a works boy came up the ladder and took it off and put on another one the same, and the old one went back across the water to somewhere Hewitt was not told about. On the fifth day the prince told Jorrin not to change it, and they ran the machine all afternoon and waited.

Late in the day the bead changed. Hewitt saw it happen. It came out of the square die square, as it always had, and then as he watched it began to round off at its edges as it left the mouth, and sag, very slightly, the way any mortar did when it came off a trowel onto a wall. Not a slump. The mix was stiff; Weekes had seen to that. But it was only mortar now, and not whatever it had been before, and it lay on the course below the way mortar lay, round-shouldered and a little soft.

Bram came up for an hour that evening, from the dock. Hewitt had not seen him in a month. He was grey with stone dust to the elbows and did not look as though he had slept, and he had a pair of things in his hands he had made at the dock forge between two other things: two flat iron trowels, a hand long, on arms, with a spring behind each. He fitted them to the die, one on each side, so that they rode along the wet bead as it came out of the mouth and pressed its two faces flat and square, the way a mason's trowel does, and went back across the water without waiting to see it run.

It laid again. It laid slower, because the prince had Jorrin run it at half pace, and lower, a hand to a lift and not a knee, and it came out uglier, the trowels leaving little ridges where they rode, and the ninth course was a long way off. But it laid a wall, and the wall stood, and it was a wall a man could live behind.

Hewitt found himself nodding at it. He noticed he was doing it and did not stop. He did not nod at the square bead that stood up by itself for no reason anybody would tell him. He nodded at the springs. A thing that did a clever thing he did not understand was a thing he would have to take on trust forever. A thing that did it worse when the clever part stopped, and still did it, was a tool.

They took it to the second quarter in the first week of the autumn, in pieces, on the wagons, up through the south gate, and put it together again over the third street.

The third street was twenty plots each side, pegged and strung, the trenches for the drains and the water already dug and laid and covered along the middle of it and marked with a row of stones, because nothing went up in New Kaldo until what went under it was in. The works laid the rails down the near side of the first row, twenty feet apart, the frame on them straddling four plots at a time, and ran a wire on poles from the ring road's line to the machine's little engine, and put up two lamps on masts at the row's ends that burned white off the same wire.

And Hewitt's men went in ahead of it.

That was the part nobody had drawn. He saw it the first morning, standing in the street with the sun coming up over the east wall. His eleven went down the row setting quoins: every corner of every house, a dressed stone out of his yard, square, bonded both ways, set true to the Tarrants' pegs with a level, two courses at a time. Every door, a sill and two jambs. Every window, a sill. His wallers came behind them with barrows of the dressed stone. And behind all of them came the machine, clicking along its rails, laying its grey bead up to a quoin and stopping and going back, filling between his stones the way a man fills between the stones he has set.

It laid the outside face in grey and the inside face in grey, a hand apart, and between the two faces, in the same pass, it ran a line of the rubbish back and forth in a zigzag like the stitching on a sack, so that the two faces were joined all along by a web and the web was filled; and where the plan said an inside wall, it switched its throats over and laid the dirt. It left gaps. Hewitt had not expected the gaps. Where the prince's plan wanted a pipe to go up a wall, or a drain to come down one, or the wire, one day, the machine left a square slot running up the wall's middle for it, course on course, as neat as a flue. And it left a flue. It left one up the middle of every party wall, all the way, where nobody would ever again have to cut one into a finished wall with a hammer and a curse.

By the end of the first week the wallers were calling it the Walker, because it walked the street, and nobody meant it as a name, and by the end of the second the works men were calling it that too, and Weekes had written it on the grey's board.

Behind the machine came Hewitt's oldest setter, Dunn, with a bundle of flat iron ties over his shoulder and a boy with another bundle, laying a tie across the wet bead every third course, the length of the row.

It went a lift high down the whole row, knee high, twenty houses, and came back to the start, and by the time it got there the first lift had taken. Then the next. It ran at night under the white lamps, with the brass-lever boy asleep on his stool and a works man awake beside him, and a relief crew for Dunn. When the ground storey stood, the carpenters came in behind with the joists and laid them on a ledge the machine had left for them along the inside of every wall, and the works men went up the legs and turned the great screws, a quarter turn each in turn, four men, until the whole beam had climbed a storey; and it went on.

It did not do anything else. It did not do the roof, or the floors, or the stairs, or the glass, or the pipes, or the hearths. The door and window heads were Hewitt's, dressed lintels, set by hand. There was not an arch in the street. The walls came out of the die in courses you could count, a finger and a half each, banded like a coil of rope, and his men's dressed corners stood up out of the banding at the end of every house pale and square and sharp, like the bones of a hand under the skin.

Weekes came up the street every evening and walked it with his level and said nothing. That was good. If it had not been good he would have said something.

Hewitt thought about the mages once, and then did not think about them again.

He had seen what they did on the wall, the old master's people from the capital, standing at the foot of the wall in a row with their hands out and the earth rising under them into the wall's core like dough rising, and the masons going up after them with the facing. They were on the rail now, most of them, and the rest on the gate towers and the palace, and nobody had spare mages for a street of houses. And a mage raised a thing once. He had watched one do it and walk away grey in the face to sit down. This machine raised the same house down a row as many times as there were plots to put it on, and when whatever was in that iron box ran down, it went on raising it, slower, on Bram's springs.

Nobody had lost a day. That was the other thing he thought about, walking home. He had been ready, three weeks ago, to be told his gangs were not wanted. His gangs were wanted more than they had ever been. His eleven set quoins down the second quarter from dawn, and his wallers carried dressed stone, and the stone that went up now was the stone that mattered: corners, heads, sills, the palace drum, the gate towers, the church, and there was not enough of any of it, and he had been told to find more men.

One of them was a northern man, from a village beyond Wayford, who had come down with the camp in the spring and could set a quoin as well as anybody in the gang. On the third evening he came to Hewitt at the yard and asked where a man could get paper to write a letter home. Hewitt told him the works would give it him, and the camp's clerk would take it on the Wayford train, and watched him go off toward the works with the look of a man who has something to tell. Hewitt knew what it would be. It would be that he had seen an iron thing in the south lay a house wall with no mason's hand on it. It would not be that he had set every corner of that house himself.

Hewitt thought the lodges up north would have something to say about that letter. He did not think they would have the whole of it.

The week before the first gale came up the coast, the ground storeys of the whole row were up, and the first pair, the house and its looking-glass house, had their upper walls.

Ferra came out on her day, with the site's book in her saddlebag, and walked the row with Weekes and wrote in it, and at the end of the row she stopped where Hewitt was standing at the first pair's corner.

He had his mallet in his hand. He had not meant to do anything with it. He had come to look at the upper walls, and he looked at them, and then he found he had put his hand out and tapped the printed wall below them with the mallet's head, the way you sound a stone in the quarry before you let a man put a chisel to it, and listened.

It rang dull and right. Not like a stone. Not like a wall of stones either, that rings different wherever you strike it. Like one thing, all through, that had been laid wet and taken and meant to stand.

He took the chisel out of his belt and cut his mark into the quoin at the corner, the dressed one, his own stone: a ring with an arrow through it, as he had cut it into every stone he had dressed since he was fourteen, as his master had cut his before him. He did not cut it into the grey. The grey was not his. The corner was.

Ferra watched him do it. She did not say anything. Then she opened the site's book on her arm, and wrote, and turned it round so he could see.

Third street, second quarter. Walls printed. Quoins and ties, Hewitt. His mark on the corner.

Hewitt read it twice. He did not know what "printed" meant, and supposed it was the prince's word, and supposed that by spring everybody on the flat would be using it. He nodded. Ferra closed the book and went to find her horse.

Down the street, in the dusk, the white lamps came on over the machine, and it began to click.
