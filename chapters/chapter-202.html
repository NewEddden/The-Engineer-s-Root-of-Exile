Chapter 202 - Five Dinners

Hollin gave him the purse at the door.

Not at the table. At the table Hollin had been everything his sloped and showy hand had promised in Sethe's boxes: a big handsome man going soft at the jaw, in a coat too good for the country, who laughed first at his own jokes and longest at the prince's, and kept the prince's cup full with his own hand, and told three stories about the old king's hunting that Emeric had heard in the capital before he was twelve. Hollin liked him. That was the trouble with Hollin; Emeric could see it, by the third course, and did not know what to do with it. The man had paid a fifth of forty Gold to have him put in a boat in the dark, and he liked him, the way a man likes a dog that has turned out to be a good-natured dog after all.

At the door, in the dark, with the horses brought round and the lamp over the step and his own steward Adlow holding the cloaks, Hollin put a hand on Emeric's shoulder, heavily, the way a man does who has had as much wine as he has given; and with the other hand put something into the pocket of the old gold-laced coat. Small, heavy. It clinked once.

"The usual, Highness," said Hollin, low and fond, close to his ear. "For your trouble with the paper. Sethe always found it eased things." He patted the shoulder. "We'll talk properly. When Placit's had his say. He likes to have his say."

Emeric said something loose and grateful and did not remember afterward what it was. He got onto his horse at the second try. Adlow, at the step with the cloaks over his arm, watched him do it, and did not smile, and did not look away.

The purse was twelve Gold. Ferra counted it in Sethe's old counting room in the hall at Wayford at midnight, on the green baize, with the lamp pulled down, and wrote it in her book, the date and the sum and the words in which it had been given, as he told them to her; and tied it up again in its own string and sealed the knot with wax and her thumb, and put it in the strongbox with Sethe's ledgers.

"The usual," she said. "Sethe's tenth. In coin, by hand." She looked at the sealed purse in the box. "He's put you on the books."

"He likes me."

"Everybody likes a man they think they've bought," said Ferra, and locked the box.



She had come north on the first of June with two clerks and the house's strongbox and a trunk of her own, to Sethe's hall, which was hers as much as anything in Wayford was; and had taken Sethe's old counting room on the ground floor for the summer and turned it inside out in a day. Sethe's chairs went into the passage. Sethe's carpet went into the yard and was beaten by a boy with a stick until he cried. Ferra's own table came up from the cart, the plain one, and her own lamp; and by the second evening the room looked so exactly like her counting room at Kaldo that Emeric, coming in at dusk from the Keelings' vat house, stopped in the doorway with the strange feeling of having walked four hundred miles and arrived home.

She was at the table with her back to the door and her sleeves pinned up, writing.

He came up behind her chair. He put his hands on her shoulders, and she did not stop writing; and he bent and put his mouth to the side of her neck, under the ear, where the red hair was pinned up off it and the skin was warm from the lamp, and left it there.

Her pen stopped.

"I'm doing Dray's rents," said Ferra.

"I know."

"Then you know I'm in the middle of Dray's rents." Her voice was perfectly level. Her pen did not move. "And you're in the middle of my neck."

He moved his mouth a half inch lower, to the place where the neck went into the shoulder. Under his hands, very slightly, she shivered.

"Work first," said Ferra. It came out a little less level.

"It's dusk. The vats have shut. The square's shut. Even Ottoline's shut." He put his chin on her shoulder and looked at the page under her pen: a column of figures in her small exact hand, half added. "Dray's rents have waited two hundred years. They'll wait an hour."

"Dray's rents," said Ferra, "are the only thing in Wayford tonight that has never once lied to me." She put the pen down, very deliberately, across the page, and turned her head; and her face was a hand's breadth from his, pink from the throat up, and composed, and her eyes were not composed at all. "Fun later," she said. "Work first. That's the order. You taught me the order."

"I don't remember teaching you that."

"You taught it to the whole barony. You had Sela paint it over the school door." She looked at his mouth. "I'll be done at ten."

"Ten."

"Ten," said Ferra, and picked up the pen, and turned back to the column, and added three figures wrong, which he watched her do, and which she noticed a breath after him, and struck through, and did again, without looking round.

He went and sat in Sethe's old chair by the empty grate with a book and did not read it. At ten exactly she blotted the page and closed the ledger and stood up, and came across the room, and took the book out of his hands and put it on the floor, and sat down across his lap with her knees on either side of him in the deep old chair and her hands in his hair; and kissed him as if she had been adding up to it for three hours, which she had.

It was a long time before either of them said anything. The lamp on her table burned down. Somewhere in the hall a clerk went past the door on the way to bed, and stopped, and went on faster. Ferra's hair came down, pin by pin, under his hands, and fell over both of them; and she pressed down into him in the chair, slowly, and then not slowly, with a small fierce sound against his mouth that he had never heard her make in daylight; and his hands went down her back to her waist and pulled her closer, and she let him, and moved against him again, deliberately, as if she were testing a figure she already knew was right.

"Ferra."

"I know." She did not stop. Her mouth was on his jaw. "I know. We said."

"We said."

"The three of us. Whatever it is." She said it into his neck, breathless, and laughed, very quietly, at herself. "I wrote it down. In my own hand. And signed it." She lifted her head and looked at him in the low light with her hair everywhere and her mouth red. "That was very stupid of me."

"You can strike it through."

"I don't strike things through." She put her forehead against his. She was breathing as if she had run up the keep stair twelve times. "I enter a correction under them. With the date." She closed her eyes. "Not tonight."

She did not get off his lap for a long while after that. Neither of them moved, much. She sat with her forehead against his and her hands in his hair and her heart going against his chest like a hammer, and slowly, slowly, it quietened; and then she kissed him once more, very lightly, on the corner of the mouth, and got up, and gathered her hair in both hands, and looked down at him.

"Dray dines tomorrow," she said. "You'll want to look drunk. You look drunk now." She went to the door. "Go to bed. Your own."



Dray dined in a house with no books in it.

That was the first thing Emeric noticed, and the last. A square, loud, new-built house on the river below the weir, with stuffed heads on the walls of every room and a great many guns in a rack in the hall, old wheel-locks from the capital, ornamental, silver-mounted, that nobody had fired in fifty years; and no books. Not one. Dray wrote, as Ferra had said, barely a hand at all; his letters in the boxes had been dictated to a clerk and signed with a mark like a thrown horseshoe. He talked about horses. He talked about dogs. He asked the prince, three times, whether it was true about the iron road, that it went faster than a horse could gallop, and laughed every time he was told it was, as at a very good lie.

Emeric gave him the case of bottles with the three bars on their shoulders. Dray handed it to a servant without looking at it, and Emeric watched it go out through the door to wherever a man with no books kept the ink he never used, and thought: that one will never write a line. That one will have his clerk do it, in the clerk's room, with the clerk's ink.

He was wrong about that. He did not find out until later.



Strade did not believe him.

Strade dined at five, early, as a man dines who means to be in bed by nine and at his desk by six; at a small round table in a small square room with good plain furniture and a fire laid but not lit, though it was June, because Strade did not waste a fire. He was the youngest of the five, fifty or so, thin, clean-shaven, quiet, with very neat grey hair and very neat grey clothes and a way of resting his eyes on whoever was speaking without moving them at all. He wrote six lines to a letter, never more. He said, in the course of the evening, perhaps sixty words.

Emeric was drunk at him for two hours. He talked about his father. He talked about Corbin. He talked about the iron road and the money and the ruin and nobody ever saying well done; and he asked, at the cheese, as he had asked Placit, how a man might get a little share of a fat trade on his own road, as a friend, a silent one. He did it well. He had done it four times now, and it came more easily each time, which he did not like; it came out of him like a song he had sung so often in the capital that his mouth knew it without him.

Strade listened to all of it, with his eyes resting on Emeric's face.

At the end, when the bottles with the key on their shoulders had been carried in and praised, and the prince had said his piece about the black, the finest black in Randillia, made it himself, Strade picked up one of the bottles. Not to admire it. He held it to the candle and looked through it, at the black inside, the way a jeweller looks through a stone for the flaw, and set it down again, exactly where it had been.

"Thank you, Highness," he said. "I'll use it."

And then, as Emeric rose, swaying a little, and put out a hand to the back of his chair, Strade said, in the same quiet voice, without getting up:

"You were a great deal drunker in the capital."

The room was very quiet. The fire that was not lit sat in its grate.

"I've had practice since," said Emeric, and laughed, the loose laugh, and heard it come out a fraction late.

"No doubt," said Strade. He looked at the hand on the chair back. "Mind the step, Highness. It's steeper than it looks."

Ward was at the foot of the step with the horses. Emeric went down it carefully and got onto his horse at the second try and rode down the lane at a walk, swaying, until they were round the corner, and then did not sit up straight. He rode on swaying all the way back to the square, slowly, in the dark, in case Strade was the kind of man who sent a boy to follow a guest home. He was. Ward said so, without turning his head, at the second corner: a boy, on foot, forty yards behind, keeping to the walls. Emeric had felt him at the first. A small quick note, frightened and eager, the size of a child who has been given a Copper and a job.

He fell off his horse in the square, in front of the posting house, in full view of the boy and the lamplighter and three carters on the inn bench, and lay on the cobbles laughing at the stars until Ward picked him up.

It was a much better fall than the keep wall. It cost him a bruise on the hip the size of a plate, and Ferra, when she saw the bruise, said nothing at all, for so long that he understood she was very angry and could not decide who with.



Lisle sent his case back unopened.

It came on the morning after Strade's dinner, on a cart from the woods road, down out of the ridge country where Lisle kept his hall and the shire's court, carried into Sethe's hall by Lisle's own steward: the case of four bottles with the stag on their shoulders, in its straw, with its seals unbroken, and a letter in a long clear gentleman's hand on paper that was not Pell's.

Lord Lisle thanked the Holder of Wayford for his generosity, which he could not accept, as it was his rule to accept no gifts from any neighbour with whom he had not first done business; and he looked forward, if His Highness should ever wish to do business, to discussing the matter of the woods road and the toll at Harrow Bridge, which had been in his family for six generations, at His Highness's convenience. He remained, et cetera.

Ferra read it twice and handed it back.

"He's the only one of the six with any sense," she said.

"He's not one of the five."

"No." She looked at the stag on the bottle in the straw. "And he wants us to know it. That letter isn't a refusal. It's a receipt. It says, I touched nothing of yours, and I can prove it." She put the bottle back in the case. "He's seen something. Or guessed it. He's a careful man and he's guessed that a careful man shouldn't touch anything you send him this summer." She closed the case. "I'd like to know what."



Maren cooked the dinner that went back the other way.

A baron dined his neighbours; it was owed. So on the twentieth of June the Holder of Wayford gave a dinner in Sethe's old hall, for four of the five and their wives, because Strade sent his excuses in six lines; and Maren came north on the train with two kitchen girls and a crate of Zale's spices, and took Sethe's kitchen in an afternoon the way she had once taken Kaldo's, at the top of her voice.

She was not allowed into the hall. She had asked. She had stood in the long room at Kaldo in June with her arms folded and asked Ferra to her face why she might not come in and serve at a table where a lot of fat old men who had paid to have the prince put in a boat were going to sit and eat her food; and Ferra had said because she would kill one of them with a ladle before the second course, and Maren had thought about it and said that was fair.

So she cooked. She cooked the best dinner Wayford had eaten in a generation, with Zale's green pods in the sauce that made the tongue go numb and Vennor's wife ask for the receipt; and from the kitchen, through the serving hatch, by way of the kitchen girls, she sent up the prince's wine.

It was watered. Two parts water to one of wine, in a jug with a chipped lip that only the kitchen girls knew to put at the prince's place. He found out at the first mouthful. He looked down the table at the hatch, where a broad red face was framed in the opening for a breath, glaring at him, and gone.

He was drunk all evening on two parts water, loudly, gloriously; and Placit at the far end of the table watched him do it with his pale eyes and his close white beard, and smiled when he asked about the share again, at the cheese; and Hollin laughed, and Vennor laughed after his wife did, and Dray asked about the iron road.

When they had gone, at midnight, Maren came up out of the kitchen with her sleeves rolled and her face scarlet from the range, and stood in the hall doorway and looked at the wreck of the table, and at him.

"Your eyes are clear," she said. "Good."

"You watered it."

"I watered it." She folded her arms. "The healer said. Before I came north. She said if I let you come home with your eyes red she'd have my kitchen made into a ward." She looked at him a breath longer. "I'd have watered it anyway."



The knots went up on the night of the twenty-fourth, in the dark of the moon.

The K Discovery came in over Wayford from the north at one in the morning, a mile up and dark, with her stones shut and her panes blacked and Fletcher at the stick; and came down, slowly, on the hover, until she hung three hundred feet over the junction above the vats with the town's last lamps below her and the weathervane on the Staple house ridge turning slowly in the night wind, a black bale against the black.

Nobody below looked up. Nobody ever did, at night.

Tobe and Cade were on the rails, with the lines. Ward was at the hatch with a dark lantern. And on the deck by the hatch, in a black suit with his face blacked and the suction pads strapped to his boots and his off hand, with a bag of knots on his back and a coil of line clipped to his belt, was Osric.

Ward had chosen him. Not Rylan, who had asked; not Declan. Osric.

"He has come off the keep wall seven times," Ward had said, at the long table, flatly, when Rylan objected. "He knows where the cups fail. Every way. He has a drawing of each." He had looked at Rylan. "You have never been on it. You do not know where the cups fail. You will learn in Wayford, on a roof, at night." He had looked back at the table. "Osric."

So it was Osric.

Tess had checked his cups on the slip at Kaldo before the ship left. Emeric had it from Cade afterward, in more detail than Cade knew he was giving it: Osric standing in front of her chair with his boot up on its footrest like a horse being shod, and Tess with her data book open on her knees at the page of little falling men, going over every cup with her thumb, the pitch rim and the boss and the pinhole, and pressing each one against the glass panel on the side of her chair, and tapping it, and pulling, and listening to it pop. All four. Then all four again. Osric had stood on one leg the whole time with his face doing something careful, and had not said anything, and had not looked at anything but the cup she was holding.

"They'll hold," Tess had said at last. "On dressed stone. Look before you put your foot down."

"Yes."

"And if one goes, it's the off hand first. Every time. The off hand's pitch goes cold first because you hold your hand out from your body." She had not looked up. "Keep your hand in when you can. Against your chest. Warm it."

"Yes."

"And don't come off a roof in Wayford," said Tess, to the cup, "because I'll not be there to write it down," and had handed him his boot back.

Now Osric went over the hatch coaming on the line in the dark, and down, hand over hand, and Emeric lay on the deck at the hatch's edge with his sense spread as wide and thin as he could make it and felt him go: a steady close note, frightened and very calm, going down through three hundred feet of night air toward the black roof below.

The first knot went on the Staple house's weathervane. Osric stood on the ridge on two cups, on the slate, the line slack above him, and lashed the knot to the iron upright of the vane under the bale where it could not be seen from the ground, two little stones bound in wire, no bigger than a pair of walnuts. Then down the slope of the roof on the cups, pop, hold, pop, hold, to the chimney stack over the room where the Staple's clerk sat at his high desk by day, and into the chimney's mouth a hand's length, out of the rain and the soot: the second. Then the line took him up again.

The Staple's counting house on the square, in the flue over the clerks' room. Placit's house, the long panelled room's chimney. Hollin's, by the square. Vennor's, by the weir, with the river loud below. Dray's, which Emeric nearly did not bother with, and did. Strade's, last, because Strade's house had a dog, and the dog had to be thought about; Cade thought about it with a piece of Maren's mutton on a string lowered from three hundred feet, and the dog, which had been bred by a man who wasted nothing, ate the mutton and went to sleep in the yard.

Between the houses and the square, and down the lane behind the square to Ambry's ink house, the relay knots: on a church weathercock, on a tree, on the gable of a barn, a little bundle of wire and stone each, a few hundred yards apart, a chain of them through the town in the dark. Osric set nine. He came off once, on the barn gable, where the stone under a cup had been patched with plaster nobody had told anybody about; and hung on the line twenty feet above a midden for a quarter of a minute, turning slowly, until Tobe hauled him up the length of a man and he found the gable again with his boots. Nobody in the barn woke.

He came back up the hatch at a quarter to four, as the sky began to go grey over the woods, with the bag empty and the pitch on his off hand cold as a stone.

"Once," said Ward, at the hatch. "On a barn."

"Plaster, sir. Under paint."

"Tess will want it written down," said Ward; and for the first time in Emeric's hearing, Ward said someone's name in a voice that was not entirely flat, and it was hers, and he said it to Osric.



The last knot was not on a roof.

It sat on a bed of small grey stones in a back room behind Ambry's ink house, a room that had been the old man's paper store and had a door into the yard and no windows, and Ambry had sold the use of it to the Holder for the summer, with the shutters nailed and the lock changed, and asked no questions, because the punches were already in his strongbox and he was an old man who knew a bargain when one had been made with him.

The mana printer filled half the room.

It had come north in a crate on the flat wagon three days before, from the cellar under the keep at Kaldo, where it had printed every form and every school book in the barony for three years. Ferra had not wanted it to come. She had said so at the long table, in so many words: that the mana printer had been built never to leave the walls of Kaldo, and they had all agreed it, because a machine that pushed ink by stone, without type, in the wrong hands was a precast-glyph waiting for a thief. She had said it twice. And then she had looked at what the knots could give and what they could not, and at the only stone in Kaldo that could read a stroke and turn it back into a stroke, the control stone that ran the printer's bed, and had said, at last, in a voice he did not like: "Then it goes. And it comes home on the day the knots are drained. And until then the school prints its own copybooks on the plain press, and my forms go back to being written by hand." She had looked at him. "Mine. By my hand. Every one."

She had meant it as a cost and he had taken it as one.

The printer worked backward now. That was all it was. In Kaldo it had read a pattern Emeric cut into its control stone and pushed ink square by square across a page to make it. Here, the last knot sat on the frame above the bed, and gave the pulse it heard from the next knot along, and the next, and the next, out through the town and up the lanes to six chimneys; and the control stone under the bed read the pulse as Emeric read a wet line with his eyes shut, stroke by stroke, the shape of each stroke and the order it went down in; and pushed ink to match. Kaldo ink, the plain grey, dry dust on a sized sheet, square by square, as the stroke went down a mile away.

Two of Ferra's clerks sat by it in turns, day and night, a man and a woman she had chosen from the counting room for the third condition. The printers, the garrison called them, because the garrison called everything by what it did. They fed it sheets. They took each page off the bed as it finished and put it, face down, in a box, unread; and twice a day Ferra came in by the yard door with her own key and took the box away.

On the night of the twenty-fourth, at four in the morning, with the knots up and the ship gone north into the grey, the box was empty.

It was still empty a week later.



He flew home on the first of July for the forge, because the forge had runs waiting that could not wait for the Staple, and came down on the slip at Kaldo at two in the morning in the dark, out of the ship's hatch onto the wet stone.

Imani was on the slip with a lamp.

She had not been sent for. Nobody had told her the ship was coming, because nobody ever did; but the wall's sensor had a stretch on the river side and the man sitting the board had seen the river stair's needle swing at a quarter to two, and had sent a boy to the gate, as he had been told to do, and the boy had gone to the ward, as nobody had told him to do. So she was there. In her ward apron, with her hair tied up and coming down, and a lamp in one hand and her bag in the other, standing at the top of the slip as the ship's lines went over the bollards.

"Come here," she said.

He came up the slip to her.

"Into the light." She held the lamp up. "I said I'd look at your eyes. Maren's letter said she watered it. I don't believe Maren. Maren would tell me the sea was dry if she thought it'd keep her kitchen." She put her free hand on his jaw and turned his face to the lamp, firmly, the way she turned a child's. "Look at the lamp. Not at me. At the lamp."

He looked at her.

"At the lamp," said Imani.

"You're in front of the lamp."

"I'm beside the lamp. Look at it." She tilted his chin a fraction. Her thumb was on his cheekbone. She was looking into his left eye from a hand's breadth away, very seriously, with her lips pressed together, and the lamplight was in her face and in her hair where it was coming down, and she smelled of the ward's soap and of the night and very faintly of whatever she had been doing before the boy came, which by the stain on her apron had been somebody's bad leg. "Clear," she said. "The left's clear. Now the right."

He put his arm round her waist and pulled her in.

She came the last step without meaning to, off balance, against his chest, with the lamp swinging out at the end of her arm and the bag knocking his knee; and did not let go of his jaw.

"The right eye," said Imani.

"It's fine."

"I'll decide if it's fine." She did not move away. She turned his face a little to the right with her thumb and looked into the other eye from closer still, frowning; and he bent his head and tried to kiss her, and she moved her face exactly the width of his mouth to one side without taking her eyes off his pupil, and he kissed the air beside her ear.

"Hold still," said Imani.

"I've been away a month."

"Forty-seven days. Hold still." She tilted his head back a fraction with her thumb. He kissed her temple, since it was there. She let him. She did not stop looking at his eye. "The pupil's slow," she said. "A little. You've not slept."

"I slept on the ship."

"You lay on the ship. I know what you do on ships." He put his mouth to the corner of hers, and she turned her face away from it a quarter inch, the same way, without hurry, as a woman moves her head out of the line of a branch on a path she knows. "I'm working," she said.

"It's two in the morning."

"I'm still working. You're my work. You're the only patient I've got who comes in at two in the morning off a ship and won't hold his head still." She let go of his jaw at last and put two fingers on his wrist, under the band; and he took the opportunity, and kissed her neck, just under the jaw, where her pulse was; and felt it jump under his mouth.

Imani stopped counting.

"That's cheating," she said.

"Yours is fast."

"Mine's my business." She did not take her fingers off his wrist. She did not take her neck away either. "Mine's fast because I ran down the slip with a lamp at two in the morning in an apron with a stranger's leg on it, because a boy said the ship was in, which is a very stupid thing for a healer to do and I'll be ashamed of it tomorrow." He kissed her neck again, lower. Her voice went up a little at the end and came down again. "Emeric."

"Yes."

"Work first."

"You've finished. Both eyes. You said clear."

"I said the left was clear. The right was slow. I've not decided about the right." She took a breath. "And I've not counted your pulse, because somebody keeps..." He did it again. "Keeps doing that," said Imani, unsteadily, and dropped the bag.

It hit the slip with a clank of bottles. She did not look at it.

She put the lamp down on the bollard beside them, carefully, because a healer did not drop a lamp on a slip by a ship full of stones whatever else she dropped; and took his face in both hands, and looked at it once more in the light, both eyes, all over, with her healer's look, the one that found the thing that was wrong; and whatever she found, she decided about it. And kissed him.

She kissed the way she did everything that she had decided to do. Thoroughly, and without apology, and as if she had been meaning to for forty-seven days and had counted every one of them. Her hands went from his face into his hair and held on. He backed her the one step to the coping of the slip without either of them deciding it and she leaned back against the cold stone and pulled him with her by the collar, and somebody, he did not know which of them, made a sound that carried, in the quiet, all the way down the slip to the ship.

On the ship, Cade dropped a line.

Imani broke off. She turned her head on the coping and looked down the slip at the K Discovery, at Cade on her deck very busy with the line he had dropped and Tobe beside him looking with great attention at the sky, and Fletcher's face at the bow cockpit's port, which disappeared.

"Oh, God," said Imani, and put her forehead against his chest, and began to laugh.

She laughed until she had to hold on to his coat. He held her up against the coping and laughed too, into her hair, helplessly, at two in the morning on the slip, while on the ship behind them three men coiled the same line several times over and did not look round.

"Ward isn't aboard," said Imani at last, muffled, into his coat. "Tell me Ward isn't aboard."

"Ward's in Wayford."

"Thank God." She lifted her head. Her face was wet at the eyes from laughing and her hair had come all the way down and her mouth was red, and she looked at him with enormous dignity. "Your right pupil," she said, "is perfectly normal. I've decided." She bent and picked up her bag, and checked the bottles in it by the lamp, one by one, and closed it. "Go to bed. Sleep. Actually sleep. If you're not asleep by three I'll know, and I'll come and give you something that'll put you under till noon, and I'll enjoy it."

"You could come and make sure."

Imani looked at him.

"Fun later," she said. "I've a leg in the ward that won't wait." She picked up the lamp. "And I'm not going up the keep stair in this apron past the night watch at two in the morning with my hair down, to make sure of anything. They'd have it all over the dock quarter by breakfast." She started up the slip; and stopped, and came back two steps, and kissed him once more, quickly, hard, on the mouth, as if to make sure of something after all.

"Forty-seven days," she said. "Don't do it again."

She went up the slip with her lamp swinging. He watched it go round the corner of the yard toward the ward and out of sight; and turned round, and found Cade and Tobe and Fletcher all three at the ship's rail, looking at him, with perfectly blank faces.

"Line's coiled, Highness," said Cade.

"Thank you, Cade."

"Coiled it nine times," said Tobe, to the sky.
