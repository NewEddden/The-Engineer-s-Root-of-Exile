Chapter 73 - Twenty-One

Hale's office was the warmest room in the keep, and Hale was the only person in Kaldo who seemed to think that was an accident.

It was a narrow room off the hall with one window onto the gate yard, a stove the size of a dog kennel, and shelves on three walls that held every paper the barony had produced since before Emeric was born, in boxes, by year, labeled on the ends in the same upright hand. Hale was at his desk when Emeric knocked, cutting a new pen. He finished the cut before he stood. Then he stood, and put the knife and the quill side by side on the blotter, parallel, and waited.

"The out-letters," Emeric said. "Everything the barony has sent since I came. Is that a thing you can give me?"

"It is the outgoing register, your highness."

"Then may I see the outgoing register?"

"You may." Hale did not move toward the shelves. "All of it, your highness, or the part since August of the year you came?"

"Since I came."

"That is the second book." He went to the shelf behind the door, without looking along it, and took down a long book bound in calf gone black at the corners, and laid it on the desk, and opened it at a ribbon that had been there long enough to leave its color on the page. "The first book ends on the day his lordship left for the capital. I closed it myself. I did not think it right to go on writing in it."

Emeric knew the first book. It had gone north in a satchel two summers ago and been read aloud in a room in the capital, and three of its lines had been Imani's, and he had not known they were going to be read until they were. He did not ask to see it.

The second book was all Hale. Every line ran the same way across the page: the date, to whom, by what carrier, and a few words on the matter, never more than the line would hold. Grain to Milford. The carter's hundredweight. The salt boatman's name, again and again, down the carrier column, like a man who kept coming to the door.

"You expected me to ask for this," Emeric said.

Hale considered the remark as if it had been offered for sale.

"I expected someone would, your highness. In a house this size the out-letters are generally the first thing a new lord asks to see." A pause, quite short. "It has been two years and two months."

"Why didn't you bring it to me?"

"It is not my place to tell your highness what to read."

That was true, and it was also, Emeric thought, as close as Hale was ever going to come to saying that he had wondered.

"The war report," Emeric said. "In May. Tell me how it went."

Hale did not look at the book. He folded his hands in front of his coat and said it the way he would have said it to a magistrate.

"I wrote it fair in three copies on the fourth day after the attack, your highness, from your draft, which you read twice before sealing. The first went to the post house on the north road that afternoon with the week's letters, and I put it into the bag myself and saw the bag tied. The second went down the river with the salt boatman the same day, addressed to the crown, to be given to the crown's post at the coast. The third went to the north road seven days later, a week behind the first, in the same way as the first." He stopped. "I wrote all three. I sealed all three. I have the entries."

"And nothing's come back."

"The register is for what goes out, your highness." Hale's eyes went, very briefly, to the window and the empty yard. "If anything had come back from the crown, I would have carried it up the stair myself. I would not have sent a boy."

Emeric closed the book on his finger.

"Can I take this to the forge?"

Hale looked at the book, and at the prince's finger in it, and at the door, and plainly ran through every way it might come to harm between here and Bram's yard, which was a quarter of a mile of mud.

"I will wrap it," he said.



Ferra had the page out before he had unwrapped the book.

It lived on the shelf over her stove, the one sheet she kept away from the rest of her papers, and it had gone soft at the folds and brown along one edge where it had leaned too long against the warm brick. Two lines, ruled in March of last year, one for Grayrock and one for the crown, and beside them the column headed answered. Grayrock's line had words in it now, in her small plain hand. The crown's line had nothing. It had had nothing in it for nineteen months, and she had looked at it, he supposed, most days.

Behind them, at the back of the forge, Bram was drawing out a bar from the tithe-house iron, and the hammer went up and came down with the long, flat patience of a man who had been told the iron was free and did not believe it.

Ferra laid the register beside her page and uncapped her ink. She took a slip of scrap from the jar she kept for scrap, and went down Hale's columns from the August of the takeover with her pen held just off the paper, not touching, the nib following each line to its end and coming back. When she came to a line that said the crown, she made a stroke on the slip. When she came to a line that did not, she went on.

Emeric sat on the stool across from her and watched her do it, because there was nothing else useful to do with his hands. The strokes went in fives, four and a bar across. A strand of the red hair had come down over her ear and she had not noticed it, and he noticed himself noticing, and looked at the register instead.

She stopped once, at a line in July of this year, with her pen over it.

"Paxton," she said.

"That didn't go to the crown."

"No. It didn't go anywhere." She looked at it a little longer than she needed to. It was the letter in the drawer, the one with Milford's seal under it, and the line in the register said only that it had been received and held. Then she went on.

When she reached the bottom of the last page she counted the strokes, and counted them again, and put the pen down.

"Twenty-one."

Behind them the hammer stopped.

It stopped at the top of its swing, which Emeric heard rather than saw, because the bar went on ringing a little after the last blow and then there was nothing. He did not turn round. Ferra did not look up. After a while the hammer came down again, and again, as if nothing had happened, a little harder.

"Read me some."

She put her finger on the register without needing the slip.

"The census return. The first autumn. Three thousand four hundred and twelve by the tax roll, two thousand eight hundred and seventy-six by mine, with the reason for the difference." Down the page. "The price at Grayrock. March of last year. That one's on my page too." Down. "The copper. October of last year. Refused to a barony of the crown without a reason given, and would the crown give its view." Over a leaf. "The school's rules. The child-labor rules you wouldn't put up till you could pay for them. Sent for the crown's approval, the first winter." She turned two more leaves and stopped. "And the war. Three copies. I've counted it once."

"And the others?"

"The quarter accounts, every quarter, with the dividend. Two letters about the aqueduct. One about the magistrate's chair being empty, which you wrote twice, so I've counted it twice." She closed her hand on the slip. "None answered. Not one refused. Not one acknowledged. Not a line."

Bram had come up behind them with the tongs still in his hand, the bar at the end of them gone from yellow to a sullen red, and was looking at the slip as if it had done something to him.

"Twenty-one what?" he said.

"Letters," said Ferra.

"To who?"

"The king."

Bram thought about it. "I've written the king none," he said, "and I've had the same answer." He went back to the anvil, and put the bar back in the fire because it had gone too cold to work while he was being clever, and swore at it, quietly, for his own benefit.



It was Emeric who started the other column. He did it out loud, because he wanted to hear whether it sounded as bad as it looked.

"The House," he said. "I wrote to the coast house in July. Saban carried it himself."

"Because he didn't trust the road," Ferra said. She had a fresh page under her hand and was ruling it while he spoke. "It arrived. The rider was back with the House's answer on the eighth day."

"And the amendment."

"Accepted as written. Eight days, there and back, with a reply." She wrote it. Eight days. The date.

"The dividend."

That one she did not need him for. Every quarter, the crown's share of whatever Kaldo made went north in a sealed bag with the quarter's account folded round it, and every quarter, inside a month, a receipt came back down the road from the crown's receiver, sealed, signed, and correct to the copper. Ferra kept the receipts in a box by themselves, every one of them, because they were the only paper from the crown the barony had ever been sent. She took the box down and set it on the table between them and did not open it.

"Coin goes north," she said, "and somebody counts it."

"And paper comes south."

"Theirs does." She wrote it. "The account goes with the coin, in the same bag. That gets read, or the receipt wouldn't say it agreed." She stopped. "So it isn't that nobody in the capital can read Kaldo's hand."

"Grayrock," he said. "The widow."

"The Lady Bryda," said Ferra, who had the name from Hale, who had it from somewhere, and had written it under Saban's news the night he brought it. "Wrote north after the burying. Writ back before midsummer. Inside a season." She wrote that too.

"The coast."

"Your letter for mages went down with the boatman at the end of April, and the mages came up with him in May." Ferra did not look up. "Five people read it and believed it enough to get in a boat. That's an answer." She wrote it. "The boatman's own letters. The salt orders. The contracts in July, by every road we had. Nobody bought, but nobody said they hadn't had them."

There were more. The carter had been sent word and come. Milford had been written to about the casks and had answered with half the money. Emeric found them as fast as he could think of them and Ferra wrote them down in two words and a date each, and the page filled up, and the other page, the one ruled in March with its column headed answered, lay beside it with its single empty line.

When they had run out she turned the new page round so that it faced him.

"Everything this barony sends arrives," she said. "Except what it sends to the crown."

"Riders lose bags."

"They do." Her pen tapped the page once. "Loss doesn't choose."

He sat back on the stool.

He had had an answer for the empty line for two years. He had never written it down, but he had carried it so long that it had worn smooth, like a coin in a pocket. He was the third son. He had been sent south to be forgotten, and a crown that wanted a son forgotten did not answer his letters, and a crown did not trouble itself with a plague barony on the far edge of the map in any case. His father had played that game with him for fifteen years, and he had learned its rules young. Silence from the north was his father's silence. It had never needed explaining.

"I thought it was him," he said. "My father. I thought I was being ignored."

Ferra waited.

"It fits," he said. "It fits nineteen of them. A census. A school. An aqueduct. If I were the king and I wanted a son to understand where he stood, I'd let every one of those sit." He put his finger on the register, on the quarter accounts. "It doesn't fit these. A crown that counts its share to the copper reads the account that comes with it, and a crown that reads an account answers a question in it, if only to say no. And it doesn't fit the war. You don't ignore a war on your own land. Not even to make a point to a son." He took his finger off the page. "I never tested it. I had a reason that felt like knowledge and I never once checked it against anything. I'd have taken your head off for doing that with a column."

It cost him to say. He could feel what it cost, a kind of heat in the face that had nothing to do with the forge. Ferra looked at him while he said it, and did not soften anything, which he was grateful for.

"No," she said. "You didn't."

Then she looked down at her own page, the old one, and her mouth went thin.

"I ruled that line in March of last year," she said, "and I've looked at it every week since, and I've written nothing in it, and I called that keeping a record." She put the pen down very straight beside the page, the way Hale had put his knife down. "It's the same thing. I counted what came back. I never counted what went."



He climbed the east tower before supper to ask one question.

It was a long stair, and the last turn of it was narrow enough that he went up it sideways. The door at the top was open a hand's width. He knocked on it anyway, and heard a pen set down, and Vantic said, "Come in," in the tone of someone who knew perfectly well who it was and would not presume.

He did not go in. He stood in the doorway.

She was at the table under the window, with the lamp lit already though it was not yet dark, and the barony's books stacked at her left hand in the order she had taken them, and her own book open in front of her. Her coat hung on the back of the chair. She sat very straight, as if the chair had been made to a measure and she was keeping to it, and she did not get up.

"Your letters to the House," he said. "Have they all arrived?"

She did not ask why. He had expected her not to, and she did not, and he still noticed it.

"Six," she said. "One a week since I came. Every one acknowledged by return. The fastest was answered in nine days. The slowest in twelve, which was the week of the rain."

"By what road?"

"The House's riders. The south road, to the capital."

The same road. Different hands on it. He stood with that in the doorway and did not say anything.

Vantic picked up her pen, dipped it, and wrote a line in her book. He could read it upside down from where he stood, because her hand was that clear: the date, and the hour, and four words. Asked whether letters arrive. Nothing else. No note of who had asked, because there was nobody else in Kaldo who would.

She blotted it. She looked up.

"Was that all, your highness?"

"That was all."

"Then good night."

He went down the stair the long way, sideways round the narrow turn, and was most of the way to the hall before he realized he had been half hoping she would ask, and that she had known that too.



The forge was banked when he came back after supper, and Bram had gone up to bed, and the yard smelled of wet ash and the tithe-house iron. Ferra was still at her table. She had moved the lamp to the near corner so the light fell across both pages and the register at once, and she had a cup of something gone cold at her elbow that she had not drunk.

She had the register open at June.

"Leave the rest," she said, when he sat down. "Look at this one."

Three lines, one under another. The same day in May, twice: the crown, the north road; the crown, the salt boatman for the coast. Then a week further down, the crown, the north road again. Each with Hale's few words on the matter: the attack on the barony of Kaldo, its dead, its prisoners, and the barony's request.

"You can say a lot of things about the other eighteen," Ferra said. "A rider drowned at a ford. A clerk at the far end put it in the wrong pile. Your father." She said that one without any weight on it at all, and he was grateful for that too. "The copper, you could say somebody read it and didn't like it. The aqueduct, nobody cares about an aqueduct. I can make every one of them go away if I want to badly enough." She put her finger on the three lines. "I can't make these go away. Two roads. A week apart. North by land, and south by water to the coast and inland from there. One copy could fall in a river. Two could be bad luck." She lifted her finger. "A road can lose a letter. Two roads and a week don't lose the same one three times."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the lane a dog was having an argument with itself.

"Where does the post go," he said, "after the north road?"

She opened her mouth, and closed it.

He did not know either. He had sent letters north for two years, and handed them to Hale, and Hale had handed them to the post house on the north road, and after that, in his head, they had simply gone on north, in a straight line, to the capital, the way a thing goes when you throw it and stop watching. He had never once asked who carried the bag from the post house, or where it stopped, or whose hands it passed through, or where the coast's crown post went when it turned inland. He had strung a telegraph with his own hands and he did not know how a letter got from Kaldo to his father's desk.

"Saban will know," Ferra said.

"He'll charge for it."

"He'll charge you." She picked up the pen. "He knows better than to charge me."

She dipped it. He thought she was going to write something more on the new page, another line, another date. Instead she drew the old page toward her, the soft brown one from the shelf, with its two ruled lines and its column headed answered and its one empty space beside the crown.

In the space she wrote 21. Small, in her plain hand, the way she wrote any figure.

Then she laid the rule under it and drew a line. Not a double line, the way she closed a sum. A single line, straight and dark, from one edge of the column to the other, so that whatever came next on that page would have to start underneath it.

Emeric sat across the table from her and did not write anything at all.
