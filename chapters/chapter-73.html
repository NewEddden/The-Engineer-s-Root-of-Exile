Chapter 73 - The Word

Ward said it at the end of a day, in the long room, reporting something ordinary.

It was the first letter from Osric at Grayrock, and it was a soldier's letter, short and in order: the guardhouse roofed, the men quartered, the Steward's orders carried out in the east gallery without trouble, the town quiet. Ward read it out standing by the window, the way he read everything, and folded it, and put it on the table.

"One more thing in it," he said. "The town's got a name for them already. Osric's six."

"What name?"

"The duke's men."

He said it plainly and did not soften it, and he did not say it as a joke. He said it the way he would have told Emeric that a bridge had a crack in it.

"I'm not a duke."

"No," said Ward.

"The Crown hasn't granted any such thing. It hasn't been asked to. I hold four baronies of the Crown on the same terms as Kaldo, which makes me a baron four times over, and that's a legal fact, and a garrison in Grayrock calling it something else doesn't change it."

Ward let him finish. He let him finish all of it, and waited a moment after it was finished, to be sure.

"Four baronies," Ward said. "A junction every road in the south goes through. A frontier the Crown forfeited to you because it couldn't think of anyone cheaper. Stewards you appointed, a Steward you chartered, a clerk you put in a dead man's counting house. Soldiers of yours standing in the gate of a town you beat on your own wall." He did not raise his voice at any point. "Nobody's given you the word. Everybody south of Wayford is already using it. I'm not telling you how it looks from the capital. I'm telling you what you turned into while you were busy doing sums."

Emeric did not answer. Ward had never once in fifteen years told him anything that was not true, and he was not going to begin by arguing with this.

Then Ward asked the question.

"So who's left," he said, "to tell you no?"

He started to answer at once, and then stopped, because he had started to answer with a name, and the name was Ferra's, and he had hired her.

So he went through them properly, in his head, while Ward stood at the window and waited, and he went through all of them, because Ward would know if he stopped short.

Ferra. Hired, by him, from her father's forge, at a wage he set.

Imani. On his payroll, running a ward in a building he had paid for, under a standard he had written with her.

Bram. Whose foundry was his, whose wheel was his, whose apprentices were paid out of his books.

Saban. Contracted to Kaldo exclusively, for seven years now, every cart and every run.

Armand. Who had crossed a kingdom in a hired cart because of him, and for no other reason, and would go back across it the day Emeric told him to.

Vesk and Orrin. Funded by him, fed by him, housed by him, and in Vesk's case alive by his decision.

Bryda. Chartered by him, and revocably, because he had made the revocation the whole point.

Paxton. A steward under his judgment, with no power of refusal in anything he chose to order.

Pennock. A clerk he had put in a chair.

The garrison. His. The school. His. The post from Wayford to his own door. His. The church, near enough, because Doran was old and the parish hall had been his classroom for eight years.

He went through the whole list, and it was very long, and there was not one person on it whose place did not run through his own hand. Every one of them could tell him no. Not one of them could do it without standing on something he had given them, and he could take any of it back.

Ward waited while he did it. Ward had known exactly how long it would take.

There were two he had not counted, and he counted them last, and neither of them helped.

Ward himself. Who was not his, not in that way, and never had been, and who had said the hardest true things to him for fifteen years without once asking for anything in return. Ward was fifty now. He had stood at the far side of a gallery in Grayrock with his hand on his sword and watched Emeric remove a mine captain, and he had not said a word against it, because he had never once in fifteen years given Emeric a sentence that was not operational. Tonight's was the nearest he had come. Emeric looked at him, standing at the window in the last of the light, and thought for the first time with any seriousness that Ward was not going to be there forever, and did not like the thought, and put it away.

And Vantic. Who was not his at all. Who worked for a lending house that was not in the south at all, and answered to it and to nobody else, and had sat at his table for five years with the right to read his books and the obligation to tell her house the truth about them. She was the only check in his life that did not run through his hand. She was the only one who could say no to him without standing on anything he had given her.

And that morning she had brought him her house's answer about the shaft at Grayrock, five weeks after she forwarded it, and the answer was no.

He sat and looked at that for a while. The only person in the south who could refuse him had refused him that very day, and he found that what he felt about it was not anger, and not frustration, but something much closer to relief.

"Send for Vantic," he said.

Ward looked at him for a moment, and then went.

He wrote it while he waited, and it did not take long, because it was very simple.

The factor's rights under the loan were rights over the security: the books of the barony pledged against the principal, the ledgers that showed the security was sound, the right to attend any expenditure above a threshold. He widened them. Not to the loan's books. To all of them. Every barony, every account, every tally house and counting house and toll book, every expenditure of every size, with right of inspection and right of record, for as long as the factor or her house cared to exercise it. And not contingent on the debt. When the principal was paid, the rights would stand. He could not revoke them. He wrote that clause twice, the second time more plainly than the first, and signed it and sealed it.

He was building himself an auditor he could not dismiss. He knew exactly what he was doing and he did it anyway, in the same room, within the hour, because if he waited until the morning he would find reasons.

Vantic came in with her small book under her arm and sat where he pointed, and he gave her the sheet.

She read it. Then she read it again. She read it the second time much more slowly, and somewhere in the middle of the second reading her hand, which had been resting on the edge of the table, went still in a way that he noticed and did not know what to do with. It lasted perhaps a breath. Then she turned the sheet over, as if to check the back of it, and turned it again, and looked up.

"Two questions, Your Highness."

"Ask."

"Does 'every account' include the private accounts of the holder? Your own purse, as distinct from the baronies'?"

"Yes."

"And the right of record. Does it extend to copies made and held outside Kaldo?"

"Yes. Anywhere you like."

She wrote both answers in her book. Then she laid the sheet flat on the table in front of her and considered it, with every appearance of treating it as nothing more than a very large expansion of her duties, which in a sense it was.

"This will require additional staff," she said. "Two clerks at the least. One for Wayford. I'll need a room at the junction."

"You'll have it."

"And I will report it to my house." She said it as a matter of course. "I report everything to my house."

"I know," Emeric said. "That's rather the point."

She looked at him. It was a considering look, the look she gave a column that did not add, and he saw her not understand what he meant, and decide not to ask, and file it. She picked up the sheet, and her book, and stood.

"Good evening, Your Highness," she said, exactly as she said it every evening, and went out. She had not thanked him. He had not expected her to, and was obscurely glad she had not.

He sat and looked at the door. He had built himself a brake, he thought. The best one available. A foreign one, owned by someone else, that he could not take off. He did not think any further than that, because there did not seem to be any further to think.

Ferra heard about it before breakfast, because Ferra heard about everything before breakfast.

She came into the long room with the copy of the instrument in her hand, which Vantic had sent across for the Kaldo book as she sent everything, and stood at the end of the table and read it through once more in front of him, as though to be sure it said what she had already read it saying.

She thought it was the best decision he had made in three years. He could not have known that, because she did not say it. What she said was:

"You've three problems."

"Only three?"

"Three today." She put the copy down. "One. Her clerks will need copies of every book in four baronies, and every copy is a clerk's day, and my clerks are already doing two days' work in one. Two. The Wayford counting house has never let anyone from outside the barony read its books in its life, and Pennock has no standing to make them start, and you'll have to write to them yourself. Three." She tapped the clause about the holder's private purse. "You've never kept your own accounts properly in nine years. I've been keeping them for you. She's going to read them. And they're a disgrace."

"Are they?"

"They're a disgrace," Ferra said again, with some satisfaction, and took the copy away to be filed, and he heard her on the stair, going down quickly, the way she went when she had decided something was good and did not want to be seen deciding it.

He sat on alone in the long room as the evening came in.

A keep he did not own. It belonged to the Crown, and he held it of the Crown, and so did everything else. A barony he had been sent south to die in, eight years ago, with a wastrel's reputation and a stone in his pocket he did not understand. And now four of them, and a junction, and a hill with an empire under it he was forbidden to dig, and a town calling six of his soldiers the duke's men. And he had just spent an hour of an evening, with every appearance of calm, arranging for there to be one person left in the south who could look at every book he kept and tell him he was wrong.

Ward came in to see about the lamps. He did it himself, as he often did, and he did it slowly, and when he had finished he stood by the door.

"You'll not say it again," Emeric said. "The word."

Ward considered that.

"I won't need to," he said, and went out, and left it in the room.
