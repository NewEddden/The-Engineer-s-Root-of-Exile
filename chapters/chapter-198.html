Chapter 198 - Kaldo Black

Maren had a barrow on the quay for the fourth Lantern Night, and a queue at it that went back past the boat stair.

She had built the barrow herself out of a handcart and an old bread trough, or rather she had told the yard's carpenter what she wanted at the top of her voice for a week until he built it to make her stop; and in the middle of the trough, packed round with straw and sacking, was a copper pot with a lid, and under the pot, in a nest of its own felt, a cold stone off the ice works' spare rack. Ferra had signed for the stone. Maren had signed for nothing, and had told Ferra so.

What was in the pot was cream. Cream and honey and the yolks of a great many eggs, cooked into a custard in the castle kitchen in the afternoon and carried down to the quay at dusk; and the cold stone, set to pull, drew the heat out of it through the copper and threw it out through a little brass fin on the barrow's side into the March night, where it did not matter. And every few minutes Maren took the lid off and went round the inside of the pot with a long wooden paddle, scraping the frozen custard off the copper as it froze and folding it back into the middle, so that it froze small and soft and smooth instead of in a block; and served it into little paper cones from the plain press, the school's spoiled sheets folded round twice, at a Copper a cone.

The first child who tasted it burst into tears. The second ran off up the quay screaming for her mother. After that the queue had formed and not stopped forming.

"Ice cream," said Emeric, when they reached the front of it.

"Cold cream," said Maren. "I don't know what ice has to do with it. There's no ice in it." She shovelled a cone. "It's cream and it's cold. That's what it's called." She shovelled two more without being asked, and held all three out over the barrow to him and Ferra and Imani, and took his Copper, and did not give change for the other two, because she had decided in the afternoon that the castle paid triple. "You want to know how it's done, I suppose."

"I know how it's done."

"Course you do." Maren pointed the paddle at him. "You told me how it's done. In August. And I've done it." She went round the pot. "Next."

They took their cones up onto the wall.



The town had come out the way it came out every year, onto the river wall and the bridge and the quay and the river stair and the far bank, with the lanterns in their hands unlit and their names written on their sides; three thousand people and more in the cold dark under the arc lamps, talking, waiting for the bell. Fewer of the lanterns were the school's this year, because the school had more than one class now and the classes had argued about whether folding lanterns was a lesson, and lost. The dock quarter had brought its own. There was a fiddle somewhere along the moorings.

Emeric stood on the wall walk between the two women with his cone, and looked down at the town in the lamplight, and could see the roofs.

That was the thing he noticed most, every year, and every year more. From the wall the lower ward lay spread below the castle like a hand of cards, and four years ago half the cards had been pale, thatch, and now there were none. Three streets behind the old corn exchange, where the plague houses had been, were not there any more; there were long brick rows instead, two storeys high, with tile roofs dark and wet and every chimney smoking, and the lamps of the new houses in their windows were lamp stones, not candles. The records hall stood at the end of them in its cement, grey and square. Past the works, across the river, the dock quarter's roofs were still half thatch, because the dock quarter did what it liked; but even there the new warehouses along the quay were slate. The town had changed under his feet while he was looking at the ship.

He had read a book once, in the other life, by a court stenographer called Howard: a round town with a garden at its heart, avenues running out like spokes, a railway round the edge and farms beyond it that nobody was allowed to build on. Kaldo had the circle already, and the farms. It wanted the rest.

"You're doing it again," said Imani.

"Doing what?"

"Counting roofs." She licked her cone. "You count them every year. You get a face like Ferra's."

"I don't have a face," said Ferra.

"You have a face," said Imani. "You have it now. You're counting the roofs too."

Ferra did not deny it. She stood at the parapet with her cone held a little away from her, as if it might do something unexpected, and ate it in small exact bites, and looked down at the lower ward with her eyes moving along the streets; and he could see her doing it, row by row, as she would have done a column.

"None inside the walls since the autumn," she said. "Two hundred and six in the dock quarter. I had them counted at Christmas." She took a bite. "I'll start on them in the summer, and they'll hate me."

The bell went in the church tower.

All along the wall and the quay and the bridge, people struck matches, and the lanterns filled from inside, one after another, then by tens, and lifted in their hands; and somebody near the water stair let theirs go early and it went up alone, tilting, gold, and a sound went along the crowd at it. Then the rest. They went up off the wall and the river stair and the quay and the far bank together, hundreds, wavering, turning slowly on their strings of heat; and the wind took them out over the river and down the valley toward the marsh in a long slow river of their own.

Ferra had written her mother's name where she always wrote it, on the inside by the hoop, where it would face the flame and nobody else would see it; he knew it was there and did not look. Imani had written her father's, large, facing the town, as she always did. He had written the same three names as two years ago, two outside and one inside by the hoop; and he let his go and watched it until it was one light among the others and he could not say which.



Fennick's fireworks went up at nine from the far side of the marsh, a long way from anything, because Ward had chosen the place.

They were smaller this year. Everybody who had seen the first year's noticed, and said so, and Fennick, who was standing on the marsh dyke in the dark with his fuses, would have heard them say it if he had been close enough, and would not have cared. He had a third less powder. He had told everyone who would listen. He had spent the whole of the third he did not have on the culvert in his head, and the culvert had been dug by hand by Ferra's men, and he had not forgiven anybody.

So he had made the fireworks green.

All of them. Every one; and every one a different green, from the pale washed green of new beech leaves to a deep cold bottle green, that hung over the marsh after the bang and dripped slowly down toward the reeds like light running off a wet branch. It was the colour the capital's powder house had taken him back for, twice, after he had blown its roof off. The crowd on the wall made the sound it made at the furnace, and the planes, and the balloon, and went on making it.

Emeric did not watch the last of them from the wall.

Imani had had a better idea. She usually did, about where to stand. She had gone up the keep stair after the lanterns, past the long room and the counting room and the doors of the family floor, to the little door at the top that let out onto the leads, and propped it open with her boot; and Ferra had followed her, and he had followed them both, because the alternative was standing on the wall walk with three hundred people who wanted to tell him about the roofs.

The leads were cold and flat and black and empty, and from there the whole valley lay open under the green.

They sat with their backs against the chimney stack, which was warm, because the kitchen fire was at the bottom of it. He sat in the middle, because there was nowhere else to sit, and took off his cloak, because the women were cold, and put it over all three of them, and they watched Fennick's greens go up over the marsh one after another and come down and drip and go out, and the last of the lanterns going away down the valley in the dark, small and gold and very far off now.

"That one," said Imani, of a green.

"That one," said Ferra, of a different one.

They argued about it. It was a short argument and a happy one and nobody won. Then Fennick's last rocket went up, very high, and burst, and hung, a great slow wheel of deep green over the whole marsh that lit the river below them like a road; and the crowd on the wall roared; and then it was dark, and the smoke went away on the wind, and there was nothing over the marsh but the stars.

Nobody moved.

The chimney stack was warm at his back. The cloak was heavy. Imani had her head on his shoulder before the last green had finished falling, and he did not notice her put it there; it was simply there, and her hair against his jaw, smelling of the ward's soap and the cold. On his other side Ferra sat very straight for a while, with her hands folded inside the cloak in her lap. Then, without turning her head, she moved one of them, under the cloak, onto his knee, and left it.

The town went home below them. The lamps on the wall burned. The fiddle on the moorings stopped, and started again, slower, and stopped.

Imani's breathing changed.

He knew it now; he had heard it on the ship. It went long and slow all at once, between one breath and the next, as if she had walked through a door. Her weight came onto his shoulder, all of it, warm and heavy, and her hand found its way inside his coat in her sleep and lay flat on his ribs.

Ferra lasted longer. She always did. She sat beside him with her hand on his knee and her eyes open, looking at the dark marsh, and he could feel her deciding not to sleep; and then he could feel her deciding it a little less; and then her head came down on his other shoulder, slowly, very carefully, as if it were a thing she was setting down on a table and did not want to scratch. She said, against his collar, so quietly he nearly missed it: "Only for a minute." And then she was asleep too.

He sat on the leads of his own keep under a cloak with a woman asleep on each shoulder and the stars over the marsh, and could not move.

Not would not. Could not. If he moved his left arm Imani would wake, and Imani asleep was the only Imani who ever rested, and he had seen her face at the end of a week at the ward. If he moved his right Ferra would wake, and she would sit up very straight and pretend she had never been asleep, and go to bed, and that would be the end of it. So he did not move. He sat with his back to the warm stone and their two heads on his shoulders and their two bodies against his under the cloak, one on each side, warm, breathing, one long and slow and one small and careful even in sleep; and the heat came up under the cloak between the three of them until he could feel it on his face in the cold.

He could wake them. He thought that very clearly, at about midnight. He could turn his head an inch to the left and put his mouth against Imani's hair, and she would wake the way she woke, all at once, and look at him from a hand's breadth away; and Ferra would wake at the movement, and the leads were flat and dark and the door at the stair's head had a bolt on its inside. He thought it for a long while. He thought about the bolt in great detail.

He did not do it. It was not the place. It was the leads of the keep on Lantern Night with the watch on the wall below and the door propped with Imani's boot; and they had a page in Ferra's book with two initials on it, and he had told them both on a berth in a ship that it should be something better than a berth. A roof was not better than a berth. He knew that, sitting there. He also knew, sitting there, that he was the only one of the three of them who thought so, at that moment, and that the other two were asleep, and that this was the whole trouble.

The watch changed on the wall at two. He heard it, the boots and the low voices at the gate tower and the new man's lantern going along the wall walk to the river tower and back. Imani moved her knee in her sleep. Ferra said a figure into his collar, distinctly, and then another, and frowned without waking. The marsh went silver under a moon he had not noticed rising. A late lantern came down out of the sky somewhere over the river, dark, a bag of cold paper, and floated, and sank.

His arms went dead at three. He stopped being able to feel his left hand at all.

At four he started counting chimneys on the town below, because there was nothing else on earth to do, and got to two hundred and twelve, and lost his place at the dock quarter and began again.

Imani woke at a little after six, as the sky went grey over the marsh. All at once, the way she slept: she lifted her head off his shoulder and looked at the grey sky, and at the roof, and at him, from very close.

"We fell asleep," she said, pleased.

"You did."

She looked at his face. Her healer's look came into her own face, the one she gave a man brought in off the road, and she reached across under the cloak without asking and took his wrist, and he could not feel her take it.

"You've not slept again."

"I couldn't move."

"You could have moved." Imani put her fingers under the band and counted, and frowned. "We'd have woken up and gone to bed. Like sensible people." She let go of his wrist and looked at it, at the way it lay where she had put it, and then at him. "You can't feel your hand."

"No."

"Idiot," said Imani, with great tenderness, and began to rub it between both of hers, briskly, like a woman wringing out a cloth.

Ferra woke at the noise, and sat up very straight at once, with the cloak falling off her shoulder, and looked at the grey marsh and the empty leads and the propped door; and then at Imani rubbing his hand, and at his face.

"I was asleep for a minute," she said.

"Eight hours," said Imani.

Ferra looked at the sky. Her face went pink from the throat up, slowly, all the way to her hair, and did nothing else at all.

"I'm going to bed," she said. "For an hour. And then I'm going to the counting room, and nobody is going to mention this." She stood up, and put her hair back, and looked down at him, and the pink did not go away. "You look dreadful."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't kind," said Ferra. She looked at him a breath longer. "It was true. Go to bed."

He did not go to bed. He had told Tess on the day before Lantern Night that he would be at the mill at seven to look at the straw liquor, and at seven he was at the mill.



The ink kitchen was a lean-to on the back of the paper mill, against the warm wall where the boiler was, with a door into the finishing loft on one side and a door into the yard on the other. It was not much of a kitchen. A brick bench along the wall with a coal fire under one end of it for boiling the gum, a stone mortar for grinding the black, a shelf of jars, and at the other end of the bench, set into the brick, the size plate: a square of iron with a fire stone under it, recharged every week in the charging room, that kept the pot of glue size for the paper at an even warmth all day without anybody having to feed a fire. The size had to stay warm and never boil. The stone was set for it.

Tess was not there. She had left a note on the bench, in her square hand: Liquor's in the third tank. Gone to Bram, the vat's leaking. Back at eight. And under it: Can you start the ink? The ink boy's sick. The grey's all ground. Lamp black on the shelf. Gum's soaking.

So he started the ink.

He had made it himself, the first pots of it, three years ago, and he could make it in his sleep, which was as well, because he very nearly was. Lamp black, a measure of it, into the pot. The gum, swollen overnight in its water, after it. The grey dust of drained stones, the charging room's sweepings, a pinch to the pot, as he had first done it. Stir. Then heat it, slowly, until the gum let go of its lumps and the whole went smooth and thick and grey and could be strained.

He put the pot on the size plate.

He did not notice that he had done it. He noticed nothing at all for some minutes; he stood at the bench with his back against the warm wall and his eyes shut and the spoon in his hand, stirring, and thought about the bolt on the inside of the door to the leads. The size plate was warm under the pot, as it always was. It was not until the ink began to move under the spoon of its own accord that he opened his eyes.

It was boiling. Gently. Which it should not have been on the size plate, which was set never to boil; except that he had taken the size pot off and put the ink on, and the ink was a quarter the weight of the size, and the stone under the iron, which pushed a set heat whatever sat on it, had pushed it into a quarter the weight, and it boiled.

He swore, and pulled it off.

And stopped, with the pot in his hand by its wooden handle and the steam going up past his face, because the ink was black.

Not grey. Kaldo ink was grey, always; a shade greyer than plain lamp black, from the dust, so that you could tell it on a page if you knew to look. This was black. A deep, flat, depthless black, blacker than the lamp black had been in the jar, as if the dust had soaked up every bit of light that came near it. He tipped the pot. It ran thick and shining off the spoon.

And it hummed.

He would not have felt it with his sense shut. He had not shut his sense since the autumn, except to sleep, and he had not slept; and so he felt it, there, in the steam over the pot: something very faint, at the very edge of what his sense could reach, like a sound too low to hear that you know is there by the way the glass in a window answers it. A pulse. Slow and even. Coming off the black in the pot like heat came off it.

He put the pot down on the cold end of the bench and stood back from it.

The pulse did not stop. It came from the ink. Not from the stone under the size plate; he could feel that too, separately, its own steady note, the one it had always had, and this was not that. This came out of the pot. As if the dust in it, the dead grey dust of a hundred drained stones, had sat on a charged stone for ten minutes at a boil and drunk something off it, and was full of it, and could not keep it in.

He took a sheet of the spoiled paper off the finishing loft's pile, and a pen off Tess's shelf, and dipped it, and drew a line.

The line pulsed.

Wet, it pulsed, faint and slow, a thread of the same low note running along the paper where the pen had gone. He watched it. He could feel exactly where the line was with his eyes shut; he shut them and put his finger in the air an inch over the paper, and ran it along the line without touching, and knew when he had gone off the end. He opened his eyes. The line was going dull at its start, where it was thinnest, drying; and as it dried, the pulse in it went faint, and fainter, and stopped.

He waited. He watched the whole line go from shining to flat. And the whole line went silent, from its start to its end, in the order it dried.

Dry, it was nothing. A black line on grey paper. A very good black. He held his hand over it with his sense wide open and felt no more from it than he felt from the bench.

He dipped the pen again, and wrote his own name, slowly, and stood with his eyes shut, and felt the letters come off the pen one stroke at a time into the dark of his own head: the downstroke of the E, the three bars, the m, each stroke pulsing as it went down and going on pulsing till it dried.

He knew where he had felt this before. Not this; something like it. In the cellar under the keep, three years ago, with Tess, the morning of the first wage forms; a spent stone on the bench beside a wet sheet that had warmed while the ink was wet and gone cold as it dried. Tess had written it on her slate, in her careful hand, under the date, and rubbed it out at the end of the day with the rest of her slate, because neither of them had known what it was.

He knew now. He stood in the ink kitchen at a quarter past seven in the morning with no sleep in him and his left hand still tingling where Imani had rubbed it, and looked at his own name drying black on a spoiled sheet, and knew precisely what it was, and what it would do, and it frightened him.



He needed a listener before he told anybody, and he knew where there were six.

The stores in the gate tower had what he needed: a tray of plain earth stones in the stores, uncut, of the same kind as the twelve he had set in the wall in the autumn of the third year. Seven earth and five water, each cut from inside to wake for the least a person carries and close a contact on its stretch of the board in the line room, with its shutter and its needle and its bell. He signed one plain stone out in the line room's book under the eye of the man sitting the board, who looked at his face and did not ask, and took it back to the mill with a telegraph sounder and a reel of wire.

Bram was in the ink kitchen when he got there, with the leaking vat's wet up to his elbows, looking at the pot.

"Tess says you started the ink," said Bram. He looked into the pot. "That's not ink."

"It's ink."

"It's black." Bram looked at him over the pot. "You look like death."

"I've been told." He put the stone on the bench and the sounder beside it and began to run the wire. "Shut the yard door. And the loft door. Who's in the loft?"

"Nobody. It's Sunday." Bram shut both doors. He stood with his back to the yard one and watched Emeric bend over the stone, his hands working, and did not say anything; which was Bram's way of saying a great deal.

Emeric cut the stone.

Not far. A finger's breadth into it, no more, the way he had cut the wall's twelve, one fixed job and nothing else. Not a body; a body held still, a steady weight in the stone's field, and the stone was to ignore anything that held still. The pulse. The low slow rise and fall that came off the black in the pot, which he could feel now with his sense as easily as he could see the pot with his eyes, too faint to trip the wall's stones and nothing like a man. When what comes into your field rises and falls like that, he told it, close the contact. When it holds still, or stops, open it.

He came up out of it with a headache to go with the rest, and sat back.

"Take it into the loft," he said. "The stone and the sounder. Put it on the far table. Shut the door."

Bram took it. He went through into the finishing loft, with the stone in one hand and the sounder in the other and the wire paying off its reel behind him across the floor, and Emeric heard him cross the loft, a long way, to the far end where the reams were stacked, and set them down. The door shut. There was a brick wall between them now, and a door, and twenty yards of loft.

Emeric dipped the pen, and drew a line.

Through the wall, faint, the sounder clicked.

He drew another, and it clicked again; and then he wrote, quickly, a word, and the sounder in the loft went off like a man drumming his fingers on a table, click and click and click, a little burst for each stroke of each letter, stopping when the pen lifted and starting when it came down. He stopped writing. The sounder ticked on, slower, slower, as the strokes dried in the order he had made them; and stopped.

The loft door opened.

Bram came back through it slowly with the sounder in his big hand, and the wire trailing, and stood in the doorway of the ink kitchen and looked at Emeric, and at the sheet on the bench with the drying word on it, and back at the sounder.

"It's talking," said Bram.

"Yes."

"It's talking when you write." Bram looked at the sounder as if it had bitten him. "Through a wall. It knew every time you put the pen down." He put it on the bench, very carefully. "What was the word?"

"Bram."

Bram looked at the sheet, at his own name drying black on it.

"Seven clicks," he said slowly. "I counted seven. B's two strokes, and three for the r and the a, and two for the m." He was very still. "I could have told you what you wrote. If I'd known how many strokes each letter takes. If I'd known how you write."

"Yes."

"And if I'd had a way to see the strokes, not just count them," said Bram, and stopped, because he had seen it, all at once, in the middle of the ink kitchen, as a smith sees the whole of a thing in the iron before he has struck it once. He sat down on the stool by the door, heavily, and put his wet hands on his knees.

"It only works while it's wet," said Emeric. "Dry, it's dead. And it's faint. Twenty yards through a wall. Not across the yard, I'd guess. I don't know yet." He looked at the pot. "And it only works with this. Kaldo ink boiled on a charged stone. Nobody's ever done that. Nobody's ever had a reason to."

"Somebody's done it now." Bram looked at the pot. Then at him. "Who knows?"

"You."

"And Tess. She's at the vat." Bram stopped. "She'll be back at eight."

Emeric looked at the pot on the cold end of the bench, and at the sheet, and at the sounder; and thought of Tess's slate, three years ago, with the line on it that she had rubbed out at the end of the day without either of them knowing what it was; and of how fast she would see it. Faster than Bram had. She would see it before he had finished saying it.

"Not yet," he said. "Not Tess. Not anybody. You, and Ferra."

Bram looked at him for a long breath. He did not like it, and his face said so; Tess was his, in the shop, the way a right hand was his. But he nodded, once, slowly, and got up, and took the pot off the bench, and poured the black off it into a stone jar and stoppered it and put a wax seal on the stopper with his thumb; and wrote nothing on the jar. Then he took the whole sheet of spoiled paper off the bench, the lines and the name and his own name, and put it in the boiler's fire door, and watched it go.

"Ferra," he said. "Today."



She was in the counting room by nine, as she had said she would be, with her hair done and her face perfectly composed and nothing about her that anybody could have pointed at and said she had slept eight hours under a cloak on the leads. She looked up when he came in. She looked at his face, and he saw her decide not to say anything about it; and then she saw what he was carrying, the stoppered jar and the stone and the sounder, and Bram behind him in the doorway with the wet still on his sleeves, and she put her pen down.

"Shut the door," she said.

He showed her. It took a quarter of an hour, with the sounder on her desk and the stone beside it, and Bram in the passage outside with the pen and a sheet of her own paper, writing at the window on the far side of a shut oak door; and she sat with her hands folded on her book and listened to the sounder tick out every stroke Bram made, and dry, and stop. She did not say anything while it ticked. She did not say anything when it stopped.

Then she opened the book to a clean page, and dipped her own pen in her own ink, which was not black, and began to write.

He had meant to tell her what it could do. He had thought about it all the way up from the mill, in the order he would say it. He did not get to. She wrote in her small fast hand down the page, numbering as she went, and he stood at her shoulder and read it upside down as it came.

1. A letter can be read while it is being written. Not after. While.

2. From another room. Through a wall. Further, with more stones between, if a stone can hear a stone. Find out.

3. Only in this ink. So only by people who write with this ink. So: who writes with it is chosen by whoever gives it to them.

4. Nobody would know. Dry, it is ink. A good ink. They would buy more.

5. It would hear everything. Not only what we want. Their wives' letters. Their debts. Their dead.

She stopped. She sat with the pen over the page and did not write a sixth.

"You wrote them before I could say them," said Emeric.

"You were going to say the first four." She did not look up. "You weren't going to say the fifth." She put the pen down, carefully, across the page, so that it lay over the fifth line. "Somebody had to."

"I'd have said it."

"Eventually." She looked at the sounder on her desk, silent now. "After you'd used it."

He did not argue. He was not sure she was wrong.

Ferra sat a while longer, looking at the page. Then she drew a line under the five, and under the line, very small, she wrote the date, and: Known to: E., B., F. And then, after a moment, a sixth line after all, under the date, which she did not number.

Not yet for anything.

She blotted it, and closed the book, and locked it in her drawer, which she did with the book perhaps twice a year.

"Go to bed," said Ferra. "Now. Not after the mill, not after the straw, not after anything. You can't think. I've watched you not think all morning, and it frightens me more than that does." She nodded at the stoppered jar. "Bram, take that to the cellar under the long room and lock it in the cold press, and give me the key."

Bram took the jar.

Emeric went to the door. At the door he stopped.

"Ferra."

"Yes."

"Last night," he said. "On the leads."

She did not look up from the drawer. The pink came up from her collar again, slowly, and stayed.

"Nobody," said Ferra, "is going to mention it."

He went to bed. He slept until four in the afternoon, and dreamed he was writing his own name over and over on a wall a mile long, in black, and that somewhere on the other side of the wall a great many people were counting the strokes.
