Chapter 198 - Saltwater at the Gate

The wind held.

It had held for six days, off the sea, not much of it, but steady, and the vane on the second tower had not stopped once in that time. Emeric knew, because Ferra had the ganger's dusk figure sent down to the long room every evening now, on a slip, in the ganger's big careful capitals, and she read it out before anything else and then put the slip on a spike. The needle on Bram's board had come up to the mark on the third evening. It had not yet been at the mark at dawn. The stay stood.

He was at the long-room table with the house on four sheets in front of him when Ward came up. It was the middle of the afternoon, hot, with the shutters half closed against the glare off the lake. He had the partition drawn for the sixth time, and Bram had it drawn for the fourth, and the two drawings did not agree about the sand, and he was trying to decide whether that was because Bram was right.

Ward did not knock. He never knocked. He came in and shut the door behind him and stood with his back to it, which was not where he usually stood, and Emeric looked up.

"Emeric, a man smelling of pure saltwater is asking for you at the gate," Ward said. "Says he's your brother."

"Corbin?" Emeric asked, already bracing.

"No. Says his name's Zale."

Emeric put the pencil down.

He did not stand up at once. He sat with his hands on the sheets and looked at Ward, and Ward looked back at him, and neither of them said anything, and in the quiet Emeric heard somebody laugh in the yard below, loudly, the way a man laughs at a thing a guard has said that the guard did not mean to be funny.

He knew the laugh. He had not heard it in half his life, and he knew it.

"Is it him?"

"I saw him last across the palace yard, the year he went," said Ward. "It's him." He paused. "He's got your chin. Nothing else of yours. He came up off an Emberfell boat at the quay an hour ago, and walked up, and asked at the bridge for the prince, and the man on the bridge sent him on to the gate because he didn't know what else to do with him." He paused again. "He's got a sword. He gave it to the gate without being asked."

"Where are the others?"

"Ferra's in the counting room. Saban's down at the quay with the boat. Bram's at the works." Ward did not move from the door. "Hull two's up tonight. I've sent to Ned to take her out late, from past the bar, and not to show a lamp till he's off the point."

Emeric had not thought of that. He should have. He looked at Ward, and Ward looked at the sheets on the table, and then at the drawing of the partition, and then back at him, and Emeric picked up the sheets and turned them over and put the pencil on top of them, and stood up.

His brother was sitting on the mounting block inside the gate with a sea bag between his boots, talking to the gate guard.

He was taller than Emeric remembered, which could not be right, because Emeric had been sixteen and nearly grown the last time. So it was the way he sat, loose, with his elbows on his knees and his hat pushed back, taking up more of the block than a man needed. He was brown the way nobody in the capital was brown, and there was salt dried white in the creases of his coat and in the seams of his boots, and grey in his beard that had not been there, and a scar through one eyebrow that had not been there either. He was saying something to the guard about a cook on a Velmoor boat and a barrel of eels, and the guard, who was one of Ward's newest and had been told a hundred times not to talk to anybody at the gate, was grinning.

He saw Emeric in the arch and stopped in the middle of the eels.

For a moment he did not get up. He sat on the block and looked, the whole length of Emeric, from his boots to his face, the way a man looks at a coast he has only ever seen on a chart. Emeric saw him find the chin, and look for the rest, and not find it. He saw him look at Emeric's hands, which were clean, and steady, and had ink on the side of the right one, and at his coat, which was plain, and at his face, and look for a long time at his eyes, as if he were looking for something that ought to be in them and was not.

Then Zale stood up, and the easy look came over his face all at once, like sun coming out.

"Well," he said. "They told me in the tavern at Dunnet's Ferry you'd drunk yourself yellow. I was going to be so kind about it."

"When was that?"

"Oh, years ago." Zale came across the gateway. He stopped a pace short, and Emeric saw him not know what to do with his hands, which was the first thing about him that was new and not only older. "I believed it. I want you to know that. I believed every word."

"Everybody did."

"Did they?" Zale looked at him. "Everybody?"

Emeric did not answer that. After a moment Zale laughed, shorter this time, and put out his hand, and Emeric took it, and it was a sailor's hand, all callus, and it held on a moment longer than a handshake needed to, and then let go.

"Your man at the bridge thought I was a pedlar," Zale said. "Your man on the gate thought I was a spy. I've told him three stories and he still thinks I'm a spy. He's right to." He looked past Emeric, up at the keep, and round at the yard, and at Ward, who had come out behind Emeric and was standing in the arch where he always stood. "That one thinks I'm something worse."

"That's Ward."

"I know who Ward is. Everybody knows who Ward is." Zale looked at Ward a moment longer, with interest. "Father's Ward. I was told you'd been given him. I thought it was a kind of joke." He picked up his sea bag. "Is there somewhere a man can wash? I've been on a sulphur boat for eleven days, and I've stopped being able to smell it, and I can tell from your guard's face that he hasn't."

Saban came up from the quay while Zale was washing. He had been talking to the boat's master, because Saban talked to every boat's master, and he came into the counting room where Emeric and Ferra were waiting and did not sit down.

He put a coin on the table.

It was silver, and not theirs. It was thin and wide and worn smooth at the edge, and on the face that was uppermost there was a ship, a round-bellied ship with one mast and a steering oar, cut the way nobody on this coast cut a ship.

"He paid the boy who carried his bag up from the quay with that," Saban said. "The boy brought it to me, because it wasn't a Rand and he wanted to know if it was worth anything." He looked at Emeric. "In the spring. On the south road. The man at the inns, asking after you. Whether you were well. Whether you kept your health."

Emeric looked at the ship on the coin.

"Silver with a ship on one face," Saban said.

"I thought it was Corbin's man," said Emeric. "I said it was Corbin's man."

"You did." Saban picked up the coin and turned it over and put it down again, ship up. "I didn't write it down that way, and neither did she."

Ferra had not. Emeric knew without asking, and she did not tell him. She had her book shut on the table in front of her, and she was looking at the coin, and she had the face she wore when a line she had left open for a long time had just closed, and closed in a way she had not expected.

They ate in the long room, because Emeric did not want him in the hall, where the whole keep came through, and because the long room was where they ate when it was only the table.

Zale came up clean, in a shirt that had been folded at the bottom of a sea bag for a long time and still had the creases in it. He came in the way weather came into a room, all at once, easily, and looked round it once, at the lamps and the maps on the wall and the long table and the people at it, and Emeric watched him count. He did not know he was being watched doing it. He did not seem to know he was doing it at all.

"Six," Zale said, and sat down at the end of the bench nearest the door, though there was a chair set for him at Emeric's right hand. "Do I get told who they are, or do I guess?"

Emeric told him. Armand, whose name Zale knew, because every child in the palace had been frightened of Armand's name at one time or another. Bram, whose name he did not know, and who looked at him from under his brows the way he looked at a casting before it was cold. Imani. Saban. Ferra. Ward, by the door, who did not sit, and whom Zale did not need explained.

Zale took them in one by one, and to each of them said something short and right, the way he had always been able to at the palace, so that the person he said it to felt, for a moment, that they were the one in the room he had come to see. To Armand he said he had once been sent out of a lesson for asking why a lamp stone was warm, and had never found out. To Bram he said he had seen an engine from the quay, walking up, a long way off across the water, with wagons behind it and smoke on it, and had not known what it was, and had asked the boy carrying his bag, and the boy had looked at him as if he were simple. Bram grunted, and did not look pleased, and was.

To Ferra he said nothing at all at first. He looked at the book at her elbow, and at the pencil in her hand, and at her, and said, "You're the one who writes it down."

"Somebody has to."

"Everything?"

"Everything I'm told."

"Then I'd better be careful what I tell you." He grinned at her. She did not grin back. She wrote something short. Zale watched her do it, and leaned a little to see, and then sat back and laughed, and Emeric understood that his brother had just been told how much of what he had said so far she believed, and had not minded in the least.

He had come up the Emberfell lane, he said, because the Emberfell lane was the only way into Kaldo that did not go past the capital.

"I've no wish to go past the capital," Zale said. "I've no wish to be seen going past it. I came in at Emberfell from the west, on a Solreth coaster, and stayed a month with my mother, and then I got on the first sulphur boat coming this way and let it bring me." He broke bread. "She sends her love, by the way. Her exact words. I don't think I've ever heard her send anybody her love. I think she's been saving it."

"She's well?"

"She's a sovereign with a sword and a law she wrote herself that nobody's ever tested. She's the same as she ever was." Zale looked at him. "She said you'd been to see her. She said you'd grown." He paused. "That was all I got. I tried for four days. I asked her how you'd come, because there's no road, and she said by sea, and I asked what boat, and she said a fast one, and then she talked about her sulphur for an hour. My mother. About sulphur." He shook his head. "I know when I'm being kept out of a room. I've been kept out of better rooms than hers. I thought, all right. I'll go and look."

Ward, by the door, did not move. Emeric did not look at him.

"And the south road?" Emeric said. "In the spring?"

Zale stopped with the bread halfway to his mouth. Then he looked down the table at Saban, who was eating, and at the coin, which Saban had put beside his plate, ship up, without saying anything about it.

"Ah," said Zale. He put the bread down. "Yes. That was me." He did not look embarrassed. He looked, for a moment, like a man who has been caught at something and finds he is glad of it. "I came in from the west overland in the spring, from the Solreth side, meaning to come straight here. I got as far as an inn two days short of your bridge, and I asked the landlord about you, and he told me. And I asked the next landlord, and he told me the same thing." He turned his cup on the table. "Not the drunk. Nobody on that road said the drunk. They said the prince who beat three barons with a garrison of farmers and took their land off them, and the Crown gave it him, and now he holds four baronies, and there's an iron road, and the water in Kaldo's clean, and you can drink it. That's what they said. The water. Every inn." He looked up. "I sat in that inn for a night, and in the morning I turned round and went to Emberfell."

"Why?"

"Because I'd come to see about a drunk." Zale said it simply. "I'd come to see about my little brother who'd been sent off to die in a plague town, because it had been years, and nobody else was going to, and I'd been thinking about it in ports for longer than I like to say. And he wasn't there." He looked at Emeric. "And I didn't know what I'd find instead. So I went to my mother first to ask. And she wouldn't tell me."

He picked up the bread again and did not eat it.

"You called my mother Amara," Emeric said. "At the inn."

"Did I?" Zale thought about it. "I suppose I did. That was her name." He looked at Emeric as if he did not understand what was being asked. "What else would I call her? She wasn't my mother. She was Amara. She used to let me sit in her rooms when mine were full of tutors, and read her books, and she never once told anybody I was there." He was quiet a moment. "She was the only one in the palace who ever asked me where I'd been, when I'd been out over the wall, and actually wanted to know."

Nobody at the table said anything. Ward, by the door, had turned his head, the way he had turned it in the spring when Saban said the name in the doorway, and he did not turn it back.

It was Imani who asked why he had come in person, in the end, because nobody else would.

"A letter would have been quicker," she said. "And cheaper. And you'd not have had to smell of a sulphur boat."

"A letter," Zale said, "from me to him, would have gone through the capital, because every letter goes through the capital, and it would have been read by three men before it got here, and one of them would have been Corbin's." He said it without heat, the way a man says how far it is to the next port. "I've been writing letters to my mother for half my life that I knew somebody read first. I write them about weather. I write them about eels." He looked at Emeric. "This one wasn't about eels."

"What was it about?"

Zale put his cup down.

"About Corbin," he said. "In the ports, they're talking about you now. Not the drunk. The other one. The one who took three baronies off three barons in a season, and built an iron road, and has something going on in the south that nobody can quite put a name to. They talk about you the way they used to talk about him." He turned the cup a quarter turn, and another. "Corbin has spent his whole life being the one they talk about. He's never had to share it. He didn't have to share it with me, because I left. He didn't have to share it with you, because you drank." He looked up. "He's not going to let this go. Not with a letter. Not with anything he signs. I don't know what he'll do. I don't think he knows yet. But I know him, and he will not let this go unanswered." He picked the cup up again. "So I came to say so to your face. Where nobody reads it first."

Emeric did not answer at once. At the other end of the table Ferra's pencil had stopped. She was not writing. She was looking at Zale, and Emeric saw her decide something about him, and saw her not write down what it was.

"Thank you," Emeric said.

"Don't thank me. I'm going back before the weather turns." Zale said it lightly, easily, the way he said everything. "I'll stay a while, if you'll have me, and eat your food, and drink your famous water. And then I'll go back down the lane on a sulphur boat, before the gales, and you won't have to feed me through a winter." He grinned. "I've never stayed anywhere through a winter since I was twenty. I'd hate to start."

Emeric heard what was under it. He did not say so. He had spent half his life wearing a face over a face, and he knew one when it sat at the end of his table.

The gifts came after, when the plates were gone and the lamps were trimmed, and Zale went down to his room for them and came back up with his sea bag over his shoulder and put it on the table and undid the neck.

"First," he said, "the cheap one." And he took out a pouch of stained leather, closed with a thong, and put it down in front of Emeric, and opened it, and tipped it.

Splinters. Long and thin, most of them, the length of a finger joint or less, some clear as water and some smoky and one or two the colour of weak tea. They came out onto the table with a small dry sound like beads, and lay in the lamplight, and caught it.

"Shard crystal," Zale said. "That's what they call it on the north coast, in the ports past Kesrath. Kesrathi sellers have it in every tavern, a splinter at a time, out of a pocket, for a few coppers. It's pretty. Sailors buy it for girls. I bought it for the stories." He pushed one splinter across the table with a fingertip, toward Emeric. "You get a story with every one, if you buy the man a drink. They don't sell it with the story. The story comes after the second drink."

Emeric did not pick it up yet. "What stories?"

"Where it comes from. Or where they won't say it comes from." Zale sat back. "There's a place. They won't name it in front of a stranger. I got three names for it in three ports and I think they were all the same place and all of them were lies. The one they use among themselves, when they think you've had too much to follow, is the sacrifice ground." He said it with a sailor's relish for a bad name. "Men go out to it and dig, and bring this back. And some of them don't come back. Not drowned. Not killed. They go down into the low places on it, the hollows, and lie down, and don't get up. One old man in Varre told me it's the ground breathing out. He said you can smell it, and when you stop smelling it, you're already lying down."

Across the table, Imani had put her cup down.

"And the water," Zale said. "Black water. Comes up out of the ground on the shore there, thick, and if you put a light to it, it burns. They use it for their boats' seams." He shrugged. "Everybody on that coast has a story about water that burns. I didn't think much of it."

Emeric had thought a great deal about water that burns. He had walked across Emberfell beside Rowena and stopped dead in front of a cask of it, and brought home what she gave him, and poured it into the first avenue in New Kaldo, and had an order in since the winter, down the sulphur lane, for more of it, from Duvrenne's factors, as lamp oil. He said nothing about any of that.

"Is that all?" he said.

"No." Zale smiled. "That's the part everybody tells. The part only the old ones tell, after the third drink, is the lamps." He leaned forward. "They say that on the sacrifice ground, at night, a lamp stone lights itself. Nobody tells it to. You carry a dead one out there in your pocket, a spent one, and at night it's burning in your pocket like a coal, and you have to take it out and put it on a rock or it'll scorch your coat." He sat back. "Now. That one I liked. I've never heard that one anywhere else, and I've heard every story there is about lamp stones."

Emeric picked up the splinter Zale had pushed toward him.

It was light, and cool, and faintly rough along one edge where it had broken from something. He held it between his finger and thumb and did not look at it. He looked at his brother, and kept his face where it was, and let the rest of him go down into the splinter, a very little way, the way he would have reached into a stone on Nevin's bench, carefully, so that nobody at the table could have told he had done anything but pick up a pretty thing and turn it over.

It was not like any stone on Nevin's bench. He did not know what it was like. He put it back down on the table with the others before he could decide, and folded his hands, and felt Armand, in his chair by the hearth, not looking at him in a way that was very like looking.

A place where lamp stones lit themselves. A place where the ground breathed out something that laid men down. A place where water burned, and where something like this lay about loose enough to be carried off by the pocketful and sold for coppers in taverns to men buying presents for girls.

He did not say any of it. He could not have said it without saying what a lamp stone did in the band, and what the band was, and how he knew. He said, "Where did you say they sell it?"

"Everywhere on that coast. Kesrathi, all of them." Zale looked at him, and something moved behind the easy look, and was put away. "You like it."

"I like the lamp story."

"So did I." Zale watched him a moment longer. Then he put the pouch's thong down beside the splinters and pushed the whole of it across the table. "They're yours. They were always yours. I didn't know anybody else who'd want a pocket of broken glass and a story about a lamp."

"And the other one," Zale said.

He took it out of the sea bag last. It was wrapped in oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth in a second wrapping of linen gone brown at the folds, and when he had taken both off he held it a moment in both hands before he put it down, the way a man holds a thing he has carried a long way and is not sure he wants to stop carrying.

A book. Not large. Bound in leather that had been good once and had been wet more than once since, so that the boards had warped and been pressed flat again, and the spine was cracked across in three places. It lay on the table in the lamplight and did not look like anything.

"Open it," said Zale.

Emeric opened it.

Signs. Rows of them, page after page, in a careful hand that was not an easy one, the hand of a man drawing a thing he did not understand and did not want to get wrong. Not letters. Not any letters Emeric had ever seen, in this world or the other. Hooks and bars and ladders, and a thing like a bird's foot, and a square with a dot in the middle. Here and there a line drawn round a block of them, like the edge of a stone, with a crack across it. And round them, in the margins, crowded into every space, a second hand, small and fast, in a brown ink gone pale, in some other script that was at least a script, that Emeric could not read either.

"I got it off a man in a port a long way west of anywhere you've heard of," Zale said. "He'd had it off a dead man's chest, he said. He didn't know what it was. Nobody did. I've shown it to every scholar in every port that had one, and a few that only had men who said they were, and nobody could read it." He was looking at the book, not at Emeric. "The little writing, round the edges, I can read some of. Badly. It's a tongue from out past the far side of the western water. I speak it like a sailor and read it like a child. The big writing, the signs, nobody can read at all. Nobody has ever been able to."

He looked up.

"Nobody could read it," he said. "I thought of you."

Emeric looked at him across the book.

"You won't remember," Zale said. "You were small. Five, six. There was a lamp in the nursery, the big one on the chain over the table, with the stone in it in a brass cage, and one night you got up on the table and took it down, cage and stone and all, and took the cage apart with a knife off the supper tray, because you wanted to read what was cut on the stone inside. Nobody could stop you. You sat there with it in your lap in the dark, with the nurse screaming, running your thumb over the stone, and when they took it off you, you cried, not because they'd taken it but because you hadn't finished reading it." He smiled, not the easy smile. "I told them I'd done it. I got beaten for it. It was worth it, to see their faces when you told Father at breakfast what the runes on it said."

Emeric did remember. He had not thought of it since he was a boy. He remembered the brass cage coming apart in his hands, and the weight of the stone, and the cuts on it under his thumb in the dark, and a voice somewhere above him in the dark saying, It was me, it was me, I did it, which he had never known was Zale's.

"Everybody said you'd be the greatest runesmith in a hundred years," Zale said. "Then you were seven, and your mother died, and you were gone a year, and when you came back you drank." He looked at the book. "I carried this round for a long time, thinking, if he'd been what he was going to be, he'd have read that. I don't know why I kept it. I think I kept it for the boy with the lamp." He pushed it an inch across the table, toward Emeric. "I don't know if he's still in there. You tell me."

It was aimed at who he used to be. Emeric felt it land there, in the place it was aimed, and felt how much of him was not there any more, and how much of him was nowhere Zale could have imagined. He put his hand flat on the open page. He did not reach into it. There was nothing in it to reach into. It was ink.

"I can't read it," he said.

"No," said Zale. "Nobody can."

"Not yet," said Emeric.

Zale looked at him, and whatever he had been going to say, he did not say it.

Armand asked for it.

He did not get up from his chair. He held out his hands, and Ferra took the book from the table and carried it to him, and he settled it on the rug on his knees and took his spectacles out of his coat and put them on.

He turned the pages for an hour. Nobody left. Zale sat at the end of the bench and watched him the way a man at a card table watches the one player he cannot read. Bram went to sleep sitting up and woke and pretended he had not. Imani went down to the ward and came back. Saban, who had seen a great many things sold that nobody could read, looked at the book once, closely, at the binding and the paper, and said the paper was not from anywhere he knew, and then did not look at it again. Ferra wrote.

Armand took his spectacles off, and held the book close, and put them on again, and held it far. He turned back. He turned forward. He stopped for a long time at a page with a drawing on it, a drawing of something that might have been a plant, and then turned on. At the end he closed it, and sat with his hands flat on the cover, and took his spectacles off, and folded them.

"I have handled books," Armand said, "from every kingdom that has books. From some that only had them once." He looked at Zale. "I cannot read one line of that. I do not think anybody in any of them could."

"That's what they all said," said Zale. "Every scholar in every port."

"Then they were honest men, which is rarer than scholars." Armand looked down at the book on his knees. "May I have it for a night?"

Zale looked at Emeric. Emeric nodded. Armand put the book inside his coat, against his chest, the way he carried his flasks, and called for his chair.

They went down late, all of them, the keep dark but for the stair lamps, and Zale last, because Zale was always last out of a room and first into the next one. At the stair's turn he stopped, and Emeric, a step above him, stopped too.

"You've got a good table," Zale said. "I've sat at a lot of tables."

"I know."

"The clerk thinks I'm a liar. The smith thinks I'm a waste of a good bed. The old man thinks I'm interesting, which is worse than either." He smiled. "And your Ward thinks I'm Corbin with a tan."

"Ward thinks everybody's Corbin with a tan."

"Good," said Zale. "Keep him." He looked up the stair at Emeric for a moment in the bad light. "You don't drink."

"No."

"You didn't, ever. Did you. Not really."

Emeric looked back at him. It would have been easy to make it smaller. He had been making it smaller for years, for everybody, and he was good at it.

"No," he said.

Zale nodded slowly. He did not ask why. He looked as though he had a hundred questions and had decided, on the stair, in the dark, that he would not ask any of them tonight, and possibly not tomorrow either, and that he did not like deciding it.

"Goodnight, little brother," he said, and went on down.

Emeric stood on the stair until he heard the guest room door shut. Then he went back up to the long room, where the lamps were still lit, and Ward was still by the door, and the splinters were still lying on the table in the light, pale and smoky, beside the coin with the ship on it.

"Hull two went out an hour ago," Ward said. "From past the bar. No lamp till the point."

"Thank you."

Ward looked at the splinters. "He's not Corbin's," he said.

"No."

"He's not anybody's." Ward said it the way he said the gate was shut. "That's the trouble with him."

He went out. Emeric sat down at the table and swept the splinters together into a little heap with the side of his hand, and sat for a long time with his fingertip resting on the nearest one, not reaching, only touching, the way a man puts his finger on a map where he has never been and means to go.
