Chapter 278 - The Last Match

The first fiber came out of the forge at a quarter past three in the morning on the second Sunday in October, and it was so fine that Bram, holding the spool up to the lamp, could not see it.

"It's there," said Emeric.

"I believe you." Bram turned the spool. "I can't see it. I can see the light on it, if I tip it. That's all." He tipped it, and a hair of light ran round the spool's barrel and was gone. "How long?"

"Forty yards."

Bram put the spool down on the bench as if it were an egg.

It had taken the forge four hours and a rack of stones. A tube, first, finer than a hair, of a clear tough stuff that was half Ivo's glass and half the coke-ovens' resin, drawn out of the melt as a glassblower draws a cane, but finer, and longer, and all of it the same to the width of a wavelength of light; and inside the tube, as it was drawn, a thread of oil, the light refinery cut, clear, that would not carry a current; and every inch or so along the inside of the tube, set into its wall like the rungs of a ladder in a well, two specks of metal, one each side, so small that the forge had placed them by sense and not by sight. Then the ends sealed. Then the next inch.

That was all it was. A tube of oil with metal in it.

"Show me," said Bram.

Emeric cut a hand's length off the spool and laid it on the bench, and clamped one end, and tied a lead weight to the other with a thread, so that it hung over the bench's edge. Then he touched the little wires at the clamped end to the induction coil from the wireless's old spark set; and the coil buzzed; and the weight, which weighed about as much as a pea, rose a quarter of an inch, and hung there.

Bram looked at it.

"That's it?"

"That's one." Emeric switched off the coil, and the weight sank back. "The charge pushes the oil along inside the tube. From one end toward the other. So one end gets fat and short and the other gets thin and long. Like a bicep and a tricep in one hair." He turned it in the lamplight. "One does nothing. A thousand side by side, all pulling the same way at the same time, is a muscle."

"A thousand of those," said Bram slowly.

"Ten thousand. A hundred thousand. As many as you like. In a bundle, like meat."

Bram was quiet for a while. Then he picked up the spool again and held it to the lamp and tipped it, and watched the hair of light go round.

"The forge can't make a hundred thousand of these," he said.

"No."

"It took four hours for forty yards."

"Yes."

"So you want a machine." Bram put the spool down. "That draws it. Tube and oil and the little bits of metal, all together, by the mile. That a man can run. With no forge."

"Yes."

Bram looked at the spool for a long time, in the lamplight, at a quarter to four in the morning, with his arms folded and his face doing the thing it did when somebody had asked him to make something nobody had ever made. Then he unfolded his arms.

"Tess'll want to do the metal," he said. "The little bits. She'll say it's electrical." He went to the door. "I'm going to bed. Don't touch that till I'm up."



It took them nineteen days.

Tess did the metal. She had said it was electrical before Bram finished telling her about it, in the Motion Shop on the Monday, and he had looked at Emeric over her head with no expression whatever. The drawing tower went up in bay three, in the corner where the old lathe had been: a tall iron frame like a gallows, with Ivo's melt pot at the top over a little furnace, and the tube coming down out of the pot's nozzle as fine as a spider's thread, cooling as it fell, through a ring of air jets; and beside the nozzle, the thing that put the oil in, a needle inside the needle; and beside that, Tess's thing, which put the specks of metal in the tube's wall as it went past, from a wheel no bigger than a coin with a hundred teeth round its edge, each tooth carrying a grain of silver paste, turning against the warm tube in time with the draw.

It failed eleven times. The tube broke. The oil went in lumps. The wheel's teeth missed, or doubled. On the fourteenth day it drew two hundred yards of fiber that worked for a hundred and ninety of them and then went dead, and Bram threw his cap across the bay; and on the nineteenth day, at a quarter past five in the afternoon, with Dov at the wheel's gear and Wren at the bottom of the tower with the take-up spool and Tess in her chair at the top on the gantry Bram had built for her, a mile of it came down out of the nozzle, whole, and went round Wren's spool, and Tess put a meter on the end of it and said, "All of it."

Wren looked up the tower at her. Then she looked at the spool in her hands.

"A mile," she said. "Of hair."

"A mile of muscle," said Tess. "Wind it on the big drum. Don't break it."

They bundled it that week. Dov cut it into lengths a forearm long and laid them side by side in a trough, a thousand at a time, and bound the bundle every inch with silk, and wired the ends; and on the Saturday, in bay three, with half the works crowded into the bay's door to watch, they clamped a bundle as thick as a man's wrist to the bench at one end and tied the other to the handle of a dumbbell from the base's gym, a twenty-pound iron dumbbell, lying on the bench.

Tess switched on the coil.

The bundle drew in. It did not jerk; it drew, smoothly, the way a man's arm draws a bucket out of a well, and shortened, and thickened, and the dumbbell came up off the bench on the end of it, and rose, and swung, slowly, on its handle, a foot in the air.

The bay was quiet.

Tess turned the coil down. The bundle let out, slowly. The dumbbell came down, and touched the bench, and lay there.

"Again," said Dov, from the gear. It was the first thing he had said all day.

She did it again. And again. And then Bram, who had stood by the bench with his arms folded the whole time saying nothing, unfolded them, and untied the dumbbell, and put his own right hand where the dumbbell had been, round the bundle's end; and looked at Tess.

"Bram," said Tess.

"Go on."

"It lifted twenty pounds."

"Then it'll lift my hand. Go on."

She turned the coil on, very slowly, a notch at a time; and the bundle drew in round his fingers, and closed on them, and held. It held his hand the way a man shakes hands, firmly, all round; and Bram stood there in bay three with his hand in the grip of a bundle of oil and hair, and squeezed it back, and it did not crush him, and it did not let go, and it did not shake.

"Hm," said Bram.

Tess turned it down. It let go of him. He took his hand back and looked at it, and flexed it, and looked at the bundle on the bench.

"That'll do," he said; and went back into the shop, and nobody in the bay door said anything until he had gone, and then they all did at once.



Emeric heard about Abel Dunning's hand on the Monday, from Abel Dunning.

He had come into bay three on his way to somewhere else, because the bay had become a place people came into on their way to somewhere else since the dumbbell; and Abel was there, on the stool at Tess's bench, with his left sleeve rolled up to the shoulder and the stump of his forearm resting on a folded cloth, and Tess bent over it with a pot of salt paste, and Imani, of all people, standing at Tess's shoulder with her own sleeves rolled, saying, "There. No. A finger's width toward the elbow. There."

And on the bench beside them, on a cloth, a hand.

It was not a beautiful thing. Tess had made it out of what she had: a steel frame for the bones, jointed at the knuckles with brass pins like the golem's, and over the frame a fiber bundle for each finger running back to the wrist, and over all of it a glove of Abloh's softest kid, stitched tight, the colour of tea; and at the wrist a socket of leather and Bakelite moulded to fit the end of Abel's arm, with two brass discs inside it, and a wire from the socket to a small varnished box with two valves in it and a battery and a coil, the size of a book, on a strap.

Tess looked up and saw Emeric in the door, and her face did several things very fast, and settled on a kind of furious calm.

"Don't say anything," she said.

He didn't say anything.

She pressed the second disc into its place and slid the socket on over Abel's arm, and buckled it, and slung the box on its strap over his shoulder, and switched the box on. The valves warmed, orange. Abel watched them.

"Now," said Tess, "you know how you told Imani, at the ward, that when you reach for a cup with that arm, you can still feel the hand you haven't got? Closing?"

"I can feel it now," said Abel.

"Do that. Close the hand you haven't got."

Abel looked at the hand on the end of his arm. He looked at it for a long time. Then his face went still, and his eyes went somewhere else, the way a man's do when he is trying to remember something he did every day of his life and has not done in years; and the muscles in his forearm moved, under the socket's leather, a flicker.

And the hand closed.

Slowly. Not all the way. The fingers drew in, the four of them together and the thumb a little behind, the kid creasing at the knuckles, the way a sleeping child's hand closes; and stopped halfway; and stayed there.

Abel made a noise. It was not a word.

"Open it," said Tess. Her voice was very steady.

Abel's forearm moved again, on the other side, the back of it. The hand opened.

"Close it."

It closed. Further this time.

Imani put a tin cup in front of him on the bench. An ordinary cup, the shop's tea cup, chipped.

Abel looked at it. Then he reached for it, not with the hook arm, with the hand; brought the hand down over the cup, clumsily, a little to one side, and closed it. It closed on the cup's handle and on the rim and on nothing, all at once, crooked; and the cup tipped, and he caught it, with the hand, and held it. And lifted it. An inch off the bench. Two. And held it there, shaking, with all his face screwed up into his eyes, a cup a man could have picked up with a hook any day of the week, held in the air in a kid glove on a steel frame on a bundle of oil and hair, by a muscle that had been trying to close a hand for nine years with nothing to close.

He put it down. He opened the hand. He sat there on the stool and looked at it.

"Well," said Abel. His voice had gone very odd. "Well. That's a thing."

"It's slow," said Tess. "It's clumsy. It's heavy, the box. It runs off a battery and the battery's good for a day. It can't do buttons." She was talking very fast. "And you'll have to learn it. Like a baby learns a hand. A month, Imani says. Maybe more. And it'll never be as strong as the hook. For work. You'll keep the hook for work." She stopped. "But it's a hand."

Abel looked at her.

"It's a hand," he said.

And then, carefully, the way he had held the cup, he reached out with it and put it on top of hers, on the bench, the kid glove over her small burned fingers; and closed it, very gently, a little way. And held it.

Tess did not say anything at all.

Emeric went out of the bay and stood in the works yard in the October sun with his back against the wall and his hands in his pockets, and stayed there until Imani came out, a little while later, and stood beside him.

"She didn't tell you," said Imani.

"No."

"She told me. In September. Under oath." Imani looked at the works' chimneys. "She was afraid you'd have known it wouldn't work and not said. She said that. That's what she was afraid of." She looked at him sideways. "You were the nerve study."

He laughed, very quietly, against the wall.

"You didn't know," said Imani. "Did you. That she was doing it."

"I knew a valve could do it. In the spring. I sat on that stool and walked it through in my head while she was winding his spring." He looked at the bay door. "I didn't tell her. I thought she'd find it." He was quiet. "She found it."

"She found it." Imani put her hand on his arm, briefly. "Go and tell her it's good. And then go away. She'll want to cry and she won't do it in front of you."

He went in, and told Tess it was good, and she said, "I know," without looking at him; and he went away.



The letter came the same week, from the crown's council, under the council's seal, on paper so heavy it would have stood up by itself.

Ferra brought it to him at his table in the study, unopened, because it was addressed to the duke and she did not open the duke's letters until he had; and stood by the table while he read it, with her book under her arm, looking out of the window at the inner ward.

It was very polite. It took two pages to be polite in. The council, mindful of the duke's elevation and of the great extent of his holdings in the south, and of the regrettable want of any tie of blood or marriage between his house and the old houses of the north, which some had remarked upon; and wishing nothing more than that the duke's standing should be as firm in the north as it was in the south; begged leave to propose a match. A lord temporal of the north, of the oldest blood, who had a daughter of twenty, accomplished, of good health, and willing. The lord would bring the north's goodwill. The daughter would bring her father. The council would bring its blessing. The duke would bring nothing but his hand.

He read it twice. Then he put it down on the table and looked at it.

"It's the last one," said Ferra, to the window.

"How do you know?"

"Because it's the best one. They've saved the best for last. That's the oldest blood in the north, and the north is the half of the kingdom that doesn't like you." She did not turn round. "If you say no to this, they'll stop asking. There's nobody better to ask about."

"I'm saying no."

"I know." She still did not turn round. "Write it."

He wrote it. He wrote it at the table with her standing at the window, on Kaldo's own paper, in his own hand, as the council's letter deserved; and it took him an hour, because a refusal to the oldest blood in the north had to be as polite as the offer and could not, in one word of it, be an insult to the girl, who was twenty and had not asked for any of this. He thanked the council. He thanked the lord, by name. He spoke of the daughter with respect and in the fewest words he could. He said that the duke had considered the proposal with the seriousness it deserved, and must, with great regret, decline it; and that he hoped the north would find in his roads and his schools and his grain trains a tie that would serve it better than any marriage.

And then, in the middle of the third paragraph, without meaning to, he wrote the reason.

He wrote it in one line. He read it back, sitting at the table, and his face went hot; and he drew his pen through it, once, firmly, and then again, and then a third time, and went on with the paragraph below it as if it were not there.

He sanded it, and folded it, and held it out to Ferra without looking at her.

"Read it," he said. "As Chancellor. Before it goes."

She took it, and opened it, and read it at the window in the October light.

He watched her read it. He knew the moment she came to the struck line, because her eyes stopped moving, and stayed stopped; and he knew, from the length of time they stayed stopped, that three strokes of a pen were not enough to keep Ferra out of a line of ink she wanted to read. She had read struck lines in ledgers since she was fifteen. She had found fortunes under them.

She read it. Then she read the rest of the letter, all of it, to the end. Then she folded it again.

"It's a good letter," she said. "It'll do."

"Ferra..."

"I'll seal it." She put it under her arm with her book. "It goes on the Wayford train tomorrow. It'll be in the council's hands by Friday."

"You read it."

"I read all of it. That's what you asked." She went to the door. "That's what Chancellors do." And she went out, with the letter, and did not look at him; and he sat at his table and looked at the door for a long time.

She sent it as it was. With the line in it, struck three times, so that anybody at the council table who held it to the light might, if they were patient, read what the duke of the south had written, and thought better of, and not been able to bring himself to write out fair without.

And that evening, in her own room, she opened her book at the back, at her own column on the back pages where nobody looked, and wrote the line out in it, in her square hand, word for word, as she had read it through the ink: and under it, the date, and nothing else.

She did not show it to him. He did not ask.

Imani had the fair copy of the letter from Ferra on the Wednesday; Ferra gave it to her at breakfast, without comment, across the table, folded. Imani read it with her porridge. She came to the copy of the third paragraph, where Ferra had written the struck line out fair in the margin in pencil, very small, and read it twice, and looked at Emeric across the table for some time with her strong straight brows doing a great deal of talking.

He ate his breakfast.

Imani took the copy to the hospital. She filed it that afternoon in the drawer in her surgery where she kept the pharmacopoeia's cards; and the label on the card she filed it behind, in her own hand, which Bettany saw on the Thursday and did not understand and did not ask about, said: Contraindicated.



Ward was in the yard at dawn on the Friday, as he was every day, and Emeric was in the yard with him, as he was most days now; and on the Friday, at about a quarter past six, with the frost on the cobbles and the breath standing out of both of them, Emeric hit him.

Not hard. It was a training sword, wood, padded at the edge. It was the first time.

Since May there had been the ghosts. Ward's word. Since the morning in May when Emeric had stood in this yard with knight's tools and nothing else and found, between one heartbeat and the next, that he could see what Ward was going to do before Ward did it: the paths, drawn faint across his sight, a sharp one where Ward had decided and two or three faint ones where he hadn't; and Ward had not been able to touch him, all that morning, not once, and had called it a draw and gone away to think. And since May Ward had not touched him. Not once. Not in five months of mornings. And Emeric had not touched Ward either. He had never struck. He had stood in the ghosts and let the paths go past him and not once put his own blade into any of them, because he had been learning to see, and you cannot learn to see and learn to strike in the same morning.

On the Friday he struck.

Ward came in high, from the left, a cut he had made ten thousand times; and the ghost of it was there before it was, sharp, decided, and Emeric was already out of its way; and as he moved he saw, beside the ghost of the cut, faint, another ghost: Ward's recovery, the half-step back Ward always took after a high cut from the left, and the instant in it, a breath long, when Ward's guard was open on the right side under the arm. He had seen it a hundred times since May. He had never put his blade in it.

He put his blade in it.

It touched Ward's ribs under the right arm, padded wood on padded coat, with a dull flat sound in the frosty yard; and Ward stopped.

They stood there, both of them, with their breath going up between them in the cold.

Ward looked down at the blade against his ribs. Then he looked up.

"Again," he said.

They went again. Ward came in low this time, and then high, and then a feint and a cut, and something else Emeric had never seen him do in eleven years; and Emeric watched the ghosts and went where they weren't, and the third time Ward recovered, there was the breath again, the instant, open; and the wood touched Ward's shoulder.

"Again."

And the third time, on Ward's thigh. And the fourth, the ribs again, the other side.

And then Ward stepped back, out of distance, and lowered his blade, and stood in the middle of the yard in the grey light with the frost on his boots, breathing, and looked at Emeric for a long time without saying anything.

Emeric lowered his.

"No stone," said Ward. It was not a question. He had felt for one, Emeric knew, the first time, with whatever a knight felt with. "No suit. Nothing."

"Nothing."

"Just the ghosts."

"Just the ghosts. And eleven years of you."

Ward nodded slowly. He looked down at his own padded coat, where the four touches had left four faint marks of the chalk Emeric rubbed on his blade's edge so that they could see a touch; ribs, shoulder, thigh, ribs.

"I've had men touch me before," he said. "Your father did, once, when he was twenty, and he never let me forget it. Rylan does, about once a year, and he's ashamed of it for a week after." He looked up. "Nobody's ever touched me four times in a row. Not since I was a boy." He was quiet. "It's a strange thing. To see it come. From a boy you carried out of a mountain."

Emeric did not say anything.

Ward raised his blade again, slowly, to his guard.

"Again," he said.

And there was something in his voice that Emeric had never heard in it before in eleven years of mornings; not anger, and not pride, though there was some of both; something that sounded, if it had been any other man's voice, very like joy.



The six met on the Sunday night, in the wine store, at the real table; and he told them about the night.

He told it plainly. He had told Ferra in August and Imani in September, so the two of them sat and let him tell it to the other four without a word: one night a year, the last night of October, when the children of Kaldo would dress up as anything they liked, and go door to door in the dark with lanterns, and be given sweets. And the costumes would be stories. Old stories. He would give them the stories himself, at the school, all of them that he knew.

"What stories?" said Saban.

"Out of a book. An old one." He looked at Ferra. "Winged ones. People of the woods. People under the hills. People in the sea. Things that come out at night. Silly things. Terrible things."

The table was quiet.

Everybody at it had seen the wall.

"You want to teach them," said Armand at last. "The children. What was on that wall. Without telling them it was on a wall."

"I want them to have heard of them," said Emeric. "Before anybody ever has to meet one. If anybody ever does. I want there to be a story about them in every house in Kaldo, so that it's in their heads already, so that it's ordinary." He turned his cup. "And I want it to be a good night. Not a lesson. A good night, with sweets and lanterns, that they'll love when they're seven, and remember when they're seventy."

"The Church," said Ward.

"I know."

"Doran's Church. The things under the hill that the Light put down." Ward did not take his eyes off him. "You'll have the children of Kaldo dressed up as the Church's devils, walking about in the dark, knocking on doors. And there's a cardinal's man in Corbin's rooms twice a month." He was quiet. "That's a page. If you want to hand them one."

"It's stories," said Ferra.

Everybody looked at her.

"It's a night for stories," said Ferra. "That's what it is. That's all it is, out loud. To anybody who asks. Children dressing up as things from stories, the way children have always dressed up as things, because it's fun, and they get sweets." She had her pen out and was turning it. "There's nothing in a story. You can't hang anybody for a story. Every grandmother in the kingdom has a dozen, Doran says. If the Church wants to say that dressing up as a grandmother's story is a heresy, let it say so, in front of Keller, and see how it sounds." She stopped turning the pen. "But that's all it is. Out loud. Stories. Not one word about where the stories came from, or why. Not on any paper. Not ever. Not until..." She stopped. "Not until."

The table looked at her.

"That's my condition," said Ferra. "As Chancellor. It's a night for stories, and only stories, in public, and that's all anybody ever says it is." She looked at Emeric. "And what it really is stays at this table."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Agreed," he said.

"Ward?"

Ward looked at Ferra, and then at Emeric, and then at the plaster by the door, where her number was pinned, and the four brass pins at its corners.

"Stories," he said. "All right." He picked up his cup. "I'll want to know what's in them."

"You'll hear them," said Emeric. "Come to the school on Thursday. Bring a stool."



The top class had the long room at the school's end, with high windows down one side and a pair of great doors at the back onto the yard; and on Thursdays in term it sat at the long tables by the windows and whoever's day it was to teach it stood at the front with a slate.

It was Emeric's day. It was usually sums, or machines, or the stars. Today he stood at the front with no slate at all, and forty children looked at him, and Ward sat on a stool by the great doors at the back with his arms folded, and Sela sat beside him with her mending.

"I'm going to tell you some stories," said Emeric. "Old ones. Out of a book."

Forty children sat up.

He had thought about how to begin. He had thought about it for a week, and in the end he did the thing every teacher he had ever had on the other side had done when they wanted forty children to listen: he lowered his voice.

"A long time ago," he said, "so long that nobody remembers, there were other people in the world. Not people like us. Other kinds."

He told them about the short broad ones first, because he had seen them on the wall and he could see them now, at their forges, with their beards. Who lived in the mountains, in halls cut in the rock, and worked metal better than any smith alive, and were stubborn, and proud, and kept their word for ever once they gave it, and loved gold, and beer, and singing very loud and very badly. Forty children laughed at the singing. In the back of the barn, on his stool, Ward looked at the floor.

He told them about the tall ones, who lived in the woods, and lived for a thousand years, and were wise, and very beautiful, and sometimes very sad, because when you live a thousand years you outlive everything you love; and who could sing a tree into growing, and walk in the woods without breaking a twig; and the trees they lived among were the greatest trees in the world, with trunks braided like rope, and long vines hanging down.

"What did they look like?" said a girl at the front. It was Nell Tanner, Hob's daughter, eleven, who had read her father's line in the census at the Assembly in July and been looking at the world with her chin up ever since.

"Tall. Slender. Long hands." He touched the side of his head. "And ears like this. Long. With a point."

"What colour?"

"Their ears?"

"Them."

He thought about it, and about the book he was supposed to be telling them out of; and found that he knew what he wanted the book to have said.

"Every colour," he said. "Like us. Look round this room." They looked round the room. There were children in it as dark as Imani and as pale as Ferra and every colour between, from the port, from the hamlets, from the freed men's families off the Isle, from Wayford and Grayrock and the Milford trains. "Some of them were dark. Some were brown. Some were pale as milk. Some had hair as straight as rain, and some had curls you could lose a comb in, and some had hair that stood up round their heads like a dandelion clock, and some wore it in locs, long, down their backs, with rings in them." He touched his own, which were tied back. "Like mine."

Forty children looked at his hair. Nell Tanner looked at it longest.

"With the ears," she said.

"With the ears."

She nodded slowly, as if that settled something important, and he went on.

He told them about the winged ones. The great ones, longer than a ship, with necks and tails and wings like sails, that flew over the woods and the mountains, and carried things: seeds, in their claws, from one grove to another, so that the trees could grow where the trees could not walk. He did not say what kind of seed. He did not say what kind of tree. He saw Ward, on his stool, lift his head.

He told them about the people of the sea, who lived under the water in lit cities and had tails like fish below the waist, and sang, and sometimes came up onto the rocks at night to look at the moon. He told them about the little people, no higher than your thumb, who lived in the long grass and stole butter. He told them about the people who lived under the hills and came out at night.

And then he told them about the others. The ones from the other kind of story. The ones that were not people at all, or had been, once.

The ones who did not die, and did not live either, and slept all day in boxes in cellars, and came out at night to drink people's blood, and could not cross running water or come into a house unless they were asked; whose names he gave them, because the book had to have names. The men who turned into wolves when the moon was full, and did not know they had done it in the morning. The slime: a thing that lived in old cellars and ate whatever fell into it, which was round and green and wobbled and had no face at all, and was the forty children's favourite at once, because it had no face and wobbled.

"Are they real?" said a boy at the back.

The room went very quiet.

"They're in a book," said Emeric. "A very old one. Older than anybody." He looked down the room at the boy. "And there's going to be a night for them."

He told them about the night. The last night of October. When anybody who wanted to could be any of them, any of the things in the stories, and dress up as it, and carry a lantern, and go to people's doors in the dark, and knock.

"And then what?" said Nell.

"And then you say, sweet or story."

"Sweet or story?"

"And the person at the door gives you a sweet. Or, if they want to, before they give you the sweet, they can ask you for the story. The story of what you are. Whatever you've come as." He looked round the forty faces. "So you have to know it. Whatever you dress up as, you have to know its story, and be able to tell it, at any door in Kaldo, to anybody who asks. Or you don't get the sweet."

Forty children looked at him with an expression he remembered, from the other side, from his own face in a mirror at seven, the week before the last night of October.

"What's it called?" said the boy at the back.

"Hollow Night," said Emeric.

And forty children said it over, under their breath, the way children say a word they mean to keep; and at the back of the room, by the great doors, Ward sat on his stool with his arms folded and his face turned to the high chapel windows, very still, the face of a man who has just heard something he will be a long time thinking about.



Saban was at the school gate when the children came out.

He had come to fetch Emeric for something about Grayrock's tolls, and stood by the gate's post with his hat in his hand while forty children came out of the great doors into the square at a run, all talking, all at once; and two of them, small, went past the post an arm's length from him, a boy and a girl, arguing.

"And they're tall," said the girl. "Taller than your father. And thin. And they live in the woods with the special trees, and they've got the long ears, and they..."

"The trees with the vines," said the boy. "That hang down."

"He didn't say vines."

"He did. He said vines. He said the trees had vines that hung down, with the seed things on."

And they were gone into the square.

Saban stood by the post with his hat in his hand.

When Emeric came out, last, with Ward, Saban fell in beside him across the square, and walked with him a while in silence, and then said, without looking at him, in the voice he used for prices:

"Tall. Thin. Long faces. Living among trees with braided trunks, and vines hanging down, and pods on the vines." He walked on. "I've seen that, Your Grace. In a room that isn't a room. On a wall."

Emeric did not answer.

"Whose book was that?" said Saban.

"An old one."

"An old one." Saban put his hat on. "Then nobody else reads it." He walked on a few paces. "Nobody. Not my clerks, not Ferra's, not the printer's boy. It stays where it is, wherever that is." He looked at Emeric at last, sideways, under his hat brim. "Because those children will be telling their grandmothers about trees with vines and pods on them by supper. And their grandmothers will tell the priest. And the priest will tell a cardinal's man in Corbin's rooms. And a cardinal's man knows what a pod looks like." He was quiet. "Tell them the stories. Tell them the ears and the wings and the woods. Leave the vines out."

Emeric walked on for a while.

"Yes," he said.

"Good." Saban stopped at the corner, where his way went down to the river. "The tolls. Tomorrow." He touched his hat. "It was a very good story, Your Grace. I'd have given you a sweet for it."



Abloh was making tents.

He was making them in the sail loft over the boat shed, where he had made sails before he ever made soap, and taught half the school to sew since; and where, since the midwinter before last, the sewing machine had stood under the long window in its wooden case, with its wheel and its needle that went up and down faster than any hand, which he had said had better not be faster than him.

Ward had asked for tents. For the Proving class, riding north at first snow; for the Hod's long runs; for anybody Kaldo sent anywhere there was no roof. And Emeric had asked Abloh for something more than tents. Something that felt like home, he had said, when you were far from it. And Abloh had looked at him for a long time and said, "Home," as if it were a cloth he had not been sent a sample of, and gone up to the loft.

What he had made, by the third week of October, was a wall tent. Ward had stood in it on the loft floor with his arms folded and said nothing for some time. It had walls a man could stand straight against, not a slope, and a ridge at a height that cleared Bram's head, on a frame of steel tubes from the welders, pinned at the corners, that went up in the time it took to say so; and it was double-walled, waxed Milford linen inside and out with a hand's width of air between, and over the whole of it a fly of linen rubberized with the Isle's sap, black, that shed rain like a duck's back. The groundsheet was sewn in. The door tied, and then laced, and then tied again. In the back wall, at shoulder height, there was a ring of tin set into the cloth with a collar of asbestos round it, for the pipe of a small tin stove that would burn anything, wood or coal or dung, and had a little grate in its belly the size of a fire stone, for when there was one. From the ridge pole hung a hook for a lantern, stone or battery, either. Along the walls there were cots that folded flat. And on the floor, laid down the middle of the groundsheet where a man would put his feet when he swung them off his cot in the morning, there was a rug.

It was a good rug. Abloh had chosen it himself, at Wayford. Red and blue, in squares.

"No," said Ward.

"It weighs four pounds."

"It weighs four pounds times forty tents times a horse." Ward did not unfold his arms. "No."

"A man wakes in the snow," said Abloh, "in a country that is not his, a hundred miles from his own bed. He puts his feet down on frozen canvas." He let that sit. "Or he puts them down on this."

"No," said Ward.

They had been at it for an hour when Emeric came up the loft stair. They had already had the liner out. The liner was felt, grey, that laced in all round the inside walls in winter and made the tent into a box of warm grey air with the stove going; and Abloh had won on the liner, after forty minutes, by the simple method of making Ward stand inside the tent with it laced in for ten minutes and then stand outside it in the loft's draught for ten. Ward had come back in at the end of the second ten minutes and said, "Keep the liner," and gone straight on to the rug.

Abloh lost on the rug.

He lost with dignity. He rolled it up, and tied it with a strip of linen, and put it on the shelf by the window, and said, "It will wait," in a voice that made clear it would wait a very long time and remember everything; and went back to the machine, where forty tents' worth of fly lay folded in a stack as tall as he was, waiting for its seams.

And at a quarter past four the school came out.

Emeric heard them on the stair. He had forgotten it was Thursday. He heard twenty pairs of boots on the loft stair at once, and the door burst open, and the top class of Sela's school came into the sail loft, which they all knew, because they had all learned to sew in it; and stopped, and looked at the machine, and at Abloh at it; and Nell Tanner came forward out of the front of them with her chin up and said:

"I need a tail."

Abloh looked at her over the machine.

"A fish's tail," said Nell. "For Hollow Night. From the waist. And Davey needs wings, and Peg needs ears, the long kind, and Tomas wants to be a slime and he says he wants to be green..."

"I am making tents," said Abloh.

"After," said Nell.

"I want wings," said somebody at the back. "Big ones. That flap."

"I want the ears!"

"I want to be the one that drinks blood."

"I want to be a wolf."

"Can I have a tail and ears?"

"You can't have both, it's not in the story..."

"It could be in the story..."

They came round the machine, all of them, round Abloh, round the stack of black fly, in a wave; children he had taught to thread a needle and children he had never seen, a boy from the freed men's families with a cloak he wanted made into wings, a girl from Milford with her mother's old green petticoat for the slime; talking, all of them, all at once, holding out cloth and string and paper and a broken umbrella, while Ward stood in the tent's door with his arms folded and his face absolutely still and did not help in any way at all.

Maren came up the stair in the middle of it with Abloh's tea on a tray, as she did every day at half past four; and stopped in the door with the tray, and looked at the sail loft full of children, and at Abloh in the middle of them at his machine with a fish's tail being explained to him on one side and a set of wings on the other.

Abloh looked at her over the children's heads.

"I am a sailmaker," said Abloh.

Maren looked at him a while longer, and then at the children, and then put the tray down on the window ledge, very carefully, next to the rolled-up rug.

"Then make them sails," she said, and went back down the stair.



He went down to the gallery under the hill that night and found the lines he did not understand.

He had found a great deal by then. Stand, step, turn, brace, strike. Bend and straighten and hold. He had found the place in the knots where each joint knew how far it could go before it broke itself; the place where the balance lived, which was not in one place but in every joint at once, each one feeling its own load and leaning against it; the way the shoulders and hips were paired against each other, opposed, so that the strike in the right arm was balanced by something in the left hip he would never have thought of. He had the shape of most of the body's grammar. He could have sat with Bram over a drawing and told him why the golem walked.

And he had found, scattered through it, here and there, a few lines that did not fit.

They were short. A knot of a dozen marks, or twenty, sitting in the middle of a run of the body's code like a stone in a stream. Each one appeared once, and nowhere else. They did not repeat. They did not touch a rail. They did not reach any joint. Nothing he had found pointed to them, and nothing came out of them; they sat there in the writing, fine and dense and finished, connected to nothing that he could see.

He spent two hours on them. He counted their marks. He looked for the same marks anywhere else in the stone and did not find them. He went down to the palace, to the book he had read at nineteen, and found the page on the parts of an old inscription that were not text: the borders, the scrollwork, the little flourishes a carver put in to fill a space or show off his hand, that a decipherer learned to see and set aside, because if you tried to read the scrollwork you would never read anything.

He came back up with the headache coming, and looked at the lines again.

Ornament, he thought. A maker's flourish. A long-lived maker with all the time in the world, writing a stone so fine that nobody would ever read it, putting a little knot of beauty in the middle of a run here and there because he could.

He had a word for them now, and it made them easier to set aside. He marked where each one sat, in his head, so that he could step over it next time; and went on to the next run of the body's code, which was the left ankle, and which he understood.



The notice went to the printer on the Saturday.

He wrote it at his table in the study, late, with the lamp, on a half-sheet: a notice for the walls and the market square and the Almanac's next sheet, to go up on the morning of the thirty-first. It said that on the last night of October, from dusk, Kaldo would keep Hollow Night: a night for stories; that any child who wished might go in costume, with a lantern, to any door that showed a lit lantern in its window, and say sweet or story, and be answered with one or the other; that doors that did not wish to be knocked on should show no light. That the school would teach the stories beforehand. That turnips for lanterns could be had at cost at the Milford stall in the market from the Monday.

And at the bottom, before his name, one more line. He wrote it without thinking, the way he had written the reason in the council's letter; and read it back; and this time he did not strike it out.

For the peoples we lost.

He sat and looked at it for a while in the lamplight. Then he blotted it, and folded it, and sent it down to the printing works in the morning by the boy, with the rest of the week's notices, for the presses to run at dawn on the thirty-first, after Ferra had seen the proof, as she saw everything that went out under Kaldo's name; and forgot, long before the thirty-first, that he had written it at all.
