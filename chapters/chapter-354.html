Chapter 354 - Her Hollow Night

The proof of the notice came up from the press on the tenth of October, still damp at the folds, and Ferra laid it on the records hall table between the coffee pot and Bram's elbow and said the printer wanted it back by Tuesday.

It was the same notice as every year. The last night of October, from dusk. A night for stories. Any child who wished might go in costume, with a lantern, to any door that showed a lit lantern in its window, and say sweet or story, and be answered with one or the other. Doors that did not wish to be knocked on should show no light. Ferra had changed the year at the top and nothing else, because for four years nothing else had needed changing.

"Cancel it," said Ward.

Nobody had asked him. He was by the door with his arms folded, where he always was, and he said it the way he said most things, as though it had been settled some while ago and he was only letting them know.

"Cancel what?" said Bram. He had come in late with grey glue to the second knuckle on both hands and a pale strand of something caught in his beard. He had been in the Motion Shop with Tess since seven, on the hand. It had four fingers that closed now, and one that didn't, and he had not been fit to speak to about the one that didn't since Thursday.

"Hollow Night."

Bram looked at the proof. Then at Ward. "I've got a turnip on my bench," he said. "Half done. It's the best one I've ever done. It's got a jaw that opens."

"Then finish it and eat it."

"You don't eat them. That's the whole..." Bram stopped, and picked the strand out of his beard, and looked at it, and put it in his apron pocket. "Why?"

Ward didn't answer at once. Emeric thought he was choosing between two things to say, and that he had chosen the shorter.

"Two stones," said Ward.

That was all. Nobody at the table needed the rest of it. They had all heard it in the dome in February, or from Emeric on the Cradle's deck: the first stone, where the water went thin and the figures with legs didn't come back; and the second, the older one, worn along the top by hands, with the fires on the shore and the shapes in the shallows that lay the way nothing living lies.

"Children in her people's skins," said Ward. "Going door to door for sugar. Every year. With her a day's sail off." He unfolded his arms and folded them the other way. "I'd not like it. If it were mine."

Saban was in the window seat with his hat on his knee. He turned it a quarter turn, which meant he was going to disagree with somebody and was sorry about it.

"If you cancel it," he said, "people ask why. That's the first thing. Clerks in the capital have been asking what Kaldo's mask night is for since year twelve. The Church's still do. Grayrock keeps it. Milford keeps it. Half the river towns have started. And this year the king who made it stops it, three weeks out, with no reason printed." He turned the hat again. "I'd want to know what changed his mind. So would anybody whose job is wanting to know."

"Then print a reason," said Ward.

"Which one?"

Ward said nothing to that.

Armand had his cane across his knees and his chair tipped back on two legs against the wall, which Ferra had asked him three times not to do on the hall's floor and had given up asking. "I'll tell you what I know about cancelling things," he said. "Every hundred years or so somebody cancels a thing out of kindness. And a hundred years after that, nobody can tell you why there's a hole in the calendar, and the hole's the only sign anything was ever there." He let the chair down. "The tower had a great many holes. I was taught in most of them."

"So keep it," said Bram.

"I didn't say that either."

Ferra had her pen in her hand and hadn't written anything. "It isn't ours to cancel," she said. "That's where I keep getting to. And it isn't ours to keep, either. Not now. It was ours when it was a story night." She looked at the proof, at the line she had struck from it four years ago, which wasn't there, because she had struck it. "I'm not sure whose it is."

Imani had said nothing. She was eating the end of Bram's bread, which he had brought for himself and forgotten, and she ate the last of it and brushed her fingers off over the plate.

"Hers," she said. "Obviously. I don't know why we're doing this in here."

Emeric had been sitting with his hands round the cup and not drinking from it.

He had been turning it over since February, on and off, and had not got it to come out. Cancel the night, and it was a hole in the calendar, as Armand said; and a hole was what their own ancestors had left. That was how a whole people had gone out of the kingdoms so completely that nobody at this table had heard of them until a cave and a dead man's book. Somebody had decided, once, not to talk about it. Keep the night as it was, and every October the town's children put on her people's shape for sweets, and called it a story, and that was all it would ever be.

Where he came from it had been the night of the dead first, and the sweets had come a long way after. He had brought the sweets over. He had left the dead behind, because he hadn't known, then, that there were any.

"I'll ask her," he said.

"Before the printer?" said Ferra.

"Before anything. Tell him he'll have it when he has it."

"He'll like that."

"And if she says burn the lot?" said Bram.

"Then we'll have a great many turnips."

Bram grunted, and took his steel rule out of his apron pocket, and looked at it, and put it back.



He went across on the eleventh with the supply run, and found her on the Cradle's deck in the sun with Zale and a chart.

She walked to him. That still took him by surprise, after the months of the trough and the tank: the long, slightly too careful step, the way she put each foot down flat and let her weight come onto it, like somebody crossing ice who had decided not to be afraid of it. Her hair was dry. She had on a shirt of Wren's and a pair of the mill's canvas trousers rolled at the ankle, and her skin in the sunlight was the pale grey it went when she wasn't thinking about it, nearly no colour at all.

"King," she said.

She had picked that up in the summer from the cook, who used it the way other people said "you there", and nobody had been able to get it out of her since. Zale said it was the cook's fault and the cook's problem.

"Not today," said Emeric.

"Every day." She looked past him at the ladder, as if somebody else might come up it. "Ferra?"

"In Kaldo. Imani's coming on the next run."

She nodded, and the click went at the back of her throat, the one Zale said meant she was satisfied with an answer and would hold you to it.

Zale rolled the chart up. He did it slowly, and tied it, and put it under his arm, and didn't go.

"I'll want you for this," said Emeric. "If you'll stay. Some of it might not have words yet."

"Most of it won't," said Zale. "She's got about four hundred of ours. Good ones. Nobody taught her maybe; she doesn't hold with it." He sat down on the hatch coaming with the chart across his knees. "Go on."

So Emeric sat on the deck in the sun, a yard from her, and tried.

He told her there was a night in Kaldo, at the end of October. That he had made it, five years ago. That it had been a night for the children, with lanterns, and that they went from door to door in it, and...

She put her hand up. Flat, palm toward him, the way she stopped Imani at the valve.

"Your night," she said.

"Mine."

"About us?"

He thought about lying to her, the way a man thinks about stepping off a roof. "Partly," he said. "Yes."

She looked at him a long while. Then she looked at Zale and said something in her own language, short, with a stop in the middle of it, and Zale answered her in the same, worse, and she corrected one sound of his without looking at him, and he said it again her way.

"She says don't tell her," said Zale. "She says show."

Emeric held out his hand, open, and stopped it short, the way he always did. Then he touched his temple.

She shook her head. It was a thing she had learned from them, and she did it a little too far each way.

"Not there." She touched her own temple, then his, the air an inch from it. "In there, you choose. What I see. You open, you shut." She put two fingers under her own eyes. "This. In the place. I see it."

"In Kaldo."

"Kaldo."

He had been ready for a great many answers on the way over and that hadn't been one of them.

"It's two hours in the Hod, each way," he said, which was the least of it. "And it's a city. Thousands of people. All of them out in the lanes that night, every door open."

"Good," she said. "All of it. Not half."

Zale said nothing, but he looked at Emeric over her shoulder with an expression Emeric had seen on him once before, in a squall off the cane islands, when the right thing to do and the safe thing to do had turned out to be in opposite directions and he had already put the tiller over.



Imani came on the next run and was against it, and then for it, inside a quarter of an hour, which was quick for her.

"Armand," she said.

Armand had come across with her, which he did now whenever he could find a reason, and sometimes when he couldn't. He stood at the tank's end with his eyes shut and his cane hooked over his arm while the guest sat on the lip with her feet in the water and looked patient about it.

"Five," he said. "And most of a sixth. More than in September. She's nearly at her own."

"Nearly." Imani looked at Ferra's register, the last line, in Ferra's hand from Sunday. "And she's on her legs on this deck four hours a day already, in this air, which is as thin as Kaldo's. So it's not the air I'm afraid of." She closed the book. "It's out of the water. All night. She's never done more than five."

"Ask her," said Emeric.

Imani looked at him. Then she crouched down by the lip, level with the guest, and asked her, slowly, mostly in Kaldo and partly in Zale's words and partly with her hands, how long.

The guest held up both hands with the fingers spread, and then two more fingers.

"Twelve hours," said Imani. "She says twelve." She stood up. "Then twelve, and not thirteen. Out of the water at dusk and back in it before it's light. If her note drops under five on the way, Armand says so and we turn round, wherever we are. If she cramps, we turn round. If she so much as looks tired, I'm going to think about turning round." She looked at the guest. "Is that a yes?"

The click.

"That's a yes," said Zale.

Wren caught Emeric at the ladder on his way down to the run that evening. She had come off the mill shift with dust in her eyebrows and was carrying her boots in one hand, and she stopped two rungs above him so that, for once, she was looking down at him.

"Is she going to see the tails?"

"She's asked to see all of it."

Wren looked down at the bay a while. She was nineteen now, and she'd been on the island since the beaching, and she had worn Abloh's tail in the lane at fifteen and been carried in it the whole night because she couldn't walk a step.

"Tell her before she sees one," she said. "Don't let her just see one."

"I'll try."

"Don't try," said Wren. "Tell her," and she went on up the ladder with her boots.



Saban's route took a week to make and four lines to explain, and he explained it in two.

"She comes in the way I come in," he said, on the evening of the thirtieth, in the records hall with the lamps low. "Nobody sees me come in."

"Everybody's in the lanes," said Ferra. "That's the problem."

"Everybody's in the lanes looking at children," said Saban. "And everybody in the lanes is in a mask, or a sheet, or a hood, or has a child on their shoulders who's in one. There's one night a year in this town when a tall stranger in a cloak with her face in shadow is the least interesting thing on the street." He put his hat on. "I didn't pick the night. I'm only using it."

The cloak was mill cloth, dark grey, cut long, with a deep hood. Abloh made it without being told who it was for, and when it was done he folded it on his table and said to nobody in particular that he was a sailmaker, and that in all his years in this town nobody had once told him what anything was for.



Zale took the Hod across on the thirty-first with the light going, low over the water, and Emeric sat at the stones and watched the dials.

Before they left the Cradle, Tess's strip had come down from the probe on the morning radio: the wind at three heights above the island and the pressure falling slowly from the west. Zale read it in the wheelhouse door with his lips moving and then put the Hod at four hundred feet instead of the six he'd planned. He had taken to the probe the way he took to any new way of knowing what the sky was doing, which was with total suspicion for a fortnight in August and then complete reliance on it ever since. He didn't say that. He said, "Smoother down there," and he was right.

The guest stood at the forward glass the whole way with her hood down and her hands flat on the sill. The sea went by under them grey, then dark. Once she said a word to Zale, low, and pointed down at a pale streak on the water; and Zale looked, and said a word back, and then, in Kaldo, to nobody, "Aye. That's where it turns," and that was all either of them said for an hour. Imani sat on the bench with her watch open on her knee and her other hand round the guest's wrist when the guest would let her, which was about half the time.

"Five and most of a sixth," said Armand, from the back, every quarter hour. Then, halfway, because he was Armand: "Still. In case anybody had dozed off."

Ferra met them at the far end of the yard in the dark with Saban and a covered cart, and the lamps at that end had been out since dark, and nobody asked why.



Saban's way in went round the back of the lower ward by a lane Emeric had lived in Kaldo for fifteen years without walking, past the backs of the dyers' sheds and up three flights of steps between two houses so narrow that Bram, had he been there, would have had to turn sideways. Saban went ahead. He didn't look round. Every so often he stopped at a corner and stood there with his hands in his pockets like a man waiting for his wife, and then went on.

The guest walked between Ferra and Imani with the hood up. In the dark of the steps she had let her skin go down nearly to black, and in the hood there was nothing of her face to see at all.

Emeric came last.

He had the first sentence ready. He had had it ready since the Cradle: that the night had started as stories, that he had told the children about creatures out of a book, that they had chosen what to be, and that he had known when he did it that her people had once lived here, and... He'd got that far a dozen times on the Hod and lost the end of it every time.

They came out at the top of the steps into the lane above the lower ward, and it was full.

There were lanterns on every step, carved turnips in rows up both sides of the lane, each with a candle or a little light stone inside, all of them grinning. There were mothers in shawls and fathers with their collars up, and the cold coming up off the river, and children everywhere in knots, with bags, shrieking. Somebody was playing a fiddle behind a shut window, badly, and somebody else was telling him to stop. A pack of small boys in black sacks went by without a word, crestless, in a line, the way Finn had started it in year eleven; Demons of the Isle, and they were good at it now, silent at every step.

The guest stopped.

A man was coming down the lane toward them with a girl in his arms. She was six or seven. She had her arms round his neck and a bag of sweets in one fist and her head turned to see where they'd been, and from the waist down she was a tail: grey mill cloth sewn tight as a sleeve, painted all over with scales in something silver that caught the turnips' light, and at the end, flopping against her father's hip at every step, a fin cut out of stiffened paper.

Emeric opened his mouth.

The man went past them. The girl looked at the tall hooded woman between the other two women, without much interest, and then at the next door, and kicked her father in the ribs with the fin to make him go faster.

The guest turned her head and watched her all the way down the lane.

Under the hood her skin had gone dark again. Not the dark of the steps, which she had chosen. This came up from the throat, and Emeric could see the edge of it where the cloak opened at her collar, black and spreading, the way it had gone on the table with Imani's spreader in her back.

She said something in her own language, short, and he didn't have it.

Then she said it in his.

"Skin."

"It's cloth," said Ferra, very low. "It's only..."

"Skin." The guest didn't look at her. She was looking up the lane now, the whole of it, and he watched her find the rest. A dragon, three children under a sheet with a stick-and-paper head, going sideways into a doorway because the head wouldn't fit. Tall ones with long paper ears. Short broad ones with wooden hammers. A slime, a boy in a dyed oilcloth sack who had walked into a wall and was being steered by his sister. Elves. Half a dozen of them on one step, all sizes, dark and brown and freckled and pale, with rope-yarn locs to the waist bound in gold paper cuffs, exactly the way the king wore his, so that the whole step of them looked like his younger brothers with pointed ears.

Her eyes went from the step of elves to him. To his eyes. And away again, quickly.

"Hunter," she said. She was hunting for the rest of it, and he could see her not finding the words and going round them. "Hunter kills. Then he wears it." She made a motion across her own shoulders, a skin pulled on. "So everyone sees. He killed it. It is his."

"It's not..." Imani began, and stopped, because it was.

The guest turned round, all the way, and faced him.

"Your people kill mine." She put her foot down on the lane's stones, flat, hard. "Here. Take this. All this. Then you forget, so you don't have to be sorry." She pointed down the lane after the girl in the tail, without looking. "Now small ones wear us. For sugar."

Nobody said anything.

Ferra's hand came into his left hand and took hold of it. A breath after, Imani's came into his right. Neither of them looked at him. Neither of them said a word. They stood there in the lane on either side of him and held on, and let her say it.

He didn't defend it. There wasn't anything to say that wasn't an excuse, and he had thought about every one of them on the Hod and they were all excuses.

"I knew you'd lived here," he said. "I didn't know what we did. I guessed, and I made a children's night anyway."

She looked at him. She was waiting for the rest, for the reason, the way he'd watched people wait for it across a hundred tables. He didn't give her one. After a while she saw that he wasn't going to.

Down the lane the father had put the girl on a step in front of a lit door, and the door had opened, and the girl held up her bag and said, "Sweet or story."

The woman in the door was one of the bakehouse wives, floury to the elbow. She folded her arms. "Story," she said. "It's Hollow Night. Earn it."

So the girl told it, loud, to the whole lane, with her fin in the gutter. It was a mermaid who lived at the bottom of the sea and wanted, more than anything, to have legs, and to walk on the land with the people there; and she gave away her voice for them, and got them, and it hurt her every step she took; and then a lot more happened that the girl couldn't remember and skipped, and the mermaid married a prince. The end.

The guest listened to all of it.

Emeric had told that story at the school in year eleven, out of a book. Not any book anybody here would ever read. He had cut the knife out of it, and the sea-foam at the end, because there had been small children on the front bench, and now it had come all the way round the town for four years and back up this lane without its ending, in a little girl's mouth, to a woman who had legs because somebody had driven her into water that took her tail.

The baker's wife gave the girl two sweets, one for the story and one for the voice, and the father picked her up again.

"This is your story," said the guest.

"It's one I told them."

"It is wrong."

"Yes."

She looked at him a while longer. Then at Ferra's hand in his, and Imani's. Then she pulled the hood further forward, and turned, and walked on up the lane into the thick of it, between the turnips, and the three of them had to let go of each other to follow her.

She saw all of it. She didn't hurry and she didn't stop again. She watched the dragon come out of the doorway and lose its head. She stood at the edge of the Demons' line and watched them say nothing at a door and get two sweets each for it, and Emeric thought she understood that one better than anything else she'd seen. At the top of the lane, by the keep's postern, a very small elf with gold paper on her wrists walked straight into her knees, and looked up into the hood, and said "Sorry," and the guest said "Sorry," back, in exactly the same voice, and the elf ran.

At the postern Saban was waiting with the gate open.

"They're upstairs," he said. "All of them. Ward's at the door." He looked at the hood, and then at Emeric, and didn't ask. "Bram's brought his turnip."



The room was the long one on the keep's first floor, with a fire in it and the shutters closed, and the six were round it the way they sat at the records hall table, more or less, except that Armand had the chair nearest the fire and wasn't tipping it. Bram's turnip sat on the table in front of him, lit. It had a jaw that opened. It had teeth, two rows, cut so fine that the candlelight came through them in a fringe, and the eyes had lids.

The guest put her hood back.

Her skin was still dark to the jaw. She stood inside the door and looked at each of them in turn, the way she had in the tank on the night she shifted, and stopped on Ward, who was at the door because the door was Ward's, and who looked back at her and didn't move.

"I said cancel it," said Ward. "Before. In the hall." He seemed to think she ought to know. "I said children shouldn't wear your people."

She looked at him. Then she said, quite clearly, "No."

Ward's eyebrows went up a fraction. It was a great deal, for Ward.

"No?"

"You stop it." She looked round the room for the word and didn't find it, and made the shape of a lid closing with her two hands. "You hide us. Again. Your old ones did this. Hide. So nobody is sorry." She shook her head, too far each way. "Not again."

Armand let out a breath that might have been a laugh, and wasn't.

"Then keep it," said Bram, carefully, as if he were testing a weld.

"Not this." She pointed at the shutters, and the lane beyond them. "Not like this."

"Then what?" said Ferra.

That was where it stuck. The guest didn't know. Emeric could see that she didn't; she had come up the lane knowing what she wouldn't have, and not what she would. She stood there with the fire on one side of her face and said nothing.

"You can't just tell them," said Saban, from the window. He said it to the room, not to her. "That's the other trouble. You can't stand a town of this size in the square and tell it that its grandfathers' grandfathers did that, on that stone, and expect it to go home and sleep. You'll get a riot. Or worse, a church." He turned his hat. "And the Church already has a story about what the Light put down under the ground. They'd only have to stand up and say, you see, he admits it."

"Then don't tell them all at once," said Imani.

They looked at her. She had sat down on the end of the table by Bram's turnip and was looking at the guest, not at them.

"It's induction," she said.

"It's what?" said Bram.

"Induction. It's what I did to her." She nodded at the guest. "She came to us at two of Armand. Her own is six. If I'd given her six in a night, because six was right, because six was what she was owed, she'd have been bleeding out of her gums by the morning and dead by the week's end. So I gave her a notch. Then another. Every second day, then every third, because the top of a cup fills slowest and that's where you crack it." She tapped the table. "A town's a body. Feed it its whole history in one go, everything on that stone, all at once, and it bleeds. Riots. Your church. People who can't look at their own street. Give it a little every year, on the same night, so it knows the night's coming and what the night's for, and nobody bleeds at all. By the time the children who're in the lane tonight have children of their own, it's just what everybody knows."

"That's not what induction means," said Bram.

"It's what it means on my ward."

The guest had followed perhaps half of it. But she had followed notch, which she had watched Imani cut into a brass dial with a nail every morning for a year, and she had followed six, and she had followed bleeds. She looked at Imani a long time.

Then she said, "Yes," and that was the click.

"Then what goes in?" said Ferra. "Each year. A notch of what?"

The guest turned to Emeric, and held out her hand, and he thought she meant a read, and began to give her his. She took his wrist instead, the way she had in the cell, and pulled him down to sit, and sat down herself on the floor in front of the fire with her legs folded under her, and the cloak spread round her, and the six watched their king be put on the floor by his guest and none of them said a word about it.

"We have not..." She made a hole in the air with her hands, a pit, the earth taken out. She looked at him for it.

"Graves," he said.

"Graves. No graves. The sea keeps the body." She opened her hands and let something go out of them, downward, a long way. "The sea. Not us." Then she brought her hands up flat in front of her, a slab, and drew on it with one finger: marks, one under another, down and down. "The stone keeps the..." She stopped. She didn't have it. She drew a small figure on the slab, and then the figure doing something, swimming, carrying, she couldn't make it plain.

"The dead?" said Ferra.

"Not dead. What they do. What they..." She struck her own chest, lightly, twice. "What they are."

"Their story," said Emeric.

She said the word back to him. "Story." She said it the way she had said home in January, round a corner on its way out, and then she looked at Bram's turnip and the light coming through its teeth, and said it again, slower, and Emeric saw her understand the thing the girl had said at the door.

"Each year," she said. "One night. Old ones stand. At the stone. Old ones say: this one did this. This one, this. Small ones listen. Small ones touch." She put her palm flat on the floor, the way she had pressed it on Ferra's slate and on Imani's hand. "Then small ones put light."

Nobody spoke.

"No names," she added, to Saban, as though he had asked. "Never names. What they did."

Saban inclined his head to her, very slightly, the way he did to the old men in the market who had been there longer than he had.

Ferra had her pen out at last. She wasn't writing with it. She was holding it, the way she held it when a sum had come out and she didn't like the answer and was about to write it down anyway.

"That city had no graves," she said. "Under the Isle. Thousands of people. I did the sum in the boat, and there wasn't a grave in it. Not one." She was talking to Emeric, and then she wasn't. "I sat at the table and asked where they'd all gone. I've been asking since."

The guest looked at her. She didn't know what city. But she understood no graves, and thousands, and she nodded, once, as though that were only to be expected of anyone, anywhere, who did things properly.

"Then that's it," said Ferra. "We don't know their stories. Most of them. We don't know who they were. But we know there were thousands, and nobody in this town ever put a light down for one of them." She looked at the guest. "A stone. In the town. For the people with no graves. And on Hollow Night the children leave their turnips on it when they're done. Their lights."

The guest put her palm flat on the floor again.

"The costumes stay," said Ferra, working it now, and her pen had started to move. "She said so. But sweet or story means a true story. The child at the door tells what we know about the people they're dressed as. Not out of a book. What we know, even if it's only that they lived here and we don't any more." She wrote. "Which means they have to know it before they knock. So the school teaches it. The week before. Every year. A notch."

"What do we teach?" said Saban. "Exactly. Because I'll need to know which words."

"That there were other peoples here," said Armand. "Before us. That they were driven off this land by men. That nobody wrote down who." He looked at the fire. "And that we're sorry. That's the only part the tower never once taught, in all my years in it. It's very short."

"Not where we learned it," said Saban. "Not the cave. And not her."

"Not her," Emeric agreed.

Ward, at the door, said nothing for a long time. Then: "The stone. Who cuts it?"

"I'll cut it," said Bram, before anybody else could answer, and looked surprised at himself, and then didn't.

The guest was looking at Emeric. She had been looking at him while the rest of them worked it out between them, and he had felt it the whole time, the way he felt her note.

"Is it enough?" she said.

"No."

She waited.

"We can't give it back," he said. "Any of it. The land, the people. We can stop forgetting it. That's all this is." He looked at the shutters, and the lane beyond them. "What nobody learns gets done again."

"Learning is cheap," she said.

She didn't say it unkindly. She said it the way Ferra said a sum was short, as a thing that was simply true and ought to be known before anything else was built on it.

"It's the first payment," said Emeric. "Not the last."

She looked at him a long while from the floor, with the fire on her face. Then her eyes went past him, to the south wall of the room, as if she could see through it and through the town and all that dark water to the island and the Cradle and whatever it was that hadn't been built there yet.

"Boat," she said.

"Yes."

She nodded, once, and the click went in her throat.

Ferra wrote on the margin of the proof, the one she had kept back from the printer for her own file, under the line about stories, in her own small hand. Then she turned it round on the table so Emeric could read it. It was the line she had struck in year eleven, word for word. For the peoples we lost.

He read it out. The guest listened.

"Lost," she said. "You did not lose us." She lifted her hand off the floor and pointed down, through the boards and the stone, toward the sea. "You know where."

Ferra looked at the line. Then she crossed out the last word of it, neatly, with one stroke, and wrote two in its place, and turned it round again.

For the peoples who were here first.

"That's for next year's," she said. "This year's has gone." She put the pen down. "Next year the school does its week. And there's a stone."

Bram pushed his turnip across the table. It went the whole length of the table on its own flat bottom with the candle still lit in it, and stopped at the edge, above the guest's head, with its jaw open.

"Have that," he said. "It's the best one I've done."

The guest reached up and took it down in both hands and looked into it. The light came out through the fringe of its teeth onto her face, and she turned it round to see the lids on the eyes, and touched one with her finger to see if it moved. It did.

"Bram," she said. She had never said his name. Emeric hadn't known she had it.

"That's right," said Bram, and went red to the ears, and looked at Imani's plate for something to eat, and there wasn't anything.



Armand read her at the Hod's ladder at a quarter to two, and again over the water at three, and the third time, when the island was a black shape against a sky that hadn't started to go grey yet, he said, "Five, and a little less of the sixth. She's tired. She's fine," and sat down on the bench and shut his eyes and was asleep himself before Zale had the Hod's head round.

The guest sat on the floor at the forward glass with the turnip in her lap. The candle in it had burned down at about the time they crossed the coast. She didn't let anybody take it off her to put another in.

Once, halfway, she said something to Zale. He was at the wheel with his back to her and the dial light on his face, and he didn't turn round. He answered her in her own language, slowly, with the stop in the middle of it right, and then said a second thing that made her give the short sound through her nose that was a laugh. Emeric didn't ask what it had been. Zale didn't offer.

Imani had her watch open on her knee again.

"Eleven hours, forty," she said, when Fletcher's men had the tank's lid back and the guest was down the three iron rungs into the dosed water and under it, and had come up again at the far end with her arms on the lip and her hair streaming. Her skin was coming back to pale. "Twenty minutes to spare. I'll take twenty minutes."

The guest put Bram's turnip on the tank's lip, at the head end, where she could see it from the water, with the grinning side out.

Ferra had the register under her arm. She'd brought it across from Kaldo without thinking about it; she always did. She sat on the bench in the low light from the valve lamp and wrote the date and the hours out of the water, and the readings, and then, in the column for remarks, after a while, one line.

Hollow Night. She came. She chose.

"That's three words too many for you," said Imani, reading it over her shoulder.

"It's November so far," said Ferra. "I'm allowed."

"It's not November. It's the first. It's barely the first."

"Then I'm early," said Ferra, and shut the book, and leaned back against Emeric on the bench without asking, and Imani leaned on his other side, and the two of them were asleep against him within a few minutes, warm and heavy, while he sat upright between them and watched the guest in the tank watch the turnip, until the light started coming in at the cabin door, grey, off the bay.
