Chapter 206 - Burning Water

The oil came up the lower water in the last week of the autumn, in the hold of an Emberfell boat, under the sulphur.

Twelve casks. They were lamp oil, lawfully, in the marks of two Emberfell merchants whose names Saban had given the order to in the spring and who had bought it in turn off the Duvrenne coast in the ordinary way of their trade, and they were entered on the boat's manifest as lamp oil, and on Saban's receipt as lamp oil, and in Ferra's book, under the works, as lamp oil. Every one of those entries was true. Kaldo burned a great deal of lamp oil. It burned it in the long ward and the counting room and every window of the first quarter, and nobody on the quay looked twice at twelve more casks of it coming ashore in the rain.

The carters took ten of them to Saban's store on the quay, where they would sit under a tarpaulin and be lamp oil. They took two up the slope to the works, on a handcart, with Bram walking beside it.

The engine had been sitting on its bench in the fitting shop for six weeks with nothing to eat.

It was not large. Bram had built it in the summer between the pair and the Drop House, in the evenings, off a set of sheets Emeric had drawn in the winter on the night they first talked about hull four, and it stood on a timber bed about the length of a man lying down: a single iron cylinder laid on its side, a crank, a heavy flywheel as high as Bram's hip, and at the head of the cylinder, standing up out of it like the bulb of an onion, a round iron chamber the size of a man's two fists with nothing inside it at all. There was no boiler. There was no firebox. There was a small brass pump on the side, worked off the crank, and a pipe from the pump to a nozzle in the side of the empty iron bulb, and a tank the size of a bucket on a shelf above.

The last of the oil in Kaldo had gone into the black road on the first avenue, three hundred yards of it, laid hot, off Rowena's casks and the Duvrenne ones. Bram had finished the engine in the late summer and filled its tank with water to see that the pump pumped, and it had pumped, and then he had drained it and oiled its bearings and put a cloth over it and gone back to the dock. Every few days since, Emeric had seen him stop in the fitting shop door on his way past, and look at the cloth, and go on.

He took the cloth off now, and filled the tank from the first cask with a funnel, and did not spill any.

Emeric had asked that only the two of them be there. It was night; the works was quiet but for the Drop House's grumble down the slope and the night gang on the dock under the white lights. Tobe was at the door, because Tobe was the prince's man in the foundry and had been since there was a foundry, and he was at the door the way Ward was at the door, without being asked.

"You heat the bulb," Emeric said. He had said it in the winter too, and in the summer, and Bram had built the thing off the sheets without once asking why it should work, the way he built everything he did not yet understand; but he said it again now because it was the moment for it. "Just the bulb. From outside. Red. Then you turn her over, and the pump puts a spray of oil into the bulb, and the bulb's hot enough to light it without a flame, and it lights, and that pushes the piston, and she turns over on her own. And once she's running the bulb stays hot from the burning inside it, and you take the lamp away."

"And she keeps going."

"She should."

Bram looked at the empty iron bulb for a while. Then he took up the blowlamp he had made for it, a brass thing with its own small tank of oil and a pump worked by a thumb, and lit it at the forge, and brought it roaring and blue and set it on its bracket under the bulb, with its flame on the iron.

It took a quarter of an hour.

They stood and watched the iron change. It went dark, and then dull, and then, in the half light of the fitting shop, a faint red came into the underside of the bulb where the flame struck it, and spread, and deepened, until the whole lower half of it glowed the colour of the last coals in a grate. Bram put his hand out toward it, not near, and took it back.

"Now," Emeric said.

Bram took hold of the flywheel's rim in both hands and pulled it over.

It went round once, heavily, and nothing happened.

He pulled it over again, harder, putting his back into it, and the wheel went round, and at the top of its turn there was a sound inside the cylinder like somebody clearing his throat in the next room. A cough. The flywheel checked, and went on a little faster than Bram had sent it.

Bram let go.

The wheel came round again, slowing, and at the top of the turn it coughed again, louder, and this time it did not slow. It came round again and coughed, and again, faster, and then the coughs were coming on every turn, a flat hard thump inside the iron and a puff of thin blue smoke from the pipe that went up through the roof, thump and turn, thump and turn, and the flywheel was a blur, and the whole bench was shaking on its bolts, and the noise filled the fitting shop like a man beating a rug.

Emeric reached down and turned the blowlamp off and lifted it off its bracket and set it on the floor.

The engine went on running.

Bram did not say anything for a long time.

He stood in front of it with his arms folded. That was all he did. Emeric had seen him stand in front of a great many things with his arms folded: the foundry the day it was first lit, and the pair's first knock, and the dynamo turning in the sawmill shed, and the hull on its legs in the lower water with no rope on her. He knew all the ways Bram's arms folded. This was a different one.

Bram had lived beside fire his whole life. He had been at a forge since he was a boy, pumping the bellows, and a smith after, and a founder, and the master of a works with a converter in it that blew a sheaf of sparks a hundred feet into the night. He had built boilers. He had built the rail works engine and seen what a boiler did to Rudd, and written the watchers' rule himself afterward in his big square hand so that it would never happen twice. Every engine he had ever put his hand to had a fire under it and a boiler over the fire and a great deal of water between, and it ran on the steam, and the steam was the thing that could kill you, and you respected it the way you respected a horse that kicked.

This had no fire in it. It had no water. It had no boiler to burst. It had an iron onion the size of his two fists with a little heat in it, and a squirt of lamp oil, and it was turning a flywheel as high as his hip faster than any engine he had ever built.

He unfolded his arms, and put one hand flat on the timber bed, beside the bolts, and felt it shake.

"It burns inside," he said.

"It burns inside."

"Every time." He watched the flywheel. "It's a gun. It's a gun that doesn't throw the ball. It fires, and the ball comes back, and it fires again."

Emeric had not thought of it that way, and it was true, and he said so.

"How long did the boiler on the rail engine take," Bram said, not to him. "From cold. To steam enough to move." He answered himself. "An hour. Near enough. On a winter morning more." He looked at the blowlamp on the floor. "A quarter of an hour. And no boiler." And then, after a while, very quietly: "No boiler."

Emeric knew he was thinking about Rudd. He did not say anything about it. Tobe, at the door, had taken his cap off, and Emeric did not think Tobe knew he had done it.

"It'll want a watcher," Bram said at last, in his ordinary voice, the voice for coal counts. "All the same. It'll want a man who stops it."

"Every engine gets a watcher."

"Aye," said Bram. "I wrote that." And he stood and watched it run.

It was Ward's knight who came, a little before midnight. Declan, at a run.

He stopped in the fitting shop door, breathing, and looked at the engine, which was still running, and then at Emeric, and said: "The Crown's man. At the powder house."

The powder house stood at the far end of the works yard, on its own, behind its own wall, where the old dry store had been: a squat stone building with a copper-sheathed door and no iron in it anywhere a spark could come from, and beside it the long low sheds over the nitre beds, and beyond those, forty paces off across a strip of bare ground that nobody was allowed to cross with a lamp, the fitting shop. The powder house's wall was the fitting shop's neighbour. The engine's thump was going through it now, and out into the night, like a man beating a rug.

"Fenner?"

"Himself. And his clerk. And a lantern. He came up from the Grayrock road in a hired gig with the raising's weigh-sheets, Ward says, and he didn't go to the keep. He came straight to the works gate and showed the licence and asked to be let in to the powder house, and the gate let him, because it's in the licence." Declan got his breath. "Ward's with him. Ward says come."

Bram looked at the engine. Emeric saw him reach for the fuel cock, the small brass tap between the tank and the pump, which would stop her in three turns of the wheel. Emeric saw him stop. A thing that has been running for two hours and stops the moment a Crown officer comes through the next gate is a thing a Crown officer writes down.

"Leave her," Emeric said. "She's a pump engine. She's on trial."

Bram took his hand off the tap.

Oswin Fenner was standing in the powder house's outer room with a lantern held well away from him, as the licence required, and the powder master's book open on the stone table in front of him, and his clerk Lusk beside him with a second book, copying.

He was exactly as Emeric remembered him from the Grayrock raisings. A narrow grey man in a chancery coat, with a face like a closed door, who wrote things down in a black book and went away. He looked up when Emeric came in, and made the small correct bow the Crown's officers made to a holder of a barony, and went back to the book.

"Your Highness. I beg your pardon for the hour." He did not sound as if he begged anything. "The licence grants the Crown's officer entry to any works where powder is made or kept, at any hour, without notice. The chancery has asked me to exercise it once in the first year, so that it has been exercised." He turned a page. "I happened to be passing."

"You're very welcome, Master Fenner."

"Thank you."

He went through the powder master's book line by line. Saltpetre from the beds, by the pound, the month. Sulphur from the Emberfell boats, by the pound. Charcoal, the works' own. Powder made, by the pound, by the batch. Powder issued: to the quarry, for blasting, so much; to the armoury, so much, under the company's count; to the old diggings in the thaw, so much, for the copper drive. Powder in store, by the pound, in the magazine behind the copper door. It was all there, and all true, and all in the powder master's plain slow hand, which Ferra checked every month. Fenner read every line. Lusk copied.

The engine went on thumping through the wall.

It was very loud in the stone room. It was not a noise anybody in the realm had heard before, Emeric thought, standing with his hands behind his back: not a mill, not a forge hammer, not a steam engine's long breath. A hard flat thump, again and again and again, very fast, like a heart that had been running.

Fenner turned the last page, and closed the powder master's book, and looked at the wall.

"What is that, Your Highness?"

"An engine," Emeric said. "In the fitting shop. A small one. We're trying it for a pump."

That was true. Everything was true.

Fenner listened to it for a moment longer. He did not ask to see it. He did not ask what it burned. He took his black book out of his coat, the one Ferra said he had written a date in at the Grayrock pithead years ago with Hask's stick-drawing of the sealed shaft in the dust at his feet, and wrote something in it, one line, and put it away.

"The powder is in order," he said. "I shall say so." He made his bow again. "I shan't keep you from your bed, Your Highness. Or your engine."

He went out into the yard with Lusk behind him and the lantern held well away, and Ward walked him to the works gate, and his gig went down the road toward the Western Gate and the inn by the bridge, and nobody said anything until the sound of its wheels was gone.

Ferra read the clause aloud the next morning, at the long-room table.

She had the licence out of the box, the chancery's paper with its seal, that had come down in the summer in answer to Kaldo's application under the settlement. She read the clause in her reading-back voice, with her finger under it.

"'The Crown's officer may enter, at any hour and without notice, any works, store or magazine in which powder or any substance of like kind is made or kept, and may examine the books thereof.'" She lifted her finger. "Works in which powder is made or kept. The powder house. The magazine. The sheds over the beds, which make the saltpetre, which is a substance of like kind, if the chancery wants to say so, and it will." She laid the paper down. "Not the fitting shop. Nothing is made in the fitting shop but fittings. He has no right to go in there and he did not try to."

"He didn't need to," said Saban. "He heard it through the wall."

"He heard a noise. He was told it was a pump engine on trial, which it is." Ferra looked round the table. "The right doesn't reach it. That is the law."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Bram said, "I'll move her to the yard tonight," and nobody argued with him, and Ferra did not say anything else about the law, because everybody at the table knew as well as she did that the law was not the point. The point was a narrow grey man with a black book who had heard a noise through a wall that nobody in the realm had ever heard before, and had not asked about it, and had written one line.

They moved her that night, on a dray, down the slope and in at the yard gate, and set her up in the corner of Bram's fitting shop behind the yard wall, where the mages could not look and the Crown's right did not run and hull four's first cylinder was already chalked out on the floor.

Saban's letter from the lane came a week later, on the next Emberfell boat.

It was not from Saban. It was from one of the two Emberfell merchants whose marks were on the casks, a careful man who had done Kaldo's business down the sulphur lane for three years and liked it, written to Saban in the merchant's plain hand, and Saban brought it to the table and read it aloud himself.

The merchant had bought the oil off the Duvrenne coast, from the houses that had always sold it, in the ordinary way. And the Duvrenne houses had asked him, in the ordinary way, as merchants ask, who wanted so much lamp oil at the end of the season, when the price was highest. Twelve casks was not a great deal, the merchant wrote, to a Duvrenne house; they sold thousands. But it was a great deal for one buyer to take at once, off the lane, at the dearest time of the year, and pay for without haggling. And the merchant had told them, because it was no secret and because a merchant who will not say who his buyer is loses the next sale: the barony of Kaldo, in Randillia, down the coast.

They had written it down. The merchant had watched the Duvrenne clerk do it, in the house's book, beside the twelve casks and the price. I thought you would want to know, the merchant had written at the foot, because they did not ask about anybody else.

Saban put the letter down.

"Duvrenne's books," he said, "are Duvrenne's. Every oil house on that coast answers to the crown there, and the crown there is hers." He did not say whose. Nobody at the table needed him to.

Ferra did the arithmetic that evening, at Emeric's asking, because he did not want to be the one to do it.

The bench engine had run for two hours on its first night and six on its second, in the yard, with Bram and the new watcher standing over her, and Ferra had had Bram measure what went out of the tank, the way she had had him measure every new thing the works had ever burned. She worked from that. She worked from the sheets for hull four's engines, which Bram had drawn off Emeric's and which were not a bench engine's size but a ship's, big enough to drive three hundred feet of iron through the water and, one day, through the air.

"Your ship," she said, "at full way, burns what's in the store on the quay in a day. All ten casks." She turned the sheet round. "Say she runs a fortnight out and a fortnight back, not at full way, most of it, and lies idle the rest. That's a cask every few hours for the whole of it. Every voyage. That's a boat like the one that brought these, full, in her hold, under the sulphur, every voyage, bought off the Duvrenne coast at the dearest time of year, from houses that write down who buys." She put the pen down. "One ship. You said a class."

"I said a class."

"Then every ship in the class is a line in a Duvrenne ledger," Ferra said. "Every cask. For as long as the ships run. And every one of those lines is in a book in a house that answers to a crown that knows your name." She did not look at him. She was looking at the sum. "You said at the banquet that her crystal price meant a deposit they hadn't declared. You were right. It cost you a kingdom and it got you here. And now you want to buy oil from her for twenty years."

"I don't want to."

"I know you don't want to. That's what I'm writing down."

He sat late in the small room after, with the book open in front of him and the lamp low, and did not read it.

Down the slope, in the corner of the yard, he could just hear the engine, running, because Bram had wanted her run through the night to see what she did when she had been running a long time. A hard fast thump, very faint at this distance, under the grumble of the Drop House and the ring of the night gang's hammers on the dock. A gun that did not throw the ball.

He thought of Zale on the window seat in this room, with his finger under a line and the pouch of shard crystal on the table between them, and the stories that had come with the crystal, off Kesrathi sellers in tavern after tavern along the northern coasts, told the way sailors told things: stones off the sacrifice ground; men who went out and did not come back; a place nobody would name in front of strangers. And one more, that Zale had told at supper in the first week, for a laugh, with the wine going round, because it was the kind of story he collected. That on the sacrifice ground there was black water. That it lay in pools on the ground, thick, and stank, and would not run; and that if you put a torch to it, it burned.

Emeric had heard it at the time and thought: pitch. Rock oil. Seeps. There were such places in his old world, and men had lived beside them for thousands of years and caulked their boats with the black and never thought anything of it. He had thought it, and filed it, and gone on eating.

He thought it again now, with the engine running in the dark at the foot of the slope, on the last of twelve casks bought off Alina's coast, and did not file it.
