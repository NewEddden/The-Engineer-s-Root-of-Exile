Chapter 206 - One Night

Ferra came down into the cellar at nine with the forge book under her arm and a blanket over her shoulder and a basket in her other hand that Maren had packed, and did not say anything about the night before.

Neither did he. Neither had Ward, at breakfast, or Imani, or anybody else at the long table, for the whole of a long hot ordinary day; and the not saying it had sat on the table among the cups like a fourth person at the end of it, until Saban, who knew nothing, asked Ward cheerfully across the bread whether he had slept, and Ward said "No," and Imani choked on her tea and had to be hit on the back by Hale.

Ferra put the basket on the bench by the cellar door. She spread the blanket on the bench beside it, and sat on it, and opened the book on her knees at a clean page, and uncapped her pen, and looked at him across the cellar.

"Reason," she said.

He was standing at the cradle with his sleeves rolled. The forge lay round him in the dark of the old wine cellar the way it always did at night, the long rails and the lattice and the ring of gravity stones round the centring cage, violet under their panes, still; and in the feed cups along the side, the stock he had chosen that afternoon and weighed out on Bram's beam. A billet of the best steel the works had ever poured, from Grayrock ore by way of the converter, three feet long and as thick as his wrist. A coil of the finest wire Bram could draw. Brass.

"One barrel," he said. "Rifled. To carry a round true to three hundred yards, in powder or on air. For one man to use on one night, so that nobody dies who doesn't have to." He looked at the billet in its cup. "And so that nobody in that house ever knows where it came from."

Ferra wrote it down. Every word, in her small hand, with the date above it. Then she looked up.

"And after?"

"After, it goes in the armoury."

"And the next one?"

He did not answer at once. He stood at the cradle and looked at the steel in the cup, and thought about what she was asking, which was not the question she had asked; and Ferra waited, with her pen over the page, in the way she had waited for him at the long table for seven years, for the answer he would give if he thought about it rather than the one he wanted to give.

"I don't know," he said.

"Then I'll write that too." She wrote it. "Begin."



He had thought about the barrel for a week. He had drawn it in Sethe's counting room in Wayford, on the backs of Strade's pages, at night, when he could not sleep, until he could see it with his eyes shut: the bore straight as a sightline, polished like the inside of a bell, and down it, cut into the steel, six shallow grooves that turned as they went, each a sixth of a full turn in the barrel's length, so that a ball pushed down it would turn too, and go on turning in the air, and fly true, the way a thrown axe flies true if it spins and wobbles if it does not. Every soldier in his other life had known that. Nobody in this one did. They had their crossbows and their stones and their mages, and had never needed to.

The forge did not care what anybody needed. It took what he declared and ran what that declaration called for, and held it in its cage of gravity to a hair's breadth, and did not stop because it was late or he was tired. It only stopped when he did.

He went into the stone at a quarter past nine and declared the steel.

He would not remember most of the night, afterward. He remembered the beginning: the billet coming up out of its cup into the centring cage on the stones' lift, slowly, turning, and the heat coming into it from the lattice, dull red and then cherry and then the colour of a summer sky at noon, until the cellar was hot as an oven and Ferra had taken off her shawl and then her outer jacket and was sitting on the bench in her shirtsleeves with her hair stuck to her forehead, writing. He remembered the bore going in, the steel drawing away from the centre of itself under the forge's pull like a crowd from a fight, a line of nothing through the middle of the glowing bar; and the first groove. The first was the worst. He had cut a great many things in the forge since it was built, punches and dies and blades and races, and none of them had needed to turn as they went. He held the twist in his head as he went down the bore, a sixth of a turn in three feet, and the forge cut what he held; and when he came out at the far end and looked, it had wandered, a hair, two thirds of the way down, where his attention had gone for a breath to Ferra wiping her face on her sleeve.

He took it out and did it again. He did not let his attention go again.

By midnight he had the six grooves, and they were true. By one he had the outside of the barrel turned and the chamber cut at the breech end, where Bram's action would close on it. By two he was so far into the stone that he could feel the barrel the way he felt his own arm, every hair of the bore, as if it were a part of him that ached; and he could feel, at the edge of things, the other thing, the thing he did not look at directly, which was the bottom of the well.

He had a well. Everybody did, mage or knight or commoner; Armand said a mage was only a man with a deep one. His had been shallow at Kaldo's gate and was deep now, deeper than any mage's in the south, Armand thought, and deeper than Armand would say aloud; and he had stood at the edge of the bottom of it a few times in his life, and every time had come back from it shaking. He felt it now. Not near. Not yet. A coolness, far down, where the warm had used to be.

At three he finished the barrel.

At a quarter past three he reached for the crystal.



Ferra stopped writing.

He felt her stop, across the cellar: her note, the small close steady note he could have picked out of a crowd of a thousand, going still on the bench. She did not say anything. She sat with her pen over the page and watched him take the air crystal out of its box on the bench, a long clear stone the length of his finger, cut from inside in the spring to do one thing and nothing else, which was to give everything it held in one push when its pin was pulled.

It was empty. It was meant to be. Bram would not have a full one in the works with the pin in it and a boy with a broom in the same room, and Emeric had agreed with him at the long table, and the plan had been settled there: the gun's crystal and the breach stone would go north empty, side by side in one box, and the box would be opened at the Band's foot on the way. A quarter of an hour up there and an open stone took everything it could hold. Every dial on the ship had said so, the morning they came home from Duvrenne.

He closed his fist on it anyway.

"Emeric," said Ferra.

"I know."

"Do you?" Her voice was perfectly level. "You're grey. I've watched you in this cellar for a year and I've never seen you this colour. What are you doing?"

"A little in it. Only a little. In case." He could feel the mana starting to go out of him into the stone, thin, slow, the way water goes out of a jug when there is not much left in it, in a thin stream with gaps in it. "In case Fletcher can't get her up. In case-"

"In case the sky's moved?" Ferra turned back through the forge book, a long way back, past the spring, and found the page, and held the book up open across the cellar so that he could see the ruled column, though he could not have read it from there and she knew it. "Every stone on that ship came home full from the Band. I entered them, one line each, and every line says full. You stood behind me while I did it and read them off." She did not lower the book. "It goes up empty. It comes down full. That's yours, at the long table, in May. I wrote that down too."

He stood with the stone in his fist.

"Open your hand," said Ferra.

He opened it. The crystal lay in his palm, clear as water, with no more in it than a thumb's worth, and he looked at it a while, and put it back in its box beside the breach stone, and shut the lid; and then he sat down on the floor of the cellar with his back against the cradle's leg, because his legs had decided for him.

Ferra came across the hot cellar in her shirtsleeves with the book under her arm and sat down on the floor beside him, close, and put her hand flat on his back between the shoulders, and kept it there, and did not say anything for a long time.

"Write it," he said.

"I'm writing it." She had the book on her knees again, on the cellar floor, and her pen. She wrote for a long while. He did not look at what. Then she turned the page round on her knees so that he could read it, and held out the pen.

It was under the night's entry, under the reason and the I don't know. A line ruled across the page in her hand, and under the line, in her best clear hand, larger than she usually wrote, as she wrote the things in her book that were meant to be read by somebody who was not her:

No second barrel is to be made in this forge without this book open beside it, and a stated reason written above the run, in the Holder's hand, before the run begins. F.

And below that, a space. A line, ruled, empty, the width of a name.

He read it twice. Then he took the pen and signed under it, on the line, his whole name, all of it, the way he signed a judgment; and gave the pen back.

"It's not a veto," said Ferra, blotting it. "I've a veto. I'm saving it." She did not look at him. "It's a rule. You've signed it. It's yours now as much as mine." She closed the book. "You'll keep it."

"I'll keep it."

"I know you will," said Ferra. "That's why I didn't write it as a veto." And put her head down on his shoulder, there on the cellar floor, among the forge's warm rails, and went to sleep before he could answer.

He did not move. He sat on the floor with her head on his shoulder and the barrel cooling in the cradle above them, ticking, and the crystal empty in its box, where it was meant to be, and the bottom of his own well a long cool way down; and watched the vent ports go from black to grey as the dawn came in at the ejection shafts. It was the second night in a fortnight he had not slept with a woman's head on his shoulder. He found, sitting there, that he did not mind it at all, and that this was new.



Bram had the action ready at six.

He had been working on it in the works for nine days, from Emeric's drawings, with the door shut and a sheet over it when anybody came in: the breech, which opened on a lever to take a cartridge and closed on it like a fist, and the lock, and the stock, which was walnut from the old pew ends Doran had sold the works when the church got new ones, and the sights, a notched iron leaf at the back and a blade at the front. Bram did not like guns. He had said so, once, in May, at the long table, and then said nothing more about it for three months and made a beautiful one.

He fitted the barrel to it in the works at half past six with the doors shut, while Emeric sat on an upturned crate with a cup of Maren's tea in both hands that he could barely lift, and watched. It went in like a key into a lock. Bram closed the breech on the empty chamber and opened it again, three times, listening to it; and then held the whole gun out across the bench, without a word, the way he had once held out the first casting from the cupola.

It was heavier than it looked. Long, dark, plain, with the walnut stock still smelling of the church, and the brass of the action new and pale, and the barrel black from the forge's last pass with the grooves just visible down it when he held it up to the window and looked through, six fine spirals going away from his eye into the light.

"It's a good gun," said Bram, grudgingly. "I'll say that. It's a good gun." He wiped his hands. "I'd rather it wasn't."



The cartridges were seven.

They should have been ten. He had meant ten, and drawn ten, and Fennick, who had been told only that the prince wanted some powder done small and very exactly for a thing he would not describe, had made up what Emeric gave him into what there was, and there was enough for seven. A few ounces of saltpetre. Saban's two most silent carters had scraped it in July, on two nights, off the walls of the oldest workings at Grayrock, the dead galleries the miners had walled off a lifetime ago, where the white bloom grew on the stone in the dark like frost; and it had come to a few ounces, and most of it had gone bad in the boiling, and the beds at the tannery would not give a pinch for a year.

Seven little brass cases, Bram's, each one turned and drawn by hand at the bench in the evenings, as alike as he could make them and not quite alike enough, with the ball seated in the mouth of each. They lay in a row in a wooden tray on the bench like seven fingers.

"Three for the test," said Emeric.

"That leaves four," said Bram.

"Four's enough. I mean to fire one, on air. The powder's for if the air won't." He looked at the tray. "If the air won't, four's more than I'll get to fire."

They tested it at dawn in the old quarry below the brickfield, where Fennick blew his rock, with Ward and Bram and Fennick and nobody else; Fennick because it was his quarry and his powder and he had said that if he was not allowed to watch it go off he would blow up the culvert, which nobody thought was a joke. A board at a hundred yards against the quarry face, with a chalk circle on it the size of a man's chest.

The first cartridge went into the breech. The lever closed. Emeric lay on a sack on the quarry floor with the stock against his cheek, as his other life's soldiers had lain, and found the blade of the foresight in the notch of the leaf, and the circle at the top of the blade; and breathed out; and squeezed.

The noise was enormous. It came back off the quarry face like a slammed door the size of a barn, and a flock of rooks went up off the brickfield shouting, and a cloud of white smoke rolled out from the muzzle and hung in the still dawn air over the quarry floor, stinking of Fennick's trade. Fennick laughed out loud, the laugh that came before the bang except that this time it had come after, delighted, like a man at a fair.

Ward walked down to the board.

"In the circle," said Ward. "Left of the middle by two fingers." He put his thumb in the hole. "Through the board. And the quarry face behind it."

The second went in the circle, a finger right of the first.

The third threw a hand low. Under the circle, through the board at the height of a man's belt instead of his chest. Bram, at the board with Ward, looked at the hole, and at the other two, and then back up the quarry at the row of four cases left in the tray.

"They're not all the same," said Bram heavily. "The cases. I said they weren't. One's a hair longer in the neck and seats the ball a hair deeper." He did not look at Emeric. "That'll be that one. My fault."

"It's not a fault. It's seven cases drawn by hand in a week." Emeric got up off the sack. His knees nearly did not hold him; he had been lying on the cold stone with nothing in him and his body had noticed. "Powder will do that. Every case a little different, every charge a little different." An army in his other life had measured every one by machine; he did not say so. He looked at the board. "On air it'll be the same every shot. The crystal doesn't care about the case. It pushes what it pushes."

Ward looked at him from the board, a long way off down the quarry, with his thumb still in the third hole.

"If it is full," said Ward.

"It'll be full by Wayford."

Ward took his thumb out of the third hole and said nothing. He would believe it when he saw it, his face said, and not before.



Imani found him in the works yard at eight, on the crate again, with the gun across his knees under a sack, and stopped in front of him, and looked at his face.

She did not say anything. She put her bag down. She took his face in both hands and turned it to the light, and looked into each eye, slowly, the left and then the right, the way she had on the slip with the lamp; and this time he did not try to kiss her. He sat and let her look.

"You did the forge," she said. "All of it. In one night."

"Yes."

"And you'd have done the stone after it, if Ferra hadn't taken it off you." She let go of his face. Her own was very still. "Ferra telephoned at seven. She said she was going to bed and that I should find you before you did anything else stupid, and that she'd told you so, and that you'd agreed, and that you were lying." She sat down on the crate beside him, close, so that her shoulder was against his. "You're cold. In August. Your hands are cold."

"I'll be warm by tonight."

"You'll be warm by tonight because I'm going to make you sleep till four, in my room, with the shutters closed and Dray's wife sitting outside the door with her mending, and nobody's going to wake you for anything less than the keep falling down." She took his cold hand off the sack and held it in both of hers, in her lap, and rubbed it. "And when you wake up, you'll eat everything Maren gives you. Everything. I've told her. She's doing the beef." She did not look at him. "And then you'll go and get on that ship and go up where it's cold and let the sky fill your stone for you, and you with it, and you'll come down the other side of it to a yard with a gun across your back, and I'll stand on the slip and watch you go, because I'm not allowed to stop you, and I'm not going to try, because you're right and I know you're right and it's a stupid, stupid thing to be right about." Her hands were very tight on his. "I hate this," she said. "I want you to know I hate it. I'll not say it again."

"I know."

"Good." She lifted his hand and kissed the back of it, the cold knuckles, once, hard; and stood up, and pulled him up by it. "Bed. My room. Now. Alone." She looked at him. "And don't say work first. If you say work first I'll put you under with something from the green shelf and you'll wake up on Thursday."

He did not say work first.

She put him in her narrow bed past the linen press with the shutters closed, in his shirt, and drew the blanket up, and stood looking down at him in the brown dark with her arms folded; and then, after a while, bent and kissed him, slowly, on the mouth, the way she had on the slip, as if she were making sure of something.

"There," said Imani. "That's for when you wake up. So you don't have to come looking for it." She went to the door. "Sleep."

He slept until four, as she had said. He did not dream. When he woke the room smelled of her soap and the shutters had a line of hot gold round them, and Dray's wife was outside the door, as promised, with her mending, humming; and he lay for a while in Imani's narrow bed with his hands behind his head and the bottom of his well a little less far down than it had been, and thought about the yard.



The throwables went aboard at six, in a padded chest that Abloh had made for them, each in its own felt nest.

Two flash stones. Ambrose Lark had loaded them in the works the week before, one hand on each, with his eyes shut and his face screwed up as if he were holding a sneeze, and the air in the room had smelled afterward the way the air smells before a storm. A lightning stone, each, filled to bursting and told to let all of it go at once: the light and the bang both, Ambrose said, because a lightning burst was both, and there was no other way to make a bang without powder. He was very proud of them. He had made Emeric promise to tell him how loud.

Two gravity stones. Six ounces each, from Grayrock's own seam, cut from inside to drag everything within about ten yards straight down at four times its own weight, for a slow count of sixty, and then stop. Pinned and hurting, not killed. They had cost, Ferra said, a Platinum and five Gold for the pair, which was more than Hollin's purse and everything in it; she had said it without looking up, and entered it.

One breach stone. An air crystal, bigger than the gun's, cut to dump everything it held in one shove, enough to take a strongroom door off its hinges. And beside it, in a tin box of its own on the other side of the chest, as far from the stones as Abloh could put it, Fennick's powder charge for the same door: a fat canvas sausage with a fuse coiled on top, the mundane answer, in case the stone would not.

Every stone had the same thing on it. A pin, brass, through an eye; and under the pin, cut into the stone from inside, the release rune. Emeric showed it to Ward on the deck, once, with the chest open, because Ward would be the one throwing the flash: pull the pin, and the rune counts five, and the stone gives everything it holds in one breath, and is empty; and a stone drained to nothing goes to dust. Every throw a stone gone. A Platinum and five Gold, two throws, and grit in the yard.

"Expensive," said Ward.

"Cheaper than a man."

"Yes," said Ward, and closed the chest.

Tobe's smoke pots went in a crate of their own, a dozen of them, plain clay, with a fuse and a lid, no magic in them at all; Tobe had made them from the recipe for the cartridges under Tess's chair, and was quietly proud of it. Five masks hung on a hook by the hatch for the gate team, to be set down with Osric and Tobe at Declan's clearing on the way: leather, with a glass for the eyes and a can at the mouth, and in the can a little block like a candle that you lit, and that gave you a quarter of a glass of breathable air in a room full of smoke. Eighteen of the candles, in a tin, with the masks. Declan's men at Sethe's hall had practised all week in two spare ones Abloh had sent north by train, in the hall's cellar, with a smudge pot, and two of them had been sick.

And in a leather case of their own, on the deck beside the chest, three small round bucklers the size of a hand, of Abloh's hard leather, to strap on the outside of the forearm; and set in the middle of each, under a ring of brass, one of Zale's clear flakes.

Armand had drawn the sign for them. He had drawn it from memory, in his chair, on a sheet of the school's paper, slowly, the soldier's sign that he had seen cut on strain shields in the tower's armoury sixty years before; and Emeric had cut it into the face of each flake with the point of a graver, under a lens, very lightly, the way the army's armourers did, from outside, in the open, no delving in it. Press your mana into the sign and the flake would hold one blow, one arrow, one bolt, and then go to dust on your arm.

"Three," Ward had said, in the yard, looking at them. "One each."

"One each."

"And the other four?"

"The armoury. In their oilcloth." Emeric had closed the case. "We'll want them again."

Ward had looked at the case for a long breath.

"You will want more than four," Ward had said; and Emeric had not argued, because Ward was very seldom wrong about how many of anything a man would want in a fight.



The ship went at dusk, from the slip.

Ferra was on the slip with the book. Imani was on the slip with nothing, with both hands empty, as she had said. Bram was at the works door with his arms folded. Tess was beside him in her chair with her data book shut on her knees for once, and Osric, who was going north with the gate team and had his mask on its strap over his shoulder, stood by her chair, and did not look at the ship, and did not look at her either, and stood there anyway until Declan's whistle went on the deck.

Vantic went up the gangway ahead of all of them, in her grey, with her book under her arm. She was going as the House's witness for the strongroom: three of the five kept their money with the House, and the House would want to know, she had said at the long table, exactly what was burned in that house and what was not, and by whose hand. Ferra had agreed to it in two words. Ward had agreed to it in none, which from Ward was the same thing. She did not look back at the slip. She went aft to the saloon and sat down by the iron box with her book on her knees. Vesk, at the stone-keeper's station with his hands on the brass, nodded to her, and she nodded back.

Emeric came down the slip in the black suit with the gun in its case on his back and the buckler on his left arm, and stopped in front of the two women.

He did not say anything. Neither did they. Ferra looked at him once, all over, quickly, the way she looked down a column for the figure that was wrong, and found nothing she could change, and looked at the book.

"Seven cartridges," she said. "Three used. Four in your belt. One crystal and one breach, empty, to be opened at the Band's foot and not before." She did not look up. "Two flash, two gravity, one charge, twelve pots, five masks, eighteen candles, three shields." She closed the book. "Bring back what you don't use. I'll want to enter it."

"I'll bring it back."

"And bring yourself back," said Ferra, to the book, "or I'll enter that, and I'll never forgive you for making me."

Imani said nothing at all. She stepped forward and took his face in both hands, there on the slip, in front of the whole crew and Bram and Tess and Osric and the garrison man at the river tower; and looked into his eyes, the left and then the right, slowly; and nodded once, as if they had passed; and let go.

He went up the gangway. At the top he looked back.

They were standing together at the top of the slip in the dusk. Ferra with the book against her chest in both arms. Imani beside her, close, with her empty hands at her sides. And as he looked, without either of them looking at the other, Imani's hand came up and found Ferra's arm, at the elbow, and held it; and Ferra let it.

The ship lifted.
