Chapter 161 - What They're For

Arthur carried the chest out of the keep on the fourth of August, down the stair and across the gate yard to the cart, by himself, because nobody else was allowed to.

He had said so. Not to Emeric; to Ward, whose department he was in, and who had passed it on, at the long table, without expression: Arthur would like it understood that if the chest was going anywhere, he was carrying it. Ward had added, in the same voice, that he had told Arthur that a man in his department did not tell the holder of four baronies what was to be done with the holder's own property, and Arthur had said that it was not the holder's property, it was the holder's property that Arthur had found, at two coppers apiece, on his own knees in the river gravel with his feet blue, and that was different.

"Is it?" Ferra had asked.

"I'm told it is," said Ward.

So Arthur carried it. A sea chest of old oak bound in iron, from the castle's store, that Hale had found for them in the second autumn when the sack had become two sacks and then a basket and then a barrel; and in it, packed in damp sand to keep them, every water stone Arthur had picked out of the gravel below the bridge after every flood for four years. Three hundred and twelve. Ferra had them in the book, by the autumn, at two coppers apiece, six hundred and twenty-four coppers, under a heading she had written in the first year and never changed: Water stones (purpose unknown).

He was seventeen and as tall as Emeric, and the chest was heavy. He carried it down the keep stair one step at a time with his face red and his jaw set, and across the gate yard, and lifted it up onto the cart's tail without a word, and climbed up after it and sat on it.

"Where are they going?" he said.

"Milford," said Emeric. "Up on the bench. Above the bay."

Arthur looked at him with Sela's eyes. He had asked the other question four years ago, standing in the river with his trousers rolled to the thigh, and been told, and had decided that the prince was simple and too well brought up to say it. He did not ask it again. He sat on the chest with his hands on his knees all the way to the railhead and did not say anything, and Emeric did not say anything either, because he wanted to see Arthur's face when he saw.



The bench above the bay was dry. That was the other half of Milford's trouble, and Linus's map had shown it as plainly as it had shown the wet: below, on the bottoms, more water than the ground could hold, which the drains were taking away now in the tiles under the trenches; above, on the bench and the high flats, the good light soil that Milford's best wheat had always grown on, and not a drop. The river ran forty feet below the bench's lip, and every bucket that went up to the bench went up on a man's back or a mule's, and the bench's farms had dug wells forty feet down and found sand.

So the bench went dry in August. Every August. The wheat on it was always good in a wet year and half a crop in a dry one, and Milford had never once in its history had a summer when it could choose.

There were three pump houses on the bench's lip now, above the river, a quarter of a mile apart. Bram's men had built them in July: square stone sheds the size of a smithy, each with an iron pipe coming up into it from the river forty feet below, and another pipe going out of it across the bench to a cistern of brick on the high ground, and from the cistern, along the edges of the squared fields Linus had laid out on the bench, a row of iron standpipes with brass heads on them that turned and threw water in a circle, like a dog shaking itself.

Inside each pump house there were two pumps. One of them was a treadle, the kind Emeric had built at the mine in the first winter: a long beam on a pivot with a step at each end, and two men walking up and down it all day, and a piston in the pipe that went up and down with it. The other was a small steam engine on a boiler, Bram's, with its own coal heap outside the door. Both of them could lift water from the river to the cistern. The treadle could do it slowly, for as long as two men could walk. The engine could do it quickly, for as long as there was coal.

And in the floor of each pump house, between the two, where the pipe came up out of the river, there was a third thing, which was not built yet. An iron collar round the pipe, open, with a row of empty sockets in it, each the size of a thumbnail.

Arthur stood in the door of the first pump house and looked at the empty sockets.

He looked at them for some while. Then he looked down at the chest, which two of the levy had carried up from the cart, under protest, because it was not the keep stair and there were limits, and which stood now on the floor by the collar with its lid shut. He looked at Emeric.

"Water stones move water," said Arthur slowly.

"Yes."

"That's all they do. Move it. That's why the coast men kept them in a ship's hold. To move the wet out of it." He looked at the pipe going down through the floor to the river. "Up?"

"Up," said Emeric. "Forty feet. All day. With no wheel and no man and no coal."



It had taken him a long time to see it. He told Arthur that, standing in the pump house, because it was true and Arthur was owed it.

He had bought the stones because he could not bear to see them go back into the river or down to the coast for a copper for ten, and for no better reason. He had put them in the castle's store, in a sea chest in damp sand, and every autumn he had bought more, and every autumn Ferra had written them in the book under the same heading, and every autumn he had looked at the heading and not known. He had thought of the wells, and the cistern, and the dry slope above the west farms, and none of it had fit. Kaldo's water had come in the end down an aqueduct, by gravity, and up a tower, by the beam pump, and out of taps; it had not needed the stones.

Then in June, at the drains, one of Armand's students had shown him how to move water.

She was a water mage, a carter's daughter from Wayford, one of the fifteen; she had come down to the bottoms with Paskin's party to see what the earth mages were doing and stayed to help them, because the trenches were full of water and water was hers. On the third afternoon she had stood on the bank above a flooded side trench where the levy could not dig, and put out her hand, and a blue glyph had come up off it, and the water in the trench had stood up out of it in a long brown rope, and gone up over the bank, and down the other side into the main trench, and on, a steady stream, up and over and down, as if it were running through a pipe nobody could see. A siphon, Emeric had thought, watching, except that a siphon cannot lift water over a bank higher than where the water starts, and this one was lifting it a man's height.

It had cost her. He had seen it cost her. After a quarter of an hour her face was grey and her hand was shaking, and the trench was empty, and she sat down in the sedge.

"How long could you hold that?" he had asked her.

"That?" She had looked at the empty trench. "Not much longer than I did. A quarter of an hour. Half, if I'd slept." She had shaken her hand out. "Why? It's empty."

"I was thinking about a stone," he had said. "That could do what you just did. A stone that moves water, the way you moved it. Up. All day. Without you." He had looked at the trench. "What would it need?"

And she had looked at him the way the water students in Armand's room looked at anybody who asked them a real question, which was as though nobody ever had; and said that a stone that moved water would need to be a water stone, obviously, a good one, and cut to push one way along a pipe and not any other, and set in a ring round the pipe so they all pushed together; and that nobody ever bothered to cut water stones to push water, because nobody had ever had a pipe that needed pushing that was worth the price of the stones. "The tower uses them for fountains," she had said. "In the capital. To make the water go up in a pretty shape for the bishop's garden. Six stones to a fountain." She had wiped her face. "You'd need a great many more than six to lift a river."

"Three hundred?" he had said.

She had laughed at him. Then she had seen his face.



He had cut them in the room in July. All three hundred and twelve, every one of Arthur's stones from the chest, a few each night; he had taken them into the room in his hand and cut each one from inside, the way he had cut the wall sensor's stones and the chair's, to do one fixed thing and nothing else: push water along a pipe, one way, up. They were small stones, river stones, weak and uncut and never charged by any tower, and one of them alone would have pushed a cup of water up a straw. But a hundred of them in a ring round a pipe, all pushing the same way at once, could push a river.

They went into the collars now. Arthur set them.

Emeric had not meant him to. He had meant to set them himself, with Vesk to charge them; but Arthur had opened the chest, and taken out the first stone from its sand, and looked at it in his palm, a small blue-grey stone faintly clouded like river ice, with the water standing on it in beads, the same as the morning on the gravel; and then he had looked at the collar with its empty sockets, and at Emeric, and Emeric had nodded, and Arthur had knelt by the pipe and put it in.

He set every one. A hundred and four in each of the three collars, which Emeric had worked out in the room to the stone, and every one pressed into its socket and turned until it seated and its cut faced the right way along the pipe, which Arthur could not see and Emeric had marked on each stone with a scratch of chalk. It took him all the afternoon. He did it the way he had picked them out of the gravel, one at a time, with his head down, without hurrying, and did not let anybody help.

When the last one was in, in the third pump house, at six in the evening, Vesk charged them. He stood by the collar with his hands on the iron and his eyes shut, the way he charged the chair's cells every morning, steady as a tap running; and the stones in the collar woke under his hands, one after another round the ring, a faint blue glow in the iron's shadow, and the pipe in the floor began to hum.

Water came up it.

Not a trickle. A rush, all at once, up the pipe from the river forty feet below and out of the pump house and across the bench in the pipe to the cistern on the high ground, and they heard it hit the bottom of the empty cistern from the pump house door, a long hollow roar like a barrel being filled from a bung. Nobody turned a treadle. The engine's fire was not lit. The stones in the collar glowed blue round the pipe and the water went up through them out of the river as if the river had decided it would rather be on the bench.

Arthur stood in the pump house door with his hands at his sides.

The cistern filled in an hour. Then the farm's man at the cistern turned the cock, and the water went out of it along the field's edge in the pipe, and up into the first standpipe, and the brass head on the standpipe began to turn, slowly, and then faster, and threw water out round it in a wide silver circle over the dry wheat in the low evening sun; and then the next, and the next, all along the field's edge, a row of turning silver wheels of water across the top of the Milford bench.

The farm's people came out of their houses to look. They stood at the field's edge in the sunset with the spray drifting over them and did not say anything at all. One old woman went out into the wheat in her apron and stood under the nearest standpipe with her face turned up into the water as it came round, and her eyes shut.

"That's what they're for," said Emeric.

Arthur did not look at him. He was watching the old woman in the wheat.

"You didn't know," he said. "On the gravel. You said you didn't know."

"I didn't."

"Four years." Arthur was quiet. "You bought three hundred stones at two coppers apiece for four years and didn't know what for."

"Yes."

Arthur thought about that, with the spray coming over the field's edge and settling on his hair.

"My mother says you're the most stubborn man she ever met," he said. "She says it like it's a bad thing." He wiped the water off his face. "I'll tell her about the stones."

The treadle and the engine stood in the pump house on either side of the collar, cold. They would stay cold until the stones went flat, or a dead zone opened over the bench, or Vesk was ill, or the war came; and then two men would get on the treadle, or somebody would light the engine's fire, and the water would still come up, slower, and the wheat would still be watered. Emeric had built all three in every house, and would build all three in every house after it, and nobody at the long table had asked him why since the second year.



The first steading went up in the bay that same month.

It was not much yet. A yard, walled, on the floor of the bay where Linus's map had put it, sheltered on three sides by the high ground; the footings of a granary, a long stone building on staddle stones to keep the rats out of the grain; the footings of a barn and a byre beside it, for the beasts that were going to be kept through the winter now, with the turnips and the hay. And a siding. A single line of rails coming in off the Milford line where it would pass the mouth of the bay next spring, curving into the yard and stopping by the granary's door, laid by Odo's men to Odo's gauge, so that when the grain came out of the granary it would go straight into a wagon and away, and never onto a cart.

There would be four more, along the bay's floor, by the next harvest. Emeric had said so at the long table, and Ferra had priced it, and Paxton had argued it down by a fifth at his own table over two evenings, and it had gone through. Four more, and the fifth one, the one at the head of the bay, furthest from the line and highest and driest, would be different. Its granary would be filled in a good year, and locked, and not sold from. Not in a good year, and not in an ordinary one. Only in a bad one.

He had said that at the long table too, the reserve granary, one in each barony, and it had not gone through. Not yet. Ferra wanted to know how much grain a barony locked up and did not sell was costing it every year it was not needed, and what the rats took, and the damp, and how often a bad year came. Imani wanted to know who would decide when a year was bad enough, and who would decide who ate first when it was. Saban wanted to know what the grain merchants on the coast would do when they heard that four baronies were keeping a year's grain in a cellar where they could not buy it. They were all good questions. He had answers to none of them that he liked.

"Next year," Ferra had said, and written it down. "When you've got answers."

He walked round the footings of the fifth steading's granary at the head of the bay in the August dusk, with the standpipes turning on the bench above him and their spray catching the last of the light, and thought about the answers.



Ward came at him in the keep yard on the night of the twelfth, and he lost, and then he did not.

It was the first time since the mounting block. Three months. He had asked Ward twice in that time and Ward had said no twice, without explanation, and he had understood at last that Ward was waiting for his knee, and for something else, and had stopped asking. On the twelfth Ward came up the keep stair a little before midnight and stood in Emeric's door with the practice blades under his arm and said: "Yard."

So the yard. The gate shut, the lamp on the mounting block, the two cracked cobbles from April by the stable, which Hale had not had mended because Ward had told him not to.

"Same," said Ward. "Everything. Nothing given."

"Same," said Emeric.

Ward came at him.

He did not open everything. That was the first thing, and he had thought about it for three months: on the bench at Milford, and in the pump houses, and on the Milford line in the rain, and in bed. A man who tries to see everything sees one thing very well and walks into a stone. So he did not hold the sense open wide and the honey open under it and the push in his legs, all at once, all the time, as hard as he could. He held the sense open thin, the way he held it on the wall at night, a breath on a cold morning, enough to feel where Ward was and where the yard was and where the block was. Only that. And he waited.

Ward's note leaned.

He felt it lean, early, the way Osric's leaned before his feet went; Ward's leaned less than Osric's and less often, because Ward thought about going a great deal less often than Osric did, and when it leaned, Ward went. And on the lean, only on the lean, Emeric reached for the honey: the slow time of the room, the long moment in which he could think three thoughts. He took one breath of it. In that breath he saw Ward's feet, and Ward's hips, and the line of Ward's blade before it moved, coming high, from the left; and he let the honey go, and the push went into his body just before the blade arrived and not one breath sooner, and he turned the blade with his own, and Ward's went past.

It cost him almost nothing. A breath of the room and a breath of the push. He was standing in the yard after it with his sense open thin and his head clear, and he could have done it again.

He did it again. And again. And on the fourth, Ward feinted, high, and the note leaned high with it, a good lean, a true-feeling lean, and Emeric took his breath of honey on it and saw the high line and readied for it, and Ward's blade was not on the high line at all. It came in low, under, and took him across the thigh with the flat, hard enough to sting through his breeches.

"Notes lie too," said Ward. "Less than eyes. Not never." He stepped back. "Again."

They went on. Emeric lost the fifth exchange and the sixth. He lost the seventh because he took the honey too early, on a lean that went nowhere, and had nothing left in the breath when Ward came on the next one. He lost the eighth because he took it too late. But he was losing them differently; he could feel it. He was not walking into stones. He was losing to Ward, not to himself.

Then he tried the Turn.

Ward had shown him the knight's way of it in April, slow, with a hand on his back: don't meet the blow, give it its road, turn with it, come out where it isn't. He had tried it since in the stable yard every night against nothing, a quarter turn on the cobbles, and found it as hard as Ward had said; a man's whole body wants to meet a blow coming at it, and turning away from a blade feels, every time, like falling. And he had thought about it in bed for three months, the way he had thought about the Light Step, and somewhere in the Milford rain he had seen why it was hard for him and would not be hard for him.

A man turns slowly because he is heavy on his feet. His weight pins him to the ground through his boots, and to turn he has to fight his own weight, and the blow comes faster than he can win the fight. Ward turned as well as he did because thirty years had taught his feet to do it lightly, a dancer's way. But a man who could take his weight off his feet at the instant the blow touched his blade would not have to fight anything. The blow would turn him itself. It would carry him round, light, like a door on a good hinge, all its own weight going past him; and when he came round he could put his weight back, all of it, at once, heavy, into his feet and through his own blade, and the counter would land with everything behind it.

Light into the turn. Heavy out of it.

The first time he tried it he went too light, too soon, and Ward's cut, which was a hard one, took him round not a quarter turn but most of a whole one, so that he came out of it with his back to Ward and his blade pointing at the stable door, and Ward, behind him, laid the flat of his practice blade very gently on the back of Emeric's neck.

"Where were you going?" said Ward.

The second time he went light too late, after the blow had already met his blade square, and it was only a block, and a bad one, and his wrist rang with it for a quarter of an hour.

The third time Ward came in from the right, a long hard falling cut, and Emeric felt the lean of it a breath early, and took his one breath of honey, and saw the line; and as Ward's blade touched his, he took his weight off. Not all of it. Most. The little violet flicker came up off his heels. And Ward's cut, with all of Ward's weight in it, took his blade and him with it, round, a quarter turn and a little more, light, as easy as a door on a hinge, so that Ward's blade went past where his body had been, sliding down his; and as he came round he put his weight back. All of it. Through his boots into the cobbles, heavy, so that the cobbles under his front foot ground against each other, and up through his hips and into his arm, and his blade came round with the turn on the other side of Ward's and laid itself against Ward's ribs, not hard, with a sound like a hand slapping a table.

They stood there.

Ward did not move. He stood with his blade down along Emeric's, still, and Emeric's point against his side, and looked down at it. Then he looked at Emeric's feet, and at the cobbles under them, and at the faint violet that was going out at Emeric's heels in the lamplight.

It was the first exchange Emeric had won against Ward in six years and four months. He had not counted. He knew anyway.

Ward stepped back. He put his practice blade under his arm.

"Light in, heavy out," he said. He was quiet. "That's not the old school."

"No."

"That's the old school with something else on top." He looked at the cracked cobbles, and at the new scrape on the ones Emeric had just stood on. "I couldn't teach you that. Nobody could. There's nobody alive who's got both halves to teach it with." He turned toward the arch. "One in eight," he said. "Do it one in two, and then we'll talk."

He went out under the arch. He did not say well done, and he did not need to; Emeric felt it go out of the yard with him, in the note, the slow even wall of it, with something in it that had not been there in six years of dawns, a warmth, very small, very carefully kept down, like a man not smiling in church.
