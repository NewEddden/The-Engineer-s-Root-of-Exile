Chapter 161 - Grey Robes

Tessaly Rook had held the north wall at Carrow Ford for eleven years, and on the morning she left it she walked the whole length of it once with her hand on the stone, the way she had walked it on the morning she came.

It was a good wall. She had raised the last third of it herself, the year of the flood, when the river took the old bank and half the town's cellars with it, and she had raised it out of the river's own gravel and clay, a hundred and forty yards in nine days, and set it hard, and it had not moved since. The lord's masons had faced it in dressed stone afterwards and put his arms over the gate, and everybody in Carrow Ford called it the lord's wall. It was hers. She knew every yard of it the way she knew the backs of her hands.

The lord gave her a dinner. He was sorry to see her go and said so, twice, and asked whether it was the pay, and when she said it was not he looked relieved and then puzzled, and asked where she was going, and when she said south he looked more puzzled still. Then he gave her a letter of character in his own hand and a purse of five Gold, which was more than he owed her, and that was all. Nobody held a mage. There was no law about it because there had never needed to be one.

She did not tell him about the letter. She did not tell anybody.

It was in her coat now, as she rode south in the carrier's cart, the way it had been in her coat since the spring. She had read it perhaps forty times. It was short, in a hand she had known for twenty years and had not seen for ten, and the hand was worse than she remembered it, shakier, as if it had been written on a bad day with the paper braced against something. Most of it said nothing much. It said he was in the south, and well, and old. It said there was work: walls, on a scale nobody had asked of an earth mage in her lifetime. It said it would pay, and pay well, and be honestly done.

And one sentence in the middle of it said, There is something here I cannot write in a letter.

She had known Armand for twenty years. She had been his pupil for three winters at the tower when she was a girl of twenty with more strength than sense, and he had taught her more in those three winters than the rest of them together. In all that time she had never once known him write a sentence he did not mean. He did not decorate. He did not hint. If he said he could not write a thing, it was because he could not, and if he said it was there, it was there.

So she had come.

There were thirty-eight of them by the time the carts reached the Kaldo road.

They had come from everywhere. That was the first thing she understood, on the road south from the capital where most of them had met, in the inns at night, round the common tables. They were not the capital's men. There were four from the capital and no more. The rest were from crown posts and lords' towns and river forts all over the kingdom: the north, like her, and the western march, and the coast, and two from the salt towns in the east that she had never heard of. Most were earth mages. There were water mages too, and a fire mage from a glassworks, and one very old air mage who talked about weather the whole way and had been Armand's pupil fifty years ago, before Armand had grey in his beard.

Every one of them had a letter. None of them showed theirs. They sat at the common tables and talked about pay and roads and where they had been posted, and nobody said the sentence out loud, and everybody knew everybody else had it.

She was neither the youngest nor the most loyal. The youngest was a boy of twenty-three from a river fort who had never been Armand's pupil at all, only assessed by him once at fifteen, and who had come because the letter was the only one anybody had ever written to him. The most loyal was a thin sour man of sixty called Paskin who had been Armand's pupil for five years and his assistant for ten, and who spoke of him as the grey robe, always, in a voice that dared anybody to call him anything else.

Tessaly had come because of the sentence. She thought that most of them had.

Bastian Farrant rode in her cart.

He had been at the tower with her, the same three winters, a big fair loud boy from a vintner's family who had been the best water mage of their year and had known it. He was forty-two now and still big and still fair and still loud, and still the best water mage she knew, and he had been posted at the capital's great cisterns for fifteen years, keeping the city's water sweet. He had a wife in the capital who had not come, and two sons at the tower's school, and he had left all of them for the summer to find out what the grey robe meant, and said so, cheerfully, to anybody who asked.

"If it's nothing," he said, on the third night, "I'll have had a holiday and seen the south. If it's something, I'll send for them."

"And if it's something you don't want them near?"

He grinned at her. He had always grinned when she said the careful thing. "Then I'll have seen it," he said, "and they won't have."

Kaldo came up on them on a hot afternoon in the last week of summer, and it was not what any of them had expected.

They had expected a small poor town. That was what they had been told, those of them who had been told anything: a barony at the far end of the south that had been half emptied by a war and then by the plague, and given to a disgraced prince, and was still half empty. They came down off the last ridge on the road out of the woods and there was a lake below them, blue and very large, and an island in its outflow with a wall round it and a keep and a church tower. That was what they had expected.

And there was a great deal more that they had not.

On the near bank below the island, across the bridge, there was a works. A big one, bigger than anything in any town she had been posted to, a sprawl of sheds and yards and heaps and stacks along the river, with smoke going up out of three chimneys, and a noise coming off it she could hear from the ridge, a long regular heavy thudding like a giant's heart. There were buildings on the flat below the mill that were new stone. There were long drying sheds. There was a stink on the air as they came down the hill, a thick yellow reek of rotten eggs and hot tar that made the old air mage stop talking about weather for the first time in three weeks.

"What in the name of the Light is that," said Paskin.

Nobody in the carts knew.

And all round the works, on the bank, up against the slag heaps and under the lee of the sheds, there were people living. Hundreds of them. In lean-tos of hurdle and sailcloth, in carts with the wheels off, in huts built of turf and old timber. Washing on lines. Children. Cook fires. A town outside the town, with no wall round it, that had grown up against the works because the works was warm.

The island was full.

She saw that the moment the carts went over the bridge and in at the gate. It had a good wall and old lanes and a market square with a new tower in it, a tall timber thing on a stone foot with sails on top, like a mill, turning slowly in the breeze off the lake, and a trough at its foot with brass taps and children at the taps. And everywhere she looked there were too many people. Three and four to a doorstep. Washing hung between the upper windows across the lanes so thick the lanes were dark. Families on the steps of the church.

And at the back of the market, a lane that had burned.

It had been cleared. The ash had been carted off and the ground raked. But the end walls of the houses still stood up out of it, black, nine of them in a row, and the houses at the market corner had fronts that were burned to the eaves and had been limewashed over the burn, so that the black showed through the white like a bruise through skin. There was a new stone tank at the corner, covered, with a pump on it, and a board beside it with a notice pinned up on it, printed, which she read as the cart went by because she read everything. It was a list of names under a heading that said Fire Company, Second Watch, and the hours they stood.

It was printed. That was what made her look again.

It was not a writ in a clerk's hand. It was not a painted board. It was black letters on white paper, every one of them exactly the same as every other of its kind, the way the letters on a coin were the same, and there were a great many of them, and it had been nailed up on a board by a pump at the corner of a burned lane as if it were nothing.

When she looked, there was paper everywhere.

In the shop windows. A cobbler's price list. A chandler's. A notice of a market day. A primer, open, in the window of a shop that sold books, with a picture of an apple. Glass in the windows, too, glass that was clear, actually clear, so you could see through it the way you saw through water, not green and wavering like every pane she had ever looked through. Lamps burning in a tavern door in the afternoon, though it was light, with a flame that did not smoke and a smell she did not know, sharp and clean and a little like pitch.

Bastian had stopped talking. She looked at him. He was looking at the glass in a baker's window with his mouth a little open.

"That's not Kaldo glass," he said. "Kaldo doesn't make glass. Nobody south of the capital makes glass like that. Nobody in the capital makes glass like that."

"Then somebody here does," said Tessaly.

Armand met them in the keep's yard.

She had braced herself for how old he would look. He was very old. He had been old when she was his pupil and that was twenty years ago. His beard was white now, not grey, and he stood in the yard with a stick, and when he lifted his hand to them she saw that it shook, badly, the whole hand, and that he saw her see it.

But his eyes were the same. And his voice, when he spoke, was the same dry quiet voice that had told her at twenty that she was the strongest earth mage in her year and the least patient, and that only one of those would matter in ten years, and that she should choose which.

"You came," he said to them, all of them. "Thank you. I'll not waste your time. You'll want to see the work, and then you'll want to see the other thing, and then you'll decide. Nobody here will think less of any of you who goes home."

He looked along the carts, face by face.

"Tessaly," he said, when he came to her.

"Master."

"Your wall at Carrow Ford is still standing."

She had not known he knew about the wall. She found that she could not say anything.

They were shown the work first.

One of the barony's clerks, a careful young man named Hobb, took them down the river road in two carts the next morning, south, below the island, for a morning's drive through farmland to a place where the valley opened out towards the sea.

It was nothing.

That was what she thought at first, and what most of them said. It was a wide dull flat of rough pasture above a salt marsh and a quay, with a grey limestone ridge behind it. There were white stakes in it. Hundreds of white stakes, in long straight lines. There were trenches, long ones, open, going away down the flat towards the sea, full of standing water from the last rain. There was a quarry at the foot of the ridge with a face of fresh grey stone and men on it, and a little railway on a spur, with cars coming down it loaded under their own weight and a horse walking the empties back. There were two square ugly kilns smoking. There was a sawmill on the river further up. And there was a line.

Across the flat, from the foot of the ridge, going away south and then curving, there was a line of pegs with string between them and a shallow trench dug along it the width of a cart, down to a grey layer that she knew without being told was rock. The line curved all the way round the flat. It went out of sight to the east behind a fold of the ground and came back into sight to the west, still curving, and met itself at the ridge.

It was a circle. It was a very large circle.

Tessaly got down from the cart and walked to the edge of the trench and looked down into it at the rock, and then along it, as far as she could see.

"That's the wall," said Hobb, beside her. "The line of it. The footing's to go down on the rock the whole way round. The prince wants it closed before the frost."

She did the sum in her head. She had done this sum for thirty years. A footing trench the width of a cart and as deep as a man, on rock, the whole length of that circle. Raised out of the ground by earth magic, packed and set, and faced after by masons. She knew what she could raise in a morning before she was spent. She knew what a middling earth mage could raise. She walked along the trench a little way and looked at it and did the sum again, and got the same answer, and stood still.

It was more than every earth mage in the kingdom had raised in her lifetime put together.

"How many of us are you expecting?" she said.

Hobb looked at her carefully. "The grey robe hasn't said," he said. "But he's still writing letters."

She saw the other thing that afternoon.

They were taken first to a room off the finishing house of a paper works on the flat below the mill, a long room with a board floor and windows, and at the far end of it, on a table, a frame of oak and iron, with rails and a carriage on the rails and a small head on the carriage, and a sheet of paper under it.

A man stood by it. Not the prince. A plain man in an apron. He did not touch it. He stood there with his hands behind his back.

The head moved.

It went along the rail, and stopped, and touched the paper, and lifted, and went along again, and touched, and went back, and forward, very fast, like a hen pecking, and where it touched there was a mark. The marks were letters. They were letters in a line, and then the carriage moved down the sheet a finger's width and the head went back along the rail and began another line. Nobody was moving it. The man in the apron was not moving it. Nobody was standing over it saying where to go next. It went, and stopped, and went, and made words.

Tessaly watched it make eleven lines. Then she understood what she was looking at and felt the hair on her arms stand up.

It was not that it moved. She had seen stones move things. She had moved things with stones herself. A stone with a push in it would push. A stone with a push in it and a man pouring into it would push for as long as the man poured. That was magic. That was what it was, and every mage in the kingdom knew its shape.

This was not pushing. This was deciding. The head went to a place, and made a mark, and then went to another place that was not the next place along, and made another mark, and nobody told it where. It knew. It was doing a thing and then another thing and then another thing, in an order, a long order, all by itself, the way a man does a sum.

She looked round. Some of the others were watching the letters. Bastian was watching the letters, grinning, and saying how clever it was. The old air mage was saying it was a very fine clockwork.

Paskin was watching the head. His face had gone grey.

He met her eyes across the room and neither of them said anything.

The knights were shown to them on the flat, the next morning, and they were shown as if nobody meant to show them.

The carts took them back down to the site early, and Hobb walked them along the footing line toward the quarry, talking about the stone. At the quarry a long timber was lying on the grass, a squared oak beam as long as a house and as thick as a man, with ropes at each end. It had been cut up the valley at the sawmill, Hobb said, and was wanted at the far kiln, the one at the other end of the flat, a mile off across the trenches, for a lintel.

Two men were standing by it.

They were in black plate. Full plate, black, dull, with cloaks over it, and the cloaks' hoods up, so that there was nothing to see of their faces but a darkness inside the hood and the line of a helm. They stood by the beam and did not look at the mages. One of them took the rope at the near end and the other took the rope at the far end.

And they went up.

Not jumped. They went up, both of them, off the ground, slowly, the ropes going taut, and the beam came up off the grass between them, swinging a little, and they went up together to about the height of a tall house and hung there with the beam hanging under them. Tessaly heard something, a faint hiss, like air through a gap in a door. Then they went off across the flat together, over the trenches, at the pace of a trotting horse, not very high, with the beam swinging gently between them on its ropes, a mile, to the far kiln. She watched them go down there, slowly, and set the beam on the ground by the kiln wall, and let go the ropes.

Then they came back, the same way, and landed by the quarry, and walked off into the quarry sheds without a word, and did not come out again.

Nobody spoke.

It went on not speaking for a long time. Thirty-eight mages of the Circle stood on the grass at the foot of a limestone ridge in the south with their mouths open, and not one of them said anything, because not one of them had anything to say.

Tessaly had spent thirty years among mages. She knew what the kingdom could do. The capital had lift hulls, great slow things over the palace quarter, and each of them had a caster in it pouring himself into the stones by the minute to keep it up, and everybody knew how many minutes a caster had in him before he was spent. You did not put a man in the air. A man was too small to carry a caster. A stone that could hold a man up needed a man to hold it up.

Those two had had nobody. Nobody was pouring. They had gone up and across a mile and down and back, carrying a beam, and walked off into a shed.

"That," said Bastian slowly, "is not in any book."

"No," said Tessaly.

She had not known anyone could do that. She did not think anyone in the world had known. She stood on the grass and looked at the quarry sheds where the two had gone, and understood very clearly that she was never going back to Carrow Ford.

They were shown the flask in the long room at the keep, that evening, after the ones who meant to stay had signed.

Most of them signed. Thirty-one, of the thirty-eight. It was a plain paper, printed, a contract of work for the barony: three Gold a month and lodging, for a season, renewable, the right to leave on any morning with the month paid. Tessaly signed it without reading it twice, because she had been paid two Gold a month for eleven years at Carrow Ford and had thought herself well paid. The seven who did not sign were given supper and their fare home and a copy of the paper to take with them, and Armand shook each of their hands.

Then the doors were shut, and the prince came in.

She had not seen him close before. He had been across the yard, on the first day, with his guard behind him, and on the flat, a long way off. He was younger than she had thought. He was taller. He had a face that listened. He sat down at the head of the long table with a plain man in a plain coat at his shoulder who could only be his guard, and did not make a speech.

Armand put the flask on the table.

It was iron, the size of two fists, with a screw lid and a black seal. It did not look like anything.

"You'll be spent," said Armand, "every day. Every one of you. That is what the work is. You'll raise footing all morning and by noon you'll have nothing left, and in the capital or at your post you'd go to bed for the afternoon and do half as much the next day, and in a week you'd be doing a third as much, and in a month you'd be ill." He put one shaking hand flat beside the flask. "Here, you won't."

He turned the lid.

Tessaly felt it before she saw anything. There was nothing to see. There was a small sound, a sigh, and then a pressure on her skin and in her teeth and down the backs of her arms, and the lamps on the table flared up tall and white, and the air in the long room was suddenly thick. Thick the way the air is thick before a storm. Thick with mana. More mana than she had felt in one place in her life, more than stood in the chamber at the tower on the feast days, and it came out of the flask and went through the room and filled it and thinned and was gone, in about as long as it took to draw three breaths.

The lamps went back down.

"It's mana," said Armand. "The medium itself. Pressed into a vessel until it's a liquid. When you open it, it goes back to what it was."

Nobody at the table had known mana could be a liquid. Tessaly could see that on every face, and on her own, she supposed. She had been taught at the tower, by this man, that mana was a medium like air or water, everywhere, thin, and that a mage drew it and shaped it and that the drawing was the limit. She had never once thought that it could be gathered. That it could be put in a bottle.

"A mage who's spent," said the prince, "and holds one of these while it's opened, fills."

It was the first thing he had said. He said it quietly.

"He fills fast. Faster than he'd fill in a night's sleep. Faster than he'd fill any other way. And that's the part I need you to hear." He looked along the table at them. "Take it when you're spent. Stop the moment you feel full. The moment. Not a breath after." He paused. "Because if you don't stop, it doesn't stop. It goes on filling past what you are. And that tears. Not all at once. A little at a time, a line at a time, under the skin. You've all seen it on knights. The tracery. That's what it is. It's a scar."

Somebody at the far end of the table made a sound.

"I'm not going to watch you," said the prince. "I can't. There's one of me and there'll be a great many of you, and a flask goes wherever you carry it. I'm telling you what it does and what it costs. What you do with it is yours." He did not soften it. "I'd rather you never touched it than touched it and didn't stop. That's the whole of what I have to say about it."

"Where does the charge come from?" said Paskin.

"The barony has a charging bench," said Armand. "Two mages have worked it for years, and their stones go out full every morning to half the works in Kaldo. The flasks are filled the same way, pressed down slowly under a lid." It was true, as far as it went, and Tessaly knew Armand well enough to hear exactly how far it went, and knew that she would not be told the rest tonight.

She looked along the table.

Bastian was grinning.

He was leaning back in his chair with his big arms folded and his fair head tipped to one side, looking at the flask the way he had looked at the glass in the baker's window, and he was grinning. He had heard the warning. She had watched him hear it. He had heard tears and scar and the tracery, and he had nodded at every word, and he was grinning, because Bastian had never in his life been told a thing could hurt him and believed it.

"I'll stop," he said, to the table in general, pleasantly. "I always know when I've had enough. Ask my wife."

Some of them laughed.

The prince did not. He looked at Bastian down the length of the table for a moment, without any expression, the way a mason looks at a crack in a new wall. Then he looked away.

Tessaly did not laugh either. She looked at Bastian's arms, folded on his chest, the big smooth forearms with the sleeves pushed up, and did not know why she looked, and made herself stop.

Armand made the pitch last, standing, with his hand on the back of his chair.

He hesitated before he began. That was how she knew it was real. In three winters at the tower she had never known him hesitate before he spoke. He stood there with his hand on the chair and looked at the table and the shut door and the flask with its lid off, and for a long moment he did not say anything at all.

"When the wall is done," he said, "you'll want somewhere to go."

Nobody said anything.

"Some of you will go home. That's your right. Some of you won't want to. You'll have seen things here that the tower doesn't teach and can't, and you'll have worked in a way the tower doesn't know is possible, and you'll find that going back to a post and a lord and a wall you raise once a decade is a smaller life than it was." He looked at his hand on the chair. "I have spent forty years at the tower. I was the one who assessed most of you. I told you what you were born with. I told you it was fixed, and you believed me, and I believed it."

He paused.

"There will be a new tower," he said. "Not in the capital. Here. A place of learning away from the Circle, teaching from books that are printed, so that every student has the same book and nothing is lost when a master dies. Teaching what this place has found." He did not say what that was. "I don't know yet what it will be. I'm too old to see much of it built. I can promise you a founding stone. I can promise you that I will lay it, with my own hands, if they'll hold still long enough." He took his hand off the chair. "I can't promise you anything grander than that, and I won't."

He sat down.

It was very quiet in the long room. Then, at the end of the table, the plain woman in the grey coat who had been sitting beside the prince the whole evening with a book open in front of her and a pen in her hand, and who had not said one word, looked up from her book.

"That's eleven," she said.

The prince looked at her.

"Women," said the woman in the grey coat. "Of the thirty-one. I've just counted." She wrote something. "I'd been told the Circle didn't take many. It seems it takes them and then posts them as far from the capital as it can reach." She did not look at anybody. "Eleven clever women in one cart. It'll be the most we've had in the long room since the table sat."

Somebody laughed. The prince did not quite, and looked at the woman in the grey coat for a moment longer than he needed to, and then looked away.

Tessaly noticed it. She noticed the woman noticing that she had noticed. She filed it, the way she filed a crack in a footing, to come back to.

She wrote her first letter home that night, by lamplight, in the room she had been given in a house on the east bank that had been emptied for them, with the new oil burning in the lamp and making its sharp clean smell.

She wrote it to her sister in Carrow Ford.

She wrote that she had arrived, and was well, and that the south was hot. She wrote that the work was walls, as the letter had said, and that there was a great deal of it, and that the pay was three Gold a month, which her sister would not believe. She wrote that the old master was very old and his hands were bad but that he was the same. She wrote that there was glass in the windows here that was clear, and paper in the shop windows, and a tower in the market with sails that pumped water, and a smell from the works like rotten eggs.

She sat for a long time with the pen over the page.

Then she wrote that she thought she would stay the season, and perhaps longer, and that she would write again when she had more to say.

She did not write about the two men on the flat. She did not write about the flask, or about the head going back and forth along its rail with nobody telling it where. She did not write about the circle. She read the letter over and it was a letter anybody might have written from anywhere, about weather and wages and glass, and she folded it and sealed it and sat looking at it in the lamplight.

It was the first letter she had ever written in her life in which she had left out everything that mattered. She thought, sitting there, that it would not be the last.
