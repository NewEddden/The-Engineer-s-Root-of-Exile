Chapter 311 - Two Courts

The escheat came down from Wayford on the morning train of the twenty-fifth of August, folded in four in the inside pocket of a carter's coat, and the carter brought it up the castle hill himself because, he told the gate, somebody ought to.

His name was Hew Pollard. He drove for the Wayford brickworks and had driven for them since before the line came, when the bricks went south on his wagon and took four days; and on the evening of the twenty-fourth he had been in Wayford's market with an empty wagon, waiting on a load, when a crown herald rode in with two men behind him and a boy carrying a pot of paste. The herald had stood on the step of the market cross and read it. Then the boy had pasted it to the cross, and the four of them had got back on their horses and ridden out by the north road without waiting for anybody to ask them anything, which Pollard said was the first sensible thing he had ever seen a herald do.

"There was a crowd by the end," he said, in the gate passage, to Ward, who had come down for it. "Not when he started. By the end. And nobody said a word, sir, not one, and then the herald went, and old Mother Tennick that sells the eels went up to the cross with her knife and started to cut it off, and the reeve's man said she couldn't, and she said she'd like to see him stop her, and he didn't." He took it out of his coat. "So I said I'd bring it. I was coming anyway, with the empties. She wanted it to go to the duke and not get lost." He held it out, and then held it a little longer, as if he had not decided. "It's not right, sir. What it says."

Ward took it. He did not unfold it in the passage.

"You'll want a receipt," he said.

Pollard looked at him as if he had said something in Kesrathi. Then he laughed, once, short, like a man who has heard about Kaldo and not quite believed it.

"They told me you'd say that," he said.



Ferra wrote the receipt. It was the first thing she did with it, before she read it: a slip from the pad on the corner of her desk, the date, Received of H. Pollard, carter, of Wayford, one proclamation, taken from the market cross, and two coppers for his trouble, which he did not want and took because she held them out and kept holding them out until he did. Then Ferra unfolded the sheet on the desk and smoothed it flat with both hands, and read it, and turned it round for Emeric.

It was a copy in a chancery hand, a good hand gone tired. The fortieth copy at least, he thought, or the four hundredth; the downstrokes had stopped being careful somewhere about the third line and never started again, and the Lieutenant's seal at the foot had been pressed in a hurry and come out a little to one side, so that the crown on it leaned. There was a smear of the boy's paste across one corner, and a hole where Mother Tennick's knife had gone through.

Stripped of the old form it said four things.

That the council of the realm, sitting at the capital, had found in April that the oath sworn at His Majesty's crowning had not been kept, and had committed His Majesty to the keeping proper to a soul in question, and that by the custom of the realm the Lieutenant governed while the king could not.

That on the night of the second of July His Majesty's person had been taken by force out of the keeping the council had appointed for it, at the crossing of the river road, by men under arms in the service of Emeric, Duke of Kaldo, by his order and in his presence; which the council had found in August to be treason in him.

That the duchy of Kaldo, with the baronies of Grayrock, Milford and Wayford and every right, rent, due, office and court belonging to them, was therefore forfeit to the crown by escheat, as from the date of the council's finding, and the holder's title void; and that every oath sworn to him was void with it.

And that every man and woman in the four baronies was commanded to keep the king's peace, pay their dues to the crown's receiver when one should be appointed, render no service to the late duke, and give up His Majesty's person to the council's keeping wherever it might be found.

Given under the seal of His Highness the Lieutenant of the Realm. God keep the king.

Emeric read it twice. The second time he read only the last line.

"They've kept that," said Ferra.

"They'd have to." He turned the sheet back to her. "If they took it out, somebody in Wayford would notice whose name was missing."

She looked at it a moment longer. Then she took a clean book out of her drawer, a new one in the duchy's grey cloth with nothing in it, and opened it on the desk beside the proclamation, and wrote the date at the head of the first page in her small upright figures; and took up the ruler and ruled a column down the right-hand side, very straight, and wrote one word at the top of it, and put the pen back in its tray. He did not see the word. He was looking out of the window, where the town went down the hill in the sun to the river, and the ring line's white train was going round under the hospital as if nothing in the world had been forfeit to anybody.



By noon there was a line at the records hall.

Nobody had told anybody. Pollard had told the gate, and the gate the market, and the market had it in every ward before the bread was cold; and by eleven people had begun to walk up the hill, without anybody saying they should, and stand in the hall's door, and ask to see their lines. Emeric went down at noon and stood across the square under the plane trees and watched.

It went back from the hall's door along the front of the building and round the corner into the court where the carts stood, three and four deep: carters and spinners and men off the printer crews in their aprons, women with children on their hips and children holding other children by the hand, a fisherman from the port with his boots still wet, a girl he knew from the Seers' Table with her book under her arm. Inside, Hobb and Wenna had the census registers open on the long counter, and Dace was going up and down the shelves behind them fetching the wards' books as they were called for, and Ottoline had come out of her own room with her sleeves rolled up and was reading lines aloud to a man who could not read them himself, with her finger under each word, and the man was nodding along with her as if it were a hymn he knew.

That was all they wanted. They did not want to complain, or to be told what would happen. They wanted to see their own names in the brown cloth books, with the dates and the wards and the children against them, written down where they had been written the day they came; and when they had seen them they thanked whoever had found the page and went out by the other door, and some of them went round and joined the end of the line again with somebody else.

"It says there's no court here," said a woman to nobody in particular, outside, a woman with flour to her elbows from one of Maren's ovens. She had her own line on a slip Wenna had copied for her. "I've got my name in a book in there and it says there's no court." She folded the slip and put it in her apron. "Well, then."

He went back up the hill.



They met in the long room at four, round the old table, with the windows open on the yard and the proclamation in the middle with its leaning seal.

Saban had brought the other paper. It had come in the night, through the attic over the drapers' street, in Ferra's figures in groups of five, and the line room had taken it down from one until a little after two; and Ferra had sat with it at five before the gauge, as she sat with every one of them, and turned it back into a man's words. It was Keller's. It was short. Keller's letters had been getting shorter all year, as if he were spending something he did not have much of.

The six were there, and Armand at the head of the table, as he sat now, with his long new legs crossed at the ankle under it and his robe riding up the shin again because nobody in Kaldo had yet made him one long enough. Ward at Emeric's right. Bram with his arms folded and the forge still on him. Imani with her feet on the rung of her chair and a ward list in her lap that she was not reading. Ferra at the end with the grey book.

And the king, at the far side, by the window, where he had sat at this table every time he had been asked to it since July, which was not every time it met; in a plain coat, with his hands on the table in front of him, folded, the way he had folded them at council all his reign, so that nobody watching could have said which of the two had something in it. Waylon was beside him, on the window seat rather than a chair, with his elbows on his knees, because Waylon did not sit at tables if there was the end of one to sit near instead.

Saban read Keller.

The crown's table had been asked, Keller wrote, on the nineteenth, to certify the instrument of forfeit as good in law before it went to the heralds. He had answered that the crown's table had it. He gave the duchy that answer, he said, because it was the one he had given, and the duchy would want to know what had been said in its name, and by whom.

Then the chain. He had set it out as he set out everything, in numbered lines, each with its warrant after it, as if it were a schedule of goods.

One. The finding of the fourteenth of April, that the oath was not kept. By two parts in three of sixty-four. Forty-three.

Two. The keeping, which the finding orders. Lawful if the finding is.

Three. The taking of His Majesty's person from that keeping. Treason in the duke if, and only if, the keeping was lawful.

Four. The forfeit, which follows treason in the holder of a fief, by escheat, as the barony of Kaldo was forfeit by attainder in the year the duke came south, and passed to him, by a ruling of this table.

Under that Keller had written that each line held by the line above it, and that none of them held by itself. The first held by one voice.

"Forty-three," said Ferra. She did not look up.

And then: His Majesty's own seal, while the finding stood, was by the same custom without force in the realm. Any paper under it would be answered with the finding. He thought His Majesty would know this.

"I know it," said the king.

Saban looked up from the sheet.

"I've known it since April," said Harrold. "I knew it in the hall, when they counted. A king the council has found in breach doesn't make law. He makes a nuisance of himself, if he's let." He unfolded his hands and laid them flat. "I could sit in your study, Emeric, and write proclamations of my own until the paper ran out, and Ferra would see they were very well copied, and every lord in the north would read them and look at the first line of that one." He nodded at the sheet with the leaning seal. "Go on, Master Saban. He's not done. He's never done until the last line."

Saban read the last line.

The realm had two courts now, Keller wrote, and one law, and the law sat in the other one. K.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Armand uncrossed his ankles. "He's a cheerful correspondent," he said. "I'd forgotten."

"He's honest," said Imani.

"That is what I said."

The king had picked up the proclamation. He was reading it at arm's length against the window, as a man reads who has had bad light in three cold rooms since April; and Emeric watched his face, as he had watched it at every table he had ever sat at with him, and learned nothing from it at all.

"Taken by force," said Harrold. "I don't remember the force. I remember holding a knight's arm in a river." He put the sheet down. "My son the Lieutenant told the council he'd keep it for me until I was home." He looked round the long room, at the open windows, the yard, the town going down the hill in the afternoon, the tram's bell somewhere below the keep. "I suppose he means this."

Waylon looked at the floor. Bram, who did not as a rule laugh at kings, made a sound in his beard and turned it into a cough.



"They'll come," said Ward.

He had his finger on the proclamation, on the third of its four things, the line about every oath. "That's what that's for. It's not for us. We know whose we are. It's for every lord north of Wayford with a second son and no land for him. It says the four baronies are the crown's and the crown will want them held, and the man who holds them for it can keep what he can stand on." He took his finger off. "They'll not come this year. Saban."

"Not before the harvest," said Saban. "Nobody marches in August with his men in the fields. After that, it depends who pays."

"They'd not get remounts before the harvest anyway," said Waylon, from the window seat.

They looked at him. He went a little red over the beard, as he had gone red in the mews yard when anybody looked at him, but he did not stop.

"Every horse in the north's on a cart in August. Or in a field. The master of horse can't get forty heavy saddle out of the whole of the river country before Michaelmas without he takes them off the carts, and if he takes them off the carts the harvest stays out." He looked at his hands. "So October. Late. When the roads are bad."

Ward nodded, once, at him: the nod he gave a man in the yard who had put his feet right. Then he turned back to the table.

"October, then. Or the spring. They'll come down the Wayford road because the line's ours and they know it, and they'll come the way Aldane came, because nobody's shown them another way. And we do what we did at the Stream Ground." He put his hand flat on the table. "We hold the ground we know. We make them come across it. Nine of ours."

"And a hundred and eighty-eight of theirs," said Emeric.

"They came."

"They came. And they'll come again. That's what I'm saying." Emeric pulled Keller's sheet across the table toward him, and turned it over, and put his finger on the back of it, where there was nothing written. "Keller counted the benches for us in April. Thirty of the forty-five on the left hand have sent a son up the tower stair. Eleven buried one. He told us what that did to their voices. Every one of them heard a road they hadn't been told about and had to choose between his son dying for nothing and Kaldo being a sorcerer, and most of them chose the sorcerer." He looked at Ward. "Now give each of those men a son who rode south behind Aldane in the year eight and didn't ride home. And another this October, behind whoever's paying. And another in the spring. We'd win every one of those fields. We'd win them as we won the Stream Ground, and every lord who lost a boy on one would have to keep coming, because the only thing worse than his boy dying in a war is his boy dying in a war his father stopped."

"You're saying we don't defend," said Ward.

"I'm saying we don't wait." Emeric took his finger off the paper. "Kaldo doesn't sit behind its walls this winter and count who comes down the road. We go to them. To the capital. Not to the north, not to the houses with the sons in them. To the one city where the paper is." He looked down the table. "And the first thing we do there is the cathedral."

Ward did not move.

"We take it down," said Emeric. "Empty. Nobody in it. Not a canon, not a lay brother, not a beggar on the porch steps. Every soul out of it and away, and the whole of the capital standing in the streets watching it come down. That's first. Before anything else. Before anybody fights anybody."

It was very quiet in the long room. Out in the yard somebody was sweeping the cobbles by the cart shed, a long slow stroke, and a short one, and a long one.

Ferra had not looked up. Imani had. Bram had unfolded his arms. Armand, at the head of the table, had put his cup down on the board very carefully, as if it might go off.

The king had not changed at all.

"No," said Ward.

"Ward."

"No. I'll say why." He did not raise his voice. He never did; it was one of the things that made his yard what it was. "You hold what's yours. You make them come to it. That's not my rule, that's every knight's who ever lived to be old. You go out of your own walls to another man's city, you've given him the walls and the ground and every man he has who knows the streets, and you've left your own people behind you with nobody on the gate. That's one." He laid one finger on the table. "Two. A city's the worst place there is. You don't know who's behind a door. You don't know who's in a cellar. You can't see the end of a street. I've been in a town after a war came through it." He did not say which. "You haven't."

"No."

"Three." He laid the third finger down, and then he did not say it. He looked at Emeric across the corner of the table instead, with the three fingers flat on the proclamation, and Emeric felt his note, which had been steady all afternoon, go very still and very hard, the way a stone goes still in a frost.

Emeric knew what three was. Everybody at the table knew what three was, except perhaps Waylon. The cathedral was where the verdict had been made, and where the close kept its keys, and where a man sat on feast days at the king's elbow with his right hand closed. It did not matter that the man had ridden north by night weeks ago. He would come back to see it. That was the third thing, and Ward would not say it at a table, because a knight did not tell his lord in front of the king that he was afraid for him, and because the last time he had been this afraid for anybody he had ended the day carrying a boy out of a channel and leaving the boy's mother behind.

"He's not in it," said Emeric quietly.

"He will be."

"Then he'll be standing in the street with everybody else when it comes down." Emeric held his eyes. "In front of all of them. And they'll all know whose house it was."

Ward did not answer that. After a moment he lifted the three fingers off the table and put the hand in his lap.

"I've said no," he said. "I've said it once."

"I know."

Ward turned his head then, and looked down the table and across it, past Imani and Bram, to the window; and it was the look Emeric had seen him give in a hundred rooms, the look of a man finding the one other person in the room whose word stood over his lord's. Ward was the king's man. He had carried the king's orders for thirty years, by his own count, and had never once pretended otherwise at this table since the night it came out.

Harrold looked back at him.

"It isn't my table, Ward," he said.

"No, Majesty."

"It's his house they've forfeited. And his people standing in a line at the records hall to look at their names." Harrold turned his folded hands over, once. "I've had my turn at deciding who goes where. You were there for most of it. I wouldn't let me have another, if I were you."

Ward was silent. Then he nodded, slowly, to the king, and turned back to the table and sat square to it, and looked at the proclamation in front of him and not at anybody.



"If you're going to knock a church down," said Bram, "why tell them?"

He had his arms folded again. He was asking the question about the job, the way he asked about any job before he would put a hand to it: what it was for, and what it would carry, and where it would break.

"You knock it down at night, nobody in it, nobody knows till morning. Same building. Same hole." He frowned at the proclamation. "You tell them, you've told him. He's got his bombards on the porch towers already. He's got all the time you give him."

"Because the hole isn't the point," said Emeric.

"Then what is?"

Emeric looked at the sheet with the leaning seal, and at the hole in its corner where an old woman who sold eels had put her knife through it in front of the reeve's man.

"They have to see it," he said. "All of them. The whole city in the streets, looking at the one building in it they'd all know, coming down, with nobody inside, at the hour we told them. That's the war. That's all of it. After that every man in the capital knows two things: that we can reach anything, and that we choose." He looked at Bram. "A short war is one where the other side can see who you chose not to kill."

Bram looked at him for a long time. Then he said, "Hm," which from Bram was an answer, and unfolded his arms.

"There's a great many people sleep in that close," said Saban. He had his hat on his knee, and he was turning it, slowly, by the brim. "Canons, lay brothers, the choir school. The sick on the porch at night; the close feeds them at the north door. The women who keep the vestry sleep over the cloister, my walkers say. Three hundred souls, perhaps, on a feast night. More." He turned the hat a quarter. "That's a great many people to tell a thing to without telling the Lieutenant."

"We tell the Lieutenant too."

Saban stopped turning the hat.

"Everybody," said Emeric. "Every soul in the city who can read, and every one who can be read to. The day, the hour, the distance to keep. And then we count, before anything falls, and if there's one left inside who won't come out, nothing falls until they do." He looked at Ward when he said it, and Ward did not look back. "However long it takes."

"That's the sheet," said Ferra.

It was the first thing she had said since the king spoke. She did not explain it, and nobody but Emeric knew what she meant: that the light sand table in the Works Office's map room held the capital now, and would raise it in light at a touch, ward by ward and roof by roof, as the year's flights and Saban's walkers had brought it home, with the close in the middle of it; and that at the printing works on the civic ring a forme had stood for five days locked in its chase, with a border set round it and nothing whatever in the middle.

Bram looked at her, and then at Emeric, and did not ask.

"It's for this," said Emeric. "Not yet."

"Not yet," said Bram, and seemed satisfied with that.

Armand had picked up his cup again. "I have been inside that cathedral on eleven feast days in my life," he said, "which was eleven more than I wished, and I was cold at every one of them. In August once." He drank. "I shan't mourn the roof. I only ask to be let watch." He set the cup down, looking pleased with himself. "I've earned it. Eleven feast days."



It was Imani who looked at Ferra's book.

She had got up to fetch the water jug from the sideboard, because nobody else had, and on her way back she stopped behind Ferra's chair with the jug in her hand and looked down over her shoulder at the open page; and Emeric saw her face do the thing it did when she found a patient's chart already filled in by somebody who had not been told to fill it in.

"When did you write that?" she said.

Ferra did not cover it. She turned the book round on the table so that it faced up the board toward Emeric, and he read it from where he sat: the date at the top, the twenty-fifth, in her upright figures; the long ruled column down the right-hand side, very straight; and at the head of the column, underlined once, in the same ink as the date, so that there was no mistaking that they had been written in the same minute, one word.

Short.

Under it was one line, the only entry. Proclamation, from Wayford, by H. Pollard, carter: two coppers.

"This morning," said Ferra. "When the carter came."

"Before he'd said a word about it," said Imani.

"He'd said it in April." Ferra did not look at Emeric. She was looking at the column, as she looked at any column she had ruled, to see whether it was straight. "The night Maeve's letter came. He said he wasn't going to sit in Kaldo keeping the books honestly while the king sat in two rooms because of eleven Gold Rand." She picked up the pen. "A man who says that in April isn't going to sit in Kaldo in October. And he isn't going to want a long column."

"You headed it before I'd said it," said Emeric.

"I headed it before you'd said it to Ward." She wrote nothing. She just held the pen. "That's not the same thing. You'd said it to me."

He had. One night that month, late, in the map room, with the capital in light on the stone between them and nobody else in the building but the night watchman two floors down, he had laid the close out on the table ward by ward and told her what he meant to do with it, and not why; and she had worked out the why for herself before he finished, and written one word at the top of a page and nothing under it, which in thirteen years he had seen her do perhaps three times.

He looked at her across the length of the old table, the strand of copper come out of its pin at her nape where it always came out by four, the pen in her fingers, the column. She did not look up. Her colour did, a little, from the throat.

"It's the first time you've ever headed a column before there was anything to go in it," he said.

"There's two coppers in it."

"Ferra."

"It's a heading." Now she did look up, grey-green and perfectly level. "I'm going to hold you to it. If it stops being short I'll draw a line under it and start another, and head that one something else, and you won't like the word."

Imani poured her water, and Emeric's, and put the jug down in the middle of the table on top of the proclamation, squarely, so that its wet foot sat on the Lieutenant's leaning seal.

"Then I want my field hospital built before you start," she said. "Not after. Never after. Everybody builds the hospital after." She sat down. "If it's going to be short, it'll be short and very bad for about a day, and I want the beds for that day ready and the people who'll work them taught, and a ship to put them in, because I'm not putting men with holes in them in carts for seven hundred miles of road." She looked at Ferra's column. "Write that in. It costs more than two coppers."

"I'll head a column for it," said Ferra.

"Head it beds."

Ferra wrote, on the left-hand side of the page, against nothing yet: Beds. I. Before. And Imani, who had not touched him all afternoon, put her hand under the table on Emeric's knee, briefly, the way she pressed a pulse she already knew, and took it away again, and drank her water.



The king stayed when they went.

He did not ask to. He simply did not get up, and Waylon, who had stood when the others stood, looked at his father and then at Emeric and went out after Bram without being told; and in a minute there was nobody in the long room but the two of them and the proclamation with its wet ring, and the sweeping in the yard, which had stopped some time ago and not started again.

Emeric stood by the table. His father sat by the window. Neither of them said anything for some time, which was not unusual between them, and had once been the only thing that was.

Then Harrold got up. He came round the end of the table without hurrying, the way he had come across a nursery floor once, a long time ago, in the evening, when it was without precedent; and stood close, at his son's shoulder, not looking at him, looking out at the yard.

"I was crowned under that roof," he said.

It was the old voice. The one that died at arm's length. Emeric had heard the pitch in Kaldo, every day, as a habit his father could not put down; he had not heard him use it on purpose, and had not, if he was honest, expected to, in a house where nobody stood at doors.

"I swore the oath under it that they've hanged me with. He stood at my elbow while I did." Harrold's eyes stayed on the yard. "I've stood at that altar rail with every one of my children, the day each was named. You too." He was quiet. "I want you to know that I know what you're taking down. So that you don't think you have to be careful of it with me."

Emeric did not say anything. There was nothing to say to it at that volume that would not have been too loud.

Then his father stepped back, one pace, and his voice came up into the other register, round and dry and carrying, for whoever was on the stair; and there was nobody on the stair, and Emeric knew it, and so did he.

"It was always draughty," said the king. "Your grandfather said so. He used to say the Light got in through the roof because nothing else could keep out of it." He went to the door. "I'll see your knight's drill in the morning, if he'll have me on his steps. I've never once watched it. I always had somewhere to be."



Ward was on the wall walk over the yard gate at ten, where he went most nights, with the town's lights below him going out ward by ward and the ring line's last train going round under the hospital. Emeric felt him there from the study, as he felt him everywhere, and went up.

Ward did not turn round.

"I said it once," he said.

"You did."

"I'll say one more thing and then it's yours." He had his hands on the parapet. "Not at the table. Here."

Emeric waited.

"In the channel," said Ward, "she couldn't use what she had. You know that now. You've seen it." It had been the better part of a month since the overlay, and neither of them had spoken of the day itself since, not once, and Ward did not speak of it now, exactly. He spoke round it, the way a man walks round a hole in a floor he knows is there. "She had enough in her to take the whole hillside off and every hood on it. And she couldn't. Because you were inside it. And me. And Halden, and the rest. So she did the small thing, and the small thing was her." He looked at the town. "A city's that channel. With everybody in it instead of twenty men. Every time. You'll not be able to put it where you want it, because there'll be somebody there."

"That's why it's the cathedral," said Emeric. "And that's why it's first. Before there's anybody in the way. I choose before."

"Before the first one."

"Before every one."

Ward was silent a long while. Below them in the yard a cat went across the cobbles to the cart shed and sat down in its mouth, under the beam where the sand sack hung, and began to wash.

"Then choose every one," said Ward. "Every time. Out loud, so somebody hears you, so it's written." He took his hands off the parapet. "That's all. It's yours."

He went down the wall stair. At the bottom he stopped, and Emeric felt him stop, and stand, and think about something; and then go on across the yard to the knights' door without coming back up.



The king was on the yard steps at five with his tea.

Hale had brought it in the old man's cup, because it was the biggest in the keep, and Harrold sat on the third step down from the keep door with his coat round him against the last of the night and the cup in both hands, and his feet together on the step below, like a man at the back of a church. He did not say good morning to anybody. The knights coming across the yard from their door saw him and straightened, every one, as men do when a king is in the yard; and then, because it was Ward's yard and Ward did not stop for anybody, they took their places and forgot him in the way soldiers forget anything that does not move.

Emeric did not forget him. His father's note sat on the steps behind his left shoulder the whole morning, quiet and very steady, a knight's note under the king's, that Emeric had never once felt in a yard. He had never, in the capital, looked up from Ward's practice blade and seen his father watching. He had not known until this morning that he had ever wanted to.

The drill was the drill, and Ward did not change it because the king was on the steps: the forms, slow, the whole line in their shirts in the grey light with the blunt blades, then fast, then slow again; then pairs; then the run up the wall stair and along the wall walk and down and round, twice. Declan ran at the back of the line, where he always ran, and Osric beside him, talking, as Osric had talked every morning since Tess stood up.

Then Ward put Rylan up with Arthur.

Rylan had been back in the yard since the middle of the month on Imani's list, which said no blows to the ribs before September and no hanging from the arm at all, and which he kept in his shirt pocket and read aloud to anybody who looked as if they were about to hit him. The burned arm had come up new and shiny and a little tight across the forearm, and he flexed it all the time without seeming to know he did it, like a man working in a new boot. The ribs had knitted. He said he could feel the weather in them now, which was useful in a fisherman's town.

"Don't hit my ribs," he said to Arthur, cheerfully, taking his blade from the rack. "It's on the list."

"I've read the list," said Arthur.

"Everybody's read the list. I make them."

They saluted. Emeric, at the end of the line with the other pairs stood down to watch, had his sense on both of them, because this was the thing Ward had put them up for.

Arthur stood the way he always stood, easy, his long legs set a little wide and the blade low, and his note was the note Emeric had known since Arthur was a thin boy hanging on the gate of this yard: clean all through, with nothing drawn at all. He was not reaching for anything. He did not need to. The mana was there already, at rest, in every bone he owned, packed close as a full stone is packed, so that he stood in the grey yard at the start of the day with his strength already on him and his hands empty. Imani had a reason for it now, since the cracked stone, if not yet a name she liked.

Rylan drew.

Emeric felt it go: the held mana coming up out of Rylan's channels into the muscle, his note brightening all at once like a coal under the bellows, and with it the small thin hiss of the leak all down the old silver of the rite's cracks, where a knight like Rylan bled a little of what he drew every moment he held it. He was drawing to stand where Arthur stood with nothing. It took him the whole of a slow breath to get there. When he was there, Emeric could not have told the two of them apart by weight.

Then they went.

Arthur was faster. Arthur was faster than anybody in the yard but Ward; his blade was simply where it needed to be before Rylan's had arrived, and twice in the first exchange Emeric saw him turn a cut that would have put Rylan's on the cobbles without his feet moving at all. Rylan gave ground. He gave it neatly, a step and a step, round toward the cart shed, with his face pleasant and intent and a little red; and on the third step back his right shoulder dipped, very slightly, the way a man's shoulder goes when he is about to come in low on the left.

Arthur's blade was there. It went down to meet the low cut before the low cut existed, and it met nothing; because Rylan's blade had never been going low, and came over the top instead in a short flat arc and laid its blunt edge on Arthur's collarbone, very gently, the way you put a cup down on a table.

Arthur stood with the blade on his collarbone and looked at it.

"Again," said Ward.

They went again. Arthur came in harder and Rylan gave ground faster, and Emeric saw Arthur watching the right shoulder now, the whole time; and on the fourth step, almost exactly where it had been, the shoulder dipped.

"Not again," said Arthur, and his blade went down anyway.

It went down because he could get to it. Emeric saw it as plainly as he had ever seen anything in that yard. Arthur's body was quick enough to answer anything Rylan showed it, and so it answered everything, before Arthur himself had decided whether it should; and Rylan, who had never in his life been quick enough to answer everything, had spent half his life learning which things to leave alone. The blunt edge came over the top and settled on the same collarbone, in the same place, as lightly as before.

Rylan stepped back and saluted, and grinned, and put a hand to his ribs, not because anything had touched them but because he had been holding his breath.

"You can get to everything," he said, "so you go to everything. I can't, so I don't." He flexed the burned arm. "It's not fair. I know. I'd hate me."

Arthur looked at Ward.

Ward was standing where he always stood, by the rack, with his book. He looked back at Arthur for some time.

"You were stronger both times," he said.

That was all. He wrote something in the book and said nothing else, and Arthur stood in the middle of the yard with his blade down and his face slowly going the colour of a man who has been told the one thing he did not want and has nothing at all to say back to it; and on the steps behind Emeric's shoulder the king drank his tea, and said nothing about who had won.



"Hold your arm out," said Emeric.

The pairs had gone back to work. Rylan had taken himself and his list off to the trough to throw water on his head, and Ward had put Declan up with Osric; and Arthur was still by the cart shed with his blade in his hand, and Mira and Bren had drifted over to him, as the three of them drifted together after anything, and were not saying anything to him, which from Bren took effort.

Arthur looked at him. "Why?"

"Because I want to hit it."

"You'll break it," said Bren.

"I won't break his. His is harder than mine." Emeric looked at Arthur. "Forearm. Flat. Like Ward shows you for a blow you can't get out of the way of."

Arthur put his blade on the rack and held out his left forearm, level, his hand in a loose fist, and set his feet; and his face, which still had the collarbone in it, went careful and curious instead.

"And if you get it wrong?" said Bren.

"Then Imani writes it down," said Mira.

Emeric hit him.

He did not hold back; there was no use in it held back. He put the whole of his weight behind the right fist from the ground up, as Ward had taught him as a boy and again on the road south, and in the blink before the knuckles met the arm he packed them: down into the bone and the thin flesh over it, in rings, the outermost to give first and the next a hair after and the next, and the joints of the hand left free so the fist would open after. He had done it on the post that morning in the dark before the drill, forty times, and most of them had been right. This one was right. He felt the stop come down through the rings like a man down a stair, and the arm under his fist not move at all, because there was a great deal of arm.

Arthur looked at his forearm. Then at Emeric.

"That was a tap," he said.

"That was everything I had behind it."

"It wasn't." Arthur rubbed the place, frowning. "Bren's hit me harder by accident. It was like being hit through a sack." He looked at the fist, which had opened, and was a hand again. "What did you do?"

"What did you do?"

"Nothing. Stood here."

"When something hits you. When Bren hits you by accident. What do you do, inside? In the arm?"

Arthur thought about it honestly. He always thought about things honestly; he had done it since he was a boy in this yard, asking Ward why. "Nothing," he said at last. "I don't do anything. It just doesn't break." He looked at his arm as though it belonged to somebody else. "It's always been like that. Since the Hod. Before. Imani says it's my bones."

"It is. They're full. All the time, everywhere, and you never think about it." Emeric held up his own right hand, the one that had cracked on a golem's shoulder in November and been strapped till January. "Mine aren't. So I do it on purpose, there, for the blink it hits. Rings, so it gives a bit at a time. If I do it early it's a club and I break your arm. If I do it late it's nothing and I break my hand." He let the hand drop. "So I have to get it right every time."

Arthur stared at him.

"You think about it?" he said.

"Every time."

"Every time you hit anything?"

"Every time I hit anything."

Arthur looked at him with an expression Emeric had last seen on Ned Tallis's face, watching a new hand coil a line the wrong way round: sympathy, nearly, for a man who had been given a very long road to a place Arthur had been born standing in.

"That sounds terrible," he said.

"It's getting better."

"I'm going to try it," said Bren. He had already made a fist and was glaring at it. "Yours. On purpose. If mine's there all the time, I can put more in it. In one place. The knuckles. Can't I?"

"Try," said Emeric, and put his sense on Bren's hand.

Bren tried. He tried as Bren did everything, with the whole of himself and all at once; he screwed up his face, and held his breath, and went slowly red from the collar up, and stared at the back of his fist as if he could frighten something into it. Emeric watched the bones of the hand. The packing in them sat exactly where it had sat before, close and even and full, and did not move by a hair. Bren's note did a great deal, all of it in his face and his breath and his neck. The bones did nothing at all.

"Anything?" said Bren, through his teeth.

"No."

Bren let his breath go, all at once, like a burst sack. "I can't feel it," he said, aggrieved. "I don't know where it is. It's like trying to make my hair grow."

"It is your hair growing," said Mira.

Bren looked at her.

"It is," said Mira. "You didn't put it there. It grew. You can't make your hair grow faster by going red at it either." She held out her own forearm to Emeric, level, unasked. "Me. I want to feel it."

He hit her. It was right. She looked at her arm the way Arthur had, and then at him, and said, "Huh," and nothing else, and put it down.



"Now the post," said Ward.

He had come over from the rack with his book. He did not say anything about the three of them, or about Arthur's collarbone, or about the steps. He held the book open at the page where the mornings were, the numbers going down it in his square hand since December, and stood by the cart shed's corner post with the pencil.

The post was oak, a foot square, black with eighty winters, and since December there was a place on it at a man's chest height on the yard side that was not black. The grain there had gone grey and soft-looking, like a step worn by feet, and in the middle of the grey was a shallow dip about the size of a fist, where Emeric's knuckles had come in three hundred times a morning through the winter and the spring, left-handed until January, both hands after. Rylan called it the duke's third, when Imani was not there to hear it, and once when she was.

Emeric set his feet in front of it.

He thought of nothing. That was the trick of it now, and it had taken him the winter to learn, and it was the thing he could do least easily of anything he had ever learned in this yard: to have nothing in his head between seeing the oak and reaching it but the oak, and the hand, and the rings. Not the leaning seal or the old woman's knife-hole or the line at the records hall. Not the table in the map room with the capital standing in light on it, or three fingers flat on the oak, or the old voice at his shoulder at arm's length. The post. The knuckles. The rings, there, now.

He hit it right-handed.

It was right. The sting came late and small and stayed in the skin of the knuckles where it belonged, and the post made the sound it made when he got it right, a short flat knock like a door being tried. Behind him he heard Bren breathe out.

"One," said Ward, and made a mark.

Emeric set his feet again.

And on the steps behind his left shoulder his father's note moved, very slightly, the way a man's note moves when he sets down a cup he has been holding a long time; and it went through him before he could stop it, a man on a step with his feet together like a man at the back of a church, who had stood at an altar rail with each of his children on the day it was named and wanted his son to know that he knew; and the post was there, and the fist was already going, and the rings went in a hair late.

It went up the arm like a bell. Up the wrist and the elbow and into the shoulder, the whole of the weight he had put behind the fist coming back to him by the shortest road there was, through two knuckles that had cracked on stone in November and remembered it; and his eyes watered, and he stood with the hand held against his chest and said something in no language anyone in the four baronies spoke.

All three of them winced. He saw it out of the corner of his watering eye: Arthur, Mira and Bren, as one, the same small wince and half turn of the shoulder, as people wince at a man who has walked into a door in front of them.

"Ow," said Bren, on his behalf.

Ward looked at the hand. He did not touch it. He looked at the post, and at the book, and at the mark he had just made, and did not make another.

"You thought about something," he said.

"Yes."

"Don't."

"I know." Emeric opened the fingers, one at a time. They opened. Nothing grated. It would be red for an hour, and Imani would see it at breakfast and look at it, and at him, and at Ward, and write it down. "Most of them, on the post," he said. "When there's nothing else. Most of them, now."

"On the post."

"On the post."

"A post doesn't hit you back." Ward shut the book on his pencil. "A post doesn't sit on a step." He glanced up the yard, once, at the king, and away again, as if he had not. "When it's most of them in a fight with a man in front of you, you'll tell me, and I'll write it down. Not before."

On the steps the king had stood up. He tipped the cold end of his tea out onto the cobbles below the step, carefully, so that none of it went on his boots, and stood a moment with the empty cup in his hand looking across the yard at nothing in particular: the cart shed, the post, the three young knights, his son with his hand against his chest. He had not said a word all morning. He did not say one now about the bouts, or the feint, or the post, or who had won anything.

"Ward," he said. "Your yard gate sticks. It's stuck every morning I've been here. It wants planing at the bottom."

"Majesty," said Ward.

The king went in. Emeric felt his note go up the keep stair, unhurried, and along the passage toward the long room, where the proclamation still lay on the old table with a wet ring on its seal beside a book in the duchy's grey with a column headed short.

He shook out his hand, and set his feet in front of the post.

"Again," said Ward.
