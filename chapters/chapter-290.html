Chapter 290 - Smokeless

The harvest on the Milford bench came in during the first week of September, what there was of it, and the spades went in behind it.

Emeric went out to see on the tenth, on the early train, because Hollis had sent a boy to the castle with a message that said only Come and look, and Hollis had never once sent a boy to the castle with anything. Ferra came too. She had the harvest's columns to take down from every steading's clerk on the bench, and she sat across from him in the carriage with her book open on her knee and her pen going, and did not look out of the window until the train came round the last curve above the river and the bench opened out below them in the early light; and then she did, and stopped writing.

It was two colours. Where the swarm had not come down in July the wheat had stood, and the harvest gangs had been through it all week, and it lay in long gold rows of stooks across the slope with the stubble pale between them; and where the swarm had come down, the ground was brown and raw, as if somebody had taken a comb to it. And in the middle of the brown, on Hollis's square mile, where three weeks ago there had been nothing but stubble eaten to the root, there was a mile of green, knee high, and all over the green, as far as the bench's edge, small red flowers.

"Beans," said Ferra.

"Beans."

She looked at it until the train went into the cutting, and then she went back to her columns, and he saw that she had to find her place on the page.



Hollis was in the middle of it, with his hat on the back of his head, walking up a row with his hands behind him. He waited until they had walked the half mile out along the headland to him, and then pulled a bean plant toward him by one of its stems without picking anything, and showed Emeric the pods coming under the flowers, thin and green, the length of a finger joint.

"Eight weeks," he said. "I said."

"You said."

"Twenty-six days in the ground." He let the plant go. "They'll be picking the middle of October. Before the frost, if the frost's polite." He looked along the rows. "Wanted you to see it with the flowers on. They don't look like much once the flowers are off."

That was all he had sent for. He walked them back along the headland without another word about it, and on the way pointed out a hare.



On the far side of the steading's lane the egg-bed crews were working the next field, the one the swarm had sat on for a night and a day at the end of July.

There were forty of them in a line across it, men and women and boys, turning the stubble a spade's depth and stepping back and turning the next; and ten yards behind them, with their heads down and their tails up, a great many hens. The pods came up out of the earth as the spades turned it, grey, in little cases the size of a barley grain, and the hens came along behind and ate them with every appearance of having been born for it.

"They'll do the bench by the end of the month," said Hollis. "Every field she sat on. Paxton's counted them. Nineteen." He watched the line. "We'll not get them all. The frost'll get some." He put his hands in his pockets. "And then we'll see in May."

The crews were on Ferra's books: Milford's own people, hired by the steadings' clerks on the duchy's rate, and since the spring hired under the Assembly's law, which was forty hours and two days of rest, and above the forty, in harvest and sowing, the hours paid at time and a half. The farms' delegates had put the harvest exception into the law themselves, on their own motion, because they had known exactly what a forty-hour week would look like to a man with a field of wheat standing in August.

The steading's clerk had the week's sheet pinned on a board by the granary door. Forty hours in the first column against every name. In the second, the overtime: twenty, twenty-two, twenty-four. A woman named Brede had twenty-six.

"That's a lot of overtime," said Emeric.

"It's the first harvest under the law," said Ferra, copying. "It's meant to be a lot. If it weren't, I'd want to know why. It'd mean somebody was writing forty and working them sixty." She wrote Brede's twenty-six down and looked at it. "I'll want to know about her anyway. Nobody does twenty-six hours over in a week because they like spades." She shut the book. "It's probably a roof."



In the porch of the church at the bottom of the lane, under a leaflet rained on twice since August, sat the parish clerk, an old man with a bald head and a great deal of white beard, with a steelyard hung from the beam, a ledger, a cash box with two compartments, and a punch like a cobbler's, one of the batch Bram's works had turned out in August for every parish clerk in the four baronies.

A woman lifted her sack onto the steelyard's hook. The clerk read the beam and wrote the weight down and counted out coppers from the left side of the cash box, two to the pound, and then from the right side, two more; and then took the sack's neck in the jaw of the punch and squeezed, so that it went through the cloth with a small flat chock, and handed it back to her with a hole in its neck the size of a fingertip. She tipped the sack's grey contents into a barrow by the churchyard wall for the hens, and went back to the field.

"Two boxes?" said Emeric.

The clerk looked up at him, and at his eyes, and went red to the top of his head, and stood up so fast that the steelyard swung.

"The duchy's and the crown's, Your Grace." He put his hand on the left side. "The duchy's comes up from the castle every Monday in a bag, and it's never short." He put his hand on the right. "The crown's comes by the bishop, when it comes. It came a week late in August. It came short last week." He looked at the box as though it had not been his idea. "I made it up out of the duchy's and wrote it down. The steading's clerk says that's right."

"It's right," said Ferra. "Write me the figure."

She had the north on the train going home, out of Saban's last report, which she had by heart. In the north there was no duchy's two. In the north the crown's coppers had come late since the middle of August and in some parishes not at all, and a man who carried his sack of eggs to the church in September was as likely as not to be told to come back after the quarter day, and carried his sack home again, unpunched, because nobody in the north punched. Some of them were digging anyway. A good many had stopped. Some of the ones who had stopped were standing at the railheads, where the bread was.

"He'll pay it," said Ferra, looking out at the gold rows and the brown ground going by. "He has to. He'll pay it out of something he'd rather have spent on something else." She turned a page. "I don't think I'd like to be the something else."

A boy came down the carriage selling apples out of a basket. Emeric bought two. Ferra took hers without looking up, and ate it, and wrote, and put the core on the windowsill when she had finished, and he thought that she had eaten nothing since five that morning and would not have noticed if he had not bought it.

"You're looking at me," said Ferra.

"You ate an apple."

"I eat apples." She did not look up. "Brede has a roof. The clerk told me. It came in under the swarm and she's paying the thatcher by the week." She wrote it down. "I'll see her paid."



Mira came out of the base's armoury on the twelfth in the MK3, and Bren, waiting on the range's rail with his arms folded on it and his chin on his arms, watched her come across the yard and said, "Gods. You look like a beetle."

"Thank you," said Mira.

Emeric had drawn the suit over the winter, plate by plate, and Bram's works had built three of them in the spring and a spare breastplate; he had seen them on their frames in bay three, and Mira's fitted on her in pieces with Abloh going round her with his cord. He had not seen one come across a yard in the sun with somebody in it who had stopped thinking about it.

It was black Kaldo steel, as the MK2 had been, but cut closer, so that it went with her instead of round her, the plates lying over one another at the joints like a lobster's tail. At each shoulder a pauldron with a lion's head worked in it, and the lions' eyes violet glass, with the held stones under them, so that when the suit woke the lions looked at you; at her lower ribs, one on each side, a round housing the size of a saucer for the other two; a disc on her breast with a window in it, and behind the window, turning slowly, the small steady's rotor. Abloh's hood lay down on her shoulders and the cape was folded over her arm.

And the helm. The MK2's had wings, and a slot of violet glass across the eyes with steel round it. The MK3 had no slot. It had one great curved visor of violet from brow to chin and from one side of the helm to the other, so that from the front there was nothing of her face to see at all, only the violet with the light lying in it; and it was that, more than anything, that made Bren say beetle.

"Can you see out of it?" said Bren.

"I can see you," said Mira. "And him." She did not turn her head, and Emeric saw that she meant Arthur, at the far end of the rail. "I can see my feet. In the old one I had to put my chin on my chest to see where I was walking." She took the helm off, and her face came out of it red and pleased, with her hair flattened. "In this one I can see everything except what's behind me."

"That's where I'll be," said Bren.

"That's where you always are," said Mira.

In August Imani had put her hand flat on the other two in the tank room, as she had said she would, and come out with the same small line between her brows: Arthur's bones packed fuller than Bren's, Mira's a shade less, and still no word for it.



The crossbow was Saban's, bought in the spring off a palace quartermaster with debts who had written it in the armoury's book as rusted past use. It was not rusted. It was a heavy thing, longer than a man's arm, with a steel bow across its front as wide as a man's two arms spread and a toothed winder at the butt, because no man could draw that bow with his back; and in the stock, under a hinged brass lid the size of a thumbnail, a small air stone in a cradle, to push the bolt as the string let it go.

Emeric had grown up watching the palace guard shoot them, on Tuesday mornings at the butts in the long court below the family stair, the winders going like a mill and the stones whining in the stocks, with a sound he had thought at six was the most frightening in the world.

Linnet Shaw charged the stone. She put two fingers on it in its cradle and shut her eyes, and it went from water to light in a few breaths, and she shut the lid on it. "Six," she said. "Then it wants me again."

"Six is plenty," said Ward.

They had set two breastplates on posts fifty paces from the rail, with sacks of straw strapped behind them where a man would be. One was the MK3's spare. The other was Bren's old MK2 plate, with the scrape across its left side from the Hod's hatch that he had refused to let the works polish out; Bren had offered it at supper before anybody else could, and gone red, and said it was only fair, since he was the one getting a new one.

Arthur shot it. He cranked the winder until the string came back over the nut with a click, took the winder off, laid a bolt in the groove, short and thick, ash, with an iron head like a square nail's, and put the stock to his shoulder and was still, and pulled.

The sound was two sounds together, the string's flat whack and something under it like a man coughing in a barrel, and there was no time at all between them and the bolt going into Bren's plate fifty paces off with a crack like a stick broken over a knee.

They walked down to look.

The bolt was through. It had gone in a hand's breadth under the left shoulder, where the plate was thickest, and its head was a finger's length into the straw behind; and the steel had not cracked round it so much as opened, folded back on itself in four little lips, like a flower.

Bren put his finger on one of the lips. "That's where my heart is," he said.

"Yes," said Ward.

"It never felt thin. Not once."

"It was not thin. It was the best plate in the kingdom until this spring." Ward looked at the bolt. "That is the bow. That is why the king's guard carries it. Now you know what it does."

Mira shot the other. She was smaller than Arthur by a head, and she cranked the winder without being offered help and without looking at anybody, and put the stock to her shoulder in a way that was not quite right for her, because it had been cut for a guardsman's arm; and took a breath and let half of it out and pulled.

The same two sounds. And then a third, at the post: a clang, flat and loud, as if somebody had hit an anvil with a sledge, and the post rocked back on its foot and came upright again, and something went spinning away into the grass end over end.

The bolt lay ten yards off in two pieces, the shaft split back from the head and the iron mashed flat on its point like a nail struck wrong. The plate had a dent under the left shoulder, a hand's breadth down, where Bren's had opened: round, bright where the black was knocked off, the depth of a fingernail. Arthur went round the back and unstrapped the straw and put his palm on the inside.

"A bump," he said. "Like a blister. Not cracked."

Ward took the plate off its post himself and turned it in the sun, front and sides and inside, with his thumb on the blister; and gave it to Mira.

"Rate it," he said.

Mira did not smile. "Fifty paces. Square on. It holds." She turned it over. "Once. I'd not stand for two in the same place. I'd want to know about thirty. And from the side. And the shoulder pieces, where it's thinner."

"Another day," said Ward.

"I'd stand for one," said Bren. "In the suit. So we'd know what it's like on the inside."

"No."

"But we'd know."

"We know what it is like on the inside. Arthur has just told you. A blister." Ward put the plate under his arm. "Nobody shoots at anybody in this suit until somebody does it who means it. Then you will be standing where I have taught you to stand, at an angle, so that it skids." He looked at the three of them. "And nobody is shooting at the visor today, Arthur, so you can stop looking at it."

Arthur, who had been looking at Mira's helm on the rail, stopped.

Ward walked back up the range. Emeric saw him stop by Bren's old plate on its post, with the bolt still through it and the four lips of steel standing up round the hole, and look at it a while; and then go on.

"Can I have it?" said Bren. "The old one. With the bolt in."

"What for?" said Mira.

"To hang up," said Bren. "So I remember to stand where he says."



The stand at the yard's north end had been built over the summer by Bram's men and two of Fletcher's riggers, who had put it at the water's edge because Emeric asked, and had decided among themselves that it was in case it caught fire. It was not going to catch fire; fire stones burned nothing. But it was going to be loud, and hot, and pointed at something, and he had wanted the something to be water.

It was an iron frame on a heavy timber bed, on four flanged wheels on two short lengths of rail that ran a yard back and stopped against a bollard; and on the frame, in a riveted housing the size of a hogshead, the K Discovery's spare main stone, lent for the day out of her spares on Fletcher's written terms and in Fletcher's presence. The housing's mouth faced the river. Bolted to it, pointing out over the water at the height of a man's chest, was a can: a plain steel tube as long as a man was tall and as wide as his shoulders, with a ring of iron pockets let into its wall a third of the way along and a fire stone in every pocket, face inward, and at the far end a mouth of steel leaves that Bram had copied from the brass irises in the K Discovery's viewports, only much bigger and much less pretty. From the frame's back to the bollard ran a chain, and in the chain, where everybody could see it, a spring scale with a dial the size of a dinner plate. When the stone blew, the frame would want to roll into the river, and the chain would not let it, and the scale would say how hard it wanted to.

They lit it on the eighteenth. Ferra was on the bank, on an upturned crate twenty yards back and upwind, with her book on her knee and the morning's proofs under it. She had been at the dome at half past five, as she was every morning; the feed gauge book lived on its shelf under the gauge outside the glass, and she read the night's fall into it before she spoke one word to anybody. She had told him once that it was the only quarter of an hour in the day when nothing in Kaldo could ask her for anything.

She had plugs of wool in her ears. Imani had sent them down with her in a twist of paper, enough for everybody, with a note in her square hand: In. All the way. I'll know.

Fennick had his in. So did Bram, who had complained, and the riggers, and Fletcher, at a distance by the K Amara's stern, with his hat in his hand and his face saying he would have that stone back in its locker by noon if he had to carry it.

Fennick had been walking round the can since six with his hands behind his back, looking at it from every side like a horse he was thinking of buying and did not trust.

"What do you call it?" he said.

"An afterburner."

Fennick took a plug out. "After what?"

"After the stone. The stone blows, and then it burns, after."

"It doesn't burn anything. You said."

"No."

"Afterburner," said Fennick, as he had said dynamite, trying the shape of it, and put the plug back in.

Bram stood by the frame with his arms folded and his spectacles on, in the voice he kept for a man about to learn something expensive.

"It's bare steel," he said. "It's a poker. You put a fire round the inside of a poker and it goes red, and then it goes soft, and then it goes like a candle in August. Two minutes. Less."

"I know. I want to know how much less."

"You've the lined one sitting in the shed under a tarpaulin. Why not put that on first?"

"Because I want to see this one go," said Emeric. "So I know what the lining's for when I look at it."

Bram made a noise in his throat, and took out his watch.



The stone first, alone.

The levers stood on a post behind a wall of sandbags at the frame's side: an ordinary main-stone lever in a notched quadrant, like every one in every Kaldo ship, and beside it a shorter one under a hinged brass guard, which was the ring. He moved the first one notch, two, three; and the stone woke in its housing, and the roar came.

It was the roar everybody in Kaldo knew, the K Discovery coming home over the port at the end of the day, a great long breath of wind that went on and on. The water beyond the can went flat and white and ran away from the frame in a long fan, and the frame went forward a hand's breadth until the chain came taut, and the scale's needle swung round and stopped. Ferra had the stone's figure in her book from the K Discovery's trials. She looked at the dial and at her page and held up one hand, flat: the same.

He lifted the guard and pulled the short lever.

The fire stones went onto their seats; he felt them wake in a ring in the can's wall and the heat come off them into the air going past. The iris opened, its leaves sliding back, and the roar went up. Not louder only: up, the way a voice goes up, into a long ripping howl that went across the river and came back off the far bank and did not stop. The fan on the water became a furrow, a white trench torn fifty yards across the river with spray standing up along both sides. The chain sang. The needle went round past the stone's own mark and on, and Fennick was on his knees by the dial shouting something, with his whole face, that nobody could hear.

The can went red at the root.

Dull at first, where the ring was, a sullen red like a stove door. Then brighter. Then the red went down the tube toward the mouth as fast as a man could walk, until the whole can was cherry from housing to iris and the air round it shook, and he could feel it on his face through the sandbags. And then the mouth dipped. A finger's breadth. A hand's. The far end of the tube was going down the way a candle goes in a hot window, the whole glowing length bending at the middle toward the water; the howl went ragged, the furrow swung and broke up, and the needle came back fast as the push went into the river at a slant instead of along the chain.

He pushed the short lever home and brought the stone down, notch by notch, and the roar stopped.

In the silence the can ticked. It hung off the housing like a wet sleeve, red still along its belly, bent a third of the way down toward the river, with steam coming off the water under it.

"One minute and fifty," said Bram. "Poker."

"Half again," said Fennick, getting up off his knees, his grey eyebrows going up and down. "Before it bent. I had the needle. Half again the stone, and climbing." He put both hands on the sandbags. "Out of nothing. Out of hot air."

"Out of hot air," said Emeric.

"Do it again."

"With the other can. When it's cool."

On the bank Ferra had written something. She did not show anybody.



It took an hour and a half for the can to cool enough to take a glove, and another hour for the riggers to get it off and drop it on the yard's stone. Bram stood over it with his hands in his apron and looked at it with a satisfaction he did not trouble to hide.

"It's a good bend," he said. "Even. No splits. I could make a gate hinge out of it."

"You were right."

"I know I was right." He watched the second can swung up off its cart. "That one'll crack."

"Will it?"

"Brick cracks if you heat it fast. Ask any potter. You'll heat it faster than any potter ever heated anything." He polished his spectacles on his apron. "It'll hold longer than that one. Then it'll crack."

The second can was a tube inside a tube. Inside, against the steel, a lining of firebrick from the pipe kiln a hand thick, cast in long curved pieces and fitted together in a sleeve, with the fire stones' pockets let through it in their ring. Outside, a second shell of thin plate a finger's width off the first, with a hose from the yard's blower going into it at the root, so that air would run down the gap from end to end and out round the iris's lip and carry the heat off before it reached the steel. A jacket, Emeric called it; the riggers called it a jacket too after a while, because it looked like one.

Fennick asked if he could pull the lever.

He asked it quite humbly, for Fennick, with his three-fingered hand on the post and not looking at the lever; and Emeric thought about Fennick sitting on the black earth where his shed had been, with his eyebrows gone, and moved aside.

"The long one first. Three notches. Then the guard, and the short one, all the way. Don't touch anything else."

"What happens if I touch anything else?"

"There isn't anything else."

"Good," said Fennick, and took the long lever in both hands.



The roar. The needle to the stone's mark. Ferra's flat hand on the bank.

Then the guard, and the short lever, and the howl.

The furrow tore out across the river again and the needle went round; and the can did not go red. The blown air came out of the jacket round the iris's lip in a hot shimmer that bent the far bank like water, and the steel stayed black, and Emeric, with his sense on the ring, could feel the heat standing inside the brick like heat in an oven wall, very great, and not getting out.

A minute. Fennick's mouth was open.

Two. Bram did not look up from his watch.

The needle stood a hair short of twice the stone's own mark and stayed there. It trembled; it did not fall. The river went white and torn for sixty yards. The welders stood along the K Amara's staging with their masks pushed up and their hands over their ears, and on the far bank a man with a cart had stopped his horse and was standing up on the board to look.

Three.

Then, very close, a crack like a gunshot.

It came from inside the can, flat and sharp and so loud through the wool and the howl that every man at the frame ducked; and Emeric felt it before he heard it, a split going through the lining along its whole length at once, like ice going on a pond. The howl stuttered. Something inside rattled. A puff of grey dust came out of the iris and went away down the furrow over the water.

"Off," said Emeric. "Short one. Now."

Fennick pushed it home. Emeric took the long lever from him and brought the stone down; and the river lay still, and steamed, and the can sat on the housing quite straight and black and ticking, with a thin line of red showing through the jacket's seam halfway along where the heat had found the steel through the split brick.

"Three and ten," said Bram, and put his watch away, and said nothing else, because he had said it an hour and a half ago.

Fennick looked at the can with an expression of complete happiness.

"There," he said. "There's the bang."

"That wasn't meant to happen."

"I know. That's why it's the best one." He took the plugs out of his ears. "Twice the stone. Three minutes. I saw the needle." And more quietly, with his hand still on the post: "I'd like to be here for the next one."

"There'll be a next one."

"Then I'd like to be here," said Fennick, and went off down the yard with his hands in his pockets, whistling, and stopped at the K Amara's stern to tell Fletcher about it at length with both hands; and Fletcher put his hat back on.



Ferra had not moved from her crate. When Emeric came across to her, wiping his face on his sleeve, which made it worse, she took the wool out of her ears and put both plugs back in Imani's twist of paper and folded it into her pocket, as though she would be asked for them later.

"What does it cost?" she said. "A burn. In stones."

"That one?"

"Not that one. That one cracked. A whole one, as long as you want it, when it holds." She had her pen up.

He had the figure. He had had it since July, and he had checked it against the ring's drop in his sense twice that morning, once for each burn, and it had come out the same both times, which he had not entirely expected.

"Ten minutes, near the ground, at full: four. The ring holds eight. Two burns, and it's empty." He looked at the frame. "In the Band the ring fills itself. But there's nothing up there worth running from."

Ferra wrote it as a figure, the one digit in the middle of a clean line; and then, slowly, with the nib, she drew a box round it, four straight sides, so that the four sat in the middle of the page on its own like a thing in a cage.

"Four stones," she said. "To go fast for ten minutes."

"To get away for ten minutes."

"That's what I said." She looked up at him. "You're black."

"Soot. From the lining."

She licked her thumb, without looking round to see who was watching, and rubbed it on his cheekbone under his eye; and looked at her thumb, which was black, and at his cheek, which by his own reckoning was now black in a larger and more even way than before.

"That's worse," she said, and wiped her thumb inside the corner of her shawl where it would not show. "The dome was down a hair this morning. I'll want an extra cartridge tomorrow." She stood. "And Fletcher wants his stone back. He's been holding that hat for an hour and a half."



The crown's inspector came on the twenty-fourth, on the morning express from Wayford, with his leather case and his box of seals and, this time, no cold.

He came every spring and autumn now, to read the powder book and count the chests. He was still thin and still polite; but he had a better coat than three years ago, and a clerk of his own, a boy of sixteen with a writing case on his back who looked at everything on the platform as though he had been told it was a village. When Ferra asked him if he had eaten, he said that he had, Chancellor, thank you; there was a car on the express now that did a soup, and it was the second-best soup between the capital and the sea.

"What's the best?" said Ferra.

"Yours," said the inspector gravely. "In the counting room, the spring the licence came. I had a cold. I don't suppose you remember."

"You were sick over the rail the whole way."

"I was. I've come by train since. I'd like it written down that I'm grateful for the train."

He read the powder book that afternoon in the counting room, every page since March, with Fennick at the end of the table with his hands behind his back; down Fennick's cramped columns, quarry charges and cutting charges and the falls dam's last shots, until he came to the column Ferra had ruled at the right of the page in May. A weight made; a weight held; and at its head, in Fennick's hand, POWDER, NEW.

The inspector put his finger on it. "What is this, Master Crake?"

"Powder."

"Of what kind?"

"The kind in that column. By weight, as the licence says. Under the licence's figure, if you add it to the rest." Fennick nodded at the foot of the page. "Chancellor's added it. Twice."

The year's total stood under the licence's figure by a margin you could have driven a cart through, and under it, in Ferra's small exact hand, the second sum, the same, initialled.

"The licence says by weight," said the inspector slowly. "It doesn't say of what kind." He looked up at Emeric for the first time since he had sat down. "Your Grace has done this to me before."

"Not on purpose."

"No. I don't suppose any of it's ever been on purpose." He wrote a line in his book, and his clerk wrote it too, at greater length. "I should like to see what it does. And what it goes into. If it's for arms, it's for the seal."

"It's for arms," said Emeric. "And it's for the seal. Tomorrow, at the base."



The base's long range ran north along the foot of the hill past the gravity hall: rough grass cleared in the spring, with a bank thrown up at its far end and in front of the bank a row of white boards on posts, each with a black ring, at a hundred paces, three hundred and six hundred.

In the morning Ward had two rifles across his knees on a sack. Fennick had a tin box. Ivo had walked down from the glass house and stood a little apart in his leather apron, with his good eye on the rifle on Ward's left knee. The inspector looked down the range at the six-hundred board as if he could not quite see it, which he could not. Declan was a dot at the bank, with a red flag and a white one.

The first rifle was the old one: one of the three hundred, a single shot, iron sights, with its air stone taken out of the housing under the breech and laid on the sack, and a black powder cartridge in it from the old sealed chests. The inspector and Ferra had broken their two seals on the nearest chest at seven that morning, and watched Ward take out one round, and written it down, and sealed it again. Ward lay down and set the rear sight's leaf up a long way, for six hundred, and loaded, and was still.

The bang was the bang every man who had stood on the bank at the stream ground remembered. A great soft cloud of white rolled out of the muzzle over the grass and hung there, thick as a fog bank, so that for a slow count of ten nobody at the line could see the boards or the bank or the hill. The smell came back over them, rotten eggs and a forge. The inspector flinched.

When the cloud had drifted off, Declan's red flag was going up and down below the six-hundred board.

"Low," said Ward. "And left. Two hands." He opened the breech and the case came out smoking. "I could not see where it went. Nobody here could. A man who fires that in a line fires into his own smoke, after the first."

He laid it on the grass and took up the other.

Bram and Ottoline had made it over the summer behind the armourers' shop door, Ottoline the parts that had to fit one another to a hair and Bram the rest, and the two of them the bolt, which had taken them all of June. It looked like the old rifle and not like it at all: slimmer, longer in the barrel, the same Kaldo walnut. Where the old one's breech had a block that dropped, this one had a bolt along the top of the action with a knob on a short arm, like a door bolt; under the action, in front of the trigger, a box let into the stock, which Emeric called the magazine; and along the top of the barrel, on two brass rings, a tube.

The tube was Ivo's. It was as long as a forearm, dull brass, glass at both ends; and it had taken him since March, because his first four had cracked across inside at the first shot, and he had sat with the pieces in the glass house for a week not speaking to anybody until he had found a way to hang the glass so that it rode the kick. This was the ninth.

"A telescopic sight," said Emeric. "It brings the board up."

"Brings it up?"

Ward held the rifle up off the sack and turned it so that the inspector could put his eye to the tube.

The inspector bent and looked, and was quiet; then straightened, quite fast, and looked down the range with his bare eyes at the white speck on the bank; and bent and looked again.

"I can see the ring," he said. "And Declan. He's leaning on a stick." He looked at Ivo. "There's a cross in the glass. Two lines."

"Hairs," said Ivo. It was the only word he said all morning.

The cartridges were Fennick's. They were brass, like the old ones, and not like them: slimmer and longer, the bullet in each long and narrow and coated in copper to a blunt point; and they lay in the tin in fives, each five held by its rims in a strip of springy brass like the teeth of a comb. Ward drew the bolt back, set a strip over the open action and pressed the five down into the box with his thumb in one push, and threw the empty strip on the grass. He pushed the bolt forward and down, and it took the top cartridge out of the box as it went and carried it into the chamber; and he lay down.

"Five," said Emeric. "In the magazine. The bolt brings up each one. A bolt-action rifle, if you want a name for your book."

The inspector wanted one. He wrote it down.

Ward was still for a breath let half out, his cheek on the walnut and his eye at the tube, and then the rifle cracked.

A different sound. Sharper, flatter, harder, like a whip laid across a table; and there was no cloud. Nothing came out of the muzzle but a thin grey breath that the breeze took at once, and the range in front of the line lay as clear as before, the grass and the boards and Declan's dot. The smell was hardly a smell at all.

Ward did not lift his head. His hand went to the bolt, up, back, forward, down, and the case came out bright and spinning onto the grass, and he fired again. Up, back, forward, down, crack. The cases went over his shoulder in a little bright arc. Four. Five. Then the bolt came back on nothing, and he lay still.

Emeric had counted. Five, in a slow count of nine.

At the bank Declan's white flag went up and stayed up.

Declan brought the board in on a cart half an hour later, with a stick in the bank two hands below and left of it where the old rifle's ball had gone, and in the board itself, inside the black ring, five holes a man could have covered with his palm. The inspector looked at them a long while, and then down the range at the place where the white cloud had hung and the place where nothing had.

"No smoke," he said.

"Smokeless," said Fennick, who had been waiting all morning to say it.

"So the line can see. Every man in it, after the first. And the man it's aimed at can't see where it came from." He was working it out aloud. "And flatter. You didn't set the sight up for it as you did for the other." He looked at Ward. "And five."

"Five," said Ward. "In the time it takes to load one."

The inspector wrote it down, slowly, as he had written down the peace gun in the quarry three years ago, and underlined something; and his clerk had stopped writing altogether and stood with his pencil in his hand, looking at the board.



They sealed it in the armoury that afternoon.

All of it. Forty rifles, and the proof rifle as the forty-first, in a row of iron racks along the back wall, which Bram had made in a week with a bar across their fronts and a hasp on every bar; the cartridges in their strips of five, in tins, in four long iron chests, eighteen hundred and some rounds by Ferra's count, every tin opened and counted by Declan and the inspector with Ferra writing; and the powder Fennick held under the new column, in a fifth chest, which the inspector weighed himself on Bram's steel scale, tin by tin, with Fennick beside him not saying a word. The inspector put his seal on every hasp in red wax with the crown on it. Ferra put hers under his, in black, with the duchy's arms.

The chests were the licence. The racks were not.

The inspector stood in front of them with his box of seals open in his hand, and Emeric watched him look for the word.

"The licence is for powder, Your Grace," he said at last. "There's nothing in it about a rifle. I can't seal a rifle."

"I'm asking you to."

"Seal the racks," said Emeric. "Every bar. Under the same seal as the chests, with the duchy's under it."

It had been Ferra's idea, the night before, at the table, across the lamp: that a thing the crown had seen fired and then sealed by the crown's own man in the crown's own wax, with the date on the paper and his name under it, was a thing nobody in the capital could say Kaldo had hidden; and that with a letter coming from the capital which nobody in Kaldo had read yet, and the Church gathering up every scrap of paper the duchy had ever printed, Kaldo might like, this autumn, to have hidden nothing at all. Saban had said he would never have thought of it, having spent his life hiding things. Ward had said nothing until the end, and then: "Sealed, it is a thing the crown has seen. Unsealed, it is a thing the crown has heard of."

"None of these forty has been fired," said Emeric. "They came off the bench and went into the racks. Write that down. Unfired. The one from this morning goes in the last rack, and you write five rounds against it, before you. And they stay sealed until the crown calls for them, or until the duchy needs them and you stand here and break the wax yourself and write down why."

The inspector was quiet a while.

"You want the chancery to know you have them."

"I want the chancery to know exactly how many I have, and where, and that its own man has his seal on every one."

The inspector looked at the racks again, and then, oddly, at his clerk, as though he wanted to be sure the boy had heard.

"My instructions this autumn were fuller than usual," he said. "I'll tell you that much, Your Grace, because it's no secret in the chancery and it'll be none here by Sunday. From the Lieutenant's office, in the hand of His Highness's own clerk. I was to write down everything." He held a stick of red wax to Ferra's lamp. "I don't think they meant me to write down that you asked me to seal your rifles."

"Write it down anyway."

"I shall have to," said the inspector, and pressed the crown into the wax on the first bar.



Emeric walked the racks while they did it, behind the inspector and Ferra with the lamp: the rifles in their iron, walnut and bolts and the little brass tubes along every barrel catching the light. And as the red went onto each bar and the black under it, he found that he was not thinking about the five holes in the board.

He was thinking about a meadow. It came as it came now and then, without asking: the stream ground after dark, the frost coming down, a row of men in good plate laid out by the water for the heralds with their shields on their chests, and himself going along the row with a lantern, counting, a hundred and eighty-eight, all the way to the alders. And a boy in plate too good for a boy, with frost in his hair.

He had drawn the first rifles at the long room table on a winter evening with Maren's tea going cold beside him, and passed the drawings across to Bram and Fennick, and he had not known what they would do in a meadow on a frosty morning. He had known it the way a book knows it. He had not known it with a lantern.

He had walked the row since. And then he had sat down at the same table in the winter of the skip and drawn this one: flatter, further, five in the box, and no smoke between a man and the thing he was shooting, so that he could watch it fall and go on. He had made the first not knowing what it did, and this one knowing, every line, and made it better.

It did not feel like anything in particular. That was what he noticed most. He had thought that it would.

The inspector pressed the crown into the last bar, and Ferra pressed the duchy's under it and wrote the date and the hour and the count, and turned her book round for him to initial; and Ward, in the armoury door with his arms folded, looked at the sealed bars along the wall, and then at Emeric, and went out.



The inspector told them about the four at the door, with his case under his arm and his seals shut, as if it were the last item on a list, which it was.

"I should say that my party this autumn is larger than it was. I'd rather you heard it from me." He looked at Ferra. "Four men of the Church came south under my warrant. They're named on it. A chancery pass, at the cardinal's office's request: to be given sight of the duchy's printed papers, and copies." He paused. "It says printed. I made a point of reading it."

"Everything?" said Ferra.

"It says printed," said the inspector again. "They stopped at Wayford to read the parish boards. Your leaflets. They're on tonight's train." He looked at Emeric, and past him at the wax along the wall. "I'm sorry, Your Grace. I'd sooner have come alone."

"You did come alone," said Emeric. "To this."

The inspector thought about that, and nodded once, and went out with his clerk to the inn.



Saban came up to the study at ten that night and did not sit. He stood by the window with his hat in his hand and gave it like a price list. Four men off the evening train in black coats, with two trunks and a list. They had asked the porter for the printing works, been told it was shut till six, and taken two rooms at the inn. Before they went up they had asked the keeper whether he had a copy of the Almanac.

"All of them," said Saban. "Every year since the first. He gave them this year's. They asked for the others, and he said he used the others to light the stove." He turned his hat once in his hands. "They wrote that down."

Ferra had come in from her room while he talked and stood in the doorway in her shawl with her book against her chest.

"Then they'll want a cart in the morning," she said. "For the rest. I'll have the works' copies brought up from the store. Every sheet. With a receipt."

"A receipt," said Saban.

"Signed by them. Item by item." Ferra did not smile. "If the Church wants our paper, the Church can sign for it."

Saban looked at her; and then, very slightly, he did smile, and put his hat on, and went down.

Emeric sat on at the desk with the lamp low. Somewhere down among the town's lights four men with a list were sitting up in an inn; and in the armoury forty rifles stood in their racks under the crown's red wax and the duchy's black, unfired, and a forty-first with five rounds against it, with a young man's name in a book beside them saying so.

Ferra came round the desk and turned the lamp the rest of the way down.

"Bed," she said. "They'll be at the works at six."

"So will you."

"At six. After the gauge." She took his hand off the desk. "Come on."
