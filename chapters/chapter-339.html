Chapter 339 - Breath on Glass

Declan's room held all night on the sixteenth of March.

Nobody announced it. He came out of it at six in the morning, when Ward's knock on the crate woke the others, and sat for a while with his stone in his two hands looking at the boards, and then said, to nobody, "It was still there," and went to breakfast. Imani wrote it in her book that night. Rylan's held on the twentieth, after a bad hour at two in the morning when the east wall walk in his head turned into the real one in the war, with the ladders on it, and he had to walk it to the gate tower and back four times, counting merlons aloud, before it would be a wall again. Osric's held last, on the twenty-third, a week before Lantern Night, all the way to dawn; he came out of it so pleased that he picked Ward up off the boards, which nobody had seen anyone do before, and put him down again, and said he was sorry, and was not.

"Five weeks," said Imani on the stair. "I said five. It was five."

"You said four if they kept it up."

"They didn't keep it up. Rylan missed two nights going to his daughter's thing at the school." She put her book in her apron. "I counted that in. I always count the daughter in."



The first sentry stood finished in the Motion Shop on the eighteenth, and Tess had stopped being able to look at it, the way you stop being able to hear a clock.

It stood at the end of bay three under the high windows in the grey light, seven feet of it, on its two broad feet on a square of rubber matting so that it would not mark Bram's floor, which Bram had asked for in writing. Dark grey dust plate over a frame of Bram's tube steel. The fiber bundles under the plate, where its muscles would be, that she and Merrin had laid by hand over the winter a strand at a time. The shield on its left arm with the shard boss at the middle like a dull grey eye. The head, a smooth plain helm with a single long slot across it, behind which the control stone sat in its cradle with Emeric's failsafe lines written in it first, before anything, so that it would always know before it knew anything else that it must stop. It was switched off. It looked switched off. Tess had spent the best part of a year on the thing in pieces and two months on it whole, and now that it was done it seemed to her mostly a very large and very patient coat stand.

Wren had come in after the school bell, as she came most days now, to sit on the end of the bench with her feet on a stool and watch Tess not let anybody touch anything. She was seventeen and had her father's way of looking at a thing as if it owed her money.

"Could you make one as big as the gatehouse?" she said.

Emeric was at the other bench with Merrin going over the hip drive. He did not look up. Tess felt him not look up, and knew it meant: yours.

"Why?" said Tess.

"Because it'd be as big as the gatehouse." Wren swung a foot. "Nobody'd come at Kaldo with one of those standing at the gate."

Tess got a slate.

She drew the sentry on it, a matchstick man, seven feet. Then the gatehouse beside it, twenty-one. "Three times as tall," she said. "Fine. Now. If you make it three times as tall, it's three times as wide and three times as deep, or it looks like a ladder." She wrote it. "Three, three, three. How much more of it is there?"

Wren looked at the slate. "Nine?"

"Twenty-seven. Three times three times three. It weighs twenty-seven times as much." Tess drew a leg bone, a fat line. "And its legs. A bone holds by how thick it is across, not how long. Across is only two of the threes. Nine times as strong. Holding up twenty-seven times the weight." She drew a crack in the bone, neatly. "It stands up, it takes one step, it snaps its own knees."

"Gravity stones," said Wren at once. "Like the ships. Take the weight off it."

Tess, who had been waiting for that and had hoped Wren would get there by herself, did not smile. "Show me."

So they did it with the real one. Merrin brought the lift harness down from the rack: four of the ship-yard's small gravity stones in a belt, set to carry, the kind the deck hands wore to move a cask; and they buckled it round the sentry's waist over the plate, and Tess set the stones until the rubber mat under its feet sighed and lifted a little at the corners, and the sentry stood on the mat weighing nothing at all.

"There," said Wren. "It's light."

"Push it."

Wren put her shoulder against the sentry's chest the way her father had taught her to put it into a door, and set her feet, and shoved.

The sentry drifted backward about a hand's breadth, very slowly, like a barge coming off a quay, and stopped where Merrin put her palm on its back.

Wren straightened up and looked at her shoulder.

"It's not heavy," she said. "It's not heavy. Why's it like pushing a wall?"

"Because the stones take away its weight," said Emeric from the other bench, without turning round. "They don't take away what it is. All that iron still has to be started and stopped. It's still twenty-seven times as much of it at the size you want. It'll still be twenty-seven times as hard to turn, and nine times as easy to hit." He turned round then. "A thing as big as the gatehouse is a gatehouse. It stands there. Everybody aims at it. You've built a castle that walks, slowly, and the enemy's had a hundred years of practice at knocking down castles."

"Then what's the point of it?"

"That's the question," said Emeric. "Name me the job."

Wren opened her mouth.

"If it's lifting, that's a crane. If it's carrying, that's a truck. If it's knocking a wall down, that's a keg of powder and a long fuse. If it's crossing the sea, that's a ship. Name me a job only a man the size of the gatehouse can do, and I'll build him." He spread his hands. "Smaller's better, mostly. Denser. More in less. Faster, harder to hit, hits harder for its size." He nodded at the sentry. "That's as big as a man needs to be to stand in a door. My mother's will be smaller."

Wren looked at the sentry for some time, and at the slate, and Tess watched her not be beaten by it, which was the thing about Wren.

"Wren," said Tess, "go and get me some chalk from Ambrose's bench. The yellow."

"There's chalk there."

"The yellow."

Wren went, with her opinion of that on her face. Rylan had come in at the door behind her a moment before, to collect her for supper, and stood against the post with his burned arm folded.

When the far door had shut, Tess said, to the sentry, "Size matters sometimes, though."

Merrin made a noise into her sleeve. Emeric turned back to the hip drive with great attention.

"That's what the big ones always say," said Rylan, from the door, and then heard it, and went a dark red to the collar, and Tess laughed so hard she had to sit down on the stool Wren had had her feet on; and by the time Wren came back with the yellow chalk all four of them were working very hard at four different benches, and she looked at each of them in turn, and said, "What?"

That night Tess wrote it in her book, under SENTRY, ONE, FINISHED, and the date: Wren: big one? And under that, after a while: what for? And left the rest of the page empty, because Emeric had not said never, he had said name me the job, and she had known him long enough to know the difference.



The Well sent down the first lump of mana ice on the twentieth, in a padded box inside a padded box inside an iron crate, in the supply hull's magazine, signed for twice.

Bram weighed it in the Works Office on the brass letter scale, because it was the most accurate scale in Kaldo and because Ferra would not have it carried any further than it had to go. Tess was there because she had asked. It came out of its last box in Orla Garran's gloved hands looking like nothing: a lump the size of a hen's egg, grey-white and faintly crusted, like old snow brought in on a boot, and a thin cold draught came off it that Tess felt in her wrists before she felt it in the air.

"Seven ounces and a quarter," said Bram.

"That's all of it?" said Ferra.

"That's three weeks of the plant forcing the liquid down past where it wants to stop." Orla put it on the scale's pan as if it were an egg. "The liquid's easier. This is just smaller."

"A quarter smaller for the same," said Emeric. "Not stronger. Smaller. And it doesn't need a seal." He had his hand flat a few inches above it, the way Tess had seen him hold his hand over a stove plate to see if it was hot. "It'll keep on a shelf. It'll go in a pouch. A knight could carry a month of fuel in his coat."

Bram's face, which still had no eyebrows on it, had lit up somewhere around pouch.

Emeric's had not. Tess watched him take his hand away, and look at the lump, and look at the iron crate it had come in, and then at the window, toward the yard where a cart was going out with a load of something, and back at the lump.

"And if you dropped it off a roof," he said.

Nobody answered.

"It's all charge," he said. "Nothing in it but charge, packed. No lattice to hold it steady. Hit it hard enough to crack it, a hammer or a fall or a ball through the crate, and it lets all of it go at once. A pocket the size of the lump. A crate of it." He did not finish that. "A crate of it is a dead zone you can carry. Put down wherever you want one."

Ferra had already turned to a clean page.

She wrote at the top of it, in her small hand, MANA ICE, and under it the date and 1. 7 oz 1/4. From the Well, the supply hull. Signed O. Garran, B. Received F.; and ruled her columns: weight, from where, carried by, where kept, used for, by whom. "Every one of them," she said. "By weight. Nobody carries a lump who isn't on the station roll or Osric's. It sits in a magazine, low, small lots, padded, nothing in the room anybody needs." She looked up at Emeric. "And you're not building the other thing."

"I'm not building the other thing."

"Say it in front of Bram."

"I'm not building the other thing," said Emeric, to Bram.

"I heard," said Bram, and looked at the egg on his scale as a man looks at a dog that has been described to him as not dangerous.



His mother came out of her body on the night of the twenty-third, an hour after Osric's room held.

Emeric had been sitting with her since ten, as he did on the nights he was not in the darkroom, on the boards beside the mattress with a lamp turned low and a book he was not reading. The knights were on their crates on the far side. Ward was asleep against the wall. Imani had come and read her and gone at eleven, and said what she said every night, that the heart was steady and the lungs clearer and that there was a very great deal of work going on in there and none of it was any of his business.

He felt it first as a change in the tree. The dome's mana was never still; it moved round the trunk the way warm air moves round a stove, slowly, always the same way. A little after one it moved differently. Something was standing in it.

He looked up.

She was standing at the foot of her own mattress, looking down at herself.

Not clearly. As you see your own breath on a window on a cold morning, the shape of it, and the light behind it showing through. A woman in a plain dress, dark-haired, slight, with her arms folded, and her head on one side in a way he knew from the inside of his own neck. He could see the trunk through her. He could see, if he looked hard, the lamp. But her note was there in the dome's mana as plain as a voice in a room, and it was hers, and it was awake.

"Well," said his mother, looking down at the sleeping woman on the mattress. "That's a sight."

He heard it. Not with his ears. The way he heard a delver in the delve, straight into the place behind the eyes.

On the far side of the boards three crates creaked at once. Osric, Declan and Rylan were on their feet, all three, staring. Osric had his hand on his belt where his knife would have been. Rylan had gone white.

"You can see me," said Amara, turning to them, interested. "Can you? All of you. Good." She looked them over, one and then the next, as she might have looked at three boys in a tower kitchen. Then she looked at Rylan, a long moment. "You. The rite. In the cathedral, at the capital, years ago. You were on the stone floor and he had his hand on your head." She said it gently. "I was in his other hand. I remember you. You didn't cry."

Rylan said nothing at all. After a moment he sat down again on his crate, quite slowly, and put his good hand over his burned one.

Ward had not woken. Ward, who was a delver and could have seen her better than any of them, slept against the wall with his arms folded and his chin on his chest and snored, once.

"Leave him," said Amara. "He'll be furious in the morning. I'd like to see it." She looked at Emeric. "How far can I go?"

"I don't know," said Emeric. He had found he was standing too, and did not remember getting up. "Nobody's done this."

"Somebody's done it," said his mother. "Or I wouldn't know how." She turned, and took three steps across the boards toward the dome's glass, as easily as anybody walks across a room, and a fourth; and at the fifth she stopped short as if she had walked into a curtain, and he felt her note thin, like a voice going away down a corridor, and come back.

"There," she said, a little breathless, for somebody who had no breath. "That's where it stops. It's like walking into deep water, and the water thins out to nothing. I'd not want to go further." She came back to the mattress and stood at its head, by her own sleeping face, and put her hand down as if to touch its hair, and her hand went into it and through. She took it away. "I'm tied to this," she said. "To the tree and to that. It's thick in here. I'm riding it, the way you'd float in salt water. Outside the glass it's thin."

"Kaldo's thick," said Emeric. "Thicker than anywhere. But it thins at the walls."

"Then I'll stay in Kaldo," said Amara. "I wasn't planning a journey."



Harrold could not see her.

He came up the hill the next night at ten in his brown coat, and stood in the dome by the mattress for some minutes looking at the place Emeric pointed to, and saw the trunk, and the lamp, and nothing else; and Emeric watched him try not to mind, and fail, and say nothing.

"I'm here, Harry," said Amara, two feet in front of him. "You're looking at my ear."

Emeric told him.

The king's face moved. "She's never called me that," he said. "In front of anybody. Not since we were twelve."

"There's nobody here," said Amara. "There's our son."

So Emeric did the only thing he could think of. He sat Harrold down on a crate, and sat on the next one, and took his father's big hand in his own, which he had not done since he was seven, and went down; and took his father with him, not into Harrold's own room, which Harrold had not got, but into his own, Emeric's, and held the door of it open behind them, and let the dome's mana into it, and let her in.

It was a strange crowded place to be. His father stood in it looking round at the shelves and the stair and the floor, astonished, the way Ferra had looked round it the first time, as at a house you did not know a friend had. And his mother stood in the middle of it, quite clear now, because in here she was as clear as either of them; and Emeric stood by the door holding it, and did not look at them, because it was not his to look at.

He heard them anyway. He could not help that. He held the door and listened to his father's voice saying her name, and then saying nothing for a very long time, and then something about a horse, and then something that made her laugh. It cost him to hold it. He felt the cost in his chest like a long climb. He held it.

Afterward, back on the crates, Harrold sat with his elbows on his knees and his face in his hands, and did not move for so long that Emeric thought he might have gone to sleep.

"I'd have given," said Harrold at last, into his hands, "the kingdom. For that. Any day of the last twenty-eight years." He lifted his head. "And you did it on a crate."

"It's not a thing I can do every night."

"No. I could see what it cost you. You went grey." Harrold looked at the empty air by the mattress. "She told me about her father."

"I know."

"I didn't know. More than fifty years. I knew her from the day she came into the yard at six with her hair in a string, and I didn't know she had a father. My father knew." He shook his head slowly. "My father knew all of it, and never once said, and treated her like his own, and let me think she'd come from nowhere."

"Maybe that was the kindest thing he had."

"It was," said Harrold. "It was the kindest thing anybody ever did for her. I'll have to think how to forgive him for it."



She told the six on the twenty-fifth.

All of them, because all of them were delvers and could see her and hear her, and because she asked for them by their offices, which she had learned in the dark from Augustine talking: the Chancellor, the War, the Arcane, the Works, the Trade and the Health. They came up after supper and sat round the mattress on crates and the edge of the boards like people at a wake who had been told the body was going to speak, which, Saban said afterward, was more or less the case. Harrold sat by the trunk. The knights stood. Ward, who had been furious all of the previous day, as predicted, had brought a chair, and put it at the foot of the mattress, and sat in it, square, with his arms folded, as if daring her to go anywhere.

She told it plainly, standing at the head of her own sleeping body with the trunk glowing through her.

Her father had been a cardinal. Randillia's cardinal, at Ostrel more than at home, liked by everybody, with the best-known face in the capital; and he had been chosen as the next pope. To make him ready they had given him the keys of the papal archive, the private one, under the great church, which no one but a pope's successor ever read. He had read it for a year. Centuries of letters, in the hands of twenty popes, and in all of them the same turns of phrase, the same small habits of the pen, a man who always crossed his sevens and was always sorry about the weather in the same words. And in the letters of popes who had never met, memories of things only one of them could have seen.

"He wasn't a clever man about people," said Amara. "He was a clever man about paper. He read his own death in those letters. The pope's one man. He's always been one man. He gets into each new pope, the way you'd get into a coat, through that." She looked at Emeric. "Through what you did with me. He goes down into the Root and up into the next one and takes him, and the next one's been raised all his life to say yes. Being chosen means being eaten. Father found that out at fifty, at a desk, with a lamp, and lost his faith before he'd blown the lamp out."

Nobody said anything.

Emeric had known it, more or less, for months. He had put it together from a one-handed man at Ostrel's stone gate, and from the last sparks of Augustine going out under his hands in the square, and from the letters he had read through the Root; and he had known it as you know a thing you have reasoned to, which is to say not at all. Now it was a fact. It sat in the dome like a stone on a table.

"And my mother," said Amara, "was Adana Tano, of the tower."

Armand made a sound.

He was sitting on the boards by the mattress with his knees up, young, in his cardigan, with his plain-glass spectacles in his hand; and he did not say anything more than the sound. He only looked at her, at the slight shape against the trunk with its head on one side, and Emeric watched forty years of a thing that would not come right finally come right in his face.

"She taught me," said Armand. "For a year. I was afraid of her the whole year. She had very still hands." He put the spectacles on, as if that would help. "You tilted your head the same way over a stone. For four years I tried to think who you reminded me of."

"I know," said Amara. "I used to watch you try. I couldn't tell you. Nobody could know." Her voice went gentler. "She said you were the best of that year and would never believe it."

Armand took the spectacles off again.

They had run, she said, her father and her mother; she was born on the road, and they had made a delver of her before she could read, both of them, because a child of that line would have to be able to keep herself; and when she was five they had come back to the capital under other names and her father had gone in the night to the old king, his friend, and told him all of it, and asked him, if they did not come back, to take care of their daughter. Then they had gone to Ostrel to kill the pope. Kingdom by kingdom, burning every record of themselves as they went.

"They nearly did," said Amara. "He was bleeding out on the floor of his own chapel. And he killed them anyway, and got into somebody else's body on his way out, and lived." She looked at the trunk through her own hand. "But he was so badly torn that he'd lost the night. All of it. He didn't remember them. For twenty-three years he didn't know who'd done it to him."

"Until Augustine wrote to him," said Ferra quietly.

"Until Augustine felt me slip, once, in the palace, and wrote to Ostrel, and the pope read my name, and remembered." Amara's mouth moved. "And sent the order. All of us. The whole line." She was quiet. "I heard them talk about it. In the dark, in the stone. A mind in a stone hears. I heard Augustine say it to his brothers, in that kind voice, the way a man reads out a grocer's list."

Waylon, standing behind his father's crate, put his hand on Harrold's shoulder.

"There's one more thing," said Amara, "and then I'm tired, and I'd like to go back in." She looked at Emeric, only at him. "I heard where they are. Father and Mother. The pope didn't kill them, not all the way. He was dying and he did what he knew how to do. He took them. Into two stones he always carried. His people picked them up off the chapel floor with the rest of his things and put them in the vault under Ostrel, unlabelled, with all the other saved dead the Church keeps there, and nobody knew whose they were for twenty-three years." She stopped. "They're awake. A mind in a stone is awake. They've been awake in the dark under Ostrel since I was six."

Emeric did not say anything.

He knew what it was that he did not say. So did Ferra; he felt her know it from across the mattress, and felt her decide not to say it either, tonight. It was a road. It went north, and over the Empire's border, and down under the largest church in the world, and there were two people at the end of it who had been sitting in the dark for longer than he had been alive.

"I know," said his mother, watching his face. "Not yet. I only wanted you to know where they were." And she went back into herself, the way a breath on glass goes when the window warms, from the edges inward; and on the mattress her sleeping hand moved, once, on the sheet.

Ward sat in his chair at the foot of the bed for a long time after the rest had gone down.



On the way down the hill, Ferra read them the stone book.

She did it on the steps of the records hall under the lamp, because the sleep was ten days off and she had promised to read it to them before they went under, every line, so that nobody woke to find a stone they had not known about. Most of it they knew. At the year twelve line, the first cycle after the tree came back, she read out the count left over when the bands and the golem and the K Amara were done, sixteen; and Armand, who had walked down beside her with his hands in his coat pockets and his face still not quite his own, said, "I wrote two. That winter. On the table. Two remain."

"You wrote two remain for the table," said Ferra. "The two set aside for the long table that winter. I've your pencil here." She showed him. "Not the drawer. You never wrote the drawer. You never wrote anything you could leave to me."

"That's true," said Armand, and seemed comforted by it, more than the figure could explain.



He went to see the old man on the twenty-sixth.

He had forgotten him. That was the plain fact of it, and he did not like it. An old man in a cell at the foot of the south-west stair in the close at the capital, walled in behind plaster so long that the plaster had gone the colour of the stone, fed through a hatch, his name gone out of every book; carried out into the air in Osric's arms on the day the close was emptied, who had asked one thing on the steps in a voice like paper, and nobody on the steps had known what he meant. Ferra had written him in, and his question beside him. Imani had kept him alive. He had come south on the K Endeavor's ward and been in the long ward at Kaldo since February, by the window, and Emeric had walked past his bed twice going to Danner and not known him.

He knew him now. He went in the morning with a bowl of Hester's broth because it was what you took, and sat on the stool by the window.

The old man was smaller than he remembered and clean, and somebody had cut his hair. He was looking out of the window at the yard, where a lad was trying to teach a goat to pull a handcart.

"Father," said Emeric. "Jerome. You asked about Jerome."

The old man turned his head.

It took him some time to find Emeric's face, and then to find whatever he was looking for in it; and then his own face changed, not much, the way a candle's flame changes when a door opens somewhere in the house.

"He used to read," he said. "In my archive. In the close. The cardinal's own. Every day for a year, before he went to Ostrel. He'd bring an apple and forget to eat it. I'd find it on the desk at night." The paper voice was very quiet. "He went to Ostrel, and came back once, and then he didn't come back again. And then they came and asked me what he'd read. Every page." He looked at the window. "I told them I didn't know. I did know. I'd brought him the boxes."

"Is that why they walled you up?"

"I expect so," said the old man, with no particular feeling, as a man says it of the weather in a year long ago. "Nobody told me." He was quiet a while. "Did he come back?"

Emeric thought of a stone in the dark under Ostrel, and a man awake in it, who would bring an apple and forget to eat it.

"No," he said. "But I know where he is."

The old man nodded slowly, as if that were nearly as good, and turned back to the window; and after a while he said, "That goat won't pull it. Somebody ought to tell the boy," and Emeric went down and told the boy.



Lantern Night came in clear and cold, with no wind, which Ferra said was the first piece of luck the town had had from the sky all year and she intended to bank it.

By dusk Lantern Square was full from the records hall steps to the fountain and out down every street that opened into it, and still they came: the works off the late shift in their jackets with their caps in their pockets, the lower wards in their good coats, the school by classes with masters at the ends of the lines counting heads, children on shoulders, old women on chairs carried from home and set down wherever there was room, the Grayrock people off the people's train and the Milford people who had driven in on carts and stood up in them. The tram stopped running at six because there was nowhere left for it to go.

Abloh's screen hung on the records hall's front wall, between the two tall windows: a whole sail's worth of new sailcloth, whitened with chalk and size, stretched on a frame of ship's spars that Fletcher's crew had raised that morning with tackle off the roof and pinned at the corners. It was very large and very white and quite blank, and the square looked at it as a congregation looks at a pulpit nobody has yet climbed into. In the hall's upper window, behind them, where the light would go out over their heads, stood the lantern on its stand, with Ivo beside it in his hat, and Ottoline, and Merrin with her hands in her coat because she would not let anybody see them shake. Two horns stood on chairs on the steps.

The six and Emeric were on the steps too, because Ferra had said if the town was going to sit in the cold for this, so were they. Emeric stood at the end. Ferra stood next to him, with her book in her coat pocket, not out, which was how he knew how much it mattered to her. Imani came up the steps last, from the hospital, with her coat buttoned to the throat, and stood on his other side, and put her hand into the pocket of his coat, beside his, without a word.

He looked at her.

"It's cold," said Imani. "It's clinical." She kept it there.

At a quarter past seven the lamps round the square went down, one after another, as the lamplighters went round with their poles; and the square went quiet in the dark the way a crowd goes quiet that does not know what it is waiting for. In the hall's upper window a light came on behind the lantern's glass. A beam went out over their heads, white, with the cold air showing in it, and struck the sailcloth.

A card. Black letters on grey. KALDO. YEAR THIRTEEN.

A murmur went across the square; somebody near the fountain read it out for somebody who couldn't. Then the card went, and the sailcloth was full of a railway line coming straight at them, the rails running in from the corners to a point, and far up at the point a smudge of smoke, and under it something dark growing larger, and larger, very fast, with its smoke going up, and the horns on the steps roaring with it, the real roar of it, the express, coming out of the wall at them at fifty miles an hour.

The front rows ducked.

All of them. Twenty deep, they went down as one, with a noise like a field of wheat going over in wind; and a woman by the fountain screamed, and a great many children screamed with her, and then the express went by the camera in a howl and a gust of white and was gone, and the sailcloth showed an empty platform with steam drifting across it, and the square was silent. Then a child somewhere near the front, flat on the cobbles, said in a high clear voice into the silence, "Again."

And the square laughed. It started at the front, with the people getting up off the stones and looking at each other, and went back through the crowd in a wave, and went on, helplessly, for a long time, until people were wiping their eyes, and Saban on the steps beside Ward said, to nobody, "I was reaching for something under the bench," and Ward laughed too, aloud, which nobody on the steps had heard him do in a year.

The pictures went on. The long room at Commencement, the Grayrock benches, the masters in their gowns, and a girl in a borrowed jacket coming up the aisle. And then, out of the horns, a voice. A woman's voice, clear and a little nervous, reading off a list. Edda Rowe. Medicine. Grayrock.

Nobody in the square had ever heard a picture talk. There was a sound from the whole of it that was not a word, an indrawn breath from three thousand people at once; and Emeric, on the steps, looked along to where Sela stood beside Ward with her shawl over her head, and saw her put her hand over her mouth, and keep it there, with her eyes very wide in the light off the screen, hearing her own voice come back to her across the town.

The works at six. Abloh's glare over the cutting table, all nine seconds of it, and the square laughed again and somebody shouted his name, and from somewhere on the far side Abloh's own voice was heard to say a word that the school's masters pretended not to hear. Maren's ladle. The trams. Children on the river steps, and one of them shouting for her mother in a voice so loud the horns rattled, and a woman in the crowd shouting back without thinking, I'm here, and being laughed at kindly by everyone near her. The harvest at Milford. Snow on the great dome's glass.

The quay.

November light, grey, off the river. The side of escort four. A gangway, and men going up it with bags on their backs. And at the gangway's foot, a heavy man with a voice like a gull, counting them up by touching each one on the top of the head as he passed; and under the gulls and the boots, if you were listening for it, and the square was listening for it now, very still, his voice, going steadily, one, two, three, four.

The square knew who it was. They had read the News. Emeric felt them know it all at once, the way you feel weather change, three thousand people who had stopped breathing. Down by the steps, in the front of the crowd, a heavy grey woman in her good shawl stood with her arms folded and her chin up and watched her son count, and did not look away from it once.

A thin dark boy with his hands in his pockets went up the gangway behind him, and took them out because someone below told him to, and put them back in at the top. Somewhere in the square a woman made a small sound and was quiet.

Then two knights stopped by the camera, a big fair boy in a scarf and a little dark girl laughing at him, and the boy put his face to the horn, and the horns on the records hall steps said in his voice, enormously, as if all the way to Wayford, Is it on? Mam. I've got my scarf.

And in the middle of the square, among the late shift off the works, a big fair young man in a brand-new red knitted scarf wound three times round his neck, with a small dark girl beside him who had nearly all her eyebrows back, put his face in his hands; and the people round him, who knew him, began to laugh, and to clap him on the back, and then the clapping went out from him through the crowd like rings on a pond, until the whole square was clapping in the dark, for the boy on the screen and the boy in the scarf and the two who had gone up the gangway in front of them and not come down it, all at once and all together, and nobody could have said for which.

The last of it was February: the fleet coming down, and the quay black with people, and the town on it, which was the town in the square, looking at itself. Near the end there was a moment, half a second, at the foot of the crane, of a woman in a blue coat looking up at a tall man, that went by too fast for anybody to see.

Ferra, on the steps, did not move at all. Emeric felt her not move.

The amber card came up last. Lantern Night, Year Fourteen. And the beam went out, and the lamplighters went round, and the square sat on in the dark a little longer before anybody thought to stand up.

They showed it three times. The front rows ducked every time, and every time a different child said again, and by the third showing the people who had seen it twice were standing at the back telling the people in front of them when to duck. At the second showing the light stone in the lantern tired, as Ottoline had said it would on a long night, and Ivo lit the arc behind it without stopping the film, and nobody in the square noticed anything except that the picture went a little whiter.



Then the lanterns.

They had been doing it in Kaldo since long before Kaldo had a tram: a paper lantern for every name you were carrying, written on the side in whatever hand you had, lit at the fountain and let go. The square went to the fountain in a long slow press with their lanterns in their hands, and the table by the basin was covered with pens and ink and candle ends, and the clerks from Ferra's office sat behind it writing names for the people who could not, as they had every year.

Rylan's detachment came up together, the divers in a body, eleven of them, and Rylan and Declan, and Wilf in his scarf and Cady beside him. They had one lantern between them, a big one off the works' paper shop, and they wrote on it standing round the table, each of them, one word or two, in turn, passing the pen. Then Rylan wrote the two names at the top, large and plain, because it was his detachment. ADE FENN. ROBIN SENNET. And under them, smaller, KALDO, because that was what the post on the dune said.

They let it go together, all of them with a hand on its frame. It went up slowly in the still air, very straight, with its light coming through the names, over the records hall and the screen and the dark roofs, toward the dome on the hill.

Behind them at the table, alone, Tolliver stood with a small lantern in one hand and the pen in the other.

He was nineteen. He had stood there some time. He had come off the lighters at the port to the K Amara's deck, and on the night in the cloud a man had come over a grapple line onto her battery out of the white with a blade, and Tolliver, clipped to the jackline for once in his life and unable to get clear, had killed him with the hook-cutter; and the man had said something as he went down that Tolliver had not heard over the guns, and Tolliver did not know his name, and never would.

Ruan was beside him. He did not say anything. He waited.

After a long time Tolliver bent to the paper and wrote, in his slow port-school capitals, A MAN; and stood looking at it; and then let Ruan light the candle for him, because his hands would not, and let it go.

It went up after the big one, a long way behind, small and steady.

On the records hall steps, Emeric watched the two lights go over the roofs, one and then the other, until they were only two more among the hundreds going up over Kaldo into the clear cold dark; and on the hill, far off, under the glass of the dome, a light that had nothing to do with any lantern moved once beside the tree, and was still.
