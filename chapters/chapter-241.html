Chapter 241 - The Long Table

Ferra finished her arithmetic on the fourteenth of December, at ten at night, at the counting room table, and pushed it across to him.

It was a bundle of Ottoline's cards, the long room's own people sorted out of the town's for her in November, each card a name and a row of figures, and the first figure on every one the year its owner was born. She had untied the tape and laid five of them out in a row, square.

"Decadays," said Ferra.

His own. Imani's. Ward's. Bram's. Hers.

"Every tenth year," said Ferra. "It's what the town keeps. What everybody keeps who isn't a lord. A feast at ten, and twenty, and thirty, and a present, and the neighbours in; and in between a word and a cup and nothing else. Lords keep every year, because they can. The king has fireworks for his." She put her finger on the first card. "You turned thirty the winter we came home from Emberfell. Imani turned thirty the autumn the war came. Ward was fifty in the smallpox winter, at the stream ground in the snow. My father was fifty in the spring, a fortnight before the investiture, while Abloh was sewing roses." She moved her finger to the last card. "And I was thirty in August."

"In August," he said.

"On a Thursday. The Hod was in the reeds and you were in the cellar with a flask. I knew. I didn't enter it anywhere; there was nobody to enter it for." She squared the cards edge to edge. "Five decadays at one table, and not one of them had so much as a cup. Nobody noticed. I noticed mine and said nothing, and that's worse."

"What do you want?"

"A day," said Ferra. "One. This winter, before the year's out, when we sit down and have the feast we didn't have and give the presents we didn't give, all five at once, and drink to them." She picked up his card from the end of the row. "Yours would have been the obvious one. Except."

"Except it's hers."

"Except it's hers." She put the card down again. She did not soften it; she never did, and he had loved her for that since the first summer. "You've not kept it since you were seven. I've watched you not keep it for nine years. I'll not make you keep it now for five people."

He looked at the card with his name on it, and the year, and the day: the day of the carriage, which he did not remember and which nobody had ever told him all of, when his mother had died, and before which there was nothing in his head at all.

"The twenty-fifth," he said.

Ferra looked at him.

"Of December. A week on Friday." He took his eyes off the card. "A day nobody's claimed. Not a saint's, not a king's, not a market's."

"Midwinter's the twenty-first," said Ferra.

"The twenty-fifth."

Ferra did not write it at once. She looked at him across the table, the long look she gave a figure she could see was right and could not yet see why; and he watched her see that there was a reason under it, and that it was not one he would give at this table with the door open on the stair.

Then she wrote it, under the five names, and beside it, very small, a question mark, which she did not show him and which he saw anyway.

"You'll tell us why," she said. "Before."

"Before."

"Both of us," said Ferra, and tied the cards back into their bundle, and went down to see her father.



Zale came up the river on the nineteenth, on the second coaster from Emberfell, with the tide.

He came up the quay steps at noon in the long dark coat buttoned on the wrong side and the gold ring in his ear, browner than he had been the winter before last, with a sea bag over one shoulder and a crate under the other arm that smelled, from twenty paces, of oranges; and at the top of the steps he stopped and looked at the town.

"You've been busy," said Zale.

"A little."

"Ferra wrote to me at Emberfell in November. In a hand like a court clerk's. She said there'd be a table and a goose at midwinter, and I was family, and if I came I was to bring spices, and if I didn't come I was to send them." He held out the crate. "So I came."

It was oranges, sixty of them in straw, small and bright and cold. On top, in oilcloth, lay a sack the size of a loaf that rattled; down the side, a cedar box with a sliding lid, twin to the one he had brought two winters ago, which Maren kept over her range and let nobody touch.

"Nutmeg," said Zale. "Cloves. Ginger, dried, and some in syrup. The bark you grate. And those." He nodded at the sack. "Beans, from a coast I'll not try to name for you. You roast them and grind them and they make a drink bitter enough to take the skin off your tongue, and the people who grow them put honey in it and drink it hot of an evening and say it's the best thing in the world. I thought your cook might know what to do with them."

"She won't," said Emeric. "I will."

Zale gave him the look he had been giving him since the first winter, of a man who had last known his brother at fifteen and was still trying to fit the man over the boy.

"Of course you will," he said.

At the keep's gate he stopped again.

"The book," he said. "Have you read it?"

"Ferra counted the signs. Thirty-four, you remember. Then there was a war." Emeric shifted the crate. "It wants your word list. And you at the table, reading the margins aloud."

"Tomorrow, then," said Zale, and went through the gate.

It was the first midwinter they had spent under one roof since Emeric was fifteen. Neither of them said so. Emeric thought of it on the stair, carrying the oranges, and supposed from his brother's back going up ahead of him that Zale was thinking of it too.

They began the next morning in the counting room, with the book open on Ferra's table and Zale's sheet of words beside it: Zale reading the little brown hand in the margins aloud as best he could, Ferra tallying how often each of the thirty-four signs turned up, page by page, in a column of her own, and Emeric hunting the numbers first, because numbers were the one thing every people wrote down. By the twenty-third they had decided the signs were an alphabet, a sound to each. It was a reasonable guess. It was wrong, and it would cost them a week of the new year to find out.



They asked him why on the night before, as Ferra had said they would.

It was past the second bell, in Ferra's room at the top of the east stair, with the shutter closed against the cold and the fire low; Zale asleep in the guest room two stairs and a passage away, and the Hod lying dark in the reeds at the far end of the lake, because Ferra had moved the week's runs to the nights Zale dined at the inn with Saban, and Saban had made sure that he dined at the inn.

Ferra sat on the end of her bed with her book on her knees, Imani in the chair with her boots off and her feet to the coals, and he on the floor between them with his back against the bed, because that was where there was room.

"It's tomorrow," said Ferra.

"Then it's before," said Imani.

So he told them, plainly, as he had told them everything about the other life in the years since the first of it, with the things he did not know left as things he did not know.

"Where I was," he said, "midwinter was the biggest feast of the year. Dark by the middle of the afternoon, and cold, and in the dark of the year everybody put lights in their windows. Little ones, hundreds of them on strings, along the eaves and round the doors, so you could walk down a street at night and every house was lit like a lantern." He looked at the fire. "They brought a tree indoors. A small dark one that kept its needles in winter, and stood it in the main room and hung bright things off it, glass and paper and tin. The children were awake before it was light. And everybody gave everybody something. Not only the children. Your mother a pair of gloves, your neighbour a cake, the man who brought the post a coin in a card." He was quiet. "And people were kinder. For a day. Not all of them. More of them than the rest of the year."

Imani had her chin on her hand.

"And it was holy," she said. She had heard something in how he said it.

"For most of them. Most of the people who kept it were keeping the birthday of their god's son. The son was born in a stable on that night, in the cold, and that was the feast." He looked at his hands. "I never knew what I believed. I don't now. My mother believed it a little. My father didn't, and went along. I kept it for the presents, and the lights, and because it was the one day of the year my whole family sat down at the same table at the same time, my grandmother and the cousins and the uncle nobody liked, and ate too much, and argued, and fell asleep in chairs."

"So you kept a holy day for the presents," said Imani.

"Everybody I knew did." He thought about it. "And the priests had borrowed the date to begin with. From an older feast, one people kept long before there was a god's son to keep, because it was the darkest night of the year and they wanted to light a fire and eat everything in the larder and tell each other the sun would come back."

Ferra made a small sound on the end of the bed.

"Then it's been stolen twice," she said. "It's used to it."

He laughed. He had not expected to, and it went up into the dark over Ferra's bed, and Imani smiled at the fire.

"The quiet song," said Imani. "In the cellar."

"That was the night's song. Everybody sang it on the night before, with candles, in the churches and the streets and the houses." He looked at the fire. "My father sang it worst of anybody."

"You'll play it tomorrow."

"If I'm asked."

"You've been asked," said Imani. "In the cellar. It stands."

Ferra opened her book on her knees, at the page with the five names and the date and the small question mark, and took her pencil and drew one neat line through the question mark, and closed the book again.

"Answered," she said.



The long room had never held so many people at once who were not there on business.

They came up the keep stair from noon on the twenty-fifth in ones and twos, in their good coats, with snow on their shoulders, because it had begun in the night, the first of the winter, and the inner ward was white. The six. The knights; Rylan with Nessa and the children, Finn in a coat that had been somebody else's and Wren in one that had been Finn's. Tess in her chair, carried up the stair chair and all by Osric and Declan, protesting. Sela, with Arthur behind her in a coat with all its buttons. Doran in his hat. Abloh, fetched from the sail loft by Rylan and complaining. Vantic in grey, with nothing in her hands. Zale, as family, at Emeric's right, where nobody had sat before; Ward stood behind him at the door, as he always had, and said nothing about it.

Fletcher had been asked, and had said a shipwright at a duke's table would be talked about in the yard till spring, and that he would be in the shed in the afternoon. Emeric meant to find him there.

Maren came last, from the kitchen, in a clean apron over her best dress, and stood at the end of the table with her arms folded, and then sat down at its foot, which she had not once done in nine years, because Ferra had put a card with her name on it at that place and would not take it away.

Doran asked before the bread. He stood with his hat in his hands and asked it carefully, in the voice he kept for things he wanted to get right.

"Is it a holy day, Grace?"

The table looked at Emeric.

"It's a day for giving," said Emeric. "And for eating too much. Nobody has to believe anything to keep it." He looked at the priest. "Midwinter. A day nobody had claimed. So we have."

Doran looked at him for a breath; and then at the table, at Bram and Ward, whose fiftieths nobody had kept, at Ferra and Imani, at Rylan's children holding very still in their chairs so as not to lose the presents they had been promised. Then he put his hand out over Maren's bread, in its ring on the board in the middle of the table, and said the short blessing he said over the school's bread at the door every morning, and nothing more, and sat down.

"There," said Doran, to his plate. "That's left there."



Maren had been cooking on the gas for three days.

She had taken the dishes from him on the Monday on a sheet in his hand, asked him a great many questions, changed four of his answers, and sent him out; and nobody had been let into the kitchen since but the kitchen girl and Bram's two apprentices, borrowed on the Wednesday to grind Zale's beans in a pepper mill Bram had altered, who came out brown to the elbows and smelling, they said, like the inside of a very expensive cake.

The slow things were hers. The goose had been in its salt since Tuesday, the ham in its glaze since Wednesday, the biscuits in their tins since the night before. But at the bottom of his sheet, under a line, he had written four more dishes in a smaller hand, and beside them: mine, on the day, if you'll have me at your range. Maren had read it twice and looked at him over it for a long while. Then she had said that she would have him at the end burner, and that if he burned anything she would put it on the tally against his name, and that she supposed he would want to stand there and tell the kitchen girl what to do.

He had said no. He would want her knives.

So when Doran had sat down, Emeric stood up, and took his coat off and hung it on the back of his chair, and rolled his sleeves to the elbow; and Maren, at the foot of the table, untied her clean apron and tied it again tighter, and got up too.

"Where are you going?" said Finn.

"To cook," said Emeric.

The table looked at him. Then it looked at Maren, who looked back at it with her arms folded and offered it nothing at all, and the two of them went down the stair.



It took about as long as it took the kitchen girl to run to the well and back.

She came through the scullery door with her bucket and saw His Grace at the end of Maren's long table in his shirtsleeves with Maren's second-best knife in his hand and a sack of onions open at his feet, and stood with the bucket swinging, and then put it down and went straight back out the way she had come. By the time the first onion was in pieces the two laundry women were in the doorway. By the second, the garrison cook from the mess, who had come to borrow a sieve and forgot what for. Then Finn, who had come down the stair on his own because nobody had told him not to, with Wren behind him holding the back of his coat; then Rylan, after them; then Nessa, after Rylan; and then, because the long room had seen the children go and had nothing to do but wait, everybody else.

Tess came down the stair in her chair on her own this time, at the speed that made Osric shout.

The kitchen had been built to feed a castle and it had never in its life held a castle. Abloh was in the flour. Saban had got himself onto the end of the dresser beside the plates and was sitting there with his legs crossed as if he had paid for the seat. Sela stood against the door to the cold store and kept a hand on it so nobody would let the cold out, and every time somebody leaned on her she leaned back. Bram was the size of two men and could not get past the copper. Three of the garrison had come in from the yard with snow on their boots and were standing in the wet of it on Maren's flags.

Maren lasted a quarter of an hour. Then she put her knife down on the board, very quietly, and turned round.

"Out," she said.

Nobody went out. They had come to see His Grace cook. She could see that they had, and she had nine years of feeding every one of them, and she drew a breath for the second time with her face going a dark plum colour from the neck up; and Emeric, beside her, wiping the knife on his apron, looked at the window over the sink, at the white light coming in off the inner ward.

"No," he said. "She's right. There's no room." He looked round the kitchen, at all of them, at Finn with his chin on the end of the table. "Everybody out. Into the yard. We'll cook out there."

Maren turned on him.

"In the snow," she said.

"In the snow."

"On what?"

He was already at the back door.



The plate was the old cooling plate out of the forge behind Bram's house, which had stood against the forge wall for twenty years since Bram's father had stopped using it: a slab of iron as long as a man was tall and as thick as two fingers, black with old scale. Bram sent his apprentices for it with the handcart, and they came back up through the gate of the inner ward with it at a run, steaming in the cold, and four of the garrison and Bram and Osric lifted it off the cart and onto two of the mess's trestles in the middle of the white yard, and stood back blowing.

The yard had filled while they were gone. The long room was there, all of it, in its good coats. The stable boys. The women from the laundry and the kitchen girl and her sister, who did not work at the castle and had been fetched. Half the watch off the wall, who would hear about it from Ward later and had decided it was worth it. The snow came down on all of them, slow and thick, and settled on their hats.

Emeric put his hand under the plate, a foot below it, flat, palm up, and held it there.

Nothing showed for a moment. Then the snow that was falling on the iron began to vanish before it touched it, and then the iron began to tick, and then the air over it began to shake. He walked down the length of the trestles with his hand under the plate, slowly, and where he walked he left the heat behind him, more at the near end and less at the far, so that a man who held his hand over it could feel the plate go from a forge to a hearth to a warm stone in the length of it.

"Three heats," he said to Maren. "Sear there. Cook there. Keep it warm there."

Maren held her hand over each end, and over the middle, and took it back.

"You've no stone in your hand," she said.

"No."

She looked at him for a moment longer, and then she went inside to fetch the beef, and did not say anything else about it.

Finn had climbed onto a water butt to see. Wren was on Rylan's shoulders with her mittens on Rylan's ears. Emeric looked up at the two of them, and at the yard, and at Ferra and Imani standing together near the front with their arms through each other's against the cold and their faces doing their best to say nothing; and something in him that had been quiet for nine years, under the work, sat up.

He was going to show off. He knew it, and decided he did not care.



He began with an onion.

He took it from the sack, a big yellow one from Milford, and showed it to Finn, and threw it straight up, high, as high as the stable roof. The yard watched it go. At the top it stopped. It hung there in the falling snow, turning very slowly, with the faintest violet flicker under it that most of the yard would swear afterward it had not seen.

Then he opened the door.

Not all the way. The hair's width, the thread of the delve he could hold open behind his eyes; and through it the yard went slow, the way it always did, the snow coming down a flake at a time like something being lowered on strings, Finn's mouth opening by stages, a breath going out of Wren in a long white feather. He let the onion down. It came down past his face so slowly that he had time to choose where the knife went, and he put it there, and there, and there, eight times, crossways and lengthways, with the mana gone down into his wrist and his fingers so that the blade went exactly where he sent it and nowhere else; and he let the door go.

The onion landed on the cool end of the plate in one piece. Then it fell open, all at once, in rings, a stack of white rings from the widest to the smallest, each sitting inside the one below, like a tower made by a child.

The yard did not make any sound at all.

He took the small jug of spirit from the oldest cask, which Maren had put by for her pudding you drink, and poured a thumb of it into the top of the tower, and put one finger to the bottom ring.

It went up like a chimney. A column of blue and yellow flame as high as his head came roaring out of the top of the onion and stood there over the plate, with the snow hissing into it, and the whole yard went back a step at once, and Finn fell off the water butt.

Then everybody was shouting. Wren was screaming on Rylan's shoulders with both hands up in the air, and Rylan was laughing with his head back and his eyes shut, and Finn was up again out of the snow with it all down his front, shouting "Again, again, do it again," and Emeric, who had not planned to, did it again.

Behind him at the cool end of the plate, Ward had come to stand with his arms folded.

"That's a deal of violet," said Ward, very low, "for a feast day."

"It's a day for giving," said Emeric.

Ward looked at Zale, at the front of the crowd with the collar of his long coat up, watching his brother with an expression nobody could read; and at Doran beside him with his hat in both hands; and then at the children. He made a sound in his nose and went back to the gate, and did not say anything else about it all afternoon.



The beef was a sirloin of Milford's, off a beast from the upland grass, and Maren had hung it a fortnight. He cut it across the grain on her board, at the trestle's end, in the cold, into slices so thin that when Abloh held one up to the white sky he could see the snow through it. He did not use the door for that. He did not need to. He had cut ten thousand things on a board on his other world, in small kitchens, most of them his own, late at night, for nobody but himself; his hands knew it the way his feet knew the stair, and the mana in his wrists only made them steadier than cold hands had any right to be.

Maren watched his knife. Then she went to the other end of the trestle and took the fish.

There were six of them, trout from the Mere, cleaned that morning. Emeric had meant to fillet them himself. Maren did not ask. She took her own small knife, the old one with the handle bound in cord, and did all six in the time he did the beef: down the back, round the head, the whole side off the bones in one long pull, and the bones out of each side after with her thumb and the tip of the blade, and the skin left on, scored, because the skin was the best of it. When she had finished she put the six pairs of sides on the plate in front of him without looking at him and wiped the knife.

"You'd have left the pin bones in," she said.

"I would have."

"Everybody does. Except me." She jerked her chin at the plate. "Go on, then."

He laid the fish on the hot end skin-down and pressed each one flat with the back of his hand, a breath each, and the skins went gold and loud. The beef went on beside it, a slice at a time, and came off almost as fast as it went on, brown at the edges and red in the middle, onto the warm end. Leeks, split and washed and cut on the slant. Carrots, cut so fine they curled. Half a cabbage in ribbons. A spoonful of Zale's ginger out of its syrup, and a knob of butter from the bay's dairy that ran across the iron and took the ginger with it in a brown tide. He moved the whole of it with two flat iron spatulas Bram had made him for the purpose without asking what the purpose was, turning it, sweeping it, tossing it, with the spatulas going faster than the eye could follow and clanging on the plate in a rhythm, until Wren on Rylan's shoulders began to clap in time to it and the yard caught it from her and clapped too.

When the fish was done he flicked a piece of it up off the plate, a small piece, crisp skin and white flake, in a high slow curve over the trestle toward Finn.

"Open," he said.

Finn opened. The piece of fish came down in the middle of his mouth as if it had been put there, and Finn shut his mouth on it and stood with his eyes very wide and chewed, and then roared, because it was hot; and then roared for more.

Every child in the yard wanted one after that. There were nine of them, counting the stable boys, who had decided they were children for the afternoon. He did all of them. One of the stable boys missed and it went down his collar.



The second dish took longer, and he made them wait for it.

Flour. Eggs, a dozen of Milford's, cracked one-handed into a well in the flour on Maren's floured board at the end of the trestle while the snow came down on the board and melted in the heat off the plate beside it. He worked it with his fingers and then his palms until it was a dough the colour of butter and as smooth as a cheek, and rolled it out with Maren's pin, thinner, and thinner, until it was a sheet so thin that when he held a corner up Wren could see his hand through it. Then he dusted it and rolled it on itself into a long loose log, and took the long knife.

He opened the door again for this, because he wanted every cut the same. The yard went slow. He cut the log across, a hair's breadth at a time, and every cut was a hair's breadth, the same, down the whole length of it; then he let the door go and took a handful of the cut pieces and shook them out, and they fell open into threads. Long pale threads, a yard long, as fine as string.

"It's a bread," said Sela, at the front. "You've cut a bread into string."

"It's a bread you boil," said Emeric.

Maren's new stockpot had come out from under the sacking in the scullery for the purpose, full of water that Osric and Declan carried between them on a pole. He set it on the plate's hot end and put his hand under the iron there and the water was boiling in the time it took to salt it. The threads went in in handfuls. He stirred them once with a long fork, and left them.

The sauce was what took the time, and he had made the start of it the night before at the end burner after Maren had gone up, and did not tell the yard that. Tomatoes from the hothouse on the warm side of the works, the last of the autumn's, cooked down for two hours on the gas with Milford sugar and the still house's vinegar and two of Zale's cloves until they were thick and dark and sweet and sharp at once, and pushed through a sieve: a red paste in a crock. Now he put a pan on the hot end, a wide iron one, and put into it butter, and an onion cut small, and three cloves of garlic crushed under the flat of the knife with one blow of his palm that made the stable boys flinch; and then the sausage, Kaldo's own, the smoked one the garrison cook made, cut into coins; and when the coins had browned at the edges he put in the red paste, and a little of the boiling water from the pot, and black pepper ground over it with a heavy hand, and the juice of two of Zale's oranges, the sour ones from the bottom of the crate that nobody had wanted to eat.

It wanted something else. A pepper. A small one, round and wrinkled like a little bright hat, so hot that a man cutting it washed his hands twice before he touched his eyes; there had been one in it every time he had eaten it, on the morning of the feast, in a kitchen on his other world, at a table with a grandmother at one end and the uncle nobody liked at the other. There was nothing like it in Kaldo. He had asked Zale, the first winter, in a roundabout way, and Zale had said there were fruits on the spice coast that burned like coals and that sailors ate them on a dare. So it was out there. Over the water, somewhere. He stood with the spoon in his hand for a moment in the snow, and thought about that, and about that table; and then he put in a little more pepper than he meant to, and a little of Zale's dried ginger, which was not the same and never would be, and tasted it, and decided it would do for this year.

When he looked up, Ferra was watching him. Not the pan. Him.

"From home?" she said, under the noise, too low for anybody but him and Imani beside her to hear.

"From home."

Imani said nothing. She took the spoon out of his hand, and tasted it herself, and gave it back.

"It wants something," she said.

"It does."

"Then go and get it, one day," said Imani, and went back to her place.

He drained the threads through Maren's big colander into the snow, where they went up in a cloud of steam that hid him from the yard entirely for a breath, and came out of the cloud with the colander in one hand and turned the whole of it into the red pan, and tossed it. The pan was the size of a cart wheel and full. He did not lift it with his arms. The violet came and went under it and the whole pan rose an inch off the iron, and he gave it a turn of his wrists, and the yard of red threads and sausage went up in the air above the pan in one piece and turned over and came down into it again in one piece, and not one thread went into the snow.

Somebody at the back cheered as if it were a horse race.



The third was for the children, and he did it last, when the light was going and the snow had turned blue.

Thin cakes first: batter of milk and eggs and flour poured out on the middle of the plate in rounds, six at once, and spread with the back of a ladle in one turn each until they were as thin as the pasta sheet had been, and freckled gold underneath in a breath. He turned them by throwing them. All six went up together off the plate, and he opened the door, and they hung at the top and turned over in the slow air, one, two, three, four, five, six, like pages, and came down on the iron again each in its own place, and the door shut.

Finn had stopped shouting. He was standing in front of the trestle with his mouth open and snow on his eyelashes.

While the cakes kept warm Emeric made the sauce in the wide pan: butter, sugar from Milford's sugar house, the zest of two oranges taken off in long curls with the point of the knife so fine that the curls held their spiral in the air when he lifted them out, and the juice of six more, until the pan was full of something bright and thick and shining that smelled like the whole of summer in the middle of the snow. He folded the cakes in four, one by one, and laid them in the orange in a ring.

Then he looked round for Wren.

She was on the trestle by then, at the cool end, because Rylan's shoulders had got tired, with Nessa's hands round her middle. He beckoned her over, as near as Nessa would let her come, and gave her the jug of spirit from the oldest cask, and closed her two small mittened hands round it with his own over them, and they poured it together, a good long pour, over the cakes in the pan.

"Now," he said. "Point at it."

Wren pointed at it.

The pan went up in a sheet of blue fire from edge to edge, low and wide and dancing, blue at the bottom and gold at the top, and the snow coming down into it vanished an arm's length above it, and the whole dark yard went blue; and Wren, who had never in her small life made anything burn, looked at the fire coming out of the end of her own finger and then up at Emeric with a face he would remember when he had forgotten most other things.

He did not tell her. Nobody told her. Rylan, with his arm round Nessa, opened his mouth to, and Nessa put her elbow in his ribs.



They ate in the yard, because by then there was no getting anybody back up the stair.

The garrison brought out the mess's long trestles and benches and set them down the middle of the ward in a row under the arcade, out of the worst of the snow, and Maren had the kitchen girl and her sister and the laundry women carry everything down from the long room, and up from the kitchen, and out: the goose on its board with the apples round it, and the ham shining like a saddle, and Maren's bread in its ring; potatoes; carrots with Zale's ginger in them that Doran said he would like to be buried with; and, between them, still hot off the iron, Emeric's beef and fish and the yard-long red threads in their pan, and the orange cakes. Hot cider with cloves and orange peel went down the benches in jugs, and red wine warmed the same; and for the children, and anybody else who would admit to it, which was most of the benches by the end, a thick dark drink in small cups, sweet and bitter at once, that Finn tried and put down and looked at and picked up again, and that Saban drank three of and then sat with his hands round the fourth and an expression of great peace.

Wren ate the red threads with her hands, which nobody stopped her doing, because there was no other way to eat them that anybody could see. Bram watched her. Then he put his fork down and did the same, and after Bram, so did the knights, and then the benches, until the whole long row under the arcade was eating string with its fingers in the snow and red to the wrists.

Maren carried the last bowl down herself: something cold and yellow and thick, with nutmeg on top.

"Eggs and cream," she said, setting it down. "And sugar. And spirit from the oldest cask in the still house, what's left of it after His Grace has set fire to most of it, and his nutmeg." She jerked her head at Zale. "He says it's a drink. I say it's a pudding you drink. Don't have more than a cup or you'll have Doran's blessing back up."

Imani had two cups.

At the end, when nobody could have eaten anything, came hard brown biscuits cut in shapes, little men and stars and the gold rose, smelling of ginger, and Sela put the broken ones in her handkerchief for the school; a sauce of sugar taken slowly to amber and let down with cream, which had not burned, because a flame on a dial held steady where a fire stone's notches would not; and in a frosted jar brought down in a bucket of ice, which nobody in that yard needed, something soft and cold and the colour of the sauce, which Maren had turned in her new ice-cream churn for an hour with a cold stone in its jacket and the kitchen girl taking turns.

The benches ate it in silence. Wren had hers on her nose.

"Where does it come from?" said Sela at last, looking at the cold stuff on her spoon as if it might be a trick. "Any of it. I've never had any of it."

"Out of a book," said Emeric.

It was true of half of what he knew. He remembered every book he had ever read, and the benches knew it.

Zale put his spoon down.

"I've had every one of them," he said. "Abroad." He looked down the row, quite serious. "That drink, in a port across the western sea, out of a bowl the size of my head. The biscuits at a fair in the north, cut in the shape of the fair's saint. The cold one at a governor's house on the spice coast, made with snow off a mountain." He picked his spoon up again. "Not as good as these. But I've had them."

Ferra looked at Imani. Imani looked at her dish.

"You've had everything abroad," said Bram.

"I've been abroad a great deal," said Zale. He looked at the red pan, scraped clean. "Not those, mind." He turned the fork over in his fingers. "And I've never in my life seen a man cook like that. Not in any port. Not in any governor's house." He looked at his brother along the bench, a long look, the kind he had been giving him since the first winter. "Where did you learn that?"

"A kitchen," said Emeric.

Zale waited for the rest. There was no rest. After a while he nodded, as if that were an answer, and went back to his biscuit.

Maren had sat down at last at the foot of the bench, with her apron still on and a cup of her own pudding in her hands. She had not eaten anything. She was looking at Emeric, down the length of the row, with her head a little on one side, the way she looked at a loaf she had not made herself to see if it had risen right.

"I thought you were a man with a sheet of paper," she said. "Nine years. I thought you read it in a book and told me, and I did the cooking." She drank. "You've burned your hand."

He looked. He had, on the heel of his thumb, on the pan, and had not noticed.

"A bit."

"A bit." Maren looked at Ferra, at his left, and Imani, at his right, both of them red to the wrists from the string like everybody else. "If I was thirty years younger," she said to them, loudly, to the whole bench, "I'd have to throw my hat in, and the pair of you would have a fight on."

The bench went up. Rylan had to put his head on the table. Ferra's ears went scarlet to the tips and stayed that way, and she did not look at Maren and did not look at him; it was Imani who leaned forward, and looked Maren up and down along the bench with great seriousness, as she looked at a patient she had not yet decided about.

"You'd win on the knife," said Imani.

"I'd win on the knife," Maren agreed. "And on the fish. And on the beef, which he cut too thick at the end, I saw it." She got up, and collected the scraped red pan off the board, and looked into it. "Write that one down for me. With the measures. Before you forget it."

"I won't forget it."

"Write it down anyway," said Maren, and took the pan inside.

He held his cup out for the cider when it came by and found that his hand was not quite steady on it. Not much. The door held open by its thread, again and again, and the heat for an afternoon, and a pan the size of a cart wheel; it had added up to more than he had let himself count, and it had come to collect now that it was over. He put the cup down rather than let anybody see it shake. Imani saw it anyway. She did not say anything. She put two fingers on the inside of his wrist under the table, as if she were holding his hand, which she was, and left them there for the count of a minute, and took them away, and went on talking to Sela about the school.



Saban stood up with his cup when the dishes had gone to the side.

"Five," he said. "I'm told. Five decadays at this table that nobody kept, because there was a war on, or a sickness, or a roomful of roses, and nobody noticed till the Chancellor counted." He lifted the cup. "His Grace's thirtieth. Mistress Imani's thirtieth. Sir Ward's fiftieth. Master Bram's fiftieth. And the Chancellor's, in August, which she knew about and told nobody, and which I'd have charged her for keeping quiet if she'd asked."

The table laughed. Ferra's ears went scarlet.

"Late," said Saban. "But kept."

They drank; Ward drank at the gate, standing. Bram put his cup down and said he had not been fifty in the spring, he had been fifty since he was about thirty, and he did not see the fuss; and Maren told him from the foot of the table that the fuss was a goose, and if he did not want a goose for his fiftieth he could give it back.



Then the presents, upstairs, with the long room's fire built up to the roar and everybody's boots steaming on the hearth.

He had been making them since the autumn, for midwinter, long before Ferra untied her cards: in the old chamber in the cellar, on the liquid feed, a small thing at a time; in Bram's shop after the shift; in Ivo's glass house and Ottoline's room and the long shop; and one of them at Bram's own anvil, by hand, with Bram kept away. He had made something for everybody at the table, because where he had been raised midwinter meant everybody got something and he could not have let it go by bare; Ferra's count had only told him that five of them were owed twice over.

Maren's came first, because she was nearest the stair. It came up in three crates on Osric's back: a kitchen. Pots and pans in a pale grey metal that shone without polishing, a stockpot she could have bathed a child in, ladles and a skimmer and five mixing bowls in a nest, and a roll of knives in a leather case, every edge charged in the forge.

"Stainless steel," he said. "It'll not rust, or pit, or dull, or turn the sauce black. Not in your lifetime."

Maren ran her thumb down the flat of one knife in the candlelight. Then she put it back, very carefully, and rolled the case up, and sat with it on her knee.

"And this," said Emeric, and gave her the book.

It was bound in brown at the works' press, with MAREN'S KITCHEN on the cover in the printer's best type and nothing else. Inside was every recipe she had ever given anybody, taken down by Ferra's clerks over a month of evenings with Maren dictating and swearing at their spelling; and at the back, the new ones. The eggs and cream. The burned sugar. The biscuits. The churn. The beans.

Maren opened it, and looked at the first page, and shut it.

"There's a mistake on page nine," she said. "The bread wants longer."

"Then it'll be right in the second printing."

Maren looked at him. Then she got up with the knives under one arm and the book under the other, and said to nobody that she had a sauce on, and went down; and Imani, who was nearest the stair, said afterward that there had been no sauce on, and that Maren had stopped on the landing below with her forehead against the wall for a count of a hundred before she went on.



Imani's was a case of dark leather the length of her forearm.

She opened it on the table and sat very still. Instruments; her own, not the ward's. Blades in a row on their small handles, three sizes and a curved one; forceps, short and long; clamps; a needle holder; a probe; scissors so small they looked made for a doll. Every handle was the same grey metal as Maren's kitchen, and cut into the end of every one was her mark, the I she signed on a chart. Every edge had been charged in the forge; they would not want a stone for years.

"Nobody borrows them," he said.

Imani took out the smallest blade, looked at it, and put it back exactly in its place.

The other thing he gave her in a box of its own. A short pale tube branched like a Y, the two upper arms ending in small plugs of polished bone, the stem running down to a round silver cup the size of a large coin with a thin pale skin stretched over its face like the head of a very small drum. The tube bent without kinking. It was the white stuff out of Rowena's oil, drawn hot through a die; the skin was the same, rolled thin as paper.

"The ends go in your ears," he said. "The cup on the chest. The skin takes the sound of whatever's under it and the tube brings it up to you. A stethoscope."

Imani put the plugs in her ears slowly, as if she expected them to bite. Then, without asking, because she never had, she leaned across and put the silver cup flat on his chest, on the left, over his coat, and held it there.

The table had gone quiet, watching her face.

Her face did something it had never done at that table. It opened. Her eyes went wide and then soft, and her mouth came a little open, and she held the cup on his chest and did not move; and he understood that she was hearing it. Not counting it through her fingers at a wrist, as she had a thousand times since the first summer. Hearing it: the heart itself, the two shut sounds of it, close and loud as a knock on a door, in her own ears.

"Sixty-two," said Imani, after a while. It was not her ward voice. "Loud."

She took the cup away and the plugs out and sat with the thing in her lap, and did not thank him, because Imani did not thank anybody at a table for anything. She said, quite quietly, "I'll listen to everybody in the ward with it on Monday. Everybody. Even the cut fingers."

"And this," he said, and gave her a card.

On it, in his hand, were the outlines of her two hands, drawn round in pencil one night in November while she slept on the long room's bench and did not know; every measurement written small along the fingers; and under them a drawing of two hand-shaped blocks of glazed clay on stands.

Imani read it twice, front and back, as she read a dosing card.

"What's this?"

"Gloves. For surgery. Thin enough to feel through, and nothing gets in." He put his finger on the drawing. "They're made from a tree's sap, and there's none of it here yet. In the spring."

"Spring," said Imani, and put the card inside her bodice, over her heart, where she had worn a gold rose to the investiture, and did not take it out again all evening.



Ferra's was a box the size of a loaf, brass and dark wood, with a crank on its side, a row of small windows along its top with figures behind them, and a row of slotted levers along its front.

"A pinwheel calculator," he said. "You set the figure with the levers. Turn the crank once and it adds. Back, and it takes away. So many times, and it multiplies. The wheels were cut in the forge, every tooth. It'll not make a mistake."

Ferra set a figure and turned the crank, and the windows clicked over. She set another and turned it four times and watched the windows change, and he saw her check it in her head and get there before the wheels did.

On the end of the crank, cut into the brass, was her rose: one rose, two leaves, a ring, the seal she had drawn for the duchy at the first table in June.

"It's slower than me," said Ferra.

"It's not for you. It's for everybody who argues with you. When you tell them the figure and they don't believe you, you turn it round and let them crank it themselves."

Ferra looked at him across the corner of the table. Then she set another figure and turned the crank, and her ears were pink to the tops.

She used it every day from then on. Never on her own figures. Always on other people's.



Ward's was a coat.

Abloh had cut it in the sail loft, in Kaldo black, from the heaviest cloth the coasters brought: long to below the knee, hooded, and lined right through with a second black so close and heavy that the seams had taken him a week of evenings. Abloh gave it to Ward himself, at the door, and stood back with his arms folded.

Ward held it up at arm's length.

"It is a coat," said Ward.

"Hang it over the door," said Emeric.

Ward looked at him. Then he hung it by the hood from the peg on the inside of the long room's door, so that it covered the door from lintel to floor, and sent Rylan out onto the landing with a candle to hold at the crack, and looked.

No light came through. Not at the seams, not at the hem, not at the hood.

He had pinned his own cloak over doors and shutters since the road south, wherever Emeric's light might be seen, without being asked and without once saying so; and the cloak was a rag, worn through at the corners where the pins went, and everybody at the table had seen it, and nobody had ever said so to Ward.

"It keeps the rain off as well," said Emeric.

Ward took the coat down off the door and put it on. It fitted; Abloh had measured him off the old one. He went back to his place at the door in it, and stood there, and did not take it off for the rest of the evening.



Bram's was a hammer.

Emeric had made it at Bram's own anvil, in the old forge behind Bram's house, over a fortnight of evenings, by hand, with Ferra keeping her father at the works. Nothing from the cellar in it. Iron from the works' yard, drawn and folded and drawn again, the eye punched with a drift he had made first, the haft of ash. He had made it as a man makes a thing for the first time with his own hands when he has only ever made it in his head. It was a good hammer. It was not a very good one.

Bram held it in his big black-seamed hand and sighted down the face against the candle with one eye shut.

"It's crooked," he said.

"A little."

"A hair. The face is a hair off the haft. You punched the eye wrong. Second heat, I'd guess; you let it cool." He hefted it. "Too much in the head, as well."

"I know."

Bram looked at it a breath longer. Then he put it through his belt at the hip, where his own hammer had hung every working day for forty years, and left it there. He used it at the forge from the next morning on, and when anybody at the works asked why the master smith used a crooked hammer, he said it was the best hammer he had ever been given, and that if they wanted one like it they could learn to make it badly themselves.



Tess had hers apart before the next present was open.

It was two things in a box: a small machine like the dynamo's younger brother, iron and copper, with a crank; and beside it a round iron motor with no brushes at all. No commutator, nothing to spark, nothing to wear, nothing to clean; a smooth iron drum inside a ring of coils.

"The little one's an alternator," he said. "It makes a current that runs one way and then the other, back and forth, fifty times a second. The motor's an induction motor. It runs on that, and nothing in it touches."

Tess spun the drum with one finger and watched it run down.

"Nothing touches," she said.

She had the end plate off with the small screwdriver she kept in her sleeve, and the drum out on the cloth, and her nose an inch from the coils, counting windings under her breath; and Osric said she might wait till the presents were done, and Tess said without looking up that he might hold this, and gave him the end plate, and he held it through the next four presents.



Armand's was a book.

One. Bound in dark blue at the works' press, plain, with no title, and a small lock on its clasp and a key on a cord. Inside it was his dissertation, every table and every word in the tower's form, set in type by one compositor at night from the manuscript Ferra had let out of its box for a fortnight in November and then locked away again; and printed once.

"The edition is one," said Emeric.

Armand unlocked it and read the first line of his own prose in print with his spectacles on the end of his nose.

"It's in the tower's form," he said.

"It's in the tower's form."

"Then it's published. In an edition of one, which is the only edition it can ever safely have." He locked it and hung the key round his neck under his shirt, and laid his hand flat on the blue cover. "This one I'll read."



Zale's was a walnut case, brass-cornered, with a lid that opened on two compartments.

In the first, in a brass bowl hung in two rings one inside the other, so that it stayed level however the case was tipped, sat a compass: not a needle on a pin, like the one Zale carried in his coat, but a round card marked all the way round with the points and the degrees, floating in clear liquid under glass on a jewelled pivot, cut true in the forge to a hair. Zale picked the case up and tipped it, rocked it, swung it as a deck swings in a sea. The bowl swung in its rings. The card did not move at all.

In the second, a clock in a brass drum behind glass, white-faced, with a second hand. Ottoline had made it, all but the hairspring and the balance, which had come out of the cellar.

"A liquid compass and a marine chronometer," said Emeric. "One keeps north. The other keeps time at sea, rolling, to a breath in a week. Set it in the harbour you sail from, and anywhere you are, the difference between it and the sun's noon tells you how far west you've come."

Zale tipped the case again, gently, as a man tests a thing he cannot believe.

"A needle on a pin swings like a drunk in a blow," he said. "In a big sea you can't read it at all." He touched the glass over the card. "Do you know how many ships the western sea takes because a man in a blow couldn't read his needle?"

"No."

"Neither does anybody. That's the trouble." He closed the case and buckled it and sat with both hands on the lid. "Where did you get this?"

"Out of a book."

"You keep saying that," said Zale.



The rest went round fast, because the children could not wait any longer and nobody could make them.

Saban's was a spyglass of Ivo's lenses that folded into itself until it was the size of a pen case; he said it would be very useful for reading other people's letters across a square, and put it away with deep content. Sela's was a lamp for the school room's night classes, a bulb under a green glass shade, which she looked at, and then at Arthur, and then at Emeric, and said would do. Doran's was a dusty bottle from the oldest cask in the still house, laid down in the second year, which he held to the candle and said was the first drink he had ever been given that he would not have to apologise for. Abloh's was a sewing machine in a wooden case, a needle that went up and down when a wheel was turned and a hook and shuttle under the plate that made a stitch faster than any hand; he said it had better not be faster than him, and then asked if there were more. Declan's was a level of brass and glass, its vial ground true in the cellar; he laid it on the table and said the table was out by a hair at the far end, and Bram said it had been out by a hair since the old baron.

And Rylan's.

His own was a stopwatch, a small brass watch that opened with a press of the thumb, white-faced, with one long hand that went round once a minute and a button on its crown that started the hand and stopped it and sent it back to the top.

"For the yard," said Emeric. "The jumps."

"I've been counting under my breath for nine years," said Rylan. "Badly."

But he was not looking at the watch any more. He was looking at the two boxes that had come up the stair last, and at his children opening them.

In Finn's was a man a hand high, in a black suit of plates, every plate and buckle and strap cut to the size of a seed and fitted, the guards on the forearms and the high collar, and on its back a black lump the size of a walnut, which was all the town had ever seen of what the suit could do. The forge could cut plates and could not cut faces, and Emeric had not made it try; but it came out of the box standing as Rylan stood on the wall before a jump, weight on the back foot and one hand open, and Finn knew at once whose it was and held it up and shouted.

It bent at the shoulders and elbows and hips and knees, on pins too fine to see. He crouched it on the table among the cups and then flung it up over the table and caught it and shouted again, because that was what his father did off the wall in the yard, and Finn had seen him do it a hundred times.

In Wren's was a glass ball on a wooden foot, the size of a large apple, full of water; and in the water, on a round of painted ground in front of a little door with a red step, four small figures: a man in a black coat, a woman with her hair up, a boy, and a very small girl. Wren held it in both hands and did not understand it, and turned it over to look at its foot, as a child does; and when she turned it back the water was full of snow, white flakes going round and coming slowly down over the four of them at their own front door, as the snow outside the window was coming down over the inner ward.

Wren said nothing at all. She held it in front of her face and watched the snow settle.

Nessa had her hand over her mouth.

Rylan looked at his son under the table, making the black soldier jump off a chair leg; and at his daughter turning the glass over to make the snow come again; and at the four small figures at their own door. Then he put the watch in his pocket and got up and went out past Ward and stood on the landing with his back to the door.

Nobody followed him. After a while Nessa got up and went out, and the door stayed open a crack, and nobody looked at it.

When Rylan came back he had his face in order. He sat down and picked up his cup.

"Somebody," he said to the table, "had better make Finn a smaller suit, or he'll try that off the wall himself."



Imani asked him to play when the children had gone quiet.

She asked it across the table as she would have asked for the salt: "You said you'd play it on the day. It's the day." The table looked at her, and then at him, because nobody at it but two people knew what she meant.

He had brought the guitar up that morning in the felt case Abloh had made for it, and left it behind the door, without Tess's box or the horn; Ferra had said that if the horn came up the stair on a feast day she would leave by the window.

"Still a box," said Bram, who had put the rod down its neck.

He played the bright one first, for the children: the four chords, quick and light, not sung. Wren put her snow globe down and stood up on her chair and began to bounce, and Finn came out from under the table and made the black soldier dance on the cloth, and Osric, who had never in his life been able to keep out of anything, began to beat time on the table with the flat of his hand the second time round, and then half the table was doing it, and Tess was laughing in her chair with the motor's drum in her lap.

Zale was not beating the table. He sat very still with the walnut case on his knees, listening with his head on one side.

"I don't know that," he said, when it had finished. "I've had every tune in every port on the western sea sung at me, in forty languages. I've never heard that one, or anything like it." He frowned a little, as if it bothered him. "Where's it from?"

"Out of a book," said Ferra from the other end of the table, before Emeric could, with perfect gravity; and Imani put her face in her hands.

Then he played the other one.

He played it slowly, in its three, as he had in the cellar; and the table, which had been laughing, stopped at the first bar without being asked. The candles had burned down. The snow came past the window. He played it through twice and did not sing it. The words were in the other language, and the tune would do on its own.

Nobody moved while he played.

When it was done Doran was sitting with his hat in his lap and his eyes on the guitar.

"That's a hymn," said Doran.

Emeric looked at him.

"I don't know it. I don't need to." Doran turned his hat once on his knee. "I've sung at gravesides and fonts all my life, and I know the shape of a thing that's sung to God in the dark. That's one."

"It's a lullaby," said Emeric.

"In my trade," said Doran, "that's the same thing."

It was Saban, with his fourth cup gone cold in his hands, who started the last thing of the afternoon, while the candles were being changed: everybody at the table was to name the best present given that day, and not their own. They went round. Bram said the snow globe. Maren, back up the stair, said the stethoscope, because she had been told about the face. Zale said the hammer, and Bram looked at him with deep suspicion. Ferra said the coat. Imani said the coat. Tess said the motor, and was told that was her own, and said she knew.

They came to Ward last, at the door, in it.

Ward considered the question with the gravity he gave a map.

"The coat was good," said Ward.

Osric went first, and then Declan, and then Rylan, with his face down in the cloth and his shoulders going; the three of them who had been on the road south with Ward nine years ago, when fifty Gold did not keep the rain off and the crystals did not do anything, laughing until they could not breathe, until Declan had to hold the table's edge and Osric was wiping his eyes on his sleeve, while the rest of the table watched them without the least idea why, and Ward stood at the door in his coat and waited, with no expression at all, for them to finish.



Late in the afternoon, with the long room loud with the end of the feast, he went down to the shed by the lake with a roll of paper under his arm.

Fletcher was at the bench at the back under the lamp, with the K Discovery's long shape behind him in the dark on her cradle and the snow blowing in at the half-open door. Emeric put the roll down in front of him without a word, and Fletcher untied it.

They were drawings: every hull Fletcher had built for him, from the K Discovery on. Not the drawings Fletcher had been given. The true ones, every line of what each hull was and what it was for, drawn fair from Emeric's own sheets; and in the bottom corner of every sheet, in the box where a builder's name goes, in the same fair hand: Fletcher.

Fletcher turned them over one at a time and read every one, and came to the end, and turned back to the first.

"You could have given me these the day I started," he said.

"No."

"No," Fletcher agreed. He rolled them up and tied the tape. "But you could have." He put the roll under his arm. "I'll keep them at home, under the bed. Anybody finds them, they'll think it's a will."

"It's not a will."

"It's the only thing I'd leave anybody," said Fletcher, and went back to his bench, and picked up his plane, and did not look round again.



Vantic was in the counting room when he came back up, as he had asked her to be, sitting at Ferra's table with no lamp lit, watching the snow.

He lit the lamp and put three things on the table between them and sat down across from her.

The first was a drawing of a tower: glass from the street to the sky, thirty storeys, standing on a broad street at the edge of a ring of water. Over its doors, cut in the stone in letters a man's height: HOUSE DUCATIS. Under it lay a map, Linus's survey of the four baronies on one sheet, with the rail lines that were not yet laid drawn on it in red: the coast to Wayford, Wayford to the border of the capital road, the branches to every market town.

The third was a single sheet of the counting room's best paper, written fair in the chancery's form. A charter, naming House Ducatis the bank of the crown of Randillia: the bank that would hold the crown's accounts and issue its paper and answer for both to a written limit and an open audit. At its foot, the place for the king's name and the king's seal, blank.

Vantic looked at the three of them in the lamplight and did not touch any of them.

"That's not a decaday present," she said at last.

"No."

"That's a proposal." She looked at the blank at the foot of the charter. "Whose name goes there?"

"Mine."

Vantic looked up.

He told her. Not where he had come from; that was not hers, and never would be. All of what he meant to do. That he meant to be king, if it came to that, and he thought it would. Not for the crown; to end what the cellar had been, and take it apart from the inside, slowly, over thirty years, office by office, until there was nothing left that only a lord could do. No more nobles. Schools and offices, and people in them because they were good at the work. And in the middle of it a bank that answered to its books and not to a lord, and he would rather it were hers than anybody's in the world.

Vantic heard all of it without moving. When he had finished she was quiet a while.

"Then I'll tell you what you'd be buying," she said, "so that it's been said once."

"Go on."

"I'll refuse you." She did not raise her voice. "When you're king, and the numbers are wrong, I'll refuse you, in front of whoever's at the table, in writing; the same as I'd not lift the build cap until the mine was promised. The House has never bent its books for a client and it'll not bend them for a crown. I'll not finance a war. Not yours, not anybody's. If you go to war, and I think you'll be made to, you'll pay for it out of what you have, and the House will sit with its books shut and watch you do it." She looked at the charter. "And the House will outlive you. It'll be answering to its books when you're old in a chair in this room, and after, when nobody remembers your name. That's what you'd be buying."

"That's why I came to you," said Emeric.

Vantic held his eyes across the lamp. Then, very slightly, at one corner, her mouth moved, which he had seen it do perhaps three times in all the years he had known her.

"Yes," she said. "I suppose it is."

She did not take the charter. She stood and put on her gloves, and looked down at the three things on the table; and then she picked up the drawing of the tower, only that, and rolled it and put it under her arm.

"The charter stays here," she said. "Unsigned, where you can see it every day. I'll take the tower. It's the only one of the three that's a present."

She went down the stair. He heard her stop on the landing below, at the window over the snow, and then go on down.



Ferra came in as Vantic went out; they had passed on the stair. She looked at the charter and the map, and at the space where the drawing had been, and did not ask. She sat at her own table and lit her own lamp and took out a book he had not seen before, new, its spine not yet opened, with the next year's number on its cover in her small square capitals.

She wrote on its first page, and he did not lean to see. When she had finished she turned it round and pushed it across.

25 December. The Long Table. Every lane in Kaldo to set one long table down the middle of its street at midday and eat at it together, whoever lives in it. That year's decadays to be toasted at it by name. Gifts small, and made by hand where they can be. F.

He read it twice.

"Ours had the name first," said Ferra, taking the book back. "Every lane. Not the castle. Not the duke's feast with the town looking in at the windows. The town's own, in the street. In the snow, if it snows."

"They'll freeze."

"They'll eat faster," said Ferra, and put the new book in her drawer, under the old one.



She wrote the other thing that night, in her own room, after the second bell, in the old book and not the new.

He was on the floor with his back against her bed again. Imani was in the chair with her feet to the coals and the stethoscope round her neck, where it had been all evening; she had listened to Ward with it, and to Bram, and to Zale, who had laughed so much she could hear nothing else, and to Finn's soldier, at Finn's insistence, and pronounced it dead.

Ferra turned to the page with the five names and the date and the struck question mark, and under the date, in her small square hand, wrote three words.

Kept, from now on.

She held the book out to Imani. Imani took it, and Ferra's pencil, and looked at the clock on Ferra's shelf, and wrote the hour under Ferra's line in her own hard upright hand, as she wrote the hour under everything that mattered, a birth or a death or a fever breaking, so that nobody could say afterward it had been some other time.

Ferra closed the book.

"There," she said. "It's yours. The day. It isn't hers any more." She did not look at him. "You can have hers back as well, if you want it. But you've got this one, and it's on a page, and it's kept."

He did not say anything. He could not have.



Later, when the fire had gone to coals and they were nothing but warm and tired and tangled in Ferra's blankets with the snow going past the shutter, Ferra asked the thing she had been waiting all evening to ask.

"After paper," she said into his shoulder. "You told Saban it was a long way off. You didn't tell him what."

"No."

"Tell me."

He thought about how to say it, in the dark, to a woman who had kept books her whole life.

"Numbers," he said. "No paper and no coin. Every man's money is a figure, written down. And the figure isn't kept in one book in one room, that can burn or be stolen or be lied in. It's kept in every book, everywhere, all at once, every copy the same; and when one changes they all change, and nobody can change one without everybody seeing."

Ferra was quiet a while.

"That's just my book," she said.

"It's your book. For everybody. All at once."

She said nothing else. He felt her thinking against his shoulder, the steady patient working he never interrupted, and knew without looking that she would not sleep for an hour.

It was Imani who started the other thing, which he should have expected; Ferra had been thinking it for three weeks, and would not have said it aloud until she had worked out what it cost.

"So," said Imani, to the ceiling. "The children."

Ferra, who had been very nearly asleep after all, was not asleep any more.

"What children," said Emeric.

"Yours. Eventually. Ours." Imani turned her head on the pillow. "You told six people round a table three weeks ago that the crown stops with you. I've been waiting for somebody to ask what that makes them, and nobody has, so I'm asking. No princes?"

"No princes."

"No princesses," said Ferra, from his other side. She said it the way she read out a total she had already checked twice. "No little duke. Nobody saying Your Highness to a child at breakfast."

"No."

"Then what are they?"

He thought about it, and they let him; it was one of the things he liked best in both of them, that neither ever filled a silence for him.

"Whatever they like," he said. "In their own heads they can be kings and queens and anything else. They just won't be able to make anybody else agree." He looked up into the dark where the ceiling was. "If they take after either of you, they won't need to. People will follow them anyway, because they'll be worth following. Not because somebody wrote it down."

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"That," said Ferra at last, "was very nearly a nice thing to say."

"It was a nice thing to say."

"It was nearly." She settled her head on his shoulder. "You said either. As if it's going to be one or the other."

Imani laughed into the pillow, the kind she could not stop once it had started, and Ferra lay perfectly still and very pleased with herself, and he lay between them and listened to the wind come round the keep.
