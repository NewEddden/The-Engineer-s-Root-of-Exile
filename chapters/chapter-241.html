Chapter 241 - Pressure

Ferra kept the page open for five weeks.

It was the page from the still week, the one with the coal sum on it and the two lines Emeric had written on a clean sheet and turned round for the table, copied in her own hand, and at the foot of it the words Loss on the wire, and beside them a blank with a ring round it. She had turned past it a hundred times since. She had never once turned past it without seeing the ring. She took the book down from its shelf in the counting room on the morning after the bulb, and opened it at that page, and laid a strip of tape across it for a marker, and left the tape there; and every evening for five weeks, when she put the day's figures in, she turned to the page and looked at the ring, and did not write anything in it, and turned back.

Zale had gone on the first southerly, three weeks after the cave, with her list in his coat next to the scholar's name. He had stood in the pattern store the night before, with the boys, and watched the black horseshoe burn in its glass, with his hand on his raw throat, and had not said anything at all; and in the morning, at the yard quay, with his bag on his shoulder and the boat waiting, he had leaned down to her and whispered, because he could still do nothing else, "Now I'll have to come home to see it," and gone down the ladder. She had written the day. She had not written the rest.

The second machine went onto the drop house's shaft in ten days.

Bram built it at the works and brought it down on a wagon under canvas and set it on a bed beside the dynamo, on the far side of the drop side's clutch, so that the shaft that came through the partition's collar could turn either one of them or both. It was the dynamo's brother. It had the same iron shoes and the same wound drum, near enough, and Ferra could not have told them apart across the room. The difference was inside, the prince said, in which way the copper went round and where the current came off it; and the difference was in what came out of it, which was a thing she could not see and he could only describe.

"The dynamo sends it one way," he said. He was standing at the new machine's bed with his hand on its iron, with Bram and Cade and Ferra round him and Wick on his stool at the gauge with his arms folded, listening without seeming to. "Down the wire, like a river. Always the same way. That's what a pump wants, and the little machine in the tower's pump house, because that turns one way and needs pushing one way." He took his hand off the iron. "This one sends it back and forth. Up the wire and back, and up and back, fifty times while you say your name. Like a saw."

"Then it doesn't go anywhere," said Cade.

"It doesn't need to. A lamp doesn't care which way it goes through the thread. It only cares that it goes through. The thread gets hot going one way and it gets hot going the other." He looked at the machine. "And a saw can do a thing a river can't. You can change it. You can take a saw going back and forth with a short stroke and a strong arm behind it, and turn it into a long stroke with a weak one, and back again, and lose almost nothing doing it. You can't do that with a river." He glanced at Ferra. "That's the coils."

She wrote back and forth, for the lamps, because she did not understand it and wanted it in his words before she did.

The coils were iron and copper, and nothing else, and the iron was the telegraph's.

Bram had drawn nine miles of iron wire on the rolls' wheel the winter before the wall closed, for the road north, and a great deal more since, on reels in the rail works' store, waiting for the poles north to catch up with it. The prince had it wound into rings. Not one coil of wire round and round like a spool, but a great many short lengths, laid side by side and bundled and bent round into a ring as thick as a man's thigh and as wide across as a cartwheel's hub, bound with tape; so that the ring was iron, all through, but was made of a thousand irons, not one.

"Why not a bar bent round?" said Bram. "Stronger."

"Because a solid iron ring gets hot," said the prince, "when the saw's going back and forth in the copper round it, and wastes what it gets hot with. A ring of wires doesn't. Not as much." He did not say how he knew. He did not say the same door. Ferra saw him not say it.

And round each ring, the copper. Two windings, one on each side of the ring, not touching each other, not touching anything but the iron they went round, and the iron between them. On one side, a few turns of thick copper, as thick as a finger. On the other side, many, many turns of fine copper, as fine as a thread; so many that when Bram worked out how long it would take him on the lathe with a man feeding the wire and a man counting, and every turn laid down beside the last one and the next layer laid over it with oiled paper between, and not one turn allowed to touch its neighbour through the paper, he put his pencil down and said a month.

"A month for one," he said. "And you want one at every tower and one at the island and one at the yard."

"I want them in a fortnight."

"Then you'd better find another me."

"I'll do the fine ones," the prince said. "In the forge."

Bram looked at him. Ferra saw him look, and saw what was in it, which was not doubt. It was the look Bram gave the Mana Forge whenever the prince went into the pattern store at night and shut the door and came out in the morning grey in the face with a thing on the bench that no smith could have made; the look of a man who has built everything he owns with his hands, and has watched a thing get made in a night that his hands could not have made in a year, and has not yet decided what he thinks about it.

"It's a spool," Bram said. "It's a thread going round a ring. A boy could do it."

"A boy could. In a month. With his hands." The prince did not look away. "The forge can lay a thread where I tell it and hold every turn off the next one while it lays it. It doesn't get tired at the three thousandth turn and it doesn't lose count." He paused. "And we have five weeks, and I want the houses lit before the snow. You'll do the thick ones. They want a smith."

Bram said nothing for a while. Then he said, "The thick ones want a smith," as if he were checking it, and went to find the thick copper.

The prince went into the pattern store that night with a ring of iron wire and a reel of fine copper as big as a cheese, and shut the door. Ferra sat in the counting room with her book and did not go home, and at the first grey she went down the stair and across the yard to the pattern store, and he was sitting on the floor with his back against the bench, asleep, with his mouth open, and on the bench above him was the ring, wound. The fine copper lay on it in a smooth brown band as wide as her hand, so even that it looked turned on a lathe, so close that she could not get a fingernail between the turns, and the paper between the layers showing as a hair of white at the edges, layer on layer on layer, like the leaves of a book seen from the side.

She did not wake him. She took his coat off the peg and put it over him. Then she went back up to the counting room and wrote it down. First fine coil. One night. E. in the forge. And, after a moment, because she was fair, Bram's thick coil, four days, by hand, not yet done.

He did six of them. Six nights, not in a row, because Imani would not let him do them in a row after the second, and said so to his face at the table in front of everybody, and he did not argue with her, which Ferra wrote down too.

They tried the first two rings on a Tuesday, a fortnight after the bulb.

One ring at the yard, in the drop house, on the far side of the new machine, with its thick winding on the machine and its fine winding on a new wire going out through the wall: a thin wire, a third the thickness of the old one, on new poles. Taller poles; Bram's men had set them beside the old ones, up the slope and in at the south gate and round the ring road, and they stood twice the height of the old ones and had a white band painted round each at the height of a mounted man's head, and a red band above it. And at the far end, in the second tower's pump house, beside the little machine that turned the pump, the second ring, the other way round: the fine winding on the thin wire coming in, and the thick winding going out to a lamp.

Two lamps. One in the drop house, on the new machine, on a short wire, a yard long. And one in the pump house at the second tower, at the end of a mile and more of thin copper and two rings of iron.

Ferra stood in the pump house. The prince had asked her to. Cade stood at the drop house with the other lamp, and a boy ran between them.

The lamp in the pump house came up white.

It came up as white as the one on Bram's bench in the pattern store, a few yards from the pair. Not a shade yellower. Not the warm colour the first one had been on the long-room table at the end of the old wire, that everybody had seen and nobody had said. White. The boy ran down to the yard and ran back up, and said Cade said his was the same, and Cade had said to say so exactly, the same, and not about the same.

The prince had a thing in his hand that Bram had made him in the week: a needle on a pin, in a little brass box with a glass front, with a coil of wire round the needle's foot, so that when current went through the coil the needle swung, and the further it swung the more current there was. He read it at the pump house with the lamp lit. Then he walked down to the yard, a mile, and read it there, with the same lamp lit; and then he walked back up, and read it again, and Ferra walked with him, because she wanted to see him read it at both ends and not be told.

At the end of the third walk he gave her two figures. One for the old wire, the thick one, the pump's, which he had read the same way at both ends every day that week. And one for the new.

She wrote them in the pump house doorway, standing, with the book on her arm, in the light of the lamp.

She turned back to the still week's page, where the tape was. She wrote the old wire's figure in the blank, the one with the ring round it; it was a fifth, near enough, of everything that went into the wire at the yard, gone before it reached the tower, into the air, into the poles, into nothing. Then under it she wrote the new one, which was a small figure, so small that she wrote it twice to be sure of it and then a third time in words, one part in sixty, in case anybody who read it after her did not believe the figure.

Then she drew a line through the ring.

She had never drawn a line through a ring in her life. A ring meant a thing not yet known, and she had always, until now, waited until she knew it and then written the thing and left the ring where it was, round it, so that anybody reading the page could see it had once not been known. She drew a line through this one. She did not know why. She looked at it afterwards for some time.

"It's a pressure," the prince said, beside her, watching her do it. "That's all. The same water, pushed very hard, through a thin pipe. A thin pipe loses less when the push is hard. And at the far end you let it down again through a wide one, gently, before it gets into anybody's kitchen." He looked up at the thin wire on its tall pole outside the pump house door. "In that wire, now, between the two rings, the push is enough to kill a horse."

She looked up at it too.

"Then that goes in the book," she said.

It went in the book. It went on a card on every tall pole, and on the pump house doors, in the prince's hand and then again underneath in Wes's larger one, so that nobody could say he had not been able to read it, and in a picture under that, which a girl in the finishing room cut the plate for the same afternoon, of a man on a ladder and a horse lying down. The high line kills. Never touch it. Never climb a pole with the red band while the house is running. The switch is at the drop house and only Cade or Wes throws it.

On the third morning there was a crow at the foot of the fourth tall pole from the south gate, on its back, with its feet up, and a black mark on its breast as if it had been touched with a hot iron. Cade found it on his way up with the morning's reading. He brought it down to the counting room in a sack and laid it on Ferra's table without a word, because he had been told to bring anything that happened on the line, and Ferra looked at it for a while, and wrote it down, and had the sack taken out to the midden, and then asked for it back, and sent it up to the gate so that it could be nailed to the fourth pole under the card for a week. Nobody touched a tall pole all winter.

After that it was only work, and Kaldo was good at work.

The drop house had the second machine and the first ring. Every tower's pump house had a ring, or would have one within the fortnight, because every duct in the city began in a tower's pump house; Weekes had laid them that way, years ago, a hand wider than the mains in every trench, before anybody knew what they were for, and every one of them came up out of the ground beside a pump. The high line went round the ring road on its tall poles from tower to tower, and at each tower it came down, and went into the ring, and came out of the ring let down and gentle, and went into the ducts; and the ducts ran under every street in the three quarters, beside every main, and came up at every door.

The copper came off the draw bench day and night. It had not stopped since the pair, and it did not stop now, and the rolls' wheel drew rail by day and wire by night, and some nights, when the rail works could spare it, both. The island women wrapped it. They wrapped the fine for the rings in thread run through the new wax off the still's trough, which took the wax better than the old and did not smell of the soap house, and they wrapped the thick for the ducts in cloth and then in a sleeve of the rubber shed's thinnest sheet, cemented along its seam with the spirit and the rubber, and the rubber shed ran all day and all night for the first time since the island. Where the wire lay in the ducts under the streets, Bram's men poured the ducts full of hot black end after it, off the still, round the wire, so that the wet could not get at it through the clay, and the ducts went down into the ground and came up again sealed.

The earth mages set the poles.

That had been Tessaly's asking, not the prince's. She came up to the palace on the third day of the high line with her hat in her hand and asked the prince, in front of Ferra, whether the line would go to the island; and when he said it would, four miles of tall poles up the road to the bridge, she said that her gang had nothing on their hands until the spring, and that a pole was a hole in the ground with the earth packed round it, and that a hole in the ground with the earth packed round it was the thing her gang did best in all the world. She did not ask what the poles were for. She had stood under the quarry lights and watched the dock's from the wall. She knew what they were for, or knew that she did not know, which with Tessaly was the same.

The prince paid them the wall's rate. They set four miles of poles in eleven days, from the north gate to the Left Bridge, forty-some a day in any weather: a mage at the foot of each with her hand on the ground, and the ground opening, and the pole going in, and the ground closing round it and setting as hard as the wall's footings, while the men with the reels came behind with the wire. Ferra went out on the fourth day to count them and stood by the road and watched it for an hour. It was the fastest thing she had ever seen done by hand in Kaldo. It was not by hand.

At the bridge, at the island's end, the high line came down into a house.

It was a house nobody lived in any more: the first on the right past the bridge foot, where a cooper had kept his shop until the fourth draw, sealed by Ilsa and swept and left. Ilsa unsealed it. The prince put the island's ring in its front room where the counter had been, and the ring let the high line down into three wires that went up the island's hill on short poles Bram's men put up the same week, which did not kill anybody. One to the ward. One to the old exchange, where the school was. One up the hill to the keep, where the tower was.

The market street got nothing.

That was the prince's rule, and Ferra had watched him make it, at the table, with his finger on the map of the island. The market street had had its gas since the year the wall closed. It had eight lamps on iron posts, and the lung behind them, and a promise on it, printed and signed and read out at the cross: that the well and the gas lamps and the watch and the ward would stay open until the last family went. The gas stayed. It had been promised. It would burn yellow on the market street over the island's empty doors every night until there was nobody left to walk under it, and then it would go on burning, because nobody had said when to stop.

Ilsa gave the order of the streets. The prince asked her to, and she did it the way she did the draws, by the book, in the order the families had come off the island.

"The first ones first," she said. "The ones who came when there was nothing up here but mud and a promise. Then the second draw. Then the rest." She looked at the prince over her book. "And the island last. It'll be a reason."

"A reason for what?"

"For the ones who haven't gone." She closed the book. "You don't make anybody go. I know. You've said. But a lamp in a window's a reason, and it's the kind nobody can call a push."

He did not answer that. Ferra wrote it down.

So the gangs went into the first quarter in draw order, a street at a time, with Ilsa's list in the ganger's hand and a cart of reels and a cart of plates behind them. They were the wire gang from the rubber shed's first sleeve, and the works' boys who had drawn the wire, and anybody Bram could spare, and a good many he could not. They went door to door. At every door the wire came up out of the duct in the street, sleeved, and in through a hole in the wall that the boy with the brace bored and lined with a collar of black horn off Bram's lathe; and inside the door, on the wall, at the height of a woman's shoulder, a black horn plate the size of a book with a brass box on it, and in the box a strip of lead and tin as thin as a stalk of grass, and beside the box a switch.

The box was what took the longest to explain, and the gangs explained it at every door, because the prince had said they must.

"If anything goes wrong in the wire," the gang boy said, at the first door on the first street, to the first woman who had come up the road from the island with her children on a handcart, years ago, when there had been nothing up here but mud and a promise. "Anything. A mouse in the wall. A nail through it. Anything at all. That goes." He put his finger on the thin grey strip in the box. "It melts. It's the weakest thing in the house on purpose. It goes before the wire gets hot, and the light goes out, and nothing else happens. Then you send for us, and we find what went wrong, and put a new one in."

"And if I don't send," said the woman.

"Then you're in the dark, mistress," said the boy, "and nothing's on fire."

Each street was tried the evening it was finished, one lamp in each house and the posts along the kerb, for as long as it took the gang to walk it and look; and then Cade took it off again at the drop house, because the prince wanted the whole of it to come on together, on one night, and not a street at a time.

Ferra was at that door. She had meant to walk the first street and go back, and she walked all of it, and the next, and was there at the door on the third street at dusk, on the evening it was tried, when the woman at it asked the thing that everybody in the first quarter asked sooner or later, in one set of words or another, and that the gangs had been told to answer every time.

It was Kit Hallam's mother. Ferra knew her; she had been in the street the morning of the cellar, and had watched Imani breathe her son back while Ferra counted.

"Can it leak," she said. "Into the cellar."

The gang boy did not laugh. Ferra would have heard about it if he had. He put down his brace and looked at her, and said what the prince had told the gangs to say, which was the truest thing anybody said about the wire all winter.

"It can't leak, mistress. There's nothing in it to leak. It can only stop."

Kit Hallam's mother looked at the black plate on her wall for a long time, and then at the switch, and then she put out one finger and pushed it, and the lamp in her kitchen came up white over the table where her son was eating his supper, and he looked up at it with his spoon in his hand, and she began, very quietly, to cry. The gang boy picked up his brace and went on to the next door without being told.

The boxes proved themselves inside the week. A boy on the second street, eight years old, on the evening his street was tried, wanted to see what was in the brass box while his mother was at the bakehouse, and opened it, and put the blade of his father's knife across its two posts to find out; and there was a flat crack, like a twig, and a flash in the box, and the knife was thrown out of his hand across the room with a black bite out of its edge, and the strip of lead and tin was gone, and the house was dark. The rest of the street stayed lit. The boy was not hurt. Ferra heard about it from the gang, who had heard about it from the boy's mother, loudly, the next morning, when she sent for them; and the gang put a new strip in, and told the boy why, and let him watch.

Ferra wrote it. Boxes. One gone, second street, a knife. House dark, street lit, boy whole. As meant. And under it, because she could see the winter coming: Strips. A great many.

She set the rate herself.

She did it the way she had done the pump shifts in the still week, with a slate in one hand and her own pencil in the other, standing in kitchens. She asked what a house burned in lane oil in a quarter, and in candles, and in rush lights when the oil was dear, and wrote down what she was told, in forty kitchens in three quarters, and added it, and divided it, and set the rate for a lamp at a good deal less than the least of them. So much the lamp, the quarter, paid at the quarter day with the rent. A house with two lamps would pay less for a winter of white light than it had paid for a winter of yellow, and would not have to buy a flint.

And a house was dark from the second hour after midnight until dawn. That was the other rule, and it was the drops', not hers; she wrote it for the drops. The streets stayed lit all night, and the gates, and the watch houses. The houses and the palace went out at the second hour, all at once, on a switch at the drop house that Cade threw at the second bell, so that the house at the yard could run lighter through the dead of the night when nobody was awake to want it, and burn one flask fewer by morning. The palace kept its oil lamps on the tables, trimmed, for the hours after, and so did the counting room, and so, Ferra noticed, did most of the first quarter. Nobody complained. Nobody in the first quarter had ever had a light at the second hour after midnight in his life.

She found out the other thing on her own, walking back from the third quarter late on a trial night, with its streets lit for the gang to look at.

She was in the ring street under the second tower, with the new lamps on their posts along it, small and white in their glass, a row of them going away round the curve; and every lamp in the street dimmed. All together. For the space of a breath. Not out: down, a shade, a little yellow, as if a cloud had gone over every one of them at once. And then they came back up, all together, and were white again.

She stood still in the street with her book under her arm and thought about it, and knew what it was before she had finished thinking. The needle at the yard, going down to its line on the brass; the drops slowing behind the partition, the knock stretching like a man walking uphill; and Cade, at the lock, with his hand on the key, waiting, the way Wes had taught him, not listening to her, and turning it.

Every lamp in the city dipped when a flask was poured. You could tell the hour of every pour from any window in the barony.

She wrote that down in the street, standing under a lamp, which she found she could now do. Then she went home.

The palace was wired last, because the prince said the houses came first; and when it was wired, Ferra had the vaults done before anything else in it.

She had walked the vaults with a lamp stone every week since the government came over from the keep. Forty of them, under the palace floor, stone-roofed, with no timber in them anywhere, because the prince had drawn them that way in the first plan so that no fire could ever reach the records. She had never once taken a flame into them. She had never once let anybody else. And she had walked them every week with a lamp stone in her hand, in the blue-white light it gave, knowing that the stone would go grey in a dead place and leave her in the dark among the rolls, and knowing that a flame was worse.

"The vaults," she said to the gang. "First."

They wired the vaults. A lamp to every vault, on the wall, in a cage of iron, where no roll could touch it. She walked them the first night it was done with no lamp in her hand at all, from the first vault to the fortieth and back, and it took her an hour, because she stopped in every one of them and looked at the shelves. Then she went up and told the prince that the vaults were done, and he asked her whether they were well lit, and she said they were lit, and he did not ask anything else, because he had seen her face.

The long room. The counting room. The gallery on the east range, and the prince's rooms, and the rooms off them, and the stair, and the court. And the table in the long room with a lamp over it on a chain, which Hobb pulled down for the prince the first evening and pushed up again, and pulled down, and pushed up, and then went away looking pleased with himself, as if he had invented it.

Not the dome.

The prince stood on the gallery at the pass-box with Bram and the wire gang's ganger, and the ganger asked where he wanted the wire to go in, and the prince said nowhere.

"It's a skin," he said. "Glass and pitch and iron, and the only reason it holds what it holds is that there's no hole in it but the ones I made and I shut. Every hole's a door." He looked through the pass-box glass at the tree. "The gallery keeps its stones. Inside, it's dark at night, the way it always has been, and the screens go up. Nothing goes through that glass that isn't already going through it."

The ganger looked at the dome, and then at the prince, and did not ask why a man who had put a white lamp in every kitchen in three quarters would leave the middle of his own house dark. He wrote not the dome on his list. Ferra wrote it in hers.

Imani came to the table on the twentieth night with a sheet of paper, and put it down in front of the prince, and did not sit.

Ferra knew the sheet. So did he. It was a clean sheet from the still week, in his hand, with two lines on it, one large and one small, and the small one under the large. Imani had asked him to write the small one down, that night, and he had; and Ferra had made a copy for the book and a copy for Farris at Milford, and Imani had kept the sheet.

What must not stop goes on what cannot stop.

And under it, smaller. The ward goes on the pumps' side of the line. With the water.

"You wrote it down," said Imani.

"I wrote it down."

"You told me at this table it would never be on a wire. Not the one I've got." Imani did not raise her voice. "It's on one now. Which side?"

He looked at the sheet for a moment. Then he did the thing Ferra had seen him do perhaps four times in all the years, which was to be told he was wrong at his own table by somebody who was right, and not argue.

"The drops' side," he said. "Like the houses. If the house at the yard goes dark in a gale, the ward goes dark with it. That's what I've built." He put his hand on the sheet. "That isn't what I wrote."

"No."

"Cells," he said.

It was the thing he had wanted to build for three years, and had not, because there had been no lead; and then there had been lead, from Grayrock's vein, and acid, and no time. Plates of lead in jars of acid, in rows, in a room; charged by day, while the drops ran and the sun was up and nobody wanted a lamp, and giving it back at night, a little at a time, as it was wanted. The jars would only drink the one-way current, not the saw, so Bram would set a small machine in the cooper's house by the bridge to take the one in and give the other out: a motor on the island line, turning a little dynamo on the same shaft. A room of them would carry the ward's lamps through a night with the line cut, and through a week of nights, if the ward went careful, and through anything a gale could do to hull two. And under them, behind them, the gas, which the ward had had for years on the pipe from the lung, and which nobody would take away.

"And the gas stays," Imani said.

"The gas stays."

"Then write it down," said Imani, "under the other one." And she pushed the sheet across the table to him, and he wrote it, and she took the sheet back, and folded it, and put it in her apron pocket, and went.

The cells went into the ward's cellar in the last week, in a room Imani chose because it was dry, in rows on slate shelves: three hundred glass jars from Orrin's shop with lead in them and the acid over the lead. Bram charged them for the first time on a Sunday, off the line, from noon to dusk, and at dusk had the island line cut at the cooper's house by the bridge, and the ward's lamps did not so much as dip. They burned all night on the jars, white, while the island around the ward went on with its gas and its candles and did not know anything had happened. In the morning Imani went down to the cellar with a lamp stone and looked at the rows, and touched one of the jars, and came up, and told nobody anything, which was how Ferra knew she was satisfied.

The line went back on.

Ferra wrote the last line for the house at the yard at the end of the fifth week.

It went in the works book, on the coal page, under the line she had written in the autumn before last, the one with the small figure in it for what the boiler burned and the large one for what it lit. She had written that line knowing whose table it would end on. She wrote this one the same way. It said what the house now lit, as it stood on that morning, the dock and the works and the pump houses and three quarters of a city and a palace and the far end of a high line on an island four miles off, in the barony's own form. And it said, above it, what the boiler burned, which was a little more than it had, because the coal engine had been stood in once in the storms, and less than a man who knew engine houses would have believed if he had not seen it written down.

Vantic would copy it at the quarter. It would go on the Thursday tide. Somebody at a table a day past Milford would read it aloud, and add it, and it would not add up; and it would not add up in exactly the way it had not added up the autumn before last, only a great deal larger.

Ferra looked at it a while. Then she blotted it and closed the book.

It was true. All of it. That was what she had always sat with, when she had done a thing like that: that every line was true. She sat with it now, and found, for the first time, that it sat differently; because the other line, the large one, was not a thing anybody could take up the road and lay on a desk and make into a case. It was a light in a window. Anybody could come and look at it. There was nothing behind it but glass, and paper, and a drop of silver going round; and it was in every kitchen in three quarters, and nobody had to believe a word she wrote to see it.

Tessaly saw it come on from the wall.

She had not meant to be there. She had meant to be somewhere warm. She had been out on the island road all afternoon with Rafe, in the cold, because the boy had wanted to walk the poles they had set, all four miles of them, standing tall and white-banded in a line along the road with the thin wire going from one to the next; and when they came back to the north gate at dusk the gate watch had said the prince was having the whole of it thrown tonight, at dusk, at once, the houses and the streets and all, and that there were people on the wall already. So they had gone up.

There were a great many people on the wall. More than she had ever seen on it. Ilsa was there, with her book shut under her arm. Weekes was there, not speaking. Half the wire gang was there, and their mothers, and the gate watch from three gates, and the night gang from the rail works, who should have been at the rail works; and along the south arc, where she had stood so many dusks on her own and watched the yard's stack, there were families from the first quarter who had come up the stair with their children because somebody had said there would be something to see, and had stood on the cold walk for an hour in the dark waiting for it, with their own kitchens dark below them and their own switches on their own walls, unpushed, because somebody had said: wait. Wait till the whole of it comes on at once.

It was beginning to snow. The first of the winter. Small dry flakes, not many, coming down slowly out of a dark sky with no wind in it, and melting on the stone.

Rafe had grown. He was taller than she was now, and he stood beside her with his hands in his coat and his breath going up white, and did not say anything, because he did not say much as a rule. The tower had his name in its register, with the tenth stone written beside it, twice, and one word in the column for the place of raising, in Master Hebden's neat hand. She had carried him there to be written. She had watched Hebden write it. She thought about that, sometimes, at night. She did not think about it now.

She was looking south, over the fold of the land, to the stack at the yard. She could see it from here on a clear evening. It was a thread.

Then the city came on.

It did not come on all at once. It came on from the south, from the yard, as though something were running up the wire from the rock. The four gates first, the arcs over them, hard and white and hissing faintly, one after another round the wall, south and east and north and west; so that for a breath the whole ring of the wall stood in the snow with a white star at each quarter of it and nothing lit inside. And then the streets.

The avenues first. Straight out from the middle to the four gates, the small lamps on their posts, white, one after another, running out along them so fast that she could not follow them with her eyes; and then the ring streets, in their long curves round the middle, lamp after lamp going away round the bend in both directions until they met; and then the streets of the quarters, the short straight ones, the lanes, all of them together, like a net being pulled up out of dark water. The whole of the city drawn in white on the dark, every street of it, the way it had been drawn in pencil on the sheets she had seen on the long-room table the year she came, a wheel, with the palace at the hub. She had built the rim of it. She had never seen the spokes before. You could not see them in daylight. You could only see them now.

And then the houses.

Somebody below her on the first quarter's first street pushed a switch, and a kitchen window came up white. Then the one next to it. Then the families on the wall began to run for the stair, all of them at once, with their children, laughing, shouting names; and down in the streets under her, one door and then another and then all of them, the kitchens came up, and the rooms above the kitchens, window by window by window, in every street in the first quarter and the second and the third, until there was no dark left in three quarters of the city but the fourth, the empty one, still stakes and snow; and the whole of the rest of it lay under her in the falling snow, white, and still, and full of people, with the light going out of every window onto the new snow in the street below it in a pale square with somebody's shadow moving in it.

In the middle, the palace. Every window of it lit, all round, the east range and the west, and the long room, and the court. And over it, in the middle of all that white, the dome, dark, with only a faint glow in it low down, behind the screens, where the prince kept whatever he kept. The one dark thing in the lit city. A round black shell of glass on its drum, with the snow settling on it.

Rafe said, "Is there a stone in it?"

He did not mean the dome. He meant all of it. She knew what he meant.

She put her hand out, the way any mage does. Not at the palace; at the nearest lamp, on its post in the ring street below the wall, thirty feet down and twenty along, burning white in its glass with the snow going past it. She reached. She had reached for the quarry lights, and for the dock's, and for the poles her own gang had set, and she knew before she reached what she would find, and she reached anyway, because she was a mage and it was what she did when she did not understand a light.

There was nothing. No stone. Not a dead one, not a sleeping one. Glass, and a thread of something, and a wire going into the ground.

"No," she said. "Nothing."

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know," said Tessaly. "Something at the yard."

She looked south again, over the fold, at the stack. It was still a thread. It was the same thread it had been at dusk, before any of this, when the city had been dark; and now the city was lit from wall to wall, three quarters of it and a palace and the gates, and the stack had not changed at all. She had worked that out once already, in a gale, on this wall, and kept it. She had kept it from Armand for two years, and the other thing after it, the night the dome let go, because it had been hers to keep, and she had not known what it would cost him to know it.

She did not have to keep this one. She saw that, standing in the snow. Anybody could stand on this wall and look south and count it. Every family in the first quarter who had ever bought a candle could count it. Armand had sat at the prince's table under the first of the glass pears and put out his hand to it, she had heard, and found what she had just found, and said so. There was nothing to keep. It was in every window.

And under that, slower, the other thought came, the one she had been not thinking since the quarry.

For as long as there had been a Circle, a light in the dark had been one of two things. A flame, which anybody could make and which could burn the house down; or a stone, which only a mage could fill, and which went grey when it was empty and waited for a mage to come. That was the whole of it. That was what a mage was for, on a winter night, in any town in the kingdom: the light that did not burn the house down. She had filled her first lamp stone as a girl, for a neighbour, and been paid a Copper for it, and been prouder of that Copper than of anything she had done since, the wall included.

Nobody down there would ever need a mage to light a street again.

She stood with that for a while in the snow. It frightened her a little. She found, standing with it, that it gladdened her more; and she did not know yet what to do with either, and decided that she did not have to know tonight.

Then, while she was standing there, every lamp in the city dipped.

All of them. The gates, the avenues, the rings, the lanes, three quarters of kitchens and the palace windows and the lamp on its post thirty feet below her, all together, at once, down a shade, as if every one of them had drawn a breath; and came back up, all together, white. Rafe felt her go still beside him and looked at her.

"What was that?"

"I don't know," said Tessaly, which was true, and which she found she did not mind.

Four miles to the north, across the fold and the lake, the island had come on too. She could see it from the wall's north corner if she leaned: a point of white on the hill where the keep was, which was Armand's, and she thought of him in his hall under it with his students, reaching for it one after another, and finding nothing, and saying so. A row of white lower down, which was the long windows of the ward. A square of white in the middle of the old town, which was the exchange, where the school was. And between them, running down the market street to the water, eight small yellow points in a row, steady, as they had burned every night for years, over doors that had nobody behind them now: the gas lamps on the market street, still lit, because somebody had promised.

She stood on the wall until the snow was lying on her shoulders, and then she took the boy down the stair into the lit streets, and walked him home through them, under the lamps, in the snow, with the thin wire going over their heads from post to post, humming very faintly in the cold, the whole way.
