Chapter 155 - The First Metal

The first plate came off the rolls at a little after the noon bell, on a wet morning in the middle of spring, and it was ugly, and it was flat, and it did not crack.

Emeric had come down to the mill yard at first light to watch the heat, and had stayed. So had Bram, who had not slept, and Jorrin, who had built the stand, and the three men who had worked the converter since its first blow and had long since stopped being frightened of it. The stand had taken Jorrin the whole winter. It was two great rolls of the converter's own steel, each as long as a man and as thick as his waist, turned true on the big lathe over nine weeks and set one above the other in a frame of iron that had been cast in four pieces and bolted, with screws at each end to bring the rolls together a hair at a time. It was driven off the beater's wheel through the same clutch as the converter, so that the paper works and the steel took turns at the river, and on rolling days the vats went cold.

The slab came out of the furnace white. Two men walked it to the stand on long tongs, and it went between the rolls and came out the other side longer and thinner and orange, and went back, and came out again, and each time Jorrin brought the screws down a turn. After the eleventh pass it was a sheet a little over two feet wide and nine long and about as thick as a man's smallest finger, lying on the iron floor plates beyond the stand with the scale flaking off it in black leaves, cooling from orange to red to a dull purple-grey with a sheen on it like oil on water.

Nobody said anything. Bram walked round it once, slowly. Then he got down on one knee at the end of it, where it was coolest, and put his eye along it, the way a carpenter looks along a board.

"It's flat," he said.

"It's flat," said Jorrin.

"It hasn't split." Bram stood up. "Not an edge. Not a hair." He looked at the stand, and at the rolls with the steam still coming off them, and at the slab of the second heat already glowing in the furnace door. "Colm's grit," he said, to nobody, the way a man says the name of a saint.

Emeric had been waiting for this morning since the autumn, since the fourth bar rolled whole in the heat of the summer while he was in Wayford and Ferra's forge-book said Found by C. He had rolled plate a hundred times in his head. He found that he had not, in all those times, imagined what it would look like cooling on a wet iron floor with the rain hissing on it, and that it was better than anything he had imagined.

"How many before dark?" he said.

"Four," said Bram. "If the rolls hold. Five if they hold and nobody's a fool."

By the evening bell everybody who had ever asked for plate had come to the mill yard to look at it, and every one of them had come for the same four sheets.

Fletcher came first, because he had the furthest to walk and had started earliest. He had heard at the yard before dawn, by a lighterman who had heard from a carter, and he had walked up from the lower water in the rain with his hat pulled down and his hands in his coat, and he stood by the stand for a long time looking at the plate with his lips moving, and then he went and found Emeric.

"The coaster," he said.

He said nothing else. He did not need to. The coaster's frames had been standing on the slip at the lower water since the autumn: a keel, and the stem, and the sternpost, and forty frames of the converter's own bar bent to Fletcher's moulds, standing up in a row like the ribs of a beast with nothing on them. They had stood there all winter under a tarred cloth, waiting. Fletcher had gone down every morning of the winter and looked at them. Emeric knew because Fletcher's yard men had told Saban, and Saban had told the table.

Bram came next, although Bram had never left. He had a tally in his hand and a figure on it.

"Boiler shells," he said. "The works engine wants a new one by harvest. The one in the steamboat's got two patches on it already and it'll want a third. And if there's going to be a second engine at the outside foundry for rail, it wants a boiler, and I'm not riveting a boiler for rail out of the old iron plate, because the old iron plate's what patched the steamboat twice." He held out the tally. "That's what it takes. For the three."

Ward came after the evening bell, off the practice yard, with Declan.

He looked at the plate on the floor beyond the stand for a while without saying anything. Then he said, "Breastplates," and when Emeric looked at him, "For the knights. Not the suits. The line. The line men, in boiled leather and old mail. A sheet like that, cut and dished, takes a sword and a bolt at thirty paces. We've never had it. Nobody south of the capital's had it. I'm asking now so I'm on the list."

"You're on the list."

"Where on it?"

Emeric did not answer, and Ward nodded, as if that were an answer.

Saban came in out of the rain with his coat over his head and did not look at the plate at all. He looked at Emeric.

"The House," he said.

"I know."

"You don't, or you'd have said it first." Saban shook the water off his coat. "There's a principal outstanding. It's not falling. It's not going to fall on sulphur and paper. It's going to fall on something nobody else in the south can make, and that's what's on your floor." He pointed with his chin, still not looking at it. "Plate sells. Every yard on the coast wants it. Every smith wants it for springs. I can sell those four sheets before they're cold, and the next forty, and I can sell them for more than they're worth, and that's the only thing I've seen in two years that moves that figure in Ferra's book the right way."

And Imani came last, at dusk, from the ward, with her sleeves still rolled.

She did not look at the plate either. She stood in the mill yard gate with the rain on her and looked at Emeric, and waited until he came over to her, which he did.

"The ward roof," she said.

He knew about the ward roof. The whole barony knew about the ward roof. The long ward was the old grain factor's store, which had been a good store and was a poor hospital, and its roof was old slate on old laths over a span that had never been meant to keep anything drier than sacks. Every winter since it had been a ward, it had leaked. It leaked in a different place every year, because the slates shifted, and every year the staff moved the beds and put down pails, and every year the leak found a bed anyway. This winter it had found the end room. It had found Hild's bed twice.

"A sheet of that," said Imani, "cut in strips and laid under the slates along the ridge and the valleys, where it comes in. It doesn't have to be pretty. It doesn't have to be the whole roof. It has to be the four places where the water comes through onto the beds, and it has to be before the autumn rains, because my people are old now, the ones in that end room, older than anyone should be at their age, and they die of wet."

She did not say anything else. She had said it the way she said a dose.

He gave the plate to the hull.

He did it that night, at the long-room table, in front of all of them, because he would not do it anywhere else. He did not give it to the hull because the hull was the most important thing. He did not think it was. He gave it to the hull because it was a promise, and he had made it to Rowena at her own table in the autumn, in front of her Treasurer and her harbour-mistress and the head of her works: that a Kaldo boat, built in Kaldo, of Kaldo's iron, would come up her inlet. He had made it, and she had asked to watch it come, and the frames had been standing on Fletcher's slip all winter.

"It's the only one of the five I've given my word on," he said. "Everything else I've said I'd do when I could. That one I said I'd do."

"You said some time," said Saban.

"I said some time. It's been some time."

He did not dress it up after that. He told Bram that the boiler shells would come off the rolls after the hull plate was cut, and that the works engine would have to limp to harvest on its own. He told Ward that the knights' plate would come after the boilers. He told Saban that nothing would be sold until the coaster was plated, and then that a tenth of every run after would go to the House's account at whatever price Saban could get. Saban said a tenth was a joke and then said he would take it.

He told Imani that the ward roof would come after the knights.

He watched her face while he said it.

Nothing happened to it. That was the thing about Imani. She did not argue at a table. She had asked him for a hospital twice, years ago, and not had it either time, and the second time she had made him sign the date in his own hand, and she had not argued at a table since. She asked for things. She asked once, clearly, with the reason. And if she did not get them, she wrote down that she had not got them, and went back to her ward.

She wrote it down.

She did not say anything to him on the stair, going out, or in the yard. She did not say anything to him the next day, or the day after. She was civil. She came to the table. She answered what she was asked. She did not speak to him about anything that was not the ward, and when she spoke about the ward she did it through Ferra, which she had not done since the first year.

He understood that he had wronged her. Not in the choice. The choice was defensible and he would defend it again. He had wronged her in the order. There had been four sheets of plate on the floor, and five people had asked for them, and four of the five had asked for a thing that would make something. She had asked for a thing that would stop old people dying of rain. And he had put her last, after armour, because he could not make himself say to Ward's face that his men would wait behind a roof.

He knew that was what he had done. He knew she knew. He did not know how to say it to her in a way that would not be an excuse, and so he did not say it, which he also knew was wrong.

The rail works was the other half of the plate, and he started it the same week.

It had been Bram's thought as much as his: that the outside foundry, which had stood on the river since the first year because it needed the river's wheel to blow its furnace, did not need the river now. The engine had freed it. A steam engine could blow a furnace on a dry hill. So the outside foundry could stop being a foundry that made a little of everything, and become a works that made one thing, and made it in lengths nobody had ever made it in.

Rail. Steel rail, rolled, not cast. The Grayrock line had been building for two years out of short cast lengths of iron, a few feet at a time, pinned end to end on oak sleepers, and every joint was a place where a wheel knocked and a length cracked. It was slow to make and slow to lay and it broke. At the pace it had been going, Ferra had told the table two years ago, the line was six summers short of the hill.

A rail works would roll rail the way the mill yard now rolled plate. Long lengths, thirty feet, of a shape Emeric drew on the back of a tally in the long room, with a head and a web and a foot, the shape every rail in his old world had been since before his grandfather. It wanted a second stand, with rolls grooved to the shape. It wanted a second converter, or the first one run day and night. It wanted a second engine and a new shed and a coke oven twice the size. And it wanted men.

"From where?" said Ferra.

"All four baronies."

"Taken off what?"

"Off whatever they're doing."

Hollis told him what they were doing.

Hollis was on the table for the farms that spring, because the table had asked the farms to send somebody after the child-wage winter, and the farms had sent the man with eleven acres and one good arm who had stood up at the back of the assembly years ago and asked the only question that mattered. He had been sitting in on the table's farm business ever since, on a chair at the end, saying very little.

He said a good deal now.

He had it written down. He had learned to write in the evening classes the same winter as Rudd, and his hand was large and careful and slow, and he read it off a sheet of Kaldo paper folded in four.

"Four baronies," he said. "You want, Bram says, a hundred and twenty men for the rail works, and another sixty for the city this summer, and another hundred when the city gets going. Two hundred and eighty men." He did not look up. "At harvest, in Kaldo, we're short forty hands every year as it is. We take the women and the children off the school for it. Milford's short a hundred, and Paxton hires drovers off the road. Grayrock's got no harvest to speak of. Wayford's short sixty, and Wayford's harvest is late." He turned the sheet over. "You take two hundred and eighty men out of four baronies in the summer and you'll have them back in the autumn when the barns are half full. The ones that come are the ones who haven't got land. The ones who haven't got land are the ones who work the harvest for the ones who have."

He folded the sheet again.

"I'm not saying don't," said Hollis. "I'm saying that's what it costs. In grain. I've written it down so nobody says later nobody said."

It was so exactly what Bram had said about the pipes at the first sight of the city that Emeric looked at Bram, and Bram looked back at him, and neither of them said anything.

"Then they come in the autumn," said Emeric. "After harvest. Not before. The rail works is built by the men who are here now, and the new men come when the barns are full. And every man who comes to the works in the autumn is paid a harvest bounty to go back in the summer, for three years, till the city's got its own people."

Hollis looked at him.

"That costs more," he said.

"It does."

"You'll lose a summer on your rail."

"I'll lose a summer on the rail. I won't lose a harvest." Emeric looked at Ferra. "What does that do to the line?"

Ferra had been working it while Hollis spoke. She had her pencil in her hand, not her pen, which meant she had not decided it yet.

"If the stand runs by midsummer," she said, "and it holds. If the second engine's in by the autumn. If Grayrock sends ore faster than it has since the mine opened, which it will, because the deep workings are better iron than the shallow ever was, and I've seen Bryda's tally. If the men come after harvest and stay." She looked up. "Two summers. And some of a third. To the hill."

Six had become two and some.

She wrote it in ink.

The steamboat came down on the spring water in the third week of the rail works, low in the river and smoking, with a hold full of Grayrock ore and coke and, lashed on her deck under canvas, a single stout crate with Bryda's mark burned into its lid.

Emeric had it brought up to the pattern store on a handcart and opened it himself.

It was full of stone. Grey stone, heavy, broken into lumps the size of a fist and smaller, and every lump broke in straight lines where it had broken, in little squares and steps, as if somebody had cut it with a knife. Where it had broken fresh the faces were bright, a dull bluish silver like a mirror breathed on. There was a letter on top in Bryda's clerk's hand: As asked, from the old spoil by the first adit, and more where it was. B.R.

He picked up a lump. It was heavier than it had any business being. He had picked one up in exactly the same way from the spoil heap at Grayrock the spring before, and put it in his pocket, and told Bryda to keep all of it, and told nobody what it was, because he had not been ready.

He was ready now.

"What is it?" said Bram.

"Galena," said Emeric.

It was the first time he had said the word out loud in this world, and it sounded strange in the pattern store, among the frames. Bram said it after him, slowly, and did not ask where it came from. Bram had stopped asking where words came from a long time ago.

"It's lead," said Emeric. "Lead and sulphur, together. You roast off the sulphur and you've got lead. A lot of it. Good lead, soft, better than the church roof." He turned the lump in his hand. "That crate's more lead than there is in the whole of Kaldo."

He saw three doors open at once, standing there with the lump in his hand, and they opened so fast and so wide that he had to put the lump down on the bench.

Nevin had been cutting punches since this time last year. Steel punches, one for every letter, struck into copper to make the moulds for type, for the screw press that was to go west to Emberfell in place of the stone printer, because a stone printer needed Kaldo and a screw press did not. He had cut a whole alphabet and started a second, and every punch sat in a velvet tray in his shop with nothing to strike into, because type was cast in lead and there was no lead. Now there was.

The iron salt came off the sulphur at three Copper a barrel, and it was good, and it was slow, because it was made in the open on the old spoil and the acid it made along the way was lost to the air. Emeric had made a finger's depth of real acid on a bench in a glass flask, once, and cracked the flask. At scale, acid wanted a chamber. The chamber wanted to be lined with something the acid would not eat. Lead.

And the third.

He did not say the third out loud. He looked at the lump on the bench, heavy and soft and grey, and thought about a sheet of Nevin's type metal, and a church roof, and a crate, and a hand-made powder that threw one charge in seven low. He thought of a small round mould in two halves, like a pair of pincers with a hollow in the jaws. He thought about how many balls a pound of lead made.

He put that door shut.

"Nevin first," he said. "Then the chamber. Get a furnace built for the roasting outside the wall, downwind of everything, and don't let anybody breathe it."

Bram nodded. He was looking at Emeric, not at the lump, with the look he had had at the table the night of the sheets, when he had said nothing and seen the rifle coming.

"And the rest of it?" Bram said.

"There is no rest of it."

Bram looked at him a moment longer, and then he picked up the lump, and weighed it in his hand the way he weighed everything, and put it back in the crate with the others.

Bram put the new watcher on the rail works' engine on the day it was fired.

He had thought about it for a month, the way Emeric had once thought about Rudd, and he had written it down, because that was how things were done now and he had come round to it. He wrote it on the back of the rule Emeric had written for the first engine, the one Ferra had countersigned: The man who watches the fire holds the works. He had never changed a word of that rule and he did not change one now. He wrote under it.

At every boiler in the barony, from this day, the man who watches is not the man who drives. The driver makes the engine go. The watcher stops it. They are not the same man, ever, even for an hour.

He had come to it by watching Rudd.

Rudd had been the first watcher. Rudd had held the first engine since the week it was built, and he had been the best man for it Bram had ever seen, and he still was. But the first engine drove a lathe and a mill, and nobody had ever needed it to go faster than it went. The rail works' engine would drive a stand, and a stand wanted heat, and wanted it now, while the slab was hot, and a man whose whole mind was on the slab and the rolls and the next pass would look at the gauge with the same eyes he looked at the slab with. Bram knew that because he had done it at his own furnace for thirty years. There was no man alive who could want a thing to go and watch it to see if it should stop, both at once.

So the rail works' engine got two. Rudd would drive it. Rudd had asked to, and there was nobody better. And at the gauge, with the lever and the cocks and the right to put the fire out and clear the shed and answer to nobody, Bram put Kester.

Kester was nineteen. He had come to the works at thirteen, off the lower water, a lighterman's boy who could not stand boats. He had fed the first engine's fire for Rudd since the week it was built and watched its gauge for the last year, and he had read everything Emeric had ever written about steam, which was not much, and asked Bram questions about it that Bram could not answer. He was careful. He was the most careful young man Bram had ever had in the shop. That was why.

Rudd read the new line standing in the rail works' engine shed with the paper at arm's length, as he read everything.

"Him," he said.

"Him."

Rudd lowered the paper. He looked across the shed at Kester, who was standing by the gauge with his hands behind his back, not looking at them, the way a boy stands when he knows he is being talked about.

"I've no quarrel with the lad," said Rudd. "He's careful. He's the best fire I ever had." He folded the paper in half. "But he's never scalded himself. He's never been in a shed when a joint went. He's never had to put his hand on a cock with the steam coming out of it round his fingers and hold it. He knows what it says in the book. That's all he knows." He handed the paper back. "A man who's never been burned is a strange man to put in charge of a boiler, Bram. That's all I'll say about it."

"I know," said Bram.

"Then why?"

"Because he's never been burned." Bram put the paper in his coat. "Everybody in this shed who's been burned has learned how much a boiler'll stand before it goes. I've learned it. You've learned it. And every time we learned it we learned it a little further along, because it didn't go." He looked at the gauge. "I want the man who doesn't know how far it'll go. I want the man who stops it where the book says, because he hasn't got a better idea."

Rudd thought about that for a long time.

"And when I tell him one more heat?"

"He says no."

"He's nineteen."

"Then he says no at nineteen." Bram did up his coat. "It's written. Nobody can countermand him. Not you, not me, not the prince. It's been written since the first engine. You're the one who taught him that line."

Rudd looked at Kester again, across the shed. Kester had not moved.

"Aye," said Rudd. "I did." And he went to look at his fire.

The blue water came in the last week of the rail works' first month, and nobody but a boy would have seen it.

Emeric went out to the spoil behind the foundry on an errand that had nothing to do with it, to see whether the salt beds would stand another summer's burning. They were the beds he had described to the table the night the sulphur came: the old spoil heaps opened up and burned over, and the rain let into them, and the seep that came out at their feet led off down a split-log trough into barrels. The seep ran green. Pale, a little cloudy, the colour of the salt. It always had.

At the far end of the last heap it ran blue.

Not much of it. A thread down one side of the trough, where the seep from that end came in and had not yet mixed with the rest. A clear hard blue, like a jay's feather, in the grey morning.

The boy who worked the beds was standing over it with his spade. He had picked the first salt out of these heaps with his fingers, the winter before the sulphur, and he watched them now as if they were his, and in a way they were.

"It's done that since the thaw," he said. "Only that end. I didn't know if I should say." He held up the spade. "And it did that."

He had left it standing in the blue end of the trough overnight, he said, because he forgot it, and in the morning the blade was red. Not rust. Rust was brown and flaked. This was a thin even skin of dull red over the iron wherever the water had been, and nowhere else. Emeric took the spade and turned it to the light and scraped the blade with his thumbnail, and the red came away under the nail, and on the nail it was pink, and bright, and soft.

Copper.

Out of the water and onto the iron, in a night, on its own. He had read of it once in his old world, at a desk: miners who had found their mine water running blue and dropped scrap iron in it and fished out copper, and decided it was witchcraft that turned iron into copper, and had not been entirely wrong about the witchcraft. He had not thought of it since. He stood on the spoil with the spade in his hands and looked at the far end of the heap. It was where the diggings' last loads had been tipped, the deep ones, the poor thin iron the foundry had cursed before Grayrock's ore began coming down the river.

He put that away with the things he would come back to.

He had the boy lay the mill yard's plate trimmings in the blue end of the trough. There were cartloads of them off the rolls and nobody had a use for them. Every morning the boy was to lift them out and scrape them over a pot and lay them back, and keep the pot, and take it to the Chancellor when it was full. The first pot went to Ferra at the end of the month, a heavy crust of red mud dried in the bottom of a stoneware crock. She had Bram melt a spoonful of it at the forge to see what it was, and entered the pot at what copper fetched on the coast, and then sat looking at the line for a while, because it was the first thing the old spoil had ever paid the barony for.

He built the bell before the first pot was full.

He built it in the pattern store at night, with Bram, out of what was on the shelves. Six crocks from the kiln, glazed, each the size of a quart. Inside each crock a pot of unglazed clay, the kind the kiln made for flowerpots, that water could sweat through slowly but not pour through. In each clay pot, green water from the beds and a strip of the mill's iron. In each crock around the pot, blue water from the trough and a strip of copper, cut from a length of pipe left over from the ship's fit-out and beaten flat. A wire from the iron in the first crock to the copper in the second, and so on down the row.

At the end of the row he wound a nail. He wound it with the forge room's finest copper wire, the roll it kept for the fire chips, wrapped first in waxed thread so that no turn could touch the next, a hundred turns and then a hundred more over them, while Bram held the nail in the vice and watched his hands and did not ask. Over the head of the nail he set a tongue of spring steel, cut from the trimmings of the first plate off the rolls, with a little brass cup riveted to its end and a screw behind it to set it by, and a wire from the tongue back to the first crock.

Then he touched the last wire to the screw.

The tongue jumped to the nail, and let go, and jumped again, faster than a man could count, and the brass cup rang against the nail's head in a thin continuous whirring buzz that filled the pattern store.

It went on.

Bram put his hand flat on the nearest crock. He did it the way he put his hand on a stone to see if it was charged, and Emeric watched him feel for the warmth that ought to have been there and not find it. He took his hand away and put it on the next crock, and the next, all six, down the row, while the bell buzzed at the end of it.

"There's nothing in it," he said.

"No."

"No stone."

"No stone."

Bram looked at the crocks, and at the nail, and at the thin line of copper looped from pot to pot along the bench. "What's pushing it?"

"The iron," said Emeric. "It's eating itself. The same as in the trough, only the long way round, through the wire." He did not have the words to give Bram the rest of it, and did not try. "When the iron's gone it stops. Then you put in more iron."

"A Copper's worth."

"Less."

Bram listened to it for a long time. He did not ask what it was for. He looked at it the way he had looked at the lump of galena on the same bench not a fortnight before, weighing it, only this time with his eyes.

Emeric stood by the bench and let it ring. He had seen three doors open in this room, over the lead, and shut one. This was a fourth, and he had not seen it coming, and he did not shut it. He stood and listened to the bell and thought about a pump at the foot of a water tower that turned all night with nobody pouring into it, and a light over a street that no mage had lit, and a flat full of open trenches with a row of dots beside every one of them on the Tarrants' sheets.

He let it ring until Ward put his head round the door to ask what the noise was, and then he took the wire off the screw, and the pattern store was quiet.

The loan was the last thing that spring, and the largest, and it was done at the long-room table on the quarter day, with Ferra and Saban and Vantic, and Emeric at the head, and Ward in the chair by the door.

It was not a new loan yet. That was what Vantic kept saying, in her quiet exact voice, with her book open in front of her. It was a figure. The House did not lend against a figure. It lent against an instrument, and there was no instrument, because there was no drawing, and there was no drawing because the prince's first rule said there would be none until it was whole.

"But you want to know," she said, "whether the House will be there when the drawing is whole."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"Then I need the figure. Not the city. What the city and the works will need, beyond anything the barony can earn, between now and the day the city pays its own way." She looked at Ferra. "Not what you'd like. What you'll need. I can send the House a figure you'd like and they'll halve it. If I send them the one you'll need, they'll read it."

Ferra gave it to her.

She had worked it for three weeks, off the survey and the quarry tally and Bram's pipe sheet and the rail works and Hollis's harvest bounty and the mages' pay, which was a figure nobody at the table except Armand had any sense of yet, and Armand had given her a range and refused to narrow it. She had it on one sheet, in pencil, with the ink figure at the bottom. She did not read it out. She turned the sheet round and pushed it across the table.

Vantic read it.

Then she took up her own pen and wrote it in her own book, in her own neat small hand, the figure only, with the date beside it.

Then she looked at it.

She looked at it for longer than it had taken her to write it. Much longer. Emeric watched her do it, and watched Ferra watch her, and watched Saban pretend not to watch either of them. Vantic sat with her pen above the page and her eyes on the figure and her face perfectly still, the face she kept for tables, and whatever she was reading in it was not the number. She had seen larger numbers. She had lent larger numbers. She was reading something else, and Emeric did not know what, and he found, sitting at the head of the table, that he very much wanted to.

"I'll send it," she said at last.

"They'll halve it," said Saban.

"They won't." Vantic closed her book. "They'll send a man. And he'll want to see the flat, and the quarry, and the works, and he'll want to walk the line of the wall with a chain. And then they'll lend it, or they won't, and either way it won't be halved." She put her pen away. "The House doesn't halve a figure like that. It either believes it or it doesn't."

"Which will it do?" said Emeric.

Vantic looked at him across the table for a moment, with nothing in her face at all.

"I've never known," she said.

The coke ovens at the mill yard had been doubled in the second week of the plate, to feed the heats. Everybody who went near the mill yard that spring noticed the same thing and nobody could do anything about it. The new ovens gave off a gas when they were charged, a thick yellowish reek that hung in the yard in wet weather and made the eyes run and smelled like rotten eggs and burning tar together. A lamp carried too close to the oven door made the air round it flicker blue. The men learned to charge the ovens with the lamps hung back, and to stand upwind, and cursed it, and the gas went up the chimney and out over the lower town when the wind was wrong, and nobody wanted it.

The coaster was plated by the end of spring.

The first plate went down the road to Fletcher's yard on a cart on a clear morning, lashed flat on a bed of straw under a tarred cloth, with two of Bram's men walking beside it and a horse that had never pulled anything so heavy for its size, because steel plate was heavier than it looked. The road to the lower water went out from the Western Gate and round by the long ward, and past it, and down.

Emeric was on the gate when it went. He watched it go down the road in the sun, slowly, the horse leaning into the collar, and the men walking, and the tarred cloth shining.

At the long ward, the door was open.

Imani was standing in it. She had a basin in her hands, which she had been carrying somewhere and had stopped carrying. She stood in her own doorway in her apron with her sleeves rolled and watched the cart go past, with the plate on it, a few feet from her, going to the hull. She did not move until it had gone round the bend in the road below the ward and out of sight.

Then she turned and went back inside, with the basin, and the door stayed open behind her. Emeric could see, from the gate, the ward's roof above it, old slate on old laths, and the dark stain on the gable end where the rain had been coming in all winter.
