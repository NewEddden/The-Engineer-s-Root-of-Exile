Chapter 155 - Sand on the Rail

The first full ore train left the Grayrock landing on the sixteenth of April at seven in the morning, twenty wagons of it, and stopped on the hill a quarter of a mile later with its wheels going round.

Emeric was not on it. He was at the top of the shoulder with Odo, where the line came up out of Grayrock's valley on Paskin's bench and went over the brow and down to the river road and Kaldo, because Odo had wanted to watch it come up the hill from the top, the way a man wants to watch a horse he has bought come up a hill. They heard it start at the landing. The long shriek of the whistle off the valley's sides, and then the beat, slow, the chimney's hard flat cough, one and one and one, and the clank of twenty couplings taking up their slack one after another down the train like a man cracking his knuckles.

It came up the bottom of the grade well. They could see it from the top: the engine small and black at the head of the long brown string, the white steam blowing back off it in the morning air, the chimney's smoke going straight up and then bending. It came round the first curve and onto the long straight of the bench, and the beats came slower, as they should on a hill, one, and one, heavy, like a man carrying a sack up a stair.

Then they came fast.

Not faster. Fast. All at once, a rattle of them, one-one-one-one-one, the chimney going like a drum, the smoke coming up in a solid black column; and the engine did not go any faster up the hill for it. It did not go anywhere. It stood on the bench with its wheels going round under it in a blur and a shower of sparks coming off the rails, screaming, and the twenty wagons of ore stood behind it on the slope, and the whole train began, very slowly, to slide back.

Odo did not say anything. He watched Bram shut the regulator, and the wheels stop, and the brakes go on all down the train with a long groan as the brakemen wound them; and the train stood on the hill.

"Wheels slip," said Odo. "On the iron. Too much weight behind and not enough on." He leaned on his stick. "Same as a horse on ice."

They split the train. Bram backed the whole of it down to the landing and uncoupled it in the middle and took ten wagons up the hill, and they came up the bench with the beats heavy and slow and even and never once broke into the rattle, and over the brow, and away down toward the river road. Then he came back light for the other ten. It took the whole of the morning to do what Bram had promised the steward of Grayrock would take an hour, and when the second half came over the brow at noon Bram was standing on the footplate with his face black from the chimney and his jaw set like a vice, and he did not look at the top of the hill where Emeric and Odo were standing, and Emeric did not wave.



It rained in the night, and the next morning it was worse.

Ten wagons this time, to be sure. The engine got them a hundred yards up the bench and the wheels went, in the wet, on ten wagons that it had walked up the hill dry the day before; went in a long shriek, and caught, and went again, and the train slid back the length of a wagon before the brakes held it. Bram took five. Five went up. Five wagons of ore to a train was not a railway. Five wagons was what the horses had been pulling.

Tess came up the line from Kaldo that afternoon on the empty return with her data book and sat at the foot of the grade in her chair under an oilskin for three hours in the drizzle, with a sand timer and a pencil, timing every slip. She drew the hill on a page: the bench and its slope, and where on it the wheels had gone, which was always the same three places, where the rails were wettest under the trees. She drew the driving wheels, with the weight on them. She did arithmetic about the weight on the driving wheels down half a page, and frowned at it.

"It's the grip," she said, at the shed that evening, to Bram and Emeric and Odo, with the page on the bench. "That's all it is. The wheel can push the train only as hard as the rail pushes back on the wheel, and the rail pushes back only as hard as the engine's heavy on it. Dry, she's heavy enough. Wet, she's not." She put her pencil on the drawing. "We could make her heavier."

"She's as heavy as the rails will take," said Bram.

"I know." Tess looked at the drawing. "Or we could put more wheels on the drive. She'd have to be built again."

Emeric said nothing. He knew what she was looking for and he knew where it was; he had seen it on a page in the other life, a drawing of a great green engine with a little pipe coming down in front of each driving wheel, and he did not open his mouth, because he had a feeling that he was not going to need to.

Odo had not looked at the drawing at all. He was sitting on an upturned bucket by the shed door with his stick across his knees, looking out at the rain coming down on the rails.

"Sand," he said.

They looked at him.

"Under the hill. Forty years ago." He did not take his eyes off the rails. "Wooden rails, in the galleries, oak, and the tubs on wooden wheels. And the galleries were wet. Always. Water coming down the walls, water on the floor, and the rails slimed with it, so a lad pushing a full tub up the slope to the shaft would push and push and his tub'd sit there and his feet'd go out from under him." He moved his stick on his knees. "So every lad had a bag of sand on his belt. Dry sand, from the bin by the shaft foot, where the fire was. And at the bottom of the slope he'd take a fistful and throw it on the rail in front of his wheels, and push, and up she'd go." He looked at Tess at last. "That's all. Sand. Wet rail, dry sand, under the wheel, just where it bites."

Tess looked at him for a while without saying anything. Then she turned to a new page.

The sandbox went on the engine in three days. A box of sheet iron on the running plate on each side, ahead of the driving wheels, with a lid; and from the bottom of each box a pipe, bent down, ending a hand's width above the rail just in front of where the front driving wheel met it; and a valve in each pipe on a long rod to a lever in the cab by the driver's hand. Pull the lever, and dry sand ran down the pipes onto the rails in front of the drivers in a thin steady stream, and the wheels rolled over it and ground it into the iron. The sand was dried in an iron tray on the shed's boiler, because, Odo said, a box of wet sand was a box of mud, and his lads had found that out before any of them were twelve.

On the twentieth, in a drizzle, Bram took twenty wagons up the grade.

The drivers went at the first wet place under the trees, with the shriek. Bram pulled the lever. Emeric, on the footplate beside him, could not see the sand go; he heard it. A hiss under the wheels like rain on a hot stove. And the wheels bit. The shriek stopped as if a door had shut on it, and the beats came down out of their rattle into the slow heavy one and one and one, and the engine leaned into the hill and walked up it, with twenty wagons of Grayrock's ore groaning behind it on the wet, past the second wet place and the third, with the sand hissing, and over the brow.

Odo was at the top. He had walked up the hill on his stick in the rain to stand there. When the engine came over the brow he did not cheer and did not wave. He took his hat off, and stood there in the rain with it in his hand while the twenty wagons went by him one after another, and counted them under his breath, and put his hat back on.

From that week the ore came down the line in trains of twenty. Grayrock's river was no longer the only road out of Grayrock.



The bracket cracked on the twenty-fourth.

Not on the grade. In the shed at Kaldo, at night, with the engine cold and nobody on it. The shed's cleaner found it in the morning with his lamp: a crack across the iron bracket that held the left-hand slide bars to the frame, from its bolt hole to its edge, as fine as a hair and as long as a finger, and when Bram put a chisel into it and leaned, the bracket came apart in two pieces in his hand.

Tess's crew welded a new one in before noon. The arc was in daily use at the works now, behind its screens, with the dark glass in the masks and Imani's sign on every door it was used behind. Rails and joints and frames and brackets, everything that had been riveted or bolted or forged in one piece last year was being welded this one, and the crew did it without fuss: the new bracket clamped, the carbons struck, the blue-white glare behind the screens and the hiss of it, and the seam laid down along the join like a bead of molten wax, and chipped, and filed. The engine went out at one with the new bracket on it.

Bram kept the old one.

He had the two halves on his bench at the works when Emeric came down in the afternoon, side by side on a cloth, with the broken faces turned up to the window. He was not looking at them. He was looking at the bracket on the other side of the engine, the right-hand one, which had come off it that morning when the new left one went on, and was whole.

"Same smith," said Bram. "Same iron. Same day. Same drawing." He put his finger on the whole one, and then on the broken one. "This one's held. This one broke sitting still. Why?"

"Different heat," said Emeric.

"I know it's a different heat." Bram did not look up. "I want to know which."

That was the thing. Every smith in four baronies knew that hot iron quenched in water came out hard, and quenched in oil came out less hard, and left to cool in air came out soft, and that a smith with a good eye could tell by the colour how hot was hot enough and by the hiss how fast it was going. And every smith in four baronies had his own eye and his own hiss and his own father's opinion about both, and there was no way at all to ask the bracket, afterward, which of them had been used on it. Only to break it. And breaking it told you that it had been wrong. Not how.

The forge had made the balls in July, for the bearings. Master balls in five sizes, each one a hair from perfect, hardened right through, harder than anything else in Kaldo. And it made, that week, at Emeric's word, a set of blocks: little squares of steel, eight of them, each brought to a hardness the forge knew and Bram did not, and each stamped with a number from soft to hard. Reference blocks. A thing to try a thing against.

The rest was Bram's. He built it in two days, and it was nothing but a lever. A heavy iron lever on a stand, with a known weight hung on its long end and one of the forge's balls set in a cup under its short end; so that when the lever came down, slowly, on a screw, the ball was pressed into whatever lay under it with a force Bram knew to the pound, and held there for a count of thirty, and lifted off. And it left a dent. A small round shining dish in the face of the steel. The harder the steel, the smaller the dish.

He pressed it into all eight of the forge's blocks, one after another, the soft one first, and Ivo came down from the glassworks with a glass on a stand and a little ruled scale he had made for it, and measured the width of every dent, and Bram wrote them down in a column against the blocks' numbers. That was the ladder. Then he pressed the ball into the whole bracket, and into the broken one, and Ivo measured.

The whole one sat between the third block and the fourth. The broken one sat beyond the eighth, harder than the hardest thing the forge had made for him to try it against.

"Hardness tester," said Emeric, when Bram had written it down. "That's what that's called."

"It's a hammer," said Bram, "that measures how much the thing it hits minds." But he wrote the name on the stand in chalk, under the lever, and it stayed there.

Then he sawed the broken bracket across, through the middle of the crack, in the vice, which took him an hour, and ground the cut face flat, and polished it on a lap with finer and finer grit until it shone like a mirror, and Tess, who had come down on the motor to watch, gave him a little bottle of acid from the acid works, very weak, and he dabbed it on the mirror with a feather. The mirror went dull. Grey. Bram washed it, and dried it, and carried it across the yard to the glassworks in both hands like a man carrying an egg, and put it under Ivo's microscope.

He looked until his eye watered. Then he made Emeric look.

The steel under the glass was not grey. It was a field, a whole country seen from very high up, of small dark grains packed against one another; and through them, everywhere, crossing them in every direction, lay long fine needles, hard and bright, like a scattering of pins on a dark cloth. The whole field shot through with them.

He knew what it was. The materials the trial had put into him whole knew it, the way they knew everything, without his ever having stood at a bench: this was what steel looked like when it had been cooled too fast, and steel that looked like this would break if you looked at it unkindly.

"Quenched too hard," he said.

"Quenched in brine," said Bram. "Cold brine. I'll lay money." He took his eye from the glass. "Kerran's lad. He's been using the brine tub for the small work since the winter because it's quicker, and the small work's never broke, so he went on." He looked at the field of needles a while longer. "And I never knew. I'd have broke the next one, and the next, and blamed the iron."

He did not blame Kerran's lad. He went back to the works and took the bracket's drawing off the wall and wrote on it, under the shape of the bracket, in the corner, a line he had never written on a drawing in his life: the number from the hardness tester that the whole bracket had made, between the third block and the fourth, and under it the words quench in oil. And then, because that was the kind of man Bram was once he had decided a thing, he did the same to every drawing on the wall of the works that had a piece of hardened steel in it, which took him the rest of the week, and from that week every piece of steel that went out of the works for anything that mattered went past the hardness tester first, and had its number written on its tag beside Tess's pressure card.



Paskin was on the grade on the twenty-sixth, packing ballast.

The rain had washed the broken stone out from under the sleepers at the second wet place, where the water came off the hill under the trees, and the rails had begun to sag there, and Odo had sent for him. Paskin did not like being sent for. He was a short square man in his forties with a farmer's shoulders and a farmer's hands and a mage's ring he kept in his pocket because it got in the way, and he had made the slipway ramp in nine days and the bench up this hill in a fortnight, and he regarded the whole of the Grayrock line, privately, as his.

He knelt by the sagged rails with both hands flat on the ballast, and the stone under them moved. Not much. It shifted, as if a man underneath it had rolled over in his sleep, and settled, and packed itself tight under the sleepers from below, and the sag came out of the rails while Emeric watched, an inch, and then the rest. A small amber glyph came up off the backs of Paskin's hands and went out.

Then Paskin got up and showed the gang how to do it with a beater and a crowbar, which he always did, and went and sat on a sleeper with his pipe.

Emeric sat down on the next sleeper.

"Show me how you do that," he said.

Paskin looked at him round the pipe. "You're fire," he said. "Everybody knows you're fire."

"I know. I want to know how you do it anyway."

"It'll cost you three times what it costs me."

"I know."

Paskin took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at it. He had been one of Armand's for two years and he had the habit of the school now, the one everybody in Kaldo had: if a man asked how, you told him. He did not have to like it.

"Earth doesn't want to be told," he said. "That's the first thing. Fire wants to go. Water wants to go down. Air wants to go anywhere. Earth wants to stay where it is. It's been where it is since before your grandfather's grandfather, and it means to go on being there after you." He put his hand flat on the ballast between their boots. "So you don't tell it. You lean on it. Like a man leaning on a door that's stuck, not kicking it. You lean, and you lean, and it gives." He took his hand away. "Go on. Lean."

Emeric put his hand where Paskin's had been.

He leaned. He did not know what he was leaning with, at first; he let the mana go down into his palm and out of it into the stone, and it was like putting his hand into a bucket of cold gravel. The stone did not care. It sat. He leaned harder, and felt the cost of it at once, behind his eyes, a dull push that his own fire had never once cost him, and the glyph came up off his hand, amber and wobbling and too big, and the stone under his palm moved.

A little heap of it came up between his fingers. The size of a man's fist. Round on top.

Paskin looked at it.

"That's a molehill," said Paskin.

Emeric did it again. It came easier the second time, the way everything did, and cost less, and the heap that came up under his hand was longer, and flatter on its face. He did it a third time, and leaned along a line and not on a point, and the stone came up in a ridge the length of his forearm, and stood, with a flat steep face on one side of it like a step.

"What's it for?" said Paskin.

"A starting block."

"A what?"

"A thing to push off." He put his boot against the ridge's steep face and pushed, gently, and it held. "When you've got to go somewhere fast from standing. The ground only lets your foot push as hard as your weight presses it down. Put a step behind your foot and you're pushing against the step, and the step doesn't care how heavy you are."

Paskin looked at the ridge, and at the boot against it, for some while.

"Most men use the ground," he said.

"Most men aren't in a hurry."

Paskin put his pipe back in his mouth. "Again," he said, round it. "Faster. And don't make the glyph so big. You're not lighting a beacon. You're asking a door."



Declan taught him feet at the dawn drill, in front of everybody, because Ward told him to.

That was the thing about Declan. Ward had said once, in Emeric's hearing, that Declan could read a level better than any surveyor and a man better than any priest, and Declan had looked faintly embarrassed and gone back to cleaning his bracer. He did not teach the way Rylan taught, laughing, or the way Ward taught, by putting a man on the ground until he learned. He stood in the yard with his arms folded and watched the pair in front of him, Osric and one of the levy, and said things.

"Feet first," said Declan. "Watch his feet. Not his blade. Not his eyes. Eyes lie. A man will look at your head and cut at your knee. Feet don't lie, because feet have to carry him, and a man can't go anywhere his feet haven't gone first." He watched. "There. Left foot. He's turned it out. He's going to come round on your right."

Osric came round on the levy man's right.

"Hips tell you when," said Declan. "Feet tell you where, hips tell you when. When his hips go, he's gone, and if you're watching his blade you'll see it a breath after you should have." He tilted his head. "There."

Osric went. The levy man was on the cobbles.

"You watch the feet," said Declan to Emeric, "and you'll lose to Osric a little less." He thought about it. "Not much less. He's quick."

"I heard that," said Osric, from the cobbles, where he was helping the levy man up.

Emeric watched feet for the rest of the drill. And with his sense open, thin, the way he held it now without thinking, he watched them a second way at the same time, and found something he had not looked for. A man's note moved before his feet did. Not by much. Before Osric turned his left foot out to come round on the right, something in the spill of Osric's note had already gone that way, a lean in it, the way water in a pail leans before the pail tips. A breath early. Less than a breath.

It was earlier than the feet. It was also noisier. Osric's note leaned three times for every time Osric went, because Osric thought about going a great deal more often than he went, and there was no telling from the lean alone which of the three was the one.

He did not say any of that to Declan. Declan could not have felt it, and Emeric could not have explained to him how he knew.



On the last night of April he asked Ward for no openings.

The keep yard, with the gate shut, and the lamp on the mounting block, and the practice blades. The knee was a month old and Imani had said run on it, so he was running on it.

"Everything," he said. "Don't give me anything. Don't wait for me. Don't let me see it coming."

Ward looked at him across the yard in the lamplight.

"Everything," said Ward.

"Everything."

Ward came at him.

He had his sense open before Ward moved. Wide, the whole yard, the way he would open it on a wall at night: the lamp's small hot nothing, the stable, the horses' warm notes behind its door, the gate, Ward. He held it open. And under it, behind it, he reached for the other thing, the thing that was not a spell and that he had no word for but the room: the slow time of the delve, the long moment in which he could think three thoughts while the world thought one. He had found the edge of it once or twice in the yard by accident, in the middle of an exchange, a breath in which Ward's blade seemed to come at him through honey. He reached for it on purpose now. He found it, or the edge of it. He held that open too.

And he put the push into his body, the way he had put it into his legs at the stable wall, and held that as well.

The first exchange was the best one he had ever had with Ward in five years. He saw Ward's note lean before Ward's feet moved and Ward's feet move before Ward's blade, and the blade came at him through honey, slow, so slow he had time to look at it, and he turned it with his own and stepped off the line, and Ward's blade went past him, and he was still standing.

The second was as good.

On the third Ward came in with his shoulder. No blade; the shoulder, the whole weight of a knight behind it, low, the way a knight goes through a door. And Emeric put his weight down. Not his strength. His weight. Into his front foot, through the cobbles, Heavy, the way Tess's chair went Heavy on the wall walk; and Ward hit him and stopped.

He stopped dead. Like a cart driven into a wall. Two of the cobbles under Emeric's front boot cracked across with a sound like a dropped plate, and Ward stood with his shoulder against Emeric's chest, quite still, and did not push again. He stepped back. He looked at Emeric's boot and the cracked stones under it, and then at Emeric's face, and said nothing at all.

On the fourth exchange Emeric tried the blade.

He had thought about it for a week. A practice blade was a thing, not a man; it did not resist the way a man's body did, only through the grip on it, and in the middle of a swing a blade was carried by the swing more than by the hand. So in the middle of Ward's swing, as it came down at him through the honey, he put out his left hand at it, small, and made it heavy. Three times its weight. The little violet glyph came up off his palm and went to it.

Ward saw it. Of course Ward saw it; a glyph glows. Ward's bracer went bright at his wrist, a coal blown on, and Ward's blade turned in the air, a flick, the charged edge of it going through the violet ring before it could close, and the ring went out in a little white flash like a spark off a grindstone, and stung, and the weight never reached the blade at all. It came on at its own weight, and Emeric only just got his own across in time.

And then Ward drove him.

Not fast. Steady, with the blade, cut and cut and cut, high and low and high, giving him nothing, as he had been asked; and Emeric met every one of them. He had the sense open on Ward and the honey round Ward's blade and the push in his legs, and he watched Ward's note lean and Ward's feet and Ward's blade, all of him, every breath, so hard that the yard went away round the edges of it like a lamp going down. He went back a step. Another. He met every cut.

His heel hit the mounting block.

He had forgotten it was there. It had been there for three hundred years, at the side of the yard by the stable, a block of stone the height of his knee, and he had stood on it to get on a horse a thousand times; and he had gone back across the yard step by step watching nothing in the world but Ward, and his heel hit the block, and he went over it backward, and sat down hard on the cobbles on the far side of it with his blade gone somewhere in the dark.

Ward stood over the block and looked down at him.

"You were watching me so hard," said Ward, "you forgot there's a yard."

Emeric sat on the cobbles. He found he was breathing as if he had run up the keep stair twice. And the honey had gone, all at once, the moment he hit the block, and the sense with it, snapped shut like a book; and where they had been, behind his eyes, there was a pain he had not felt since the winter he first learned to let his sense out, a tight hot ache like a hand closing on the back of his skull. Four exchanges. He had held it all for four exchanges, and it had cost him more than the balloon on the quay had cost him in the whole of the time it took to wind it down.

"It costs," he said.

"Everything costs." Ward did not offer his hand yet. "You held it all. All the time. The sense and the other thing, the thing that makes you look like you're reading my blade off a page." He had seen that, then. "And the push in your legs. All of it, open, all the time, as hard as you could." He looked at the block. "Nobody can do that. Not you. Not anybody. A man who tries to see everything sees one thing, very well, and walks into a stone."

He held out his hand then and pulled Emeric up.

"One more thing," said Ward. "Then bed."

He picked up both practice blades and gave Emeric his, and stood square to him in the lamplight, and lifted his own blade slowly, very slowly, so slowly that a child could have watched it, and brought it down at Emeric's head.

"Meet it," said Ward.

Emeric met it, square, blade to blade, and felt the shock of it go down his arm into his shoulder.

"Again," said Ward. "Don't meet it."

The blade came down again, slowly. And this time Ward, standing beside him now, put a hand flat on Emeric's back and turned him with it as the blade came, a quarter turn, so that Emeric's own blade did not meet Ward's square but lay along it, and Ward's blade went on down past his shoulder where his head had been, sliding off his, with all its weight still in it and nothing to stop it. Ward's hand on his back finished the turn. And Emeric was standing beside Ward's blade, not in front of it, with his own blade's point a hand from Ward's ribs.

"You give the blow its road," said Ward. "You don't stop it. You let it go where it's going, and you go round it, and it takes you round, and when you come out of the turn you're where it isn't." He took his hand off Emeric's back. "That's the old school. The capital doesn't teach it now. It teaches meeting. It's quicker to teach."

"Who taught you?"

"A man who's dead." Ward lowered his blade. "And you can't do it yet. You can't do anything yet, the way you're doing it. You're too busy watching." He looked at Emeric in the lamplight, and then, briefly, at Emeric's left forearm, at the place under the sleeve where the long white line ran from the wrist halfway to the elbow.

"The passage," said Ward. "In the capital."

Emeric put his right hand over the scar without meaning to.

"You let the fire go," said Ward. "All of it. Out. The flare. It was right; I said so." He was quiet. "And then you were empty behind it. Half a breath. Everything you had was out in the air as fire, and none of it was in your arms, and before it could come back in, the knife came. That's where it went. Into the half breath." He looked at the scar under Emeric's hand. "It wasn't deep because there's always a little in a man even when he's sent it all out, the way there's always a little warmth in a hearth. That's luck. Don't spend it."

"You've known that since the capital."

"Since the passage." Ward put the blades in the rack. "Every time you go out, you come back in late. Half a breath. Every time. I can see it from across the yard, because I know where to look." He turned toward the arch. "Somebody else will find it. Somebody who's looking. And they won't be using a practice blade."

He went out under the arch, and Emeric stood in the yard alone by the lamp with his hand on his arm and the ache closing on the back of his skull, and looked at the two cracked cobbles where his boot had been.
