Chapter 367 - A Heartbeat Is a Lighthouse

BR-01 stood on a trestle in the middle of the Motion Shop floor with its hatch open and its insides out, and Ambrose was lying underneath it on his back on a sack.

It wasn't much to look at yet. A drum of grey plate about the height of a man's chest and the width of a rain barrel, riveted down every seam, with a white K stencilled on its side and the call sign over it in letters Wren had cut the stencil for and then made Tess check twice. A short mast on top for the wind dynamo, blades not yet on. A boom through its upper body, folded, with a beam antenna at each end. A cage of wire round its lower half like a lobster pot. And underneath, where Ambrose was, a ring of six empty sockets for the lift stones and in the middle a round glass port, very thick, with nothing behind it yet.

"It's a dustbin," said Saban.

"It's a dustbin that's going to sit five miles up for a year in a wind that'd take your hat off in Grayrock," said Tess, "and talk to the next dustbin a hundred and fifty miles away, and the one after that, and it's going to do it without anybody touching it." She was on a stool with her stick across her knees. "Be nice to it."

It was the eighteenth of September. They had the long bench cleared at the side of the shop and were sitting round it on whatever there was: Emeric, Tess, Ambrose when he came out from under, Saban on an upturned crate with his hat on his knee, and Armand in the only real chair, because he had walked in and taken it. Wren was at the far bench pretending to work on a gearbox and actually listening, which everybody knew, and which Tess allowed because Wren was going to be the one keeping these things alive in a year.

"Go on, then," said Tess. "Tell us how it dies."

Emeric had been waiting for that, and it showed, and he didn't mind it showing.

He'd done this before, on the other side of everything. Not this, not drums in the Band; but he had sat in rooms where men put a thing up somewhere nobody could reach it and then spent a year finding out all the ways they'd never hear from it again. He knew the list. He'd known it since July. It came up whole, the way everything did, and he gave it to them the way Ward read a duty roster, flat, one line at a time, so that nobody would remember it as a lecture.

"It talks every quarter of an hour," he said. "Whether there's anything to say or not. A short call: its sign, its battery, the load on its pin, the charge in its lift. Nothing else. The two relays either side of it hear that and write it down. That's the heartbeat."

"Why bother if there's nothing to say?" said Ambrose, from under the drum.

"Because the day it stops, you want to know it's stopped. Not find out a week later when a message doesn't come." He held up a second finger. "It doesn't move. The pin holds it where it was let go, and the ship's log says where that was, and every beat it sends says it's still there. So we always know where it is before anything goes wrong. Nobody has to look for it." A third. "If it misses one beat, that's weather. A storm, a bad hour. If it misses three, its neighbours shout. Up the chain, to the Well, to the line room here. Which relay, where it was, what time it last spoke, what it said." A fourth. "And if something hits it, really hits it, the shell or the pin or something grabbing hold, it gets one last word out before it goes. What happened, and when. Ambrose can run that off a charged capacitor so it goes even if the battery and the stones are both gone."

"A dying gasp," said Ambrose, sliding out from under on the sack.

Emeric looked at him.

"That's what you'd call it," said Ambrose. "Isn't it? That's what it is." He sat up and wiped his hands. "I like that. I'll put that on the drawing."

Nobody was much impressed by any of it. Emeric hadn't expected them to be. That was the trouble with a good list; once it was said, it sounded like something anybody would have thought of. Tess was nodding along as if he'd told her that water ran downhill. Armand had his eyes half shut.

Saban hadn't moved at all.

"Every quarter of an hour," he said.

"Every quarter of an hour."

"On the same note, I suppose. The same call sign. Regular as a clock."

"That's the point of it."

"Yes," said Saban. "I know it is." He turned his hat a quarter turn on his knee. "There's a light on every bad point on this coast. It goes round on a count and it never misses. That's the point of it. Every ship for forty miles knows where that point is, any night of the year, without anybody having to tell them." He looked at BR-01 on its trestle. "So did every wrecker."

The shop had gone quiet. Wren had stopped pretending to work on the gearbox.

"A heartbeat is a lighthouse," said Saban. "That's all I'm saying. A thing that speaks on a rhythm, the same call, regular as a clock, is a thing that can be listened for. Anybody with a set can hear it. Anybody with a set and that loop of Ambrose's, the one he finds the island station with, can stand on a deck and turn it till the call's loudest and draw a line. Do it twice, from two places, and he's got a cross on his chart." He put the hat back on his knee. "You've just put a light up in the sky over the sea that says here I am, four times an hour, for a year. Anyone with a set can see it."

Emeric had known it. He had known it the moment he said the word regular, the way you know a step's loose as your foot comes down on it. He had been going to say it next. He hadn't said it, and Saban had, and Saban had said it better, the way a man who had spent his life listening at doors would say it.

"Yes," said Emeric. "It is."

"Then what do you do about it?"

"The beat doesn't go all round. Not on the whip, where anybody underneath could catch it." He reached across and tapped the folded boom on top of the drum, where the two beam antennas sat pointing opposite ways. "Down the beams only. Straight to the two neighbours and nowhere else. Short. In Ferra's cipher, so a man who hears it hears nothing he can read." He thought. "And not on the quarter hour. Inside the quarter hour, at a time that wanders, on a pattern only the deciders know. So it isn't a clock. It's a heartbeat with something wrong with it."

"Better," said Saban. "Not gone."

"No. Not gone."

"I'd like that written down too."

"I'll write it down," said Tess, and did, on the margin of her drawing, S: lighthouse, and underlined it.

Armand opened his eyes.

"It's all very good," he said. "The cipher. The beams. The wandering. I couldn't have thought of any of it." He was looking at the empty port under the drum, where the pin would go. "None of it makes any difference to me."

They looked at him.

"I don't need a set to find that," said Armand. "When it's built, it's going to have six gravity stones round its bottom and a stasis stone in the middle holding a charge you could light a street with. I'll feel that from my bed." He shifted in the chair. "I've been feeling for stones for eighty years. So has every mage in the tower who's any good, and so has the pope, and so, I'm told, has anything with wings that lives up there. You can make your radio as quiet as you like. The stones sing whether it's talking or not."

This one Emeric hadn't known.

He sat with it. It was so obvious, once said, that he was ashamed of it; and he could see exactly why he'd missed it, which was worse. Every list he'd ever learned had been made by men who couldn't feel anything. On the other side of everything, a thing in the sky that kept its mouth shut was hidden, and that was that. Here, the thing in the sky was held up by stones, and stones could be heard by anyone with ears for them, and there was no switch for that.

"What do we do about it?" said Tess.

"Nothing," said Armand. "There's nothing. You can't hide a stone from a man who can feel stones. The shell can't do it; it holds onto the air round it to work, and that makes it louder, if anything." He shrugged. "Put them where nobody is. High, and over the open sea, and a long way from anyone's coast. And accept that somebody who goes looking will find one."

"Then that's what we do," said Emeric.

Tess wrote A: felt, not heard. No fix. Open sea, high, far. She looked at it for a while.

"I've got one," she said.

"Go on."

"Your three missed beats." She tapped her pencil on the drawing. "A relay goes quiet. Its neighbours shout. Fine. But what if it isn't quiet? What if it's talking perfectly well and the neighbour's ear's gone? The neighbour hears nothing, and it shouts, and we send a hull out a thousand miles to look for a relay that's sitting there happy as a cat." She drew on the margin. "So each one listens for its second neighbour too. The one past the next. If it can't hear its neighbour and it can't hear the one past it either, the trouble's more likely with itself, and it checks itself before it accuses anybody." She looked up. "Somebody gone deaf doesn't get to say everybody else has stopped talking."

"That's good," said Emeric.

"I know it's good. I'll do the stone for it. It's a lot of keywork." She wrote. "Anything else?"

"One thing," he said. "For this one only. BR-01."

They waited.

"It's going up over our own bay. It's going to sit over Kaldo, five miles up, between the Well and the line room, for as long as it lasts. It's the test." He looked at the drum. "I want an eye in it."

"A what?"

"A telescope. A real one. Ivo's mirror, the way he did the one on the castle roof: a glass dish, ground, silvered on the face, looking down." He could see it already. "From up there it'd see the whole bay, and the coast both ways, and anything coming. Ships. A fleet. A cloud that isn't a cloud. Weather, two days before it gets here." He turned to Tess. "And the ones that go out on the voyage get small ones. Wide, for spotting. They'll be over open water; there's not much to look at but what's coming."

"How's anybody going to look through it?" said Ambrose. "It's five miles up."

"That," said Emeric, "is a question for another week."

Tess was looking at him with her pencil stopped. She was going to ask, and then she didn't, which meant she'd guessed something, and was going to keep it until she'd guessed the rest. She wrote BR-01: the eye (Ivo, mirror). Voyage: small eyes. and drew a little circle beside it.

"Ivo's going to want to grind that one himself," said Wren, from the far bench.

"Ivo can grind this one himself," said Emeric. "Tell him I said so. He's still sulking about the roof one."



Maeve came in on the one o'clock train from the capital with two bags and no warning and her hair in its long steel-ringed braid down her back, and she was at the Motion Shop door before anybody had thought to tell the castle she was in Kaldo at all.

"I paid," she said, before anybody could ask. "Second class. I sat next to a man with a pig." She put her bags down inside the door. "Not a whole pig. Most of one. He was taking it to his daughter." She looked round the shop at Tess, at Ambrose, at the drum on its trestle, at Wren, at Saban getting slowly to his feet, and last at Emeric, and her face did the thing it had done at his wedding, at the end of the trestles: decided, and pleased about it. "Hello. I've come for my stone. And I've come for the winter. Is that all right? I've written to Father. I don't know if he's got it."

"You've come for the Hearing," said Emeric. "It's in November."

"I know when it is. I've come early." She came across the floor and touched his arm, going past, the way she always did, and looked at the drum. "Is this one of your things? Is it about drains?"

"It goes in the sky."

"Oh, good." She bent and looked into the open hatch, at Ambrose's valve sets on their shelf, and the battery bank under them, and the empty cradle where the decider would go. "What's it for?"

"Talking. From a long way away."

"Like the phones."

"Like the phones, but further."

"How much further?"

"The other side of the sea," said Emeric, and she straightened up and looked at him, and he saw her understand that he meant it, and that it was for the voyage, and that she had come early for a reason of her own that she wasn't going to say in front of Saban.

"I see," said Maeve.

Saban had found his hat. He bowed to her, which he had never been seen to do to anyone in the Motion Shop, and said he'd see her at dinner, and went out. Armand stayed in his chair and watched her with the expression he wore for very young mages, which was a mixture of pity and appetite.



The lightning rod was Bram's, and Bram wasn't there, and it had to be tested before the stones went in.

It sat on the top of BR-01's mast, a short copper spike with a cluster of fine wires at its base that Bram called static wicks and that Emeric had drawn for him in June, to bleed off the charge the Band built up on anything that sat in it long enough. The spike went down inside the drum through an insulated sleeve to a heavy strap that ran round the hull to the stone cage and out, so that whatever hit the top would go round the drum's insides and not through them. In theory. Tess wanted it hit before anyone trusted it. Ambrose had his meter on the strap.

"You do it," said Tess.

"Ambrose can do it with the coil."

"The coil's a tickle. I want a strike. A real one. Something like what it'll get up there." She looked at him. "You can do lightning."

"I can do lightning," said Emeric.

He'd learned it years ago, out of one of the tower's books that Armand had brought south, because he'd wanted to know whether he could, and he could. He could do water too, and air, and light, a little. Any of them could be learned. What nobody told you, because nobody in the tower thought it worth saying, was how much it cost to work an element your body wasn't built for. He'd measured it once, on the bench, with a stone: about three times what the same work cost in fire. Most mages tried it once, went white, and never bothered again. He'd kept at it out of stubbornness.

He stood ten feet from the drum and lifted his hand toward the spike.

The glyph came up in front of his palm, the zigzag one, pale and blue and not as crisp as he'd have liked, wavering a little at the edges, the way a man's handwriting goes when he's writing with the wrong hand. He pushed. It cost him; he felt the cost go out of him all at once, the way it always did with this one, like a bucket kicked over. And the bolt went. It went from his hand to the spike in a crack that made Wren jump. It hit the spike and went round the drum along the strap and out into the floor plate, and Ambrose's needle swung across and back.

And then the end of it, the tail, which he hadn't put anywhere, came back up his own arm in a thin blue crawl, and set fire to the cuff of his shirt.

He put it out with his other hand. It took two goes.

"Well," said Tess. "The rod works."

"It works," said Ambrose, peering at his meter. "It went round, not through. Good."

Maeve had been sitting on Saban's crate with her bags at her feet, watching. She hadn't said anything during any of it. She was looking at his cuff, which was smoking, and then at his face. Then she got up.

"Who taught you that?" she said.

"Nobody. A book."

"It shows." She came over. She didn't touch his arm this time; she took his wrist and turned it, palm up, and looked at the hand as if it had done something to her personally. "You threw the whole of it. You threw it like a rock. All at once, as hard as you could, at the end of the spike, and then you let go of it and walked away." She shook her head. "It's not a rock. It doesn't want to go where you throw it. It wants to go home. You've got to give it a way home or it'll find one, and the nearest one's you." She let go of his wrist. "And you pushed far too much. You've got a rod. A rod's doing the work for you. You only have to show it the way."

Tess had gone very still on her stool.

"How do you know that?" said Emeric.

Maeve looked at him. Then at the shop: at Tess, at Ambrose, at Wren by the far bench with a spanner in her hand, at Armand in his chair with his eyes no longer half shut.

"Is there anyone in here," she said, "who'd tell a court?"

"No," said Emeric.

"Anyone who'd tell my father?"

He thought about it honestly. "Armand might."

"I wouldn't," said Armand. "I'm too old to get into your father's business. I'd only enjoy watching it."

Maeve nodded, slowly. Then she turned round to face BR-01 on its trestle, and the copper spike on its mast, and she didn't lift her hand very far at all. She lifted it the way you'd lift it to point a friend at something across a street.

The glyph came. It came up in front of her fingers crisp and blue-white and very small, no bigger than a coin, and so steady that it might have been painted on the air. And the bolt went out of it in a thin bright thread, without any crack, almost without a sound, a long bright stitch through the air from her fingertips to the top of the spike. It stayed. It hung there between them, a line of white fire as fine as a hair, humming, for the length of a slow breath. Ambrose's needle went across his dial and stayed there, steady, as if somebody had put a finger on it. Then she closed her hand, gently, as you'd close it round a moth, and the line wasn't there.

Nothing was on fire. Nothing smelled of anything.

"That's my base," said Maeve.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Your base," said Armand. He had sat forward in the chair. "Your affinity. Not something you learned. Born."

"Born. My mother saw it when I was four. I made the cat's fur stand up and set the curtains going." She looked at her hand. "She taught me the way she taught my brother air, at Emberfell, in the summers, out on the moor where nobody could see. And then she taught me never to do it anywhere else." She put the hand in her pocket. "At court, a girl who throws lightning gets married to whoever needs a war won. Mother said she'd not have it done to me. So at court I do pretty water tricks that anybody could learn and nobody's impressed by, and I lose at cards." She shrugged. "Father doesn't know. I don't think he does. He may have guessed. He's not stupid, he just doesn't look."

"Why show us?" said Tess.

Maeve looked at her for a moment. Then she looked at Emeric's cuff, which had stopped smoking and had a neat brown hole in it.

"Because I've just watched the king of Randillia set himself on fire," she said, "in front of a dustbin. And I'm going to be here all winter with nothing to do but wait for a stone. And somebody ought to." She took her hand out of her pocket. "Again. Not now. Your arm's still buzzing, I can feel it from here. Tomorrow. Six o'clock, before anybody's about, somewhere with a rod and nothing that'll burn." She considered him. "And you'll do exactly what I tell you, and you won't argue, because you're very bad at this, and I'm not."

"I'm not that bad."

"You set your shirt on fire."

"The rod worked."

"The rod worked," said Maeve, "in spite of you."

Wren laughed out loud at the far bench and then turned it into a cough, not very well. Tess didn't laugh. Tess was looking at Maeve's hand in its pocket, and then at the copper spike on top of BR-01, and Emeric could see her putting something away to look at later, the way she always did, and he thought he knew what it was. A girl who'd been hiding the thing she was best at, all her life, from the people who'd have used it. In a room where everybody had learned to keep something back.



She was at the Works yard at six the next morning in a borrowed oilskin, standing beside the old test mast with Bram's spare rod on it and the dew still on the gravel, and she made him do it twenty-two times.

The first eight times he threw it like a rock, and she stopped him, and made him start again. On the ninth she put her hand flat on his back between the shoulder blades and said, "There. That's where it starts. Not your hand. There," and he felt her own charge, very faintly, through his coat, like a cat's fur in a dry room, and something in him that had been wrong for years went a little less wrong.

On the fourteenth he gave it a way home. He didn't know how, quite; she told him to feel the ground under the rod, and the strap, and the earth plate Bram had buried at the mast's foot, and to think of the whole of it as one road, his hand to the spike to the strap to the ground, and to put the bolt on the road instead of throwing it at the end. It went. Not like hers; it cracked, and it was too thick, and it cost him twice what it ought to. But nothing came back up his arm.

"Better," said Maeve.

On the twenty-second his hand was shaking and she said that was enough, and they stood in the yard while the first of Bram's men came in at the gate with their dinner tins and looked at the king and the princess and the smoking test mast, and decided not to ask.

"Every morning," she said.

"Every morning?"

"Until the Hearing. Then every morning after that, until you go." She was coiling the earth strap over her arm, the way she'd seen Ambrose do it. "You won't be good. You'll never be good, it's not yours. But you'll stop being dangerous. And one day you'll need it and you'll be glad." She didn't say why she thought so. She didn't seem to know why she thought so herself.

The Hod went up over the castle at seven on her charging run, low and slow over the yard, her racks empty, and they both looked up. Along her rail in the early light was a row of knights going up to sit the Band's foot, in their flight coats, with Ward's broad shape at the end. Third from the end, Mira Holt was leaning on the rail looking down at the yard, her head turned a little further than anyone else's, the way she always turned it.

Maeve watched the Hod all the way out over the sea wall. She didn't say anything about it. When it had gone, she handed him the coiled strap.

"Six o'clock," she said, "tomorrow," and went off across the yard to find her breakfast, with the long braid swinging and the steel rings in it catching the sun.
