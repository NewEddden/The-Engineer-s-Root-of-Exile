Chapter 35 - Wolves

Saban had been on the road for nineteen days and his hands had cracked open in four places, which was the only part of winter trading he had never found a way around.

Everything else had a method. You went north before the frost hardened and came back after it did, because a rutted road that has frozen is a better road than a rutted road that has not. You paid for the inn and not for the stable, and then you slept in the stable, because an innkeeper who has been paid stops caring where you go. You kept the ledger in oilcloth and the oilcloth inside your coat and not in the cart, because carts burn and coats do not, usually.

For the hands there was nothing. Goose grease and a rag at night and a fortnight of it hurting every time you took up a rein.

The soap went out and the money came back and the money went out again.

That was the shape of the run and it had been the shape of the run for two years, and it had got bigger every season in a way that still surprised him when he looked at the totals. Kaldo soap into Wayford, into the two market towns above it, into Milford and past Milford to the river villages that had never in their lives seen soap arrive by the crate. Coin back. Coin out again as ore, coal, tin, lard and wool, and the ore was the big one and got bigger every quarter.

What Saban understood, and what he had never troubled to explain to anybody in Kaldo because he assumed a man who could build a foundry could work it out, was that a trade route leaks in both directions.

The soap told every market between the lake and the northern road that a place called Kaldo made a thing people wanted and could make a great deal of it. That was not a secret and could not be made into one. You cannot sell a thing quietly.

And the ore told one man, precisely and in writing, exactly how much Kaldo had to spend.

The questions came in the ordinary way, which is why nobody remembered them.

In a bonded warehouse in Wayford that smelled of wet rope and old tallow, a factor with a cough asked where the soap was coming from now, since it was plainly not coming from where it used to come from.

In an inn on the northern road, a man selling nothing at all asked how much more of it there was going to be, and whether Saban could commit to a volume in the spring.

In the yard at Milford, a steward who had bought eleven crates asked who owned it. Not who made it. Who owned it.

And at the Grayrock scale house, a clerk with ink on three fingers asked, while weighing out an order that took two hours to weigh, who exactly was paying for all this.

Saban answered all four the same way, which was pleasantly and with the truth, because the truth was not worth hiding and a man who hides a cheap thing is telling you he has an expensive one.

The reaction was the same every time and came in two beats, and it was the second beat that he had begun to dislike.

The first beat was the laugh. Kaldo was a joke and had been a joke for a decade, and the joke had a shape to it that everyone in the region knew by heart: the plague town, the dead barony, the place where they buried four hundred and stopped counting. Men laughed because the name arrived attached to that and the attachment was automatic.

The second beat was the recalculation, and it took about three seconds, and you could watch it happen behind a man's face.

Because the carts were real. The weight was real. The scale house had weighed it and written it down and a plague town does not move this much of anything. Whatever Kaldo had been a year and a half ago, the thing standing in this yard at this moment was four loaded carts and a merchant with clean books, and the two facts would not sit together, and every man Saban spoke to spent three seconds making them sit together and came out the other side with a different face on.

At the Grayrock scale house the clerk wrote the total into a long book with a ruled margin, and blotted it, and turned the page back to check something, and then forward again.

That book went up the hill at the end of every month. It had been going up the hill at the end of every month for eleven years, and for most of those eleven years the southern column in it had been a line of small numbers and a good many blanks. There was no rumour in it. There was no report in it, no whisper out of a market, nothing a man could be accused of having listened to.

There was a column, in one hand, in the same ink, running four years down the page, and what the column showed was a barony that everyone agreed was destitute buying iron in increasing quantity, quarter after quarter, and paying in coin, and not once, not in four years, arguing about the price.

Nobody had to tell the man who read that book anything at all. He counted it himself, out of his own ledger, in his own hall, and the arithmetic was not difficult and did not require a single thing that anybody would have called intelligence.

Saban, in the yard, took his receipt and thanked the clerk and went to eat.

What he had, by the end of the nineteenth day, was not information. He was extremely clear with himself about that, because the difference between information and a feeling was the whole of his trade and men who blurred it went bankrupt.

What he had was a reading of rooms, and he had been reading rooms for twenty years.

It was not hostility. He wanted to be careful about that too. Nobody had been cold with him, nobody had refused him a table, nobody had said one word against the southern barony in his hearing.

What he read was appetite.

And appetite was worse than hostility, and he knew why, and he wrote it in the notebook that night at a table with a bad candle, in his own shorthand, without dressing it up, because he never dressed anything up.

Hostility needs a reason. It comes out of something that happened and it can be argued with, paid off, apologised for or waited out. Appetite does not need a reason. Appetite only needs an opportunity, and it waits, and it is perfectly polite the entire time it is waiting.

They are hungry, and Kaldo is on the table.

Then he closed the notebook and went and did the job.

And the job went beautifully. That was the last thing, and he noted it with professional satisfaction and no suspicion whatsoever, because there was nothing in it to be suspicious of.

Everything loaded clean. Nobody short-weighed him anywhere. Nobody suddenly discovered a tariff. The Grayrock ore came in at the standard rate and came in on time and came in at full tonnage, and the scale house crew helped him rope it and one of them made a joke about the state of his hands.

That is exactly what a man buys when he is not ready yet, and it costs him nothing, and there is no way on earth to see it from inside.

Saban stood in the yard in the cold counting sacks off against his own tally, and got to the end of the list, and found it correct to the sack, and allowed himself the small private pleasure of a run with nothing wrong in it.

It was the last full ore shipment Kaldo would ever receive, and not one person in the barony would know that for another four months.

He gave the report in the small room with Ferra at the end of the table.

Goods first, always, because goods were what he was paid for. Tonnage, condition, price against the contracted price, two items short-supplied and the reason, a dispute at Milford that he had settled himself for less than it would have cost to bring home. Ferra took all of it down and asked six questions and every one of them was about a number.

Then he shut the goods book and said the other thing in four sentences.

"There is an appetite in the region and Kaldo is what it is pointed at. I have no name and no document and nothing I would repeat in front of a magistrate. What I have is that four men in five weeks asked me who owns the soap and who pays for the ore, and none of them had any business needing to know."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"They know Kaldo was weak. They do not know it has stopped being weak."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"How old is their picture," Emeric said.

"A year and a half. Perhaps two."

"And where did they get it."

Saban looked at him, and did not answer, because he had worked it out on the road and had been hoping not to have to be the one to say it.

Emeric worked it out in the room.

It took him perhaps four seconds and it was not a pleasant four seconds, and what made it worse was that there was nothing wrong with any of the reasoning that had brought him here.

Wexler's men had been turned out publicly. That had been the correct decision. It had been the correct decision for six separate reasons and he could still recite all six: the town had needed to see it, the remaining garrison had needed to see it, a quiet dismissal would have left the rot in the building, and a man who has been sacked in the dark tells a better story about himself than a man who has been sacked in daylight.

And somewhere between nineteen and twenty-odd men had walked out through the Western Gate carrying an exact and accurate description of a barony with a dead farm, an empty treasury, fifty-eight demoralised men on a wall and a foreign prince who did not know what he was doing.

Every word of which had been true on the day they left.

And they had walked, because men walk toward food, to the nearest halls that would feed them in exchange for a story about the place they had come from.

He had thrown them out and they had landed somewhere, and the somewhere had been Grayrock and Wayford, and he had personally armed three baronies with a map of a place that no longer existed.

He said none of that out loud. He did not have to.

Ferra did not offer him anything, which he was grateful for later and was not grateful for at the time. Saban, who had spent twenty years in rooms where men found out expensive things about their own past decisions, looked at the table and gave him the length of time it took.

"There is no proof in any of this," Saban said eventually.

"I know."

"I want it said plainly, because you are going to spend money on it. No document. No name. Nobody has sworn anything to anybody in front of me. It is a feeling built out of twenty years and a great many small things, and I have been wrong with a feeling before and it cost a man in Wayford his warehouse."

"That is why I believe you," Emeric said.

And that was true and was not flattery. The reason he had trusted this man's tonnage for two years was that Saban told him when a number was soft. A man who qualifies his own evidence is a man who has a scale in his head. A man who brings you certainty every time has only ever got one setting and it is not worth anything.

Renner's answer to the meeting request was nothing.

No refusal. No acknowledgement. Not a line from a steward regretting that the baron was engaged. It had gone north in the autumn with the carts and the spring date in it had come and gone and no reply of any description had ever arrived.

So Emeric decided to find out where he stood, and there was exactly one instrument available to a man in his position, and it was a pen.

He wrote to all three. Renner, Sethe, Paxton. Formal, courteous, short. Trade terms that were frankly generous, laid out in plain language so that no clerk anywhere could pretend to be confused. A proposal to meet, at their convenience, on their ground if they preferred. Ferra read every one of them twice for anything that could be taken badly by a man looking to take something badly, and found two phrases and he changed both.

Nothing came back.

And what he did next was, Ferra thought afterward, the single most characteristic thing she ever watched him do, because he assumed failure before he assumed malice, and he kept assuming it long past the point where most men would have stopped.

A lost letter is likelier than a snub. Roads are bad, couriers are men, seals break, a steward drops a packet behind a chest and finds it at midsummer. So he wrote copies and sent them.

Nothing.

So he used different couriers, three of them, separately, on three different days, because one bad courier is a coincidence and three is not.

Nothing.

So he stopped using couriers at all. He paid a merchant, a real one with a real business who had no interest in any of this beyond the fee, to carry sealed letters in his own hand, to put them into the hands of a steward at each hall, and to stand there and watch the man take them.

The merchant came back and reported, and named the three stewards, and described the rooms, and was paid.

Three baronies. Confirmed delivery. Still nothing.

His conclusion took him one evening and he wrote it out and it was not stupid.

Three men had received a courteous letter from a neighbour, in their own hands, in front of a witness, and not one of them had sent back so much as a refusal. A refusal costs four lines and a seal. Any one of them could have declined and cost himself nothing. None of them had, and they had all not done it, and they had all not done it at the same time.

They have decided something, he wrote, and they have decided it together, and the silence is not an accident. Three baronies acting as one.

The first half of that was correct. The second half was not, and he would carry it for a long time, and the distance between a thing three men have separately agreed to and a thing three men are is enormous and does not look like anything from a hundred miles away with no reply in your hand.

There were no letters to Armand. There had never been any letters to Armand. That silence had a different cause entirely and it was sitting in the same room as him every evening, and he did not put it on the same page as the other one, and he did not put it on any page at all.

Paxton's letter came six weeks later and did not come by courier.

It came inside a consignment of hides, folded into oilcloth, and the carter who brought it did not know he was carrying it and was not paid for it and was never told. Emeric worked that out from the state of the oilcloth before he had read a line, and understood what it meant about the man who had sent it, and sat down before opening it.

It was not a friendly letter and it did not pretend to be. It opened without any courtesy at all.

I have nothing against you. I am writing this at risk to myself, to my people and to my land, and I would ask you to burn it, and I expect you will not.

Renner and Sethe, it said, were not happy with the progress Kaldo had made. That was the whole of the stated grievance and Paxton did not elaborate on it, either because he did not know more or because he did not want to put more on paper.

He had no stake in it. He said so twice. He also did not have the strength to stand out of it, and he explained why in a single sentence that Emeric read three times: a man who refuses to come is the next man they come for, and Milford is open plain with one product and no walls worth the name.

Nor could he side with Kaldo. He was blunt about that and did not hide behind it. Everything he had been told said Kaldo was weak, and he said plainly where everything he had been told came from, which was Renner, and men who used to stand on Emeric's own walls.

They intended to march.

Emeric could submit, in which case he would be pushed into the position that Milford was already in, and Paxton wrote out what that position was in four lines with no self-pity in any of them. Or he could fight, and perhaps win. Paxton could not advise which, because he did not know Kaldo's strength, and said so.

The last part was the part that did the damage.

My actions from here are not taken in full conscience. I am aware of what I am. My hands are tied by a responsibility to my own that I did not ask for and cannot put down.

Emeric put the letter on the table.

"What the fuck," he said.

There was nobody in the room. He said it anyway, flatly, at the table, in a language nobody in this world had ever heard.

Because here was the whole thing in one piece of paper. Somewhere out there, three days west, there was a decent man. An actually decent man, the first one he had encountered outside his own walls since the day he was exiled, and this man had taken a real risk to give him a real warning for no gain of any kind.

And that man was going to march on him anyway.

Both of those facts were in the same handwriting, on the same sheet, in the same ink, and there was no version of the world where you got the first without the second, and Emeric sat with it for a long time and could not find anywhere to be angry at.

He answered once.

He did not beg and he did not threaten and he did not make a case. He told Paxton, in four lines, that Kaldo was not what he had been told, and that he was not going to offer a single thing to prove it, because anything he offered as proof would travel.

And then the fifth line, which was the only one that mattered.

If Milford's colours hang back on the day, Milford's colours will not be engaged. You have my word on it and you have no reason to take it.

Paxton did not answer.

He did not believe it, and he was not a fool for not believing it, because there is no reason on earth for a man outnumbered five to one to give away the only thing he has, and a promise of that kind out of a weak barony is exactly what a desperate man writes.

He hung back anyway, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the letter, and he did not understand that he had been told the truth until he had watched Renner die.

Two people read Paxton's letter, and neither of them was one of the forty.

Ferra read it because she costed everything and this was going to cost everything. Ward read it because Ward had to know.

Nobody else. Not the garrison, not Farris, not Bram, not Doran. Forty men do not need a piece of paper in their heads that says a neighbour is coming to kill them and feels bad about it, because that paper does not make one of them shoot straighter and it makes all of them talk. A year earlier that would have cost nothing, because most of the forty could not have read it if it had been left open on a table. Enough of them could now that it went into a drawer with a lock.

Ward read it twice and gave it back.

"When," he said.

"It does not say."

"No," Ward said. "They will not have decided."

And that was the worst sentence anybody said to him that winter, because it was obviously true and Emeric had not thought of it. There was no date. There was no date because three men in three halls had not yet finished arguing about whose men went first, and when they finished arguing the date would exist, and the first Kaldo would hear of it would be a column on the river road.

A war was coming, it was dated by nobody, and the only man who had warned him was going to be in it.
