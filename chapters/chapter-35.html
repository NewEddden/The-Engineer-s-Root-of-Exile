Chapter 35 - The Register

The masons started on the span in the first week of April, as soon as the mason said the frost was out of the ground and not a day before.

He had his twenty men. Some of them had laid stone before the war and some had only carried it, and he put the carriers under the layers and did not explain the arrangement to anybody. The carpenter's frame went up first, a great timber hoop on legs standing in the gap between the two broken ends, braced to the piers and wedged at its feet, with the trough still running over the top of it on its trestles and dripping on the men below. The stones went onto the frame one course at a time, from both ends toward the middle, and every one of them was set down by the mason or by somebody he was watching.

Emeric went out to look on the second morning and was told, civilly, that the frame was not a bench and could not be sat on.

"When does it come out?"

"When the keystone's in and the mortar's had its month." The mason did not look round. "Then we knock the wedges and see."

"See what?"

The mason set a stone, tapped it twice with the heel of his hammer and moved to the next.

"Whether I was right," he said.



He went down to the ward on the evening before he rode out.

He had meant to go since August. He had meant to go on her step in October, when she stood with her terms in her hand and told him she had sent letters through that castle before, and he had said nothing, and he had gone on meaning to go every week since and finding a reason in each week why it should be the next one. He was leaving for the hills in the morning, and he would be gone a week at least, and he did not want to ride two days with it still unsaid behind him.

He stood at the side door. She came to it with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a basin in her hands, and looked at him, and looked at the hour.

"Somebody hurt?"

"No."

"Then it'll keep till morning."

"I'm gone in the morning." He did not move off the step. "It's about your letters."

She put the basin down on the bench inside the door. Then she came out into the yard, where the kettles were cooling on the laundry hearth and a line of sheets hung white and stiff in the last of the light, and pulled the door to behind her, so that whatever was said would be said out here and not in her ward.

He told her plainly, because there was no other way to tell it that did not make it worse.

Her three letters had never left the castle. They had been entered in the castle's outgoing register, each one, with its date, in the first year of the plague, the way every letter that went out of that house was entered. And then they had stopped. No rider had carried them. Nobody had forwarded them to the crown or the Circle or anyone else. Wexler had had them written into a book and left them there.

"And you know that how?"

"Because they were read out at the hearing. In the capital, in August. From the register." He made himself go on. "Your name, three times, in a clerk's hand, on the table in front of the magistrate. On my side of the table. I didn't know it was going in until it was read, and when I did know, I didn't tell you. I didn't tell you when I came home, and I didn't tell you in October when you stood on that step and told me yourself."

She did not say anything.

The light was going. Over the wall of the yard somebody's child was being called in, twice, and then a third time with the full name.

"Who read them?"

"A clerk."

"Out loud?"

"The dates and your name. And what they asked for."

"In front of who?"

"The magistrate. His clerks. Wexler. Me. Whoever else was in the room."

"And they're in his book now. The judge's. With my name on them."

"Yes."

"For what?"

"To show what kind of man he was."

She turned her head and looked at the sheets.

"They were mine," she said.

She did not raise her voice. He had been ready for her to, and this was worse.

"I wrote those at night. The first winter. I had the rows going in every week and nobody to tell me what I was doing wrong, and I wrote three times to anybody who could read, asking for somebody who knew more than I did. Three times. I said what we had and what we hadn't and how many." Her hands had found the edge of the bench by the hearth and were holding it. "He put them in a book and kept them. And then somebody took them out of his book and put them in another one, to win something, and nobody asked me. Nobody came. Nobody came then and nobody asked me now."

"No."

"And I can't say any of it to him."

"No," Emeric said. "He's dead."

"I know he's dead." She let go of the bench. "There's nobody left to say it to. You understand that? I've been angry at that man for five years, and I'm angry now, and he's in the ground somewhere north of here, and the book's in the capital, and the only one standing in my yard is you."

"Then say it to me."

She turned from the sheets and looked at him.

"I just did," she said.

She picked up the basin and went back in, and the door stayed open behind her about the width of a hand.

He stood in the yard. The sheets moved a little on the line. He did not go in, because she had not said he could, and he did not go home, because he did not want to, and in the end her voice came out through the gap in the door, flat and busy.

"If you're stopping, the kettles want scalding before they go back on."

He scalded the kettles.

"Sheets are dry," she said through the gap. "Bring them in."

He brought them in, and folded them the way her two women folded them, in three and then in three again, and got it wrong, and was shown once how it went, without comment, and got it right. A sheet took two. She had the other end, and each time they walked the ends together her hands met his at the fold, and she took his corners from him as she would have taken them from any of her women. He held a lamp at the end of a bed while she cleaned a carter's hand that had been opened on a hook. He emptied what needed emptying into the covered drain under the east wall and came back and washed his own hands twice at the corner tap, because she was watching, and she nodded once at the second time.

Neither of them talked much. There was a woman in the CATCHING room with a cough, and an old man in the third bed who wanted to tell somebody about a horse he had owned before the war, and Emeric heard about the horse.

When it was full dark and the lamps were lit along both rows, Imani took the lamp out of his hand and gave him a smaller one for the hill.

"You'll be on the road early," she said.

He went up the hill with it.



Bram rode in the cart.

He rode on a sack of oats among the tools, with his back against the tailboard and his arms folded and his face set against the whole idea of travel, and when Rylan offered him a horse at the first stop out of pure kindness he looked at the horse as though it had said something about his daughter.

"I'll walk before I get on that."

"You'd be two days behind us."

"Then I'd be two days behind you," Bram said, and got back on his sack.

It was two days, as Hale had said. The first was the road north along the river, and then a track that left the river and went up into the barony's hills between walled fields that had not been worked since the plague and had gone back to thorn and bracken. They slept the first night in a farmer's barn, for which the farmer refused money and then accepted it, and Ward put Emeric on the ground in the farmyard at first light with the farmer's children watching from the hay, because the road was no reason to stop. On the second day the track got worse and the hills got steeper and the cart went at walking pace, and then slower, and Rylan and Emeric walked beside it for the last of the afternoon with a hand on the tailboard to help it up.

They smelled the hamlet before they saw it. Woodsmoke, a little. Goats. And under both, faint and sour, a smell Emeric did not place until Bram, behind him, said one word.

"Spoil."



The hamlet sat in a fold of the hill below the workings, a single crooked street of stone houses with turf on the roofs, most of them dark. Eight or nine had smoke. The rest had doors that had been shut a long time and grass growing up against them.

The men came out to look at the cart. There were perhaps a dozen of them, and most were past forty, and all of them had the same hands, thick across the knuckle and scarred along the backs, and the same way of standing with their weight a little forward, like men who had spent their lives bent under a low roof. They knew who he was. They had not expected him and did not seem to think much of it now that he was here.

The oldest of them did the talking. He had worked the hill since he was nine, he said, and his father before him, and he would show the prince what there was to show, which was holes.

It was holes.

He walked them along the hillside the next morning, from one working to the next, a long uneven line of them following the seams along the slope: pits like opened graves, and short tunnels driven straight into the hill to the depth of a few yards, and here and there a shaft going down. Spoil heaps lay below each one, rust-red and grey, grown over with a thin sour grass that the goats would not eat. Every working was cut by hand. Every working stopped.

And every one stopped in water.

Emeric went into the nearest of the tunnels as far as the water let him, with a lamp, and Ward behind him because Ward would not stay at the mouth. The roof was low enough that he went bent double, and the walls were close enough to touch on both sides, scarred all over with the marks of bars and wedges. The seam ran through the rock at the height of his shoulder, a dull rusty band a hand wide with a glitter in it when the lamp came near. He put his palm on it. It was wet. Everything was wet. The water did not come in at any one place. It came through the rock itself, a slow sweat that gathered and ran and dripped and found the floor and went on down the tunnel ahead of him into the black.

He counted drips at the face, and timed them against his own pulse, and did a sum, and came out pleased with it.

The old miner listened to the sum with his head on one side.

"That's April," he said. "Hill's full of snow-water in April. Come back in August and it'll be half that. Come back in November and it's twice."

Emeric did the sum again with both of those in it. It was a different sum, and a much worse one, and the old man watched him do it with every sign of satisfaction.

At the bottom of the pits it lay black and still, with a skin on it. In the tunnels it came up to the knee a few yards in and kept rising into the dark. At the deepest shaft the old miner stopped, and picked up a stone, and dropped it, and they all listened to it go a long way down before it hit water.

"That's where the good ore went," he said. "Down. The seam turns. You follow it down with your bar, and the hill starts to weep, and you bail, and it weeps faster than you bail, and in the end you're stood to your waist with a bucket and the ore's under your feet. My father bailed that one three summers. Then the plague came and there was nobody to buy what he got out of it anyway."

"What did you break the rock with?"

The old man looked at him as though it were a child's question.

"Hammer. Bar. Wedges." He shrugged. "Fire, when we had the wood. Light it on the face, let it burn, throw water on it, and it cracks. My grandfather did more of that. Then the baron started fining us for the wood, so we did less."

"Nothing else? Nothing that breaks rock for you?"

The men exchanged a look, the look of men asked by a stranger whether they had ever thought of using the sky.

"There's nothing else," the old man said.

There was not. Emeric had known it, more or less, since the capital, where nobody had ever spoken of anything that went off with a bang except lightning and a mage. He knew what would do it. He knew roughly what it was made of and roughly how, and he stood at the mouth of that drowned shaft with the knowledge sitting in him, and kept it there. The first thing anybody in this kingdom would do with a powder that broke rock was not going to be mining.

"Water's the trouble," Bram said beside him. He had been crouched at the edge of the pit with a lump of the spoil in his hand, turning it. "Not the rock. The rock's soft enough. It's the water."

"Yes."

"You can't hammer water."

"No," Emeric said. "You have to lift it."

The old miner heard that and gave a short laugh with no unkindness in it.

"Lift it with what?"

Emeric looked at the shaft, and at the slope, and at the stream that came down the fold of the hill past the hamlet's houses, quick and cold, with nothing on it.

"I don't know yet," he said, which was true, and which the old man seemed to like better than anything else he had said all morning.



That night they ate with the hamlet, because it was offered and because refusing would have been worse.

It was a thin meal, goat and barley and a sour dark bread, eaten in the biggest of the stone houses with a fire of turf on the hearth because there was no wood. Rylan made the children laugh by losing to them at a game with knucklebones and then complaining about it. Ward ate in silence by the door. The men asked Bram about the forge in Kaldo, and whether it was true he had made plows that winter, and how much iron a plow took, and Bram told them, and for the first time since the cart left Kaldo he looked almost content.

Afterward one of the younger men brought in a basket of ore from the nearest heap, because the prince had asked to see it burned.

Bram picked out a lump the size of a fist and set it in the heart of the turf fire with the tongs, and they waited.

The turf was slow to take it. Then the smell came up off the fire into the low room, thick and yellowish and foul, and two of the children shrieked and held their noses, and their mother laughed at them for it, and the old miner sat back from the hearth with the patience of a man who had smelled it every day of his working life.

Rotten eggs.

"There," Bram said, to Emeric, with grim satisfaction. "Hill ore. Told you."

Emeric was leaning toward the hearth instead of away from it. He was not thinking about iron.

"Yes," he said. "You did."
